# The Impact of Situational Achievement Goals on Online Learning Behavior: Results from Field Experiments

Nasim Mousavi,<sup>a</sup> Sina Golara,<sup>a,</sup>\* Jesse Bockstedt<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Despite their prevalence, online learning platforms have difficulty sustaining user motivation, resulting in low engagement and poor performance. Addressing this challenge, we study how these platforms can boost learner motivation by fostering an effective learning environment and inducing different situational goals. We conducted a randomized field experiment with behavioral interventions inspired by the achievement goal theory in a massive open online course with more than 2,000 learners from 171 countries. Using various econometric analyses, we estimate the effects of interventions based on three situational achievement goals: learning, performance-prove, and performance-avoidance. In contrast to the traditional (offline) education literature, which finds learning goals to be the most effective and performance goals to be inferior, we demonstrate that performanceprove goals are the most effective in enhancing online engagement and performance. We trace the roots of this finding to the differences in the structure of online and offline environments and the psychological needs of online learners. We uncover the underlying mechanism by empirically examining how situational achievement goals stimulate different achievement emotions necessary to maintain engagement in the learning process. We fur ther find that the effectiveness of each goal depends on learners’ characteristics: prior performance and social activity. Learners with stronger prior performance benefit more from the performance-prove goal, whereas those with moderate performance levels gain from the performance-avoidance goal, and those with lower prior performance are positively influenced by the learning goal. Socially isolated learners respond best to performance goals. Through a second field experiment, we explore the optimal combination of situational goals. We find that combining learning and performance-prove goals leads to the highest learning outcomes. Our study offers theoretical contributions and practical implications for scholars and platform providers on how to effectively leverage situationa achievement goals and related achievement emotions for improving online user outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

Online learning platforms have become popular tools for delivering education, providing convenient and affordable learning opportunities to people worldwide without the usual constraints of time and location (Hamilton and Beagle 2024). The online learning market has grown substantially, reaching a value of more than \$300 billion by 2022, and is projected to expand at a compound annual growth rate of 14% through 2032 (Global Market Insight 2023). This trend has accelerated since the COVID-19 pandemic, with more than 200,000 massive open online courses (MOOCs) attracting more than 220 million users globally by the end of 2021 (Pickard et al. 2023). Despite its widespread adoption and importance, the online learning industry faces significant challenges in keeping learners motivated, resulting in reduced engagement and performance (Hartnett 2016).

Recognizing this challenge, scholars, especially in information systems, have shown a growing interest in studying user behavior in online learning environments, the fundamental mechanisms driving it, and strategies for improving learner engagement and performance (Diaz-Infante et al. 2022, Leung et al. 2022, Huang et al. 2023). Building on previous research, in this study, we aim to deepen our understanding of the motivational behavior demonstrated by online learners. By implementing behavioral interventions rooted in the achievement goal theory, we examine how various situational goals influence learner behavior and how providers can structure online courses based on achievement goals to optimize user engagement and performance.

## 1.1. Research Motivation

Online learning provides substantial flexibility, enabling learners to acquire knowledge and new skills at their own pace, free of classroom constraints, and access content on demand. Despite these advantages, high flexibility and lack of external motivators are recognized as major contributing factors to low engagement rates, poor performance, and high dropout rates in online learning (Hartnett 2016). Under the conventional platform designs, learners are typically granted maximum control to work through courses independently and in isolation, with minimal interaction or guidance from instructors and peers (Huang et al. 2023). Learners are expected to motivate themselves and autonomously direct their attention, cognition, and behavior to complete courses successfully. However, research has shown that learners face challenges when trying to self-direct their learning in such an environment, often resulting in a decline in motivation (Santhanam et al. 2008).

Research in motivation reveals that the structure of a task environment can strongly impact motivation and performance, particularly within achievementoriented settings such as education (Ross and Nisbett 2011). Educators and providers can create an environment that supports learners’ motivational needs by considering various behavioral factors affecting their emotions, cognition, and motivation. Adopting a particular goal has proven to be an effective method for maintaining motivation (Locke and Latham 2013). According to the achievement goal theory, instructors and providers can influence learners’ motivation and engagement by directing them toward adopting a specific achievement goal (Dragoni 2005).

The achievement goal theory identifies three general goals: learning, performance-prove, and performanceavoidance (Brett and VandeWalle 1999). The learning goal emphasizes the intrinsic value of learning and mastery, and the performance goals focus on outcomes and ability (Dweck and Leggett 1988). Specifically, the performance-prove goal emphasizes realized outcomes, ability, and competence demonstration, whereas the performance-avoidance goal focuses on avoiding unacceptable outcomes and failure (VandeWalle 1997). Based on the theory, achievement goals can be classified as dispositional or situational. Dispositional achievement goals represent persistent traits that reflect the value individuals inherently place on learning or performance (Brett and VandeWalle 1999). Situational achievement goals reflect the current inclination of individuals toward specific goals, shaped by their prominence and relevance in the surrounding environment (VandeWalle et al. 2019). Essentially, situational goals are those that individuals adopt in their current situation and in response to environmental cues.

Most existing research in achievement goal theory focuses on the relationships between dispositional achievement goals and individuals’ behavior (Dragoni 2005). However, dispositional goals are, by definition, hard to change, and thus give practitioners limited ability to influence individuals’ behavior. Given the adaptability of situational achievement goals, they have attracted more attention in recent years (Bardach et al. 2020). Unlike dispositional goals, which are inherent and fixed, situational goals can be manipu lated and adjusted based on their emphasis within a task, influencing individuals’ behavior.

Only a handful of studies explore situational achievement goals in offline settings. They highlight a positive relationship between learning goals and individual performance in offline settings (Church et al. 2001, Turner et al. 2002, Karabenick 2004). However, the results are not consistent for performance goals. Some studies show a positive relationship between performance goals and individuals’ performance, others found a negative relationship, and some showed no relationship (Brett and VandeWalle 1999, Jaakkola et al. 2016). These studies are primarily descriptive and correlational, offering limited insight into the causal impact of achievement goals on user behavior. Furthermore, rather than capturing users actual behavior, these studies have focused on students perceptions measured by surveys, which do not accurately measure individuals’ actual behavior (Grant and Dweck 2003). Also, many current studies adopt a onetime trial approach, where treatment is administered, and students’ perceived behavior is assessed through surveys. This highlights the need for longitudinal investigations into situational achievement goals (Bardach et al. 2020).

Additionally, studies in this area primarily focus on traditional face-to-face education. Yet, results from these settings may not directly apply to online environments due to differing dynamics of motivation and learning (Hartnett 2016). There are critical differences between online and offline education that are relevant for the adoption of achievement goals. In traditional physical classrooms, teachers typically exert more control over the learning environment, and students progress through the course is closely monitored; in such situations, further emphasizing performance goals might create a stressful environment for students, negatively impacting their behavior (Halvari et al. 2011). Creating an environment that fosters learning goals might put less pressure on students and result in more positive outcomes in these settings. However, in online learning, learners have more autonomy and limited guidance from instructors (Wong et al. 2019). In such an environment, motivation is often driven by intrinsic interests and task value, reducing the effectiveness of emphasizing learning goals and the inherent value of learning compared with offline settings (Hartnett 2016). The lack of physical presence and the limited communication in online environments can also affect students’ ability to demonstrate competency and feel a sense of competition, potentially influencing their reaction to performance goals.

Another limitation of the existing literature is that studies have primarily focused on contexts with a homogeneous population of individuals. In contrast, online learning environments, especially MOOCs, tend to have a more diverse learner population from different countries and backgrounds with various needs. Hence, there is a need to investigate and comprehend the efficacy of each situational goal within such a diverse population. We need a better understanding of the contingencies that make users more or less prone to benefit from situational achievement goals. The existing research offers limited guidance in this regard.

As a result of these limitations, there has been little exploration and validation of practical interventions that online learning platforms can use to enhance learning outcomes, along with the conditions that determine the effectiveness of each goal. Scholars who pioneered the achievement goal theory have emphasized the scarcity of research examining the efficacy of situational achievement goals as interventions in realworld scenarios (Elliot et al. 2017, VandeWalle et al. 2019). In response to this call for action, our study takes a step forward by examining how situational achievement goals can influence learners’ motivations when they are applied as behavioral interventions. Notably, this study represents the first exploration of the role of situational achievement goals in learners behavior within online learning environments.

## 1.2. Research Model and Contribution

Given the significance of online learners’ engagement in practical applications, our aim in this paper is to explore how designing a learning environment centered on situational achievement goals can address the challenges faced by learning platforms. To this end, we developed behavioral interventions based on the achievement goal theory to explore our primary research question: How do different situational achievement goals impact user engagement and performance in online learning platforms? After exploring the main effect, it is natural to investigate whether all users react the same to achievement goals. Hence, we pose our second research question: Which online learning users benefit more or less from each type of situational achievement goal? The achievement goal literature in the offline setting has found users’ abilities and skills to be relevant to their motivation, impacting the effectiveness of various goal structures (Linnenbrink-Garcia et al. 2008). Furthermore, learners’ level of social interaction has been shown to affect their motivation in online learning (Zhang et al. 2017). Hence, we explore the moderating roles of users’ prior performance and social activity.

To address these research questions, we conducted multiple randomized experiments. First, we conducted a large field experiment on a MOOC with 2,165 users, offered and administered by a leading U.S. business school. By tracking user behavior over time, before and after the interventions, we find evi dence that users’ online engagement and performance can be enhanced by inducing situational goals. Contrary to the findings from the existing offline literature (Van Hooft and Noordzij 2009), we observe that the performance-prove goal is more influential on user outcomes in the online environment than the learning goal. The users treated with the performance-prove goal spent, on average, 3.785 additional hours engaging with course materials and achieved 8.4% (8.4 out of 100 points) higher grades. By comparison, the users treated with learning and performance-avoidance goals spent 2.709 and 2.826 more hours, respectively, on the course content, but their average gain in performance was not significant.

Our results show that prior performance and social activity moderate these results. The performance-prove goal primarily benefited top performers, and the performance-avoidance goal enhanced midperformers outcomes. Interestingly, performance-avoidance goals do not demonstrate the adverse effects that the offline litera ture ascribes to them (Karabenick 2004) and are helpfu for the subset of learners performing in the middle range. Low performers, on the other hand, benefit only from the learning goal. Pushing for higher performance is ineffective for students with poor prior performance. This finding highlights the importance of personalization and the risks of broad-based motivational interventions without considering student characteristics. Moreover, we observe that the effects of performance-prove and performanceavoidance treatments are moderated by prior social activity. These treatments are only effective for less socially active students, suggesting that loss of the social aspect in online education leaves these learners with a motivationa deficit that can be effectively mitigated by performancebased goal treatments.

Based on these findings, we also investigate whether platform operations can combine learning and performance treatments (goals) to maximize benefits. To investigate this, we conducted a second field experi ment within an online course, monitoring learners behavior longitudinally. Our findings reveal that the most favorable outcomes occur when learning and performance-prove goals are combined.

We also investigate the mechanism driving these results. Situational achievement goals are believed to impact individuals’ behavior by significantly affecting their achievement-related emotions and thought processes (Pekrun et al. 2006). Achievement goals guide individuals’ attention, shaping how they perceive situations and feel about themselves in relation to these situations (Pekrun et al. 2023). Relying on these theoretical arguments, we assess the underlying mechanism of our findings by empirically testing the mediation effect of achievement emotions. Results indicate that achievement emotions significantly mediate the relationship between treatments and learning outcomes, empirically validating our hypothesized mechanism.

This paper seeks to contribute to the information systems literature by suggesting that the lack of consideration for achievement goals and associated emotions may be a significant factor contributing to low learner engagement and weak performance. By exploring various situational goals, we demonstrate that focusing on superior outcomes can motivate learners best. However, the effectiveness of each explored goal can differ based on learners’ characteristics. Instructors can boost learners’ engagement and performance by aligning the environmental aspects with learners’ characteristics. The results of our study also emphasize the critical role of achievement emotions in learners’ motivation. Our results also contribute to the literature on achievement goal theory by exploring situational achievement goals as interventions in a real-world achievement setting.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the role of situational goals in online learning. We extend the current literature by providing evidence from large-scale field experiments on the causal effects of altering goal structures in online platforms. Our paper offers significant guidance for managers and online course designers by developing a simple and effective strategy to address the pervasive engagement problem in online learning. Our results emphasize the importance of considering the dynamics of learners’ emotions, especially achievement emotions, in the learning journey. This aspect, although fundamental to the motivation and engagement of learners, has received comparatively less attention in online learning environments (Liu et al. 2022). We demonstrate how providers can regulate learners’ behavior by considering the different aspects of learners’ psychological and emotional needs. The interventions in this study are virtually costless to MOOC providers yet effectively induce positive learning outcomes.

## 2. Background and Theory

Our paper is related to the literature on achievement goal theory and its applications in online learning.

## 2.1. Achievement Goal Theory

Achievement goal theory is a prominent framework in motivational research, especially within educational settings. It examines the impact of individuals’ pursuit of achievement goals on their attitudes, effort, and performance in learning tasks (Meece et al. 2006). This theory explores how various goals contribute to individuals’ perceptions of success and failure, shaping their motivation, emotions, and cognitive processes and influencing their engagement and performance outcomes (Ames 1992, Pintrich 2000b). According to this theory, there are three broad achievement goals: learn ing (mastery), performance-prove, and performanceavoidance (VandeWalle and Cummings 1997).

The learning goal drives individuals to focus on the intrinsic value of learning and gaining new skills (Ames and Archer 1988). This achievement goal motivates individuals to learn for mastery and presents challenges as opportunities to develop new capabilities (Ames and Ames 1984). The performance-prove goal emphasizes demonstrating ability and a sense of self-worth. This can be achieved by meeting normative standards or expectations and outperforming others (Ames 1992, Ford et al. 1998, Brett and Vande Walle 1999). Finally, the performance-avoidance goal focuses on preventing negative evaluation or criticism of one’s performance. It stems from a fear of failure, a desire to protect one’s self-esteem, and to avoid potential negative consequences of not meeting performance standards or expectations (VandeWalle and Cummings 1997).

2.1.1. Dispositional vs. Situational Achievement Goals. Achievement goal theory explains the roles of both personal traits (dispositional achievement goals) and contextual factors (situational goal structures) (Ames 1992, Solmon 1996). A dispositional achievement goal, known as goal orientation in the literature, reflects an individual’s innate inclination to embrace a specific achievement goal across diverse situations and contexts (Dweck 1986). Dispositional goals represent enduring traits that can shape an individual’s approach to tasks, motivation, and overall behavior in academic or other achievement-oriented environments. Dispositional goals have traditionally received more attention in motivation research, with the majority of studies focusing on the relationship between dispositional achievement and behavior (Brett and VandeWalle 1999). Generally, in this stream of research, dispositional goals are surveyed and then linked to their behavior in a specific task, such as effort or performance (for a meta-analysis, see Hulleman et al. 2010).

Recognizing the crucial role of achievement goals in individual motivation, scholars have increasingly concentrated on uncovering how goals are developed and whether they can be shaped by the environment (Bardach et al. 2020). This exploration has resulted in an increased focus on the concept of situational goal structures, which distinctively influence individuals motivation, effort, persistence, and eventual performance. As we explain in Section 2.1.2, the distinctive aspect of situational goal structures is that they are not fixed and can be shaped by the environment. This concept opens up the possibility for scholars and practitioners to examine whether individuals can be prompted to adopt specific achievement objectives within specific contexts (Bardach et al. 2020). Consequently, scholars are now directing their attention to comprehending and designing effective goal structures to foster desirable outcomes, such as higher motivation and performance (VandeWalle et al. 2019, Bardach et al. 2020, Urdan and Kaplan 2020).

Despite the significant implications, only a few studies have investigated situational goal structures (Urdan and Kaplan 2020). This limited knowledge of situational goals and their influence on user behavior underscores the need for further research, particularly from the original theory developer (Elliot et al. 2017, VandeWalle et al. 2019). This exploration is crucial as findings from dispositional research may not necessarily apply to situational goals (Bouchard et al. 2004, Cohrs et al. 2006). In this paper, we address this gap by examining the impact of situational goals on online learning behavior. The subsequent section reviews the existing literature on situational achievement goals in learning environments, elucidating how our study contributes to this area.

2.1.2. Situational Achievement Goals. Situational achievement goals are context-specific goals that individuals adopt based on their prominence in a particular environment (VandeWalle et al. 2019). Adoption involves dynamically adjusting achievement-related goals, motivations, and behaviors based on specific circumstances or demands in a given situation. Situational achievement goals can influence learners’ behavior by activating/deactivating various achievement emotions, such as joy, pride, hope, anxiety, and boredom (Pekrun et al. 2006), as explained in Section 2.1.3

The learning goal structure focuses on the inherent value of the task, cultivates interest and intrinsic motivation, and facilitates the utilization of adaptable learning strategies and self-regulation (Ames 1992, Hulleman et al. 2010). This type of goal structure usually results in enhanced motivation and academic performance by activating positive emotions, such as enjoyment (Pekrun et al. 2006). Studies in in-person education have shown the positive relationships between learning goal structure and students’ out comes, such as effort, grades, and help-seeking (Lau and Nie 2008, Van Hooft and Noordzij 2009, Won et al. 2020).

Goal structures based on performance-prove and performance-avoidance emphasize the value of outcomes rather than the learning journey (Pekrun et al. 2006). The performance-prove goal can increase students’ motivation by directing their attention toward the positive value of successful outcomes (Pekrun et al. 2023). Performance-avoidance is generally believed to focus on the negative value of failure (Pekrun et al. 2023). However, the effect of these two goal structures on individuals’ behavior in an achievement setting is more complex and variable, depending on context. Although some studies in the traditional education setting show a positive relationship between these two situational goals and students’ outcomes, others show null or negative relations.

Related to performance-prove, some studies show a positive relationship between this situational goal and students’ outcomes. For example, Elliot and Harackiewicz (1996) conducted a laboratory experiment with undergraduate students and showed that performanceprove goal structure was related to students’ effort and performance. Church et al. (2001) studied undergraduate students in a chemistry course and demonstrated that the performance-prove goal was positively associated with students’ final grades. Furthermore, Linnenbrink (2005) focused on elementary students involved in a mathematical task and reported that focusing on situa tional performance-prove goals was related to higher scores.

However, the results of other studies have shown a null or negative relationship between the performanceprove goal and students’ behavior. For example, Turner et al. (2002) explored elementary students help-seeking behavior in a mathematics course, and they did not find any significant relationship between performance-prove structure and students’ behavior. Karabenick (2004) studied an undergraduate-level course and showed that the situational performanceprove goal was negatively associated with helpseeking. Lau and Nie (2008) examined fifth-grade students’ math achievement in Singapore and determined that the performance-prove goal was negatively related to mathematics achievement. Skaalvik and Skaalvik (2013) studied the role of situational goal orientation on fourth through tenth-grade Norwegian students effort and help-seeking. The results showed that the performance-prove goal was negatively related to students’ reported behavior. Van Hooft and Noordzij (2009) conducted a field experiment on a re-employment training workshop with unemployed people looking for a job. The authors did not find a significant relationship between performance-prove and individuals’ behavior. Finally, by focusing on high school students, Won et al. (2020) observed that students’ perceived performance goal structure was unrelated to their motivation.

A few studies have explored performance-avoidance goals with mixed findings. For example, Elliot and Harackiewicz (1996) conducted a laboratory experiment with undergraduate students and demonstrated that the performance-avoidance goal improved students’ effort and performance. Studying an undergraduate-level course Karabenick (2004) found that the situational performance-avoidance goal was negatively associated with help-seeking. Moreover, a study by Church et al. (2001) on undergraduate students in a chemistry course showed that performance-avoidance was negatively related to students’ grades.

Factors that might impact the inconsistent findings related to performance goals may be different research settings, populations, methodologies, variable definitions, and control variables used in models. For example, the current studies have different populations from various backgrounds, cultures, and ages. In the educational setting specifically, some studies focused on K-12 students with similar backgrounds, and others involved undergraduate students with more diverse groups of students. Another critical factor is the task environment of each study, which can result in conflicting results. For instance, the outcomes associated with performance goals can vary depending on the school and classroom design and whether these designs support more collaborative or competitive approaches.

Moreover, the level of autonomy and control of teachers and students in a learning environment can determine students’ responses to each situational achievement goal. In settings where teachers have the highest level of control, emphasizing performance can put excess pressure on students, resulting in adverse outcomes. Therefore, the existing literature lacks sufficient guidance regarding the potential influence of situational goals in an online learning environment where learners from diverse backgrounds worldwide are enrolled and have significant autonomy over their actions, with limited instructor guidance.

In addition to the above factors, the current studies have other limitations that render the results far from being generalizable, even for the learning goals (Guo et al. 2023). First, in the majority of current studies, goal structures have predominantly been evaluated through surveys that rely on students’ self-reported perceptions of the classroom goal structure. This approach poses challenges when providing recommendations to edu cators, as the objective impact of contextual changes on student-related outcomes remains unclear (Linnenbrink 2005). Users’ self-report responses can be a less accurate measurement of their actual behavior, making the findings less dependable (Grant and Dweck 2003).

Furthermore, the results of current studies are mostly descriptive and correlational. These limitations significantly hamper efforts to understand the causal impact of situational goal structures on learners’ behavior and the underlying mechanism. Recognizing the existing limitations, the scholars who first introduced achievement theory have called for further empirical research to explore the behavioral effects of situational goal structure on user behavior and to identify the underlying mechanisms (Elliot et al. 2017, Urdan and Kaplan 2020). Specifically, there is a need to focus on studying goal structure via interventions in real-world achievement contexts to enhance our knowledge of the practical implications and tangible benefits of the prior theoretical insights (VandeWalle et al. 2019).

Because almost all current studies are focused on the physical learning setting, there is a lack of guidance on the behavioral impact of situational goals in an online setting. Because of the fundamental differences between online and face-to-face education, the effectiveness of goal structure can be different (Wong et al. 2019). For example, although the traditional off line setting revolves around teachers’ autonomous control of the classroom environment and students behavior, in the online learning environment, learners have full autonomy in their learning processes. In such an environment, learners’ intrinsic interests and the value of learning tasks are the main forces for motivation (Hartnett 2016). Hence, it is not evident that reinforcing the intrinsic value of learning through the learning goal would be the most effective strategy in online learning, as predicted by offline studies.

Additionally, in online settings, the lack of physical presence together with the limited communication between students and instructors, can significantly impact learners’ ability demonstration and sense of competition. This can lead to different user behaviors toward performance goal structures that have not been studied. Therefore, our understanding of the behavioral implications of this theory and how goals can be effectively manipulated in an online setting remains very limited (Hartnett 2016). In this paper, we aim to fill this gap by examining the causal effect of situational achievement orientation on objectively and robustly measured outcomes in a large, diverse, and global student sample. 2.1.3. Achievement Emotions. Achievement emotions are defined as emotions related to achievements and outcomes of performing tasks, such as joy, pride, hope, and anxiety. They have been shown to be the main mechanism through which achievement goals can impact individuals’ behavior (Pekrun et al. 2005). These emotions are directly linked to activities associated with achievement, such as academic study, work, and sports, as well as the outcomes of those activities, including success and failure (Pekrun 2006).<sup>1</sup> Achievement emotions can significantly impact individuals cognitive resources, attention, interests, motivation, use of learning strategies, and performance (Pekrun et al. 2023).

Such emotions can be positive or negative and vary depending on their focus on the task or outcome (Pekrun et al. 2005). Task emotions primarily center on the learning process, emphasizing the experiential aspect, whereas outcome emotions underline the significance of final results (Pekrun et al. 2006). The learning achievement goal is usually considered to positively impact individuals’ motivation by activating positive taskrelated emotions, such as joy, and deactivating negative emotions, like boredom (Pekrun 2017). Goals based on performance-prove and performance-avoidance emphasize the value of outcomes rather than the learning journey (Pekrun and Stephens 2009). The performance goals can induce outcome-based achievement emotions, such as hope, pride, disappointment, anxiety, and so on (Pekrun et al. 2006). The performance-prove goal is shown to induce positive achievement emotions, such as hope and pride, resulting in positive outcomes, while performance-avoidance creates negative emotions, such as anxiety (Pekrun et al. 2006).

2.2. Achievement Goal Theory in Online Learning Achievement goals have also attracted attention as a means to enhance user engagement in online learning platforms, with most of the current studies focused on the relationship between dispositional orientations and users’ online learning behavior. For instance, Adesope et al. (2015) focused on the task of reading a book in an online environment and measured how learning and performance dispositional orientation impact students’ note-taking behavior as a measure of learning. The correlational analysis showed that both mastery and performance traits positively predicted note-taking behavior. Moreover, Neroni et al. (2018) measured users’ traits through surveys at the begin ning of their online learning journey and assessed their performance through multiple courses over 14 months. The results of their study showed that performance-prove traits could be a positive predictor of academic performance, whereas performanceavoidance traits were a negative predictor. However, they did not find a relationship between learning orientation and users’ performance.

Wang and Baker (2018) explored which dispositional orientations were correlated with earning a certificate in a MOOC. Using a survey method, the authors showed that certificate earners reported being higher in learning orientation, while learners who did not earn certificates reported being higher in performance orientation. In addition, Zhou and Wang (2019) surveyed users who attended an online course and found that learning orientation traits partially predicted user performance. They further showed that performance-prove traits positively predicted user performance, but there was no significant relationship between performance-avoidance traits and user performance. Finally, Polso et al. (2020) studied the relationship between users’ dispositional orientations and their performance on a programming MOOC. Their factor analysis results showed a positive relationship between users’ learning and performance-prove traits and their performance in the course.

However, to the best of our understanding, no study has investigated situational achievement goals in the online setting and examined how learners’ behavior can be influenced through digital interventions grounded in achievement goal theory. Exploring this relationship is especially crucial because, in psychology, disposition, and situation are usually considered two distinct constructs that can impact individuals’ behavior differently (Fridhandler 1986). Hence, the current findings on dispositional achievement goals in online learning environments cannot necessarily be generalized to the behavioral impact of situational goals. In this study, we try to fill this gap by investigating the differential impact of situational achievement goals on online learners’ engagement and performance. Table 2 summarizes the current studies on achievement goals in online learning and the contribution of our paper.

Summarizing the gaps in the literature, our current understanding of the effect of situational achievement goals in online learning is significantly limited due to various factors. First, because of the methodological limitations of the current studies, there are no robust findings or predictions on the effectiveness of each situational goal. Second, almost all the current studies are related to traditional face-to-face education, and there is a dearth of research theorizing the effect of these goals on learners’ behavior in the online learning environment. Last, the current studies are limited in exploring how learners’ characteristics can moderate the effectiveness of each goal and the potential underlying mechanisms. Hence, we take a step forward to address these gaps by investigating the influence of situational goals on online learners’ motivational behavior by implementing each goal as an intervention in randomized field experiments.

## 3. Study 1: Randomized Field Experiment in a MOOC

## 3.1. Research Context

At the beginning of every instruction week, new material was released to students by the instructor, with a quiz due by the end of that week; hence, eight quizzes in total.

## 3.3. Experimental Design

The most straightforward experimental setup would have resulted if we were able to treat learners under different conditions perfectly randomized. Full randomization requires full compliance, that is, every learner who is randomly invited to take a treatment complies with it. However, in the field conditions, full compliance is rarely achieved. It is not desirable to force users to participate in an experiment, nor will force provide genuine participation and attention. Hence, we opted for a voluntary participation design, where we first invited users to the study and only treated those who chose to participate. Learners’ participation choice, in turn, introduces a self-selection process that undermines randomness and challenges the researchers’ ability to draw causal inferences. We address this issue by employing the instrumental variable method, which extracts the selection bias from the outcome and recovers the causal effect of treatment (Angrist et al. 1996, Imbens and Rubin 1997).

3.3.1. Randomize-Then-Invite Design. Voluntary participation raises the question of when to randomly assign users to treatments: Should we randomize before or after users agree to participate? One common approach is to first ask users to participate and then randomize only among those who agree and drop the others (invite-then-randomize). When randomization occurs after securing participation, the sample is first reduced to a fraction of the original size, and then the treatment and control groups are defined. This approach results in a significant data loss, reduction of statistical power, and inefficient use of experimental data (Armstrong and Watts 1981). Hence, an extensive literature in clinical trials pioneered by Zelen (1979) has used randomization before securing participation (randomize-then-invite<sup>2</sup>). With this approach, researchers first randomly assign subjects to the treatment and control groups. They then obtained the subjects’ consent to participate in the study and only treated those who agreed to be treated. One of the main advantages of this design, among others, is that it allows the researchers to begin their analysis with the whole sample and obtain a much larger control group while still obtaining proper consent from participants. Hence, a wide range of studies have adopted this design (Legrain et al. 2011, Simon et al. 2016, Denison-Day et al. 2019, Gabbard et al. 2021).

Following this literature, we adopted a randomizethen-invite design in this study. An essential consideration in such designs is that the assignment to the control condition should not meaningfully alter participants’ experience because their consent is not yet obtained (Simon et al. 2021). For example, in a clinical trial, if patients assigned to the control group receive meaningfully different care than regular (noninvited) patients, a randomize-theninvite design would not be feasible. This issue does not exist in our context, as learners simply receive an invitation link to the study with no change to their learning experience before they choose to participate.

Although we randomly assigned learners to treatments, due to the aforementioned noncompliance issue, we cannot be sure that users’ participation choices are random. This issue commonly exists in settings where participants cannot be required to take part in the experiment and thus comply selectively (Hirano et al. 2000, Bijwaard and Ridder 2005). In randomize-then-invite designs such as ours, the experimental procedure is comprised of three phases:

• Phase I (Randomization): Subjects are randomly assigned to the treatment and control groups.

• Phase II (Compliance): Subjects decide whether to comply with the assignment and participate.

• Phase III (Treatment): The complying participants receive the treatment.

Users who do not comply would self-assign to the control condition and proceed with the course materials as usual. As a result, the actual treatment is influenced not only by the random assignment but also by the unobserved user choice. Directly estimating the treatment effect from phase III could result in the omitted variable bias because the factors explaining participation (phase II) are omitted (Angrist et al. 1996). Specifically, in our case, it is possible that users motivation or mood impacts their likelihood of participation, potentially contaminating the otherwise random groups and leading to biased estimates. Later, in Section 3.7.3, we discuss how our methodology addresses this issue using instrumental variables.

## 3.4. Treatment Execution

Consistent with the common approach in the achievement goal literature, we focused on the three situational goals of learning, performance-prove, and performanceavoidance (O’Keefe et al. 2013, Lim and Lim 2020). We started our experiment after midterm (i.e., at the end of week 5; Figure 1) by randomly assigning users to four experimental groups: the control (C), learning (L), performance-prove (PP), and performance-avoidance (PA) groups. All users received a default message on the platform asking them to provide their overall feedback about the course content via a link. After this message, the control group received a message thanking them for their response and wishing them well in the remainder of the course. The treatment groups received a message inviting them to participate in the experiment voluntarily. Those who agreed to participate and entered the experiment received the corresponding motivational cues. These cues included motivational text followed by assignments that required thinking and writing about one’s goals and plans as related to the treatment. The experimental cues were based on the prior related work (Brett and VandeWalle 1999) and can be found in Section EC.2 of the Online Appendix. Those who did not agree to participate exited from the study and did not receive any motivational messages, rendering their experience practically identical to that of the initial control group.<sup>3</sup>

The learning treatment highlighted the situational learning goal. It focused on the importance of learning new skills and a deep understanding of the concepts for future success. Users were asked to think about the capabilities and skills they had learned so far that made them proud. Then, they were asked to explain what skills they aspired to learn from now on and what concepts in the course would be the most effective in the career they want to pursue. The performance-prove treatment highlighted performance-prove goals. It was focused on the importance of outcome (grades) as a measure of success and the value of achieving high grades.<sup>4</sup> Users were reminded of the external reward they could obtain by receiving high grades. The participants were then asked to think about a previous assignment in which they received a high grade and explained the opportunities and benefits associated with high performance in the course. Finally, we asked them to select a challenging and aspirational target grade and keep that in mind as a goal for the rest of the course. Finally, the performance-avoidance treatment highlighted performance-avoidance goals. It focused on the consequences of failing and the importance of avoiding this outcome by receiving an acceptable grade in the course. Users were then asked to think about a previous assignment in which they had not done well, explain what went wrong and why, and choose a target minimum grade to overcome. Ultimately, we asked them to think about and write down the personal consequences of failing the course.

## 3.5. Treatment Reminder

Researchers have shown that subjects sometimes do not follow through on treatment instructions even if they initially intended to. Limited self-control partly explains this, and another driver is simply forgetting to act (Rogers and Milkman 2016). Studies have proposed the use of reminders as an effective and efficient tool to increase compliance (Sonntag and Zizzo 2015, Rogers and Milkman 2016, Vann et al. 2018). Therefore, we followed up with each group in week 7 with a reminder (detailed in Section EC.2 of the Online Appendix). The reminder serves two purposes: (a) to increase the salience of the initial treatments for users and (b) to generate a sense of accountability and encourage learners to act on the treatment they received.

The reminder message shown to the learners included a review of their respective cues from the original treatment and an open-ended question. The question for the L group asked them to explain what skills they have gained since the last exercise and what steps they have taken to maximize their learning. The PP group was asked to explain what measures they had taken to achieve the aspirational target grade they had chosen. The PA group was asked to describe what they had done to avoid failing the course and how close they were to passing. The control group received a message to keep up the work and continue to the finish line.

At this point, the treated groups received (a) one treatment (including two motivational messages, and then (b) they were asked to ponder it, perform a task, and make plans accordingly with the expectation that they will be asked about it later on; then, in the reminder, (c) users were asked to recall the treatment and report back on the progress of the original plan. The combination of these activities provides a high level of salience for the treatments that should persist for an extended period of time. The strength and salience of our treatment compare favorably to similar prior experiments (Zhang et al. 2017), where the treatment typically included only one motivational message for a course of similar length and no task assignment or reminder. In Section EC.11.1 of the Online Appendix, we discuss the design elements that enhance the effectiveness of our manipulations relative to the prior studies. ## 3.6. Data and Measures

Our main dependent variables are (a) performance, operationalized via the weighted sum of assignment grades after the treatment (including quizzes 6–8 and the final), and (b) engagement, operationalized via the total hours spent on the course content after the treatment. Our core independent variables are the treatment indicators $\mathrm { L } , \mathrm { P P } ,$ and PA for the learning, performance-prove, and performance-avoidance treatments, respectively. We also measure user status before the experiment: (a) prior performance, operationalized via the weighted sum of grades prior to the treatment (including quizzes 1–4 and the midterm), and (b) prior social activity, operationalized via the total number of posts made in the course’s discussion forum before the intervention. These factors can interact with the treatments to influence user outcomes. For example, learners with high prior performance may adopt performance goals more readily. Furthermore, we control for learners’ characteristics, such as their achievement traits, demographics, and their enrollment track (paid versus free). We also include data from learners’ prior engagement, operationalized via the total hours spent on course materials before the intervention, as well as prior courses they have taken, the cumulative grade in those courses, and the number of courses simultaneously taken with the current course. ## 3.7. Empirical Analysis and Causal Inference

In this section, we detail our empirical analysis. We first report the manipulation checks and then perform an intention-to-treat analysis. Finally, we present our two-stage instrumental variable model based on the experimental setup discussed in Section 3.3.

3.7.1. Manipulation Check. The unique value of experimental methodology lies in its ability to isolate causal effects through treatment manipulation. The effectiveness of manipulation needs to be checked to ensure participants understood the treatments and reacted to them as expected. A successful manipulation leads to differences among the experimental groups in the expected directions. This ensures the independent variable is actually manipulated by researchers, supporting the experiment’s internal validity (Hoewe 2017). Section EC.11 in the Online Appendix provides details of our manipulation checks. We first discuss the design elements in the treatments to ensure effective manipulation (Section EC.11.1) and then perform formal manipulation tests using a text analysis approach that measures users’ adopted goals based on their written responses (Section EC.11.2). The results indicate that the learning, performance-prove, and performanceavoidance treatment groups indeed understood the treatments and adopted the proper corresponding goals at a higher rate than other groups.

3.7.2. Intention-to-Treat Analysis. Scholars have used a variety of methods to address noncompliance issues in randomized experiments, including as-treated anal ysis (AT), preprotocol analysis (PPR), and intention-totreat analysis (ITT). The as-treated analysis compares the outcomes across the actual (rather than assigned) treatment groups. This analysis estimates the coefficients of the actual (rather than assigned) treatment but at the cost of sacrificing group comparability and the ability to infer causality. In contrast, PPR excludes participants who do not comply with their assigned treatment. It also disrupts the group equivalence and balance by not addressing self-selection through non compliance. Thus, like AT, it often yields biased esti mates (Ten Have et al. 2008, Little et al. 2009).

The ITT analysis is frequently used in randomized controlled experiments to estimate the impact of treatment assignment on the outcome of interest. The ITT analysis maintains the integrity of the original randomization, making the groups comparable and enabling causal interpretation without requiring additional assumptions beyond those needed for randomized experiments (Gupta 2011). The ITT analysis includes all participants who are randomized and compares the outcomes of those assigned to the treatment group to those assigned to the control group, regardless of whether they actually receive the treatment. This maintains group comparability and the inference of causality, but it sacrifices the ability to measure the effectiveness of the actual treatment received.

To resolve this issue, scholars have attempted to directly address the endogeneity caused by noncompliance and use econometric methods that can correct it. An instrumental variable (IV) approach was introduced by Angrist et al. (1996) as an expansion of Bloom’s method (Bloom 1984) to remedy the identification issue due to noncompliance and enable causal inference. The randomly assigned treatment in phase I (Randomization) (see Section 3.3) can be used as an instrument for the actual endogenous treatment in phase III (Treatment) (Imbens and Rubin 1997). This approach is the standard practice in the experimental literature with noncompliance (Bijwaard and Ridder 2005, Jo 2008).

The dynamics of the experiment are captured via a structural model. The following system of equations models the causal effect of the intervention. Equation (1) models the treatment (D) as a function of the assignment (Z). It captures the variation in each treatment caused by the exogenous random assignment. Noncompliance implies a $\beta _ { 1 }$ less than one. Equation (2) models the effect of treatment on user outcomes.

$$
D _ {i} = \alpha_ {1} + \beta_ {1} Z _ {i} + \gamma_ {1} x _ {i} + \epsilon_ {1 i},\tag{1}
$$

$$
D V _ {i} = \alpha_ {2} + \beta_ {2} \hat {D} _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} \pmb {x} _ {i} + \epsilon_ {2 i},\tag{2}
$$

where i is the user index, $D _ { i } : = \left( L _ { i } , P P _ { i } , P A _ { i } \right)$ represents the indicators for the actual treatments received by the user, $Z _ { i } : = ( L _ { i } ^ { \prime } , P P _ { i } ^ { \prime } , P A _ { i } ^ { \prime } )$ is the vector of randomly assigned treatments, $x _ { i }$ is the vector of other regressors, $\hat { D _ { i } }$ is the predicted actual treatment from Equation $( 1 ) , D V _ { i } : = \overline { { ( P e r f o r m a n c e _ { i } , E n g a g e m e n t _ { i } ) } }$ is the set of dependent variables, α<sub>:</sub> are the sample intercepts, and $\epsilon _ { . i }$ are the user-specific random errors.

For such an IV setup to be valid, three assumptions must hold: instrument relevance, exclusion restriction, and monotonicity. The relevance assumption requires that our group assignments be correlated with the ultimately chosen treatments. In our experiment, each learner only had access to their assigned group; therefore, no cross-contamination could occur across treatment groups (e.g., users assigned to L cannot participate in PP), and control units could not self-assign to either treatment (i.e., no noncompliance for controls). However, users assigned to treatment could self-assign to control; therefore, the assigned and actual treatment variables do not have a perfect correlation.

To assess the instrument’s relevance, we examine the first-stage regression. Table EC.6 provides the first-stage regression summary statistics (Section EC.6 in the Online Appendix). The robust F statistics are 156.07, 210.63, and 129.56 for L, PP, and PA indicators, respectively, demonstrating a strong association between the assigned and selected experimental groups, well above the conventional value of 10 (Stock and Yogo 2005, Andrews et al. 2019). The partial $R ^ { 2 }$ metrics, which measure the level of association between the instruments and the endogenous predictors, were 0.4215, 0.3796, and 0.5125 for L, PP, and PA, respectively. Additionally, we perform a Kleibergen and Paap (2006) weak instruments test. The F statistic for the joint instruments was 252.212, which again demonstrates very strong instruments.

The exclusion restriction assumption requires that the instruments only impact the outcome via the treatment and have no impact on it, conditional on the explanatory variables $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ participation in the experiment and other controls). First, we note that the random nature of assignments causes them to be independent of unobserved variables that might impact user outcomes. Second, the content of the treatment only includes stimuli related to the situational goal orientations. It does not include any new learning material nor reveal novel information that can materially impact user behavior beyond a direct effect on situational achievement goals. This suggests that the experimental cues only impact the outcomes through participation in the experiment and alteration of goal orientation, lending support for the exclusion restriction assumption.

The monotonicity assumption requires that the effect of the randomized invitation on participation be weakly positive, meaning that the invitations to participate do not make users less likely to participate (Angrist 2006). Our invitations have no information that can plausibly discourage users from participating, so this assumption also holds. In addition, we do not allow users assigned to the control group to take any of the treatments or any participant to change their treatment type. Finally, a general assumption required in experimental research is the stable unit treatment value assumption (Cox 1958, Rubin 1980), which asserts that there is only one version for each treatment and that the treatment of one unit does not interfere with the treatment of another unit. In Section EC.17 of the Online Appendix, we provide substantive and empirical evidence on the validity of this assumption in our context.

With these assumptions, we can leverage instrumental variable estimation to recover the treatment effects (Angrist and Pischke 2009). The estimates obtained through this method will be the local average treatment effects (LATEs). They represent the average effect of treatment on compliers; that ${ \mathrm { i } } s ,$ those users who, if assigned, would comply and participate in the treatment (Imbens and Angrist 1994).<sup>5</sup> In Equation (2), $\beta _ { 2 } ^ { ' } \mathrm { s }$ estimate is the LATE. To recover $\beta _ { 2 } ,$ we use a two-stage least squares estimator. The results of these analyses are reported in Section 3.8. Because our dependent variables are continuous, we use a linear regression model. However, we test the robustness of results to a variety of alternative models and specifications, such as fractional regression, Tobit, Poisson, and log-linear models.

Situational achievement goals can impact learners behavior differently in online and traditional settings due to the structural difference between the two settings, including the emotional and motivational mechanisms. For instance, in the traditional classroom environment, teachers exert greater control, and students have rich in-person interactions with their instructors and peers. In such an environment, there is already a higher chance that students feel performance pressures from teachers and peers and experience related emotions. Thus, emphasizing performance goals may not further stimulate learners and hence would be less effective (Urdan and Kaplan 2020). However, in the online environment, learners typically have a high degree of autonomy in their learning journeys and limited interactions with instructors and peers (Darnon et al. 2007). Such an environment usually causes learners to focus on the intrinsic value of learning and experience the related achievement emotions. Hence, further emphasis on learning goals may be redundant and not significantly stimulating anymore. By contrast, because there is much less social interaction with peers and instructors, students may not be experiencing strong performance-related emotions and pressures. In such an environment, focusing on performance goals may effectively stimulate achievement emotions and enhance learners’ engagement and performance (Pekrun 2017).

3.8.3. Underlying Mechanism. As outlined in Section 2, the situational achievement goals have been shown to impact individuals’ behavior through activating/ deactivating different types of achievement emotions, subsequently influencing their thought process and behavior (Pekrun and Stephens 2010). Achievement emotions can significantly impact students’ academic performance by influencing their motivation, effort, utilization of learning strategies, self-regulation, and the availability of cognitive resources required for effective learning (Pekrun 2006). These types of emotions can be positive or negative (Pekrun and Stephens 2010). Positive achievement emotions, such as joy, hope, and pride, can effectively direct learners’ cognitive capacity and attention toward relevant course topics, thereby enhancing their motivation and engagement. However, negative achievement emotions like disappointment, anger, and boredom are usually found to divert users’ attention from course materials toward irrelevant subjects, reducing motivation and engagement (Smith and Firth 2018). For instance, boredom could lead to a reduction in learners’ attention to course materials, making them more easily distracted (Pekrun 2006). Consequently, learners’ motivation and outcomes can be significantly enhanced by fostering an environment where positive emotions outweigh negative ones.

Furthermore, depending on their focus, achievement goals can stimulate task-related or outcome-related emotions. For instance, learning goal structure usually stimulates task-related emotions, such as joy, anger, and boredom (Pekrun et al. 2023). Performance-prove goals usually induce outcome-related emotions like hope, pride, anxiety, and disappointment (Pekrun and Stephens 2010). Although performance-prove goals can induce positive achievement emotions, such as hope and pride, the emotional impact of performanceavoidance can be more complicated. Although the performance-avoidance goal emphasizes attempting to avoid failure and can create negative short-term feelings, such as anxiety, it can also evoke motivation to invest the effort to avoid failure in the long run (Smith and Firth 2018).

Studies have shown that the effectiveness of each of these achievement emotions is contingent on the specific context (Pekrun and Stephens 2010). For instance, in a highly competitive situation, further emphasizing performance goals can create a stressful environment, increasing anxiety and deteriorating students’ motivation and performance (Brophy 2005). Hence, we should consider what learners typically experience in a regular online learning environment and how our interventions could influence the emotions they experience and, consequently, their behavior.

The online learning environment typically relies on intrinsic motivation and the associated characteristics of curiosity and self-regulation to engage learners (Martens and Kirschner 2004). It has been observed that online learners often exhibit a high level of intrinsic motivation (Hartnett 2016). Particularly with MOOCs, which are commonly pursued as supplementary learning opportunities for knowledge acquisition and skill improvement, learners typically possess an intrinsic motivation to participate and engage in the course.

Intrinsic motivation is usually associated with taskrelated achievement emotions, like joy and boredom (Pekrun 2017). Nonetheless, intrinsic motivation and task-related emotions alone may not always be enough to keep learners engaged, as they cannot consistently rely solely on the inherent value of learning (Ryan and Deci 2000). To foster strong motivation, it is essential to provide an environment that considers learners’ various achievement emotions and needs (Schunk and Zimmerman 2006). Thus, in addition to the inherent value of learning and task emotions, the learning environment should accommodate other needs such as ability demonstration, competency, and a sense of accomplishment and the related emotions (Meece et al. 2006). To experience these, learners need to realize that they are capable of achieving desired outcomes and avoiding undesirable ones (Connell and Wellborn 1991). However, online learning has paid less attention to these needs and emotions (Hartnett 2016). Therefore, by stimulating these achievement emotions through situational achievement goals, learners’ existing task emotions can be complemented, leading to a substantial improvement in their motivation, engagement, and overall performance.

To explore learners’ achievement emotions, we follow the literature to focus on the seven most crucial achievement emotions in the learning process, joy, hope, pride, anger, anxiety, disappointment, and boredom (Pekrun and Stephens 2010), and studied their mediating effects. To do this, we analyze users’ textual responses to the intervention questions to measure their achievement emotions. We first collect all the textual responses from the participants during the intervention and the reminder (as explained in the treatment cues in Section EC.2 in the Online Appendix). Then, we use a simple text analysis based on the linguistic inquiry and word count (LIWC) (Pennebaker et al. 2015) and the National Research Council Canada word-emotion association lexicon (EmoLex) (Mohammad and Turney 2013) to measure the strength of emotional content in every user’s textual responses. Details of the measurement are reported in Section EC.20 in the Online Appendix.

Aligned with the previous explanations, low performers benefit more from the learning goal because, for these learners, focusing on the task value and the result ing enjoyment can be beneficial. Interestingly, mid performers benefit from the performance-avoidance goal the most. Although the performance-avoidance goal can create anxiety by reminding them of failure, our findings indicate that this reminder can be motivating for mid per formers, incentivizing the allocation of more effort to address the shortcomings and improve performance.

These results also help explain why the main effects of L and PA are not significant on performance. L and PA mainly impact the behavior of learners with low and medium prior performance. As these learners have a weaker grasp of the material than high performers, it takes more initial effort on their part to overcome inertia, build a foundation, and get up to speed so they can begin experiencing positive performance outcomes. It follows that L and PA should generate performance results more slowly (than PP). 3.8.4.2. Instructor-Set vs. User-Set Goals. We perform two post hoc analyses on the effect of goal levels on effort in the PP and PA groups. The first analysis compares users’ actual performance with their self-set goals (Section EC.13 in the Online Appendix). We find that users with larger under-performance with respect to their self-set goals (i.e., lower prior performance relative to the self-set goal) obtain high engagement after the PP treatment, and this effect weakens as the gap grows larger. In other words, these individuals work harder when they have previously underperformed their goal but lose engagement and motivation if this gap is too large (Table EC.14 in the Online Appendix). The second post hoc analysis looks at the instructorset grade thresholds (Section EC.14 in the Online Appendix). As noted in the explanation of the treatment cues (Section EC.2 in the Online Appendix), the instructor-set target grades were 75% for the PP group and 60% for the PA group. The results, presented in Table EC.15 in the Online Appendix, indicate that in the PP group, users are more engaged when their prior score is within five points above the instructorset goal than when it is five points below. This suggests a postachievement excitement effect for these learners when the achievement goal is set by the instructor. We have not found any effect on the PA group.

3.8.4.4. Additional Post Hoc Analyses. We furthermore conducted additional post hoc analyses that can be found in Section EC.18 in the Online Appendix. First, we examine the role of national culture. Our learners come from a diverse set of countries and there could be cultural factors and social pressures that impact their reaction to the treatments. Based on the literature on cross-cultural psychology (Hofstede et al. 2010), we identified a dimension of national culture, “indulgence versus restraint (IND),” that is most relevant to our learning setting (Zhao et al. 2015). The results are reported in Section EC18.1 in the Online Appendix. Aligned with our expectation, individuals from countries with low IND scores exhibited a more pronounced reaction to the PP treatment, highlighting the importance of considering culture in behavioral interventions on online platforms.

Second, we perform a difference-in-difference analysis using a weekly panel of student activity to shed light on the temporal evolution of the treatment effects (Section EC18.2 in the Online Appendix). Our results show that the learning situational goal (L) has a stable positive effect on learners’ engagement, and this effect does not grow significantly over time. However, for performance-prove and performance-avoidance, we observe significant longitudinal growth. Furthermore, our findings indicate that the performance effects emerge with a delay compared with the engagement effects.

## 4. Study 2: Randomized Field Experiment in an Online Course

Study 1 demonstrates the impact of each of the situational achievement goal treatments (learning, performanceprove, and performance-avoidance) on learner behavior. Our results specifically demonstrate that the performance-prove goal, on average, could enhance learners’ outcomes the most, followed by performance avoidance and learning goals. Importantly, these treatments are inexpensive nudges and offer platform operators a simple tool to enhance user experience. Given the low cost of treatment, a question that arises is whether the platform operator can combine performance and learning treatments, as the two broad classes of situational achievement goals, to achieve even better results. If so, what order of treatments is optimal?

To examine this question, we conducted a second randomized field experiment in a separate online course. We specifically focused on the combinations of the learning goal with the performance-prove and performanceavoidance goals and their different orderings. We did not consider the combinations of performance-prove and performance-avoidance as both are focused on the same general theme (performance). This experiment provides an additional opportunity for us to triangulate prior analyses and confirm the findings about the underlying mechanism because the first part of this two-part experiment replicates the first experiment. This is especially important because, in the original experiment, we conducted the manipulation check and mechanism test using textual analytics. In this study, however, we had the opportunity to ask participants directly about their thoughts and emotions after the treatments.

## 4.1. Research Context

We conducted a second experiment in a course about business data analysis at a leading business school in the United States. The course was delivered as an online undergraduate asynchronous class, offered and managed through the learning management system Canvas. Although the business school offered it, the course was open to the entire university, and nonbusiness students also attended it. A total of 650 students registered for the course, among which 74.54% were business majors, such as management, marketing, and information systems, 12.58% were economics majors, and the rest were other majors, such as psychology and sociology. Furthermore, 34.36% of participants were female, and 17.79% were international. The course duration was one semester, and it contained weekly video lectures, quizzes, and projects. The weekly quizzes and projects comprised 40% of the final score. At the end of the semester, students took a final exam and completed a project, each contributing another 30% to the final score.

## 4.2. Experimental Procedure

We started our experiment at the beginning of the semester by measuring learners’ demographics and achievement traits using the exact same instrument as the original experiment. In the second week of the semester, learners were randomly assigned to a control group (C) and three treatment groups based on the three situational achievement goals: learning (L), performance-prove (PP), and performance-avoidance (PA). These treatment groups then received the first treatment round, and the treatment cues were similar to those of the first study (see Section EC.2 in the Online Appendix for details of treatments), with minor modifications based on the context. Learners received the treatment message through the Canvas learning management system, and learners’ behavior was tracked for four weeks after the treatment. Regarding the outcome variables, we have access to user grades as a measure of performance and the number of page views (the number of pages a student views) as a measure of engagement.

In the second-round treatment, in which learners were assigned to a different achievement goal than the first one. For instance, learners who were treated by the performance-prove achievement goal at first were treated by the learning achievement goal in the second treatment. We treated learners in separate rounds with a time gap because prior research has shown that focusing on two achievement goals simultaneously in one round can potentially confuse learners and negatively impact their behavior (Pintrich 2000a). As previously explained, our primary focus is to examine the potential impact of combining learning goals with performance goals and thus only consider these goal combinations. This leads to five experimental conditions: L-PP, L-PA, PP-L, PA-L, and C-C.

## 4.3. Manipulation Check

To ensure learners comprehend the treatment messages as desired, one week later, we asked them multiple Likert-type questions related to the treatment they received and their achievement goals (see Section EC.19 in the Online Appendix for details). As shown in Tables EC.21–EC.23 in the Online Appendix, the perceived goals of the participants are aligned with their respective experimental conditions suggesting effective manipulation.

This means that learners being treated first with L and then with PP have 5.856 more grade points (out of 100) and 120.717 more page views. Emphasizing the value of learning and enhancing learners’ intrinsic motivation at the beginning of a learning journey would increase learners attention to the task and learning strategies, which can then be complemented and further enhanced by empha sizing the importance of extrinsic outcomes, like grades.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

Considering the widespread adoption of online learning, examining how the associated platforms can effectively maintain learners’ motivation and engagement is imperative. In this paper, we focus on situational achievement goals and study how building an online learning environment around different achievement goals positively impacts user behavior and learning outcomes. Our multiple experiments based on the achievement goal theory (two in the field setting and one in the laboratory) show that counter to prior findings, focusing on learning goals may not be the best way to motivate online learners, and focusing on performance goals does not result in adverse effects.

We find that performance-prove situational goal interventions result in the greatest improvement in online learner engagement and performance. Furthermore, the performance-avoidance goal does not negatively impact online learners’ behavior and shows the potential to improve motivation for some learners. We explain these results by identifying learners’ achievement emotions as the underlying mechanism. Various goals can trigger distinct emotions in learners, influencing their behavior in distinct ways. The specific structure of the learning environment can lead learners to experience certain emotions while potentially missing out on others. In such situations, introducing a construct to activate various achievement emotions can fulfill learners’ psychological needs and enhance their motivation levels.

Based on our findings, different learners react differently toward situational goals. Low performers react more positively to the situational learning goal, whereas the performance-prove goal mainly helps high performers. Interestingly, mid performers respond more posi tively to the performance-avoidance goal. Moreover, the level of learners’ social interaction in the course discussion forum can moderate the effectiveness of situational goals. The performance goals, both performance-prove and performance-avoidance, are more effective for learners with lower prior social interactions. We further demonstrate that learners’ engagement and performance can be enhanced by combining different situational goals. Our findings indicate that combining learning and performance-prove yields the most benefits for learners’ engagement and performance.

This research contributes to the literature on user behavior in online learning platforms by enhancing our understanding of the importance of contextual factors in online learning environments. Our results demonstrate how situational achievement goals impact learners’ behavior in online platforms. The combination of large-scale online randomized field experiments and instrumental variable method enables us to show the causal relationship between situational goals and learners’ engagement and performance at a larger scale than has previously been possible in education studies. The replication of the results in our second study provides strong assurance about the underlying mechanism. We show the importance of considering learners emotions, especially achievement emotions, in online learning and address the current gaps in the online learning literature (Wu and Yu 2022).

This study holds significant implications for platform designers and administrators. It proposes that online learning providers can augment platform engagement by implementing motivational interventions rooted in situational achievement goals. Notably, these interventions are both cost-effective and require minimal user effort yet yield consistent and substantial benefits. Moreover, our findings underscore the importance of considering various dimensions of learners’ psychological needs and emotions, which have previously been less emphasized in online learning environments. The prevailing platform design approach is hands-off and self-directed, which tends to rely solely on learners intrinsic motivation. Our results reveal that this approach might overlook crucial aspects of learning, such as diverse forms of achievement emotions, and lead to disengagement.

We establish that introducing performance goals can stimulate outcome-oriented achievement emotions, thereby elevating engagement and performance levels among learners. Consequently, managers must pay close attention to learners’ emotions and behaviors throughout each learning phase to create an effective learning environment. Furthermore, our results indicate that a generic course design for all might not be the best strategy for having highly engaged learners and highlight the importance of customization based on learners’ characteristics. This customization can happen by understanding learners’ motivational behavior and designing simple, costless interventions and activities based on learners’ prior performance and social activity on the platform. Our results demonstrate that these design principles help tackle the vexing engagement deficit in online learning, and online platforms provide an advantage over traditional in-person learning environments by allowing for the possibility of low-cost, personalized goal interventions at scale.

The variety of experiments and analyses presented in this paper point toward the generalizability of our findings. We perform multiple experiments that together paint a consistent picture of the effects of achievement goals on learner outcomes in different contexts. For example, Study 1 demonstrates that the performance-prove goal has a strong positive impact on performance and engagement. Study 2, conducted in a different online course, shows that the combination of performance-prove and learning (L-PP and PP-L) yields strong positive effects on performance and engagement. Furthermore, the laboratory experiment on self-referent and other-referent performance goals confirms the positive impact of both types of performance treatments in yet another context, thereby providing further assurance of the robustness of our findings. However, it is essential to consider that all three experiments are focused on business courses, and thus, the results may not generalize to topics and environments that are significantly different. In the future, research can extend our findings by experimenting with different course topics and targeted student populations so that motivational interventions and activities can be optimally customized for individual students.

Because we conducted our experiment in the second half of the course, not many dropouts occurred after the experiment, which limited our ability to test how the treatment impacted the dropout rate. That being said and viewing dropout as the final step in the process of losing engagement and enthusiasm, we can expect that the improved engagement after the experiments could have a bearing on dropouts as well. Future research could formally explore this impact. Additionally, all our findings result from treatment estimates on compliers. Thus, they only apply to the population of users who do comply with voluntary treatments. This compliance pattern analysis in Study 1 does not reveal statistically significant effects for many of the control variables. However, we do observe some influence from learners’ traits and prior courses on their likelihood of complying with treatments. Nevertheless, the platform operators need to consider that these results may not apply to contexts where users are not sufficiently motivated to participate in treatments.

As discussed, achievement emotions have been recog nized as the primary pathway through which situational achievement goals influence individuals’ behavior in an achievement setting. Thus, we focus on this pathway as the underlying mechanism. However, other potential mechanisms may be at play. For instance, the results could be attributed to the self-regulation that occurs in goal setting. It is possible that achievement goals prompt learners to plan a goal and then monitor and evaluate their performance more meticulously, leading to the development of better learning strategies and performance enhancement. However, achievement emotions and self-regulation are highly intercorrelated. Hence, examining achievement emotions can also partly capture the effect of the self-regulation mechanism.

Additionally, our treatments may have prompted learners who the instructors care about their success, fostering increased engagement and activity. However, the variation in treatment effects in our studies shows that the outcome depends on the content of the message, and varying the content to emphasize various achievement goals leads to different results. Also, in each study, the instructor was already communicating with the learners regularly, both before and after our treatments, offering general weekly updates on class outcomes and progress and showing that learners’ success was very important to them. Thus, the learners may already be sufficiently aware that the instructor was attentive to their progress. Given these, we conclude that the achievement emotion pathway remains the primary mechanism driving the results, while other mechanisms may also be influential. These findings open avenues for future research to explore other potential mechanisms that may play a role.
