# Navigating the Storm: Toward a Theory of IT Portfolio Diversity, Leadership Power, and Organizational Resilience to Major Shocks

Abstract. Contemporary firms face an environment marked by major shocks, which are characterized by economic stagnation, mounting debt burdens, rapid technological disruption, profound geopolitical volatility, and escalating environmental crises. Under these cir cumstances, organizational resilience to major shocks (ORMS) is critical for firms to mitigate disruptions and thrive in turbulent environments. Although information technology (IT) resources are increasingly recognized as vital for building resilience, theoretical insights into how firms strategically leverage IT diversity and leadership structures to enhance ORMS remain limited. Grounded in the strategic flexibility framework, this study investi gates how IT portfolio diversity (ITPD), structural power of IT leaders (SPT), and crisisinduced industry turbulence (CTU) interact to shape ORMS. We conceptualize ITPD as the variety of IT resources a firm possesses and SPT as the formal authority of IT leaders to orchestrate cross-functional coordination. Drawing on a firm-level data set before, during, and after the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, we address three research questions: (1) How does ITPD affect ORMS? (2) How do ITPD and SPT jointly influence ORMS? (3) How do adverse CTU (ACTU) and beneficial CTU (BCTU) moderate these effects? Ou analyses reveal an inverted U-shaped relationship between ITPD and ORMS, indicating that whereas moderate IT diversity enhances resilience, excessive diversity incurs coordination costs that undermine it. This curvilinear effect is temporally contingent, peaking during the crisis. SPT positively moderates this relationship by shifting the turning point, enabling firms to harness greater ITPD benefits. Further, industry turbulence differentially moderate these dynamics: ACTU amplifies the inverted U-shaped effect by steepening the curve and shifting the turning point, whereas BCTU flattens the curve without altering its peak. These findings advance the strategic flexibility framework, information systems, and organizational resilience literatures, showing how contextual timing, IT resource diversity, leadership power, and industry conditions interactively shape organizational resilience.

## 1. Introduction

Organizational resilience, particularly in the face of major shocks, is crucial for firms’ performance in turbulent environments (DesJardine et al. 2019). This importance is amplified by converging global pressures such as economic strains, transformative technologies, and geopolitical fractures, which heighten uncertainty and increase both the frequency and severity of major shocks. Successful navigation through such shocks, which allows firms not only to shield themselves from adverse impacts but also to potentially thrive in turbulent environments, is indicative of a high degree of organizational resilience (DesJardine et al. 2019). Consequently, there is growing scholarly interest in how firms leverage information technology (IT) resources to enhance their organizational resilience during times of major shocks (Tremblay et al. 2023). This trend is driven not only by the frequent occurrence of major shocks, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the Russia–Ukraine war, and the U.S.–China trade disputes, which severely disrupt firms’ operations and jeopardize the very existence of numerous firms (Katare et al. 2021), but also by an incomplete theoretical understanding of how firms integrate and utilize IT resources to build organizational resilience during major shocks.

In this paper, we define a firm’s organizational resilience to major shocks (ORMS) as the firm’s ability to minimize the unexpected disruptions in business performance caused by major shocks or even enhance its business performance (Dimitriadis 2021). To strengthen our knowledge on how firms leverage IT resources to build ORMS, we use the strategic flexibility framework (Sanchez 1995) to investigate this issue. The strategic flexibility framework posits that firms’ strategic resilience in turbulent environments is influenced by two core components: (1) resource flexibility and (2) coordination flexibility (Sanchez 1995). Importantly, the hallmark for promoting ORMS is that coordination flexibility effectively balances resource flexibility (Sanchez 1997).

Resource flexibility concerns the extent to which the scope or range of a firm’s resources can be used to produce a competitive advantage for tackling shocks or uncertainties in its environment (Wright and Snell 1998). Contextualized to our investigative context, firms’ diverse IT resources reflect resource flexibility. Whereas prior studies on IT and resilience have either treated IT resources as single applications (e.g., Zoom; Scott et al. 2006) or broadly viewed IT resources as generalized capacities (Oh and Teo 2006), we propose a more granular approach in this study. To capture the flexibility of a firm’s IT resources, we introduce IT portfolio diversity (ITPD), defined as the degree of variety of ITs constituting the available digital resources (Duchek et al. 2020) in a firm that can be strategically used to build the firm’s competitive advantage. This nuanced conceptualization allows for a more detailed examination of how the diversity of available IT resources may contribute to a firm’s ORMS by providing a range of digital capabilities tailored to respond to and recover from major shocks.

Although it may be intuitive to assume that greater diversity in a firm’s IT portfolio improves ORMS, the impact of ITPD on ORMS demands further scrutiny. On the positive side, a rich array of IT resources can help firms craft flexible IT-enabled responses to significant disruptions, potentially strengthening their ORMS. This view aligns with perspectives that consider diversity as a source of innovative solutions in the face of adversity (Duchek et al. 2020). However, on the negative side, the integration of a diverse IT portfolio during a crisis leads to higher costs associated with gen erating, evaluating, and exercising strategic options, thereby potentially undermining ORMS. Thus, ITPD may exert dual opposing effects on ORMS, which requires a more detailed examination. Given the limited theoretical insight in this regard, the above discussion leads to our first research question (RQ1): How does ITPD affect ORMS during a major shock?

The second prominent component of the strategic flexibility framework is coordination flexibility, which refers to the extent to which a firm can strategize, configure, and mobilize resources adeptly (Sanchez 1995, Wright and Snell 1998). Firms that excel in coordination flexibility are poised to navigate the turbulence of major shocks with greater adeptness, utilizing their functiona capacities more effectively (Li et al. 2023). In our research context, coordination flexibility necessitates collaborative synergy between business and IT leadership, who must plan, organize, and implement IT resources in concert (Liu and Preston 2021). In times of crisis, IT leaders’ ability to orchestrate cross-functiona coordination is crucial (Smaltz et al. 2006) for enabling the execution of digital strategies and the reconfigura tion of IT resources toward organizational resilience. As a key factor that influences cross-functional coordination within firms (Feng et al. 2021), the structural power of IT leaders (SPT)—defined as the formal position of the IT leaders within a firm (Hambrick 1981, Finkelstein 1992)—is a critical enabler for achieving ORMS. Infor mation systems (IS) research has underscored the significance of SPT in converting IT resources into favorable organizational outcomes (Preston et al. 2008b, Chen et al. 2021, Feng et al, 2021). However, extant literature has provided limited insights into the role of SPT in facilitating the transformation of ITPD into ORMS. To this end, we contend that SPT can effectively balance ITPD by extracting more of the potential of the increased ITPD and by attenuating costs in generating, evaluating, and implementing diverse alternative solutions to counteract major shocks. As a result, SPT and ITPD could work together to further strengthen resilience amid major shocks (i.e., ORMS), leading to our second research question (RQ2): How do ITPD and SPT jointly affect ORMS?

Moreover, the strategic flexibility framework indicates that environmental contexts may influence how resource flexibility and coordination flexibility form ORMS (Sanchez 1997). Prior literature has shown that external major shocks may differentially reshape the landscapes of various industries (Grewal and Tansu haj 2001). The repercussions of such shocks are dual faceted: these shocks can destabilize some industries while concurrently presenting new opportunities for other industries (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011). For example, the COVID-19 pandemic exemplifies this dichotomy as it triggered a downturn in the transportation sector while propelling the pharmaceutical industry to higher profitability (Liang 2021). Recognizing the differential impact of industry-specific changes, scholars have advocated for a closer examination of environmental factors as a contingency of ORMS outcomes (Guiette and Vandenbempt 2014). Following this line, this study focuses on the external contingency of crisisinduced industry turbulence (CTU), defined as degree to which a major shock adversely or beneficially affects the industry of a focal firm. Given the dual nature of CTU, it is necessary to differentiate between industries adversely affected by a shock versus those that benefit. Thus, we further distinguish between adverse crisisinduced industry turbulence (ACTU) and beneficial crisisinduced industry turbulence (BCTU), leading to our third research question (RQ3): How do ACTU and BCTU moderate the joint effect of ITPD and SPT on ORMS?

Building on the strategic flexibility framework, we developed a conceptual model to address the above research questions. We empirically tested our hypotheses using comprehensive firm-level data collected from periods before, during, and after the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic in China. Our findings reveal a sophisticated interplay among ITPD, SPT, and CTU in influencing ORMS. Specifically, we find an inverted U-shaped relationship between ITPD and ORMS, with this curvilinear relationship becoming particularly pronounced during, rather than before or after, the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting the critical role of temporal conditions under which ITPD exerts its impacts. Furthermore, SPT positively moderates the curvilinear relationship by shifting its turning point to the right, suggesting that enhanced IT leadership power enables firms to better leverage the advantages of IT diversity. Moreover, our results reveal distinct moderating effects of ACTU and BCTU on the joint curvilinear effect of ITPD and SPT on ORMS: ACTU amplifies the inverted U-shaped relationship by shifting the turning point further to the right and steepens the curvature of the inverted U-shaped relationship, indicating heightened impacts on both promoting and suppressing forces of the joint curvilinear effect of ITPD and SPT on ORMS. Conversely, BCTU moderates the relationship by flattening the curve without shifting its turning point.

Collectively, these results extend the strategic flexibility framework (Sanchez 1995) by demonstrating how contextual timing, IT resource diversity, structural IT leadership power, and different industry conditions interactively shape firm resilience under crisis conditions. Our findings advance our theoretical understanding of when and how firms can strategically balance ITPD and SPT to optimize resilience outcomes during major industry disruptions, significantly contributing to the IS and organizational resilience literatures.

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1. The Role of IT in Firms’ Organizational Resilience to Major Shocks

Firms’ ORMS is a crucial concept that has gained increasing attention across various disciplines as it pertains to firms’ ability to withstand and adapt to turbulent environments. The significance of ORMS has been further emphasized in recent times because of the frequent occurrence of major shocks, which are characterized by three salient attributes that compound their threat to organizational viability: the severity of the existential risk they pose, their inherent unpredictability, and the acute time constraints they impose on firms for effective responses (van der Vegt et al. 2015). As such, firms face significant challenges in efficiently and effectively mitigating the negative consequences of major shocks (Sajko et al. 2021).

From a strategic perspective, ORMS entails a firm’s latent ability to resist, recover from, and/or adapt to major disruptions caused by significant external shocks (Sajko et al. 2021). Regarding the IS research on how firms leverage IT resources to influence ORMS, our literature review identified several gaps. First, prior studies have rarely examined how firms leverage a variety of IT resources to build ORMS (Liu et al. 2023), representing a valuable research opportunity as IT resources are indispensable for making firms more productive and resilient (Heeks and Ospina 2019). Although it is intuitive to believe that the richer the variety of IT resources a firm has, the more likely the firm is to develop alternative solutions to counteract unforeseen exogenous shocks (DesJardine et al. 2019), integrating and utilizing diverse IT resources often come with increased costs from generating, evaluating, and exercising alternative solutions (Karhade and Dong 2021a). Hence, the overall impact of diverse IT resources needs to be carefully theorized and assessed. To that end, this study investigates how firms’ diverse IT resources influence ORMS and mitigate the impact of major shocks.

Second, our literature review indicated that the specialization of and collaboration among a firm’s top management team (TMT) play a crucial role in effective managerial decision making to maintain the firm’s core organizational attributes, structure, functions, and operational efficiency in turbulent environmental contexts (e.g., major external shocks). Rather than being determined by a single authority, firms important decisions are often influenced by TMT executives from various functional areas (Feng et al. 2021). In this case, the presence of IT leaders in a TMT could be instrumental in improving IT governance and addressing IT-related managerial problems arising from major shocks. Prior studies have suggested that when IT leaders hold a strong formal position in a firm, it facilitates the assimilation of enterprise systems into the firm by coordinating IT functions with various business functions (Shao et al. 2016), enhances the strategic alignment between IT and business functions (Wunderlich 2018), and promotes IT governance by facilitating coordination among different business functions (Bradley et al. 2012). Against this backdrop, the current literature’s silence regarding whether and how the formal position of IT leaders, together with firms’ integration and utilization of diverse IT resources, affects ORMS thus represents a unique research gap and opportunity for valuable contributions.

## 2.2. Strategic Flexibility Framework

To address the above knowledge gap, we use the strategic flexibility framework as our overarching theoretical lens to explain how firms’ flexible use of resources and effective managerial coordination help them cope with unforeseen adversities. Specifically, the strategic flexibility framework identifies two key components— (1) resource flexibility and (2) coordination flexibility— that jointly influence ORMS (Sanchez 1995). Moreover, previous literature on the strategic flexibility framework has suggested that the hallmark of ORMS is that coordination flexibility effectively balances resource flexibility (Sanchez 1997).

On the one hand, resource flexibility means the extent to which the scope or range of firms’ resources can be used to generate a variety of alternative solutions to build a competitive advantage (Wright and Snell 1998). This component focuses on how available organizational resources contribute to firms’ competence in tackling environmental uncertainty (Sanchez 1995). Resource flexibility is characterized by the resources firms can use to counter exogenous shocks and maintain resilience. It includes having a wider range of alternative uses for available resources, lowering the costs and difficulties associated with integrating and using various resources, and/or shortening reaction times (Sanchez 1995). For example, during the pandemic, many employees worked from home, so firms with diverse IT resources utilized remote meeting tools, management information systems, cloud platforms, and other IT resources (e.g., mobile apps) to quickly establish a virtual office environment to tackle the challenges associated with the pandemic.

In the context of our study, when firms apply a variety of IT resources to withstand major external shocks, they must effectively leverage different types of ITs, often in combination, to realize benefits. Thus, appropriating the notion of resource flexibility to our investigative context, we consider ITPD to represent the available digital resources that firms can integrate and strategically use to foster ORMS. Note that we focus on how firms integrate a diverse set of IT resources to develop novel IT solutions (rather than switch between IT solutions) to counteract major shocks. We opted for this focus because during major shocks like the COVID-19 pandemic, many firms that previously operated solely offline become compelled to expand their operations to include multichannel approaches (e.g., online plus offline) rather than completely abandoning the offline channel and transitioning solely to online operations (Priyono et al. 2020). Finally, although it seems intuitive that ITPD can contribute to ORMS, we argue that this relationship requires further examination. As we elaborate in Section 3.1, the impact of ITPD should be understood as a hybrid effect of two countervailing forces, thereby rendering a nonlinear relationship.

On the other hand, coordination flexibility represents firms’ managerial capabilities to define strategies, configure resources, and deploy resources to maintain their structure, functions, and operational efficiency in the face of exogenous disturbances (Wright and Snell 1998). Specifically, coordination flexibility concerns the extent to which firms coordinate and integrate business activities across different functions to support the flexible use of resources by aligning their efforts and resources in response to environmental changes (Sanchez 1995). This component involves the flexibility to foster collaboration, communication, and coordination among various stakeholders within a firm. For example, firms may adjust business processes, modify administrative procedures, and allocate resources to ensure the smoothest operations possible during a major external shock (Li et al. 2023).

To effectively enhance ORMS, it is crucial for firms to improve their coordination competency. Firms that excel in coordination flexibility can effectively orga nize and balance flexible resources to mitigate the adverse impact of external shocks (Sanchez 1997, Li et al. 2023). Andrews (1980, p. 121) suggested that the essence of coordination is the “way in which subdivided functions and interests are (re)synthesized.” Contextualized to our investigative context, the concept of SPT (structural power of IT leaders) is critical in facilitating such cross-functional coordination and synergy for digital innovation (Garms and Engelen 2019). Our review of the IT leadership literature in leading IS journals (see Online Appendix F) revealed that a higher formal position of an IT leader suggests a higher level of SPT within the focal firm. Strategically speaking, SPT reflects an IT leader’s structural position to effectively coordinate organizational digital resources that could be applied to counteract major shocks (Vier egger et al. 2017). SPT can help improve internal coordination and collaboration between IT and other functions in firms. When IT leaders are part of a firm’s TMT, they not only gain more business knowledge but can also help business executives acquire more IT knowledge, facilitating mutual understanding and shared knowledge between IT and business functions. With higher SPT, IT leaders have more interactions with diverse TMT members to discuss the coordination of IT resources to support different functions (Preston and Karahanna 2009a). In this vein, SPT facilitates collaborative relationships between IT leaders and other top business executives, thus enhancing organizational performance (Chen et al. 2010). Prior studies have further suggested that cross-functional mutual understanding and shared knowledge among IT and business executives increase the likelihood of effective planning and the application of IT resources to attain desirable organizational outcomes (Ocasio and Joseph 2018).

Importantly, although higher resource flexibility and coordination flexibility both seem desirable for bolstering ORMS, the hallmark of ORMS is that coordination flexibility should effectively balance resource flexibility (Sanchez 1997). For example, increased resource flexibility may help firms develop a variety of alternative solutions to mitigate exogenous shocks (Kortmann et al. 2014). Excessive resource flexibility, however, may enhance the likelihood of significant costs in generating, evaluating, and implementing diverse alternative solutions to counteract major shocks, thus jeopardizing ORMS. To avoid this situation, enhanced coordination flexibility should be implemented to balance increased resource flexibility (Sanchez 1997) by facilitating efficient management and resource allocation to extract the full potential of increased resource flexibility as well as by enhancing coordination and collaboration among different functions to attenuate the costs mentioned above (Li et al. 2023). In this vein, firms’ functions, structure, and operational efficiency can be further tailored to complement enhanced resource flexibility and thus develop organizational resiliency.

Building on the above, we contend that SPT could balance ITPD to elevate firms’ ORMS. First, during major shocks, IT leaders without sufficient structural power face challenges in generating IT-enabled initiatives, implementing IT-led strategies, and allocating critical IT resources effectively (Preston et al. 2008a, Feng et al. 2021). The benefit of firms’ ITPD (i.e., resource flexibility) cannot be fully realized without strong SPT (i.e., coordination flexibility). Second, the unpredictability of and the tremendous time pressure resulting from major external shocks together impose severe constraints on resource allocation within firms (Williams et al. 2017). In this case, SPT (i.e., coordination flexibility) may act as a catalyst in facilitating communication and collaboration between IT and busi ness leaders. Such interdisciplinary collaboration is crucial for prioritizing IT-oriented initiatives, resources, and strategies as well as for resolving cross-functional conflicts and disputes when firms leverage ITPD (i.e., resource flexibility) to attain ORMS. SPT can thus balance ITPD to further strengthen ORMS. We illustrate the theoretical mapping of our key concepts in Table 1.

## 2.3. The Contingency Role of Crisis-Induced Industry Turbulence

Past research has shown that these environmental shifts—whether in the form of economic crises, unpredictable fluctuations in supply and demand (de Haan et al. 2011), or variations in how shocks manifest (as opportunities or threats; Hamlin et al. 2012)—frequently require firms to develop stronger organizational resilience (Combe et al. 2012). Building on this premise, ORMS research has suggested that major external shocks can substantially reshape the competitive landscape (Grewal and Tansuhaj 2001), thereby influencing the ways firms cultivate and sustain resilience.

Meanwhile, the strategic flexibility framework underscores the importance of external contingencies as critical boundary conditions (e.g., Nadkarni and Narayanan 2007), positing that the effects of both resource flexibility and coordination flexibility can vary depending on environmental situations (Sanchez 1995). These different environmental situations can influence the value of particular resource configurations and coordination mechanisms, shaping both how firms develop resilience and the extent to which they rely on it during crises. Recent calls within the literature similarly encourage deeper investigation into how dynamic environmental changes influence resilience amid major shocks (Floetgen et al. 2021).

In response to these calls, we explore crisis-induced industry turbulence as a vital external contingency that may affect how firms combine IT resources with powerful IT leadership to build ORMS. Specifically, whereas many industries experience substantial losses during major shocks, others may see heightened demand for their products or services, such as vaccine manufacturing and express delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic. ACTU stands for situations where shocks significantly disrupt and harm firms’ operations, whereas BCTU denotes environments where shocks generate industry shifts that favor firms’ performance. By focusing on these industrial environmental variations, we examine how the combined influence of resource flexibility (i.e., ITPD) and coordination flexibility (i.e., SPT), as posited by the strategic flexibility framework, shapes ORMS under contrasting external conditions.

## 3.1. IT Portfolio Diversity and Firms

During a major shock, both the promoting and the suppressing forces of ITPD on ORMS can be activated because of the urgent need to respond swiftly to the shock (Bailey and Breslin 2021). We argue that the promoting force of ITPD can improve ORMS. In our research context, ITPD represents the extent to which diverse IT resources can be integrated and used by a firm. Hence, the promoting force is made possible through such integration and use of diverse IT resources to facilitate firms’ ability to develop alternative solutions to enhance their resilience (Kohli et al. 2012, Park et al. 2023) during a major shock. For example, increased ITPD may be applied to facilitate the development of alternative business solutions to meet the market’s emerging needs when the external environment is unstable (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011), thus enhancing ORMS. We argue that increased ITPD can positively affect ORMS but is unlikely to do so in an exponential manner. We thus adopt a linear proposition to approximate the promoting force of ITPD. This linear approach is in line with prior research that has examined the diversity of other types of organizational resources (e.g., partner diversity (Hagedoorn et al. 2018) and alliance portfolio diversity (Shukla et al. 2020)) and discovered positive linear, rather than exponential, promoting effects.

We now pivot our attention to the suppressing force, which indicates that increased ITPD, especially excessive ITPD, may impair ORMS. We argue that increased ITPD could lead to higher costs associated with generating, evaluating, and exercising strategic options. In particular, the costs could be severely exacerbated because of the high unpredictability and intensive time pressure associated with major exogenous shocks (Yin and Jing 2014), thus extensively impairing ORMS. We elaborate our reasons below.

First, the unpredictability of a major shock could dramatically exacerbate the costs of generating, evaluating, and exercising the strategic options afforded by increased ITPD. High-ITPD firms, compared with low-ITPD firms, have a broader and more diverse range of options in terms of combining different IT resources. In the face of a highly unpredictable major shock, a firm may generate and evaluate numerous options, as permitted by its ITPD (Kendra and Wachtendorf 2003), but the adjustment and coordination efforts required to effectively exercise these options may bring significant challenges (Karhade and Dong 2021a). In this vein, high-ITPD firms, compared with low-ITPD firms, are more likely to have higher costs related to cross-functional process redesign and governance (Williams and Karahanna 2013) when exercising and realizing the options enabled by their high ITPD. Prior research has empirically shown that the costs of exercising options can be much higher when involving a richer diversity of IT resources (Karhade and Dong 2021b). Therefore, we suggest that the suppressing force is drastically higher for high-ITPD firms than for low-ITPD firms, thereby negatively affecting ORMS during major shocks.

Second, the time pressure to resist the effects of a major shock emphasizes the urgency of combining diverse IT resources to counteract the external turbulence caused by the shock. This intensive time pressure, which demands swift action for survival (Yin and Jing 2014), is likely to further exacerbate the suppressing force. The process of generating, evaluating, and implementing strategic options enabled by high ITPD is time-consuming (Kendra and Wachtendorf 2003). Unfortunately, major shocks like the COVID-19 pandemic allow very little time for firms to effectively and efficiently generate, evaluate, and exercise the options provided by their high ITPD (Billings et al. 1980), thus bringing about a significant challenge for firms.

Third, the impacts of unpredictability and time pressure often intertwine and compound each other, resulting in a “double curse” effect (Billings et al. 1980). As discussed earlier, with increased ITPD, the suppressing force, which entails generating and evaluating crisissolving options, can increase significantly. In addition, the time pressure associated with a major shock can further amplify this suppressing force. In other words, the negative impacts of time pressure can exacerbate the negative impacts of unpredictability. Consequently, with increased ITPD, the costs of integrating and utilizing high ITPD to counteract exogenous shocks can surge exponentially during major shocks (Starr and Wassenhove 2014).

Considering both the promoting force and the suppressing force, we follow prior literature (Haans et al. 2016) to conclude that there is likely a significant inverted U-shaped relationship between ITPD and ORMS during major shocks, as illustrated in Figure 2. This proposed inverted U-shaped effect is logically consistent with prior studies that have theoretically and empirically identified an inverted U-shaped relationship between diversity in organizational resources and the associated organizational impacts after considering the linear promoting and exponential suppressing forces (Hagedoorn et al. 2018, Seo et al. 2020, Shukla et al. 2020). We thus propose the following.

After a major shock (i.e., the “new norm” period), because of their own experience as well as observations of others’ failure and success, firms are likely to alter their visions regarding the role of available IT resources for ORMS (Megyeri 2019) such that they can develop solutions based on their ITPD to adapt to the new norm (Megyeri 2019). The above discussion suggests two points: (1) the logic of the promoting force is intensively activated when progressing from a normal period (i.e., before the major shock) to the acute period of a major shock (Park et al. 2023), and (2) once intensively activated during the major shock, this promoting force is likely to maintain momentum when progressing to the period after the major shock (Tremblay et al. 2023). Combining these two points, we argue that the difference in the promoting force between before and during the major shock, if any, is more significant than that between during and after the major shock.

We now turn to the suppressing force. On the one hand, the suppressing force of ITPD may have little to no impact before a major shock because existing IT solutions are often sufficient to handle minor shocks (e.g., bad weather). Firms need not generate, evaluate, and implement novel IT-based crisis-solving solutions to urgently and drastically counteract these minor shocks. We thus contend that the costs associated with generating, evaluating, and exercising IT-based solutions are significantly less before versus during a major shock.

On the other hand, the suppressing force after a major shock is likely less pronounced than during the shock. In particular, the unpredictability and time pressure to integrate and use a variety of IT resources within a short period of time is reduced after a shock (Pearson and Clair 1998). Although novel IT solutions may still need to be developed to adapt to the new norm after a major shock, firms do not need to urgently generate, evaluate, and implement many crisis-solving solutions in a short period of time. Meanwhile, afte experiencing a major shock, firms’ ability to utilize a larger number of IT-based solutions is likely further strengthened. As such, the costs associated with generating, evaluating, and exercising IT-based solutions may not be as demanding as during the major shock. Thus, although the suppressing force may still show a curvilinear shape (Banker et al. 1998) after a major shock, the curvature of this shape should be less pronounced than the curvature during the major shock.

The above discussion suggests that (1) the suppressing force is minimally present prior to a major shock but is intensively activated during the major shock, and (2) once intensively activated during the major shock, the suppressing force may subside to a certain extent, if not disappear entirely, after the shock, as the unpredictability and time pressure that drive the suppressing force during the acute period of the major shock are likely to decrease after the major shock. Combining these two points, we argue that the difference in the sup pressing force between before and during a major shock, if any, is more significant than that between during and after the major shock. Synthesizing our discussion above about the differential effects of the promoting and suppressing forces before versus during the acute period of a major shock and those during versus after this period, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 1b. Regarding the inverted U-shaped effect of ITPD on ORMS, the differential effects during versus before the acute period of a major shock are more significant than the differential effects during versus after this period.

## 3.2. The Joint Impact of IT Portfolio Diversity and the Structural Power of IT Leaders

We consider it crucial for SPT to balance ITPD to fur ther elevate ORMS. We discuss how SPT works with

ITPD to achieve improved ORMS by affecting both the promoting and suppressing forces. To begin, we argue that SPT can balance ITPD by making the promoting force more effective. In the context of major shock, SPT facilitates efficient management and IT resource allocation as well as coordination and collaboration between IT and other functions within firms. Consequently, SPT becomes an important catalyst for integrating and using diverse IT-related resources (Chen et al. 2010) and for fostering mutual understanding between IT and business functions, all of which are critical for planning and applying IT resources to achieve desirable organizational outcomes (Ocasio and Joseph 2018). As Buyl et al. (2019, p. 1380) noted, “a sufficient amount of internal resources [i.e., ITPD] and the ability to rearrange, transform, and adapt these resources to the uncertain and changed economic conditions $[ \mathrm { i . e . , S P T } ] ^ { \prime \prime }$ are the key ingredients for achieving ORMS. Hence, a higher level of SPT, relative to a lower level of SPT, allows firms to better enhance their efficiency and effectiveness when configuring and deploying diverse IT resources to develop alternative solutions, thereby amplifying the benefits of ITPD for attaining higher ORMS during major shocks.

Next, we argue that SPT can further balance ITPD by attenuating the effect of the suppressing force. As mentioned for Hypothesis 1a, the suppressing force primarily arises from the drastically increasing costs of generating, evaluating, and exercising the alternative strategic options enabled by ITPD under the circumstances of high unpredictability and intensive time pressure to counteract major shocks. We contend that a high level of SPT can mitigate such challenges for the following reasons. First, strong SPT can facilitate firms’ ability to orchestrate effective cross-functional coordination (Wang et al. 2006), thus reducing the costs related to process redesign among different functions within firms when generating, evaluating, and exercising the strategic options enabled by high ITPD in unpredictable environments. Second, strong SPT can enhance strategic alignment between IT and business functions as well as enhance corporate strategies through IT leaders’ decision-making authority (Wunderlich 2018). Thus, firms’ core functions can be further strengthened so they can react swiftly to generate, evaluate, and implement crisis-solving options enabled by high ITPD (Liu et al. 2009), thereby decreasing the aforementioned costs.

Considering the above discussion on the promoting and suppressing forces, a high level of SPT, compared with a low level of SPT, may better balance increased ITPD, thereby elevating firms’ ORMS. Figure 3 visualizes our reasoning about the joint effect of SPT and ITPD. Following Haans et al. (2016, p. 1185), the moderating curvilinear relationship is represented by (1) a right or left shift in the turning point of the curve, which means the promoting force is strengthened or weakened by the moderator; (2) a flattening or steepening of the curvature, which means the suppressing force is weakened or strengthened by the moderator; or both (1) and (2). Hence, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 2. During a major shock, SPT influences th effect of ITPD on ORMS by (a) shifting the turning point to the right and/or (b) flattening the curve of the inverted U-shaped relationship.

## 3.3. The Contingencies of ACTU and BCTU

Major shocks affect industries by creating various types of turbulence, which may in turn yield distinct impacts on how firms leverage their ITPD and SPT to build ORMS. Following the principle of moderating curvilinear relationships (Haans et al. 2016), we further examine how ACTU and BCTU affect the moderated promoting force (i.e., ITPD × SPT) and suppressing force $( \mathrm { i . e . , \bar { I } T P D ^ { 2 } \times S P T } )$ articulated in Hypothesis 2.

3.3.1. ACTU: Industries Damaged by Major Shocks. For industries adversely affected by major shocks, we argue that the heightened ACTU intensifies the moderated promoting force of ITPD and SPT for ORMS. Firms in high-ACTU environments face heightened uncertainty and unpredictability, requiring more agile and coordinated responses (Larraneta et al. 2014). To that end, firms may depend more on ITPD to adapt swiftly, capitalize on emerging prospects, and thus attain resilience. Powerful IT leaders (i.e., high SPT) can also complementarily guide the selection, integration, and deployment of diverse IT resources, thereby ensuring ITPD can be effectively leveraged for ORMS. Accordingly, firms with greater ITPD and stronger SPT are better positioned to seize the opportunities induced by ACTU to promote ORMS.

In this case, integrating and coordinating a diverse IT portfolio under intensified complexity and difficulty become more costly, time-consuming, and prone to judgment errors (Winter 2003, Yin and Jing 2014). Although structurally powerful IT leaders may mitigate some of these challenges, the intensified complexity and difficulty induced by ACTU may compromise the positive influence of SPT. This challenge could exacerbate the aforementioned moderated suppressing force of ITPD and SPT $( \mathrm { i . e . , \ I T P D ^ { 2 } \times S P T }$ , per Hypothesis 2(b)), making it harder for firms—even well-prepared firms—to fully realize the benefits of ITPD without incurring operational friction or inefficiency (Karhade and Dong 2021a). 3.3.2. BCTU: Industries Benefiting from Major Shocks. For industries benefiting from major shocks, we argue that the high BCTU may weaken the moderated promoting force of ITPD and SPT on ORMS. Substantial opportunities, such as surging demand, may arise as firms in such industries have already been supplying products or services that customers ultimately need to overcome crises caused by major shocks. It is thus less critical for these firms to urgently explore a wide-ranging IT portfolio for radical innovation, decreasing their reliance on ITPD to build resilience during major shocks. For instance, during the COVID-19 pandemic, online communication technology providers experienced surging demand. As these providers could easily meet such demand with their existing or incrementally improved solutions, they did not face urgency to explore diversified IT portfolios to create radically different products or services. In the meantime, although powerful IT leaders are instrumental in converting ITPD into ORMS (per Hypothesis 2(a)), the overall need for this conversion, as mentioned earlier, is significantly reduced in industries benefiting from major shocks. Following this vein, high BCTU may decrease the moderated promoting force of ITPD and SPT on ORMS.

We further submit that high BCTU may lessen the moderated suppressing force of ITPD and SPT. As elaborated in Hypothesis 1a, uncertainty and time pressure to rapidly integrate and coordinate diverse IT resources are the key reasons amplifying the sup pressing force of ITPD. However, firms in industries benefiting from major shocks may experience little unpredictability and time pressure because they have already been providing the products or services customers ultimately need during these major shocks, thereby alleviating the suppressing force. ## 4. Research Method

## 4.1. Data Sources

We built a unique data set by collecting firm-level data from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database and the Wind database, both of which have been widely used by researchers, as well as from annual reports of public firms in China. We also validated our data using third-party data (e.g., TianYanCha). After merging various sources, our final sample consisted of 2,926 A-share public firms.

## 4.2. Variables and Measures

## 4.2.1. Dependent Variable and Independent Variables

ORMS. Prior studies have used changes in firms business performance, such as changes in revenue (or profit; Dimitriadis 2021), as proxies for organizational resilience. The rationale is that the more resilient a firm is, the more likely its performance will be less impaired or will be restored, maintained, or even enhanced when counteracting a major shock (DesJardine et al. 2019). Thus, following prior studies (Li et al. 2023), we used firms’ revenue change as a proxy to measure ORMS.

Based on COVID-19 cases and local policy, we chose the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic in mainland China (the first three quarters (Q1–Q3) of 2020) as the “during the major shock” period.<sup>1</sup> The COVID-19 pandemic broke out in mainland China in January 2020 and quickly entered the acute phase. The Chinese government then gradually loosened its lockdowns, and most business activities began to recover and reach prepandemic levels by September 2020 (Reuters 2020, World Health Organization 2020). We collected the firms’ quarterly financial report data from the Wind database. We used the revenue data from the first three quarters of 2020 as the numerator and calculated the average revenue during the first three quarters of 2017, 2018, and 2019 as the denominator. We thus measured ORMS as a ratio. The large the ratio value, the higher the confidence for the claim that a firm withstood the shock (if the ratio value is less than one) or even achieved improved performance (if the ratio value is greater than one). The larger the ratio, the better a firm’s ORMS. As a robustness check, following Dimitriadis (2021), we also used firms’ change in profit as a proxy to measure ORMS and obtained the similar results (Online Appendix C).

ITPD. The availability of IT resources for use and integration to build firms’ competitive advantage is often reflected in firms’ annual reports (Steelman et al. 2019), which typically include information about a firm’s IT-related strategic directions, goals, resources, and initiatives and are thus an important and commonly used data source in management research (Li et al. 2021). We collected Chinese public firms’ annual reports. Next, we coded the IT resources that were available to the focal firm based on the firm’s annual reports, which indicated whether the firm owned, developed, or implemented IT resources that could readily be used to build a competitive advantage. For this purpose, we developed a software program using computer-aided text-mining techniques (for details, see Online Appendix A; Bao and Datta 2014). We removed duplicate and unrelated descriptions of ITs to ensure the clarity, accuracy, and completeness of our textmining results. We recruited two experts to develop the coding scheme. We then trained three coders with sufficient experience to reach an aligned understanding of ITPD. Each coder was given an identical set of 30 randomly selected items and independently performed manual coding. Next, they independently coded all extracted citations. The results were then compared, and any disagreements were resolved via discussions among the coders and authors. The intercoder reliabil ity test returned a Fleiss’s κ value of 0.75, which is higher than the required 0.707, suggesting substantial intercoder agreement (Landis and Koch 1977). Finally, we identified firms’ ITPD based on the keywords that were developed. We measured ITPD as the sum of the number of distinct types of IT categories. We also considered other alternative measures of ITPD, and the analysis results remained consistent (see Online Appendices C and E).

SPT. The CSMAR database provides demographic information about firms’ senior managers, such as name, age, gender, and job title. We identified IT leaders via managerial job titles, such as chief information officer (CIO), vice president of IS, director of IS, IT manager, and so forth (Lim et al. 2013). We manually scrutinized the role descriptions of these members through their curricula vitae to ensure validity. SPT was measured as an ordinal variable based on the position of the focal IT leader within their firm. We coded SPT as zero if a firm’s annual report listed no IT leader among the senior managers, as one if the IT leader was mentioned in the senior manager list but held no executive-level roles, as two if the IT leader was part of the TMT, as three if the IT leader served on the firm’s board, as four if the IT leader served as chief executive officer (CEO), and as five if the IT leader acted as board ${ \mathrm { c h a i r } } ^ { 2 }$ (Finkelstein 1992, Chen et al. 2006, Preston et al. 2008a).<sup>3</sup> We also coded SPT in other ways, such as using multiple dummy variables to represent different levels of SPT, and the results remained consistent (see Online Appendix C).

ACTU and BCTU. CTU was measured as the change in industry-level profit growth during the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic (Bailey et al. 1986, Hambrick and Cannella 2004). Specifically, we retrieved the profit growth rate (PGR) for the first three quarters of 2020 for all publicly listed firms and calculated the industrial PGRs (IPGRs; the average PGR of all other firms (excluding the focal firms) with the same threedigit Standard Industrial Classification industry code). To remove seasonal effects, we developed sameperiod IPGRs (SIPGRs) by subtracting the average IPGRs for the first three quarters of 2017, 2018, and 2019 from the IPGRs for the first three quarters of 2020. Industries with negative SIPGRs were identified as those adversely affected by major shocks, whereas industries with positive SIPGRs were classified as beneficiaries of such shocks. To capture the extent of industry turbulence, we calculated ACTU as the absolute value of SIPGRs when SIPGRs were negative and set it to zero otherwise. Similarly, we measured BCTU as the original value of SIPGRs when SIPGRs were positive and set it to zero otherwise.

4.2.2. Control Variables. We controlled for firm-level, region-level, and industry-level characteristics. The detailed description of control variables can be found in Online Appendix B.

All these results collectively confirm our research findings.

## 5.2. Theoretical Implications

This study makes several novel and valuable contributions to the IS and organizational resilience literature. For IS research, this paper is among the earliest to leverage the strategic flexibility framework to offer a nuanced understanding of how the integration and use of diverse IT resources (i.e., ITPD) influence ORMS.

Our first theoretical contribution is related to RQ1 and Hypothesis 1a and Hypothesis 1b regarding how ITPD influences ORMS. Specifically, this study offers an insightful explanation for how firms leverage the strategic options afforded by distinct types of IT resources (i.e., ITPD) to build ORMS. In particular, limited research on IT resources and resilience has systematically evaluated how firms’ IT resources benefit ORMS (Donnellan et al. 2006). Moreover, prior studies have treated IT resources either narrowly as single applications (Scott et al. 2006) or broadly as general IT capabili ties (Oh and Teo 2006) but have rarely considered the diversity of IT resources. Inspired by the strategic flexibility framework, this study advances this stream of research by examining how ITPD acts as a key driver of ORMS.

Furthermore, extant research on IT resources and resilience has predominantly assumed a linear positive view that only considers the benefits of IT resources without considering the costs associated with converting diversified IT resources into ORMS (Karhade and Dong 2021b). Anchoring in the strategic flexibility framework, our paper pushes the envelope by simultaneously considering both the promoting and suppressing forces of ITPD (Sanchez 1995) when formulating its effect on ORMS. By considering both the promoting and suppressing forces, we offer nuanced explanations to theoretically inform the curvilinear effect of ITPD on ORMS. To the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to (1) conceptualize both the promoting and suppressing forces of ITPD and (2) empirically discover the inverted U-shaped effect of ITPD on ORMS, thereby significantly contributing to the IS literature. Future studies on IT resource diversification may further advance the theorizing of such curvilinea effects to hopefully arrive at a better understanding of how ITPD exerts organizational impacts in different contexts.

Moreover, our findings reveal that the curvilinea relationship between ITPD and ORMS is most pronounced during, rather than before or after, a major shock. In other words, although organizational resilience always remains relevant to firms, its significance varies in magnitude. The temporal distinction of before, during, and after a shock was pivotal to our theoretical development of Hypothesis 1a and Hypothesis 1b. This temporal distinction facilitated our discovery of the inverted U-shaped relationship between ITPD and ORMS, which is notably more pronounced during, rather than before or after, a major shock. Interestingly, our results further uncover that the differential effects between before and during a shock are more substantial than the differential effects between during and after a shock (per Hypothesis 1b). Interested scholars may further investigate the applicability of our temporal distinction to other crisis contexts, particularly those marked by unpredictability and time constraints.

First, the strategic flexibility framework provides a novel lens to investigate the role of IT leaders in exerting their managerial discretion to coordinate organizational IT resources to foster ORMS. Although prior research has accumulated a rich body of knowledge about the role of IT leaders (e.g., CIOs), most of these studies have examined IT leaders’ characteristics (Bassellier et al. 2001), authority (Preston et al. 2006), and relationships with TMTs (Preston and Karahanna 2009a). With the increased importance of IT resources for firms, prior studies have also examined the strategic role of IT leaders, such as senior IT leaders’ influences on organizational outcomes (e.g., strategic alignment between IT functions and business functions; Preston and Karahanna 2009b) and stock market performance (Banker et al. 2011). SPT has thus gained increasingly more attention in recent years (Chen et al. 2021).

However, there has been limited insight into the strategic role of SPT in coordinating various types of IT resources (ITPD) to enhance firms’ ORMS. Against this backdrop, we draw on the strategic flexibility framework and submit that SPT has a joint effect with ITPD on ORMS. Our findings confirm that SPT facilitates the promoting force of ITPD on ORMS by shifting the turning point to the right (per Hypothesis 2(a)). This finding underscores the strategic significance of SPT as IT leaders’ stronger structural power enables firms to coordinate higher levels of IT resource diversity, thereby significantly enhancing resilience. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first to theoretically conceive of SPT as a representation of coordination flexibility and empirically prove how SPT and ITPD jointly determine firms’ ORMS, hence making a key contribution to the IS literature in general and to IT leadership research in particular. Future research should further examine SPT to achieve a deeper understanding of its role in shaping organizational resilience, which is critical for firms’ survival and excellence in the digital era.

Our third contribution is associated with RQ3, Hypothesis 3, and Hypothesis 4 regarding how environmental factors influence the formation of ORMS, advancing the strategic management literature on strategic flexibility and organizational resilience. Specifically, responding to the call to incorporate environmental factors when studying strategic flexibility, some have looked into external factors like economic recessions (Pal et al. 2014) and adverse climate conditions (Huang et al. 2018). Our study further enriches this stream by examining the contingent role of ACTU and BCTU. Our findings reveal that ACTU and BCTU exert distinct contingency effects on the combined influence of ITPD and SPT on ORMS. Specifically, ACTU shifts the turning point of the inverted U-shaped relationship to the right and steepens its curvature, whereas BCTU flattens the curve without significantly changing the turning point. As a result, in industries adversely affected by shocks, the synergy of strong IT leadership and diverse IT resources has a more pronounced effect on building resilience. Conversely, in industries that benefit from shocks, firms enjoy greater flexibility in IT resource management, as their resilience remains relatively consistent across varying levels of IT resource diversity and leadership strength. These findings move beyond the prevailing tendency to view environmental change or turbulence as a single-dimensional construct. By differentiating industries harmed by shocks from those that benefit, this study enriches our understanding of how major shocks shape organizational resilience, highlighting the importance of considering distinct industry contexts.<sup>4</sup>

## 5.3. Managerial Implications

This study also provides actionable guidelines for firms seeking to strategically implement IT resources to facilitate organizational resilience to counteract unexpected shocks. First, our research demonstrates that ITPD has a salient inverted U-shaped relationship with ORMS during a major shock. Thus, using our findings to inform practitioners should focus on the strategic decision to leverage IT resource diversity. When ITPD is too low or too high, it can lead to inferior performance related to organizational resilience. Firms can use our research findings as an empirical reference to navigate the strategic decision to leverage IT resource diversity to enhance ORMS before major shocks occur.

Second, our results point to the importance of empowering IT leaders with appropriate structural power to help firms realize the impact of ITPD on organizational resilience. Firms that intend to build digitally enabled organizational resilience to withstand unforeseeable shocks should pay special attention to enhancing SPT.

Third, our findings underscore the need for firms to deliberately cultivate organizational resilience, enabling them to strategically align ITPD and SPT in ways that account for varying industrial environments. For example, when a firm’s industry is severely impaired by a major shock, the firm should elevate SPT when utilizing ITPD to build resilience as the joint impact of ITPD and SPT becomes more salient in this case. Also, prioritizing SPT (e.g., developing adequate SPT) can help firms sustain resilience and mitigate turbulence-induced challenges during periods of major shocks. However, such implications may not be suitable for firms in industries that benefit from major shocks. In short, a firm should select the most appropriate strategy to withstand the adversity caused by a major shock based on the situation at hand.

## 5.4. Research Limitations and Future Directions

Our study has several limitations that also offer opportunities for future study. First, we operationalized ORMS as the change in firms’ revenue and profit. Although we followed the approaches used by seminal papers (e.g., Dimitriadis 2021) and used revenue (profit) change to measure this construct, ITPD might have varying effects on different business performance metrics. Although we strived to collect a variety of metrics, there was a scarcity of available data. Thus, firms’ revenue (profit) change was the most appropriate data we were able to collect. Future studies could gather different performance measures to further assess ORMS and assess its relationship with ITPD. Moreover, we did not leverage machine learning techniques to code ITPD. As a promising method, future research could employ machine learning techniques to improve the measurement of ITPD.

Second, because ORMS may have far-reaching impli cations, future studies should explore the effects of ITPD and SPT on ORMS for firms in distinct cultural and market settings. We collected data only from Chinese publicly listed firms in specific sectors. Although this approach established internal validity in the Chinese market, the generalizability of our model and findings is subject to further scrutiny in other cultural and economic contexts. Replication and further assessment in other settings, such as the U.S. and EU markets, is important as evidence suggests that China may differ from other economic bodies not only in cultural values (e.g., collectivism; Fang et al. 2007) but also in corporate governance (e.g., the structure of TMTs). Consequently, we encourage future studies to assess country-level effects based on our findings. Moreover, our sample comprises publicly listed firms, which may introduce generalizability concerns, as smaller or entrepreneurial firms may exhibit different responses. Future research could address this limitation by exploring such firms to determine whether these patterns hold across diverse organizational contexts.

Third, although we replicated the main effect of ITPD on ORMS using U.S.–China trade dispute data in the post hoc analysis, the results regarding the impact of SPT are insignificant. After further scrutiny, we found that the SPT data are limited as only 96 out of the 832 firms in this sample had data on IT leaders. Hence, we acknowledge this as a limitation and call for future studies to reexamine this issue with sufficient IT leadership data during major shocks.

Fourth, the learning effect detected in our post hoc analysis could be exemplified by a wide range of measures. However, our post hoc analysis did not provide a theoretically sound explanation of what the learning effect is and how it happens, which represents opportunities for future exploration. We encourage followup studies to further examine the learning effect regarding building ORMS to attain a more in-depth understanding of this important phenomenon.

Fifth, the prevailing IT categories in each industry may be different. For that reason, we included industry fixed effects—a widely used practice to control for industry heterogeneity—in our main model. We also found support for our findings in a robustness test by adding dummy variables to represent IT categories (to be provided upon request). However, we acknowledge that the effect of specific types of ITs (e.g., supply chain management systems) is an interesting topic that deserves further scrutiny.

Sixth, the conceptualizations of organizational resilience in the extant literature can be categorized into two predominant types: outcome-based and processbased conceptualizations. Given that the process-based conceptualization is still in the developmental stage, we decided to adopt the more established approach to conceptualize organizational resilience as an outcome. This decision was informed by the greater maturity and robustness of the outcome-based perspective, which offers a clearer and more measurable benchmark for evaluating organizational resilience. However, the process-based view has the potential to offer more details about the “way” leading to organizational resilience. We acknowledge our choice of the outcomebased approach as a limitation and encourage study on process-based organizational resilience.
