Abstract. Many online platforms have utilized information technology, such as artificial intelligence (AI), to reduce consumers’ information search costs and facilitate their decision-making processes. Given the variety of online information, these technologies are often effective in reducing the search cost for only specific information types: a concept we refer to as search cost reduction for partial information. For rational consumers, this can lead to improved decision making. However, consumers do not always behave rationally and may exhibit behavioral biases in their decision-making process. In this study, we propose that search cost reduction for partial information can induce cognitive miser behavior in consumers, ultimately leading to worse decision-making. To explore this understudied puzzle, we leverage a natural experiment on Yelp to examine the effect of enabling search cost reduction for partial information on the quality of consumers’ decisions regarding restaurants. We constructed a unique panel data set using matched pairs of restaurants across Yelp and TripAdvisor. By applying a difference-in-differences design, we aim to casually infer how consumer decision quality is affected following the introduction of Yelp’s new AI-powered image categorization feature in August 2015, which was designed to reduce the search cost of review images. We find that adding the AI-powered image categorization feature has a negative effect on consumer decision quality. Delving into the text analysis of consumer complaints using deep learning techniques, we further find that the inferior decision quality of consumers postfeature introduction is primarily due to reduced awareness of restaurants’ service quality—information that is readily available in review texts but not in review images. Our findings suggest that reducing search costs for partial information may hurt consumers as it may incentivize cognitive miser behavior. This occurs as consumers disproportionately pay attention to the product information of which the search cost has been reduced, whereas paying less attention to other relevant product information. We discuss the implications of these findings for online platforms.

## 1. Introduction

Online platforms such as Yelp and CNET have become integral to consumers’ lives, serving as rich information sources for evaluating products before purchase. Previous research has demonstrated that such platforms facilitate consumers’ purchasing decisions and enhance consumer welfare (Lynch and Ariely 2000, Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Kuksov 2004, Duan et al. 2008, Kim et al. 2010, Zhu and Zhang 2010, Jiang et al. 2018, Huang et al.

2019, Li et al. 2019). With the increasing volume and diversity of information available on these platforms, including texts, images, and videos, facilitating access to the most relevant information at a low cost has become increasingly important. To this end, many online platforms have employed information technology, such as artificial intelligence (AI), to make the process more convenient and efficient, allowing consumers to quickly locate what they need. Because the technologies required to process and search each type of information differ, these tools are often tailored to specific types of information (Jiang and Zou 2020). For example, to reduce users’ search costs associated with textual information, Quora.com uses text mining algorithms to categorize the topics of each question and assign relevant tags. This helps users swiftly find questions related to a particular topic by clicking the associated tag. Similarly, for video content, YouTube has introduced the AI-generated video chapters feature that segments videos into meaningful sections, thus allowing users to navigate through a video with ease and jump directly to specific sections as desired.

In this study, we focus on such features implemented by digital platforms aimed at reducing the cost of searching for partial (not all) product information prior to the purchase or consumption decision. We term such features as search cost reduction for partial information and examine its impact on consumers. In theory, assuming that all existing information is available and consumers are entirely rational, reducing search costs for partial information should increase consumers’ decision quality. This is because consumers could access at least the same amount of information as before but with reduced effort and time investment.<sup>1</sup> This view is backed up by research indicating that lower search costs correlate with higher search frequency and better search outcomes (Guo 2001, Kulviwat et al. 2004, Strebel et al. 2004, Jiang and Zou 2020). However, prior studies have also suggested that consumers may not be rational in decision making (Tversky and Kahneman 2000, Gilovich et al. 2002). For example, recent research has shown that consumers’ decision making could be easily distorted by various factors such as environmental cues, emotional states, and self-selection bias (Han et al. 2007, Hu et al. 2017, Haggag et al. 2019).

We propose that search cost reduction for partial information might induce cognitive miser behavior among consumers. Cognitive miser theory posits that individuals may not process all the information in detail and prefer ways to minimize their cognitive effort (Zipf 1949, Risko and Gilbert 2016). From this perspective, when search cost reduction for partial information occurs, consumers may shift their attention and rely more on comparatively easy-to-process information, neglecting other aspects of product information that demand more cognitive processing. Given that different types of information can highlight different facets of a product, the attention shift due to the cognitive miser effect might lead consumers to overlook certain information or details without realizing it. If the more cognitively demanding product information contains unique insights that are not available in the less cognitively demanding information, the quality of consumer decision making could be compromised. This theoretical ambivalence calls for an empirical study to assess the effect of search cost reduction for partial information on consumers’ decision making. Although it has become a common practice for online platforms to design and implement features to reduce consumers’ search costs for partial information, to the best of our knowledge, few studies have systematically examined the phenomenon. Our study attempts to fill this void by addressing this research question: how doe search cost reduction for partial information on online platforms affect consumer decision quality?

We leverage a natural experiment on Yelp to conduct a difference-in-differences (DID) design to address our proposed research question. Yelp, as a leading consumer platform for restaurant information and reviews, implemented AI technologies through deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to revamp its image gallery features in August 2015. This new feature, pow ered by AI, automatically categorizes images for a restaurant into predefined categories, such as food, interior, outside, drink, and menu,<sup>2</sup> thereby simplifying users’ search for visual information. Prior to this, Yelp’s review images were uncategorized and displayed in a singular gallery. The new AI-powered image categorization feature effectively reduces consumers’ search costs for visual information, such as surveying a specific restaurant attribute (e.g., interior versus exterior). In contrast, TripAdvisor.com, a comparable platform in the same business, maintained a single, uncategorized image gallery throughout the study period. This exoge nous shock on the treated platform Yelp, coupled with no change on the control platform TripAdvisor.com, allows us to apply a DID design, which casually infers how search cost reduction for partial information affects consumer decision quality. We collected panel data from 4,136 restaurants across 10 U.S. cities on both platforms for the analysis.

We offer three notable findings. First, we find that the AI-powered image categorization feature, as a search cost reduction tool for visual information, has a significant and negative impact on consumer decision quality. Second, we employ deep learning techniques to analyze written complaints from consumers. We find that the primary factor contributing to inferior decision quality of consumers is an increase in complaints about restaurant service quality: an attribute that is pre sented rarely in review images but abundantly in review texts. In contrast, there are no significant changes in consumer complaints regarding restaurant ambiance and worthiness, attributes that can be conveyed well to consumers through review images. This finding indicates that the introduction of the AI-powered image categorization feature may inadvertently lead consumers to overlook critical information about restaurant service quality- information that requires more cognitive processing efforts to extract from review texts. Our findings suggest cognitive miser behavior induced by the search cost reduction in review images. Similarly, we find a marginal increase in consumer complaints about the food, which includes aspects like taste and ingredients that are not easily depicted through images. Third, to further validate the underlying cognitive miser mechanism, we investigate the heterogeneous effects of the AI-powered image categorization feature across restaurants with different service qualities. We find that the negative impact of the AI-powered image categorization feature on consumer decision quality is significantly mitigated for restaurants providing high-quality service. This suggests that when consumers shift their attention toward categorized images that have limited information about service quality, their decision quality is less likely to be compromised if the chosen restaurant offers high service quality. Likewise, we find that the negative impact of the AI-powered image categorization feature on consumer decision quality is marginally mitigated for restaurants with high food quality. Our analyses provide further support for the cognitive miser mechanism.

This study offers several theoretical and empirical contributions by documenting a case that reveals the negative effect of search cost reduction for partial information on consumer decision quality and uncovers the underlying mechanism behind this effect. First, to the best of our knowledge, our study is among the first to show the unintended negative case of search cost reduction for partial information on consumer decision quality. Although existing literature has primarily highlighted the benefits of lowered search costs for consumers (Bakos 1997, Jiang and Zou 2020), our findings suggest, counterintuitively, that search cost reduction for partial information might harm consumer decision quality. Second, we delve deeper into the theoretical understanding of why search cost reduction for partial information may lead to lower decision quality for consumers. Our findings indicate that search cost reduction for partial information might prompt consumers to disproportionately focus on a certain type of information over another due to the cognitive miser issue, thereby reducing their awareness of other product attributes. Third, our study also contributes to the body of knowledge of web page design (Macinnis and Price 1987, Lin et al. 2012). Prior studies have underlined the important and positive role of high-quality images in shaping consumer perceptions and decision making (Wansink et al. 1998, Underwood and Klein 2002). We extend this line of literature by showing that enhancements in the display of product images may, counterintuitively, have negative consequences on consumers. Finally, our study broadens the existing literature on system changes in online platforms. We show that feature changes, intended to aid platform users, may inadvertently alter user behavior and lead to unforeseen consequences. Our findings provide valuable implications for online platforms and businesses aiming to assist and improve consumer decision making.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Consumer Search Cost Reduction and Online Platforms

To make the best purchase decision, consumers must invest time and effort in gathering sufficient information about a product or service (Kulviwat et al. 2004, Hu et al. 2007, Wu et al. 2014). In the information economics literature, the direct and indirect costs of engag ing in such search processes to identify an appropriate product or service are referred to as search costs (Weitzman 1979, Diamond 1985, Feinberg and Huber 1996). A large body of research has identified the negative impact of search costs on consumers’ decision making. For example, Branco et al. (2012) demonstrated that consumers will reduce the amount of information they acquire due to high search costs. Anderson and Renault (2013) found that consumers facing high search costs tend to consider fewer sellers. Similarly, Dukes and Liu (2015) showed that high consumer search costs can prevent consumers from thoroughly evaluating sellers and their products.

With the help of information technology, consumers search costs have decreased over time. Numerous stud ies have indicated that the proliferation of online plat forms has substantially reduced consumer search costs in various contexts, such as books, hotels, and movies (Blake et al. 2016, Chen and Yao 2017, De Los Santos et al. 2017).<sup>3</sup> Against this backdrop, many studies have investigated the impacts of reduced search costs on consumers and firms (Hong and Shum 2006, Blake et al. 2016, Chen and Yao 2017, Ghose et al. 2019). Findings suggest that reduced search costs for consumers yield many benefits, such as decreased product prices (Lynch and Ariely 2000), higher product differentiation (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Kuksov 2004), increased online demand (Kim et al. 2010), and lower unemployment rates (Autor 2001). Despite a large number of studies underscoring the positive effects of reduced search cost, there has been a scant focus on cases where search cost reduction tools could potentially impair consumers decision making. Our study attempts to fill this void by providing an example of how search cost reduction for partial information can negatively impact consumers decision-making quality.

## 2.2. Feature Changes of Online Platforms

As online platforms frequently implement feature changes or new designs to improve their functionality for users, a growing body of scholarly research has focused on understanding the consequences and economic impacts of these system changes initiated by platforms (Adomavicius et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2018). The benefits of introducing feature changes that facilitate users in searching and filtering information have been well documented in several studies. Fo example, Brynjolfsson et al. (2006) showed that search engines and recommendation systems adopted by online platforms have contributed to the proliferation of the long-tail phenomenon, ultimately benefiting consumers. Similarly, Jiang and Benbasat (2007) found that the incorporation of augmented reality (AR) by e-commerce platforms can significantly improve consumers’ product understanding. Ghose et al. (2012) observed that online platforms with ranking systems can markedly reduce consumer search costs. In another study, Chen et al. (2018) found that changing the feedback system from a unidimensional design to a multidimensional design can significantly increase consumer satisfaction. Deng et al. (2022) investigated the impact of introducing an editorial review feature on a leading review platform, concluding that this new feature helps consumers filter out noise when gathering product information.

Our study joins this emerging stream of literature on system and feature changes in online platforms. We focus on the introduction of a search cost-reduction tool on a digital platform and examine its potential impact on consumers’ decision-making quality for offline consumption. Prior research has suggested that features introduced with good intentions can sometimes lead to unforeseen negative consequences (Grinbaum and Groves 2013). A distinctive contribution of this study is the application of cognitive miser theory to present an exceptional case to conventional wisdom, providing novel theoretical and empirical insights into the design of AI-powered search cost reduction tools for online platforms.

## 3. Theoretical Development

## 3.1. Positive Side of Search Cost Reduction for Partial Information

Although online platforms have enhanced search efficiency, they also aggregate an overwhelming amount of information (Chiang and Dholakia 2003, Branco et al. 2016). As a result, consumers often need to expend significant time and energy filtering a large volume of information on an online platform to extract what they need (Branco et al. 2012). It is therefore important for online platforms to facilitate consumers in acquiring information more efficiently and comprehensively (Browne et al. 2007).

An increasingly common practice among online platforms is to develop tools or introduce new features that aid consumers in searching and filtering specific types of information. For example, to reduce the search cost for video information and assist users in quickly locating the particular sections of a video they are interested in, YouTube introduced the AI-generated video chapters feature, which automatically segments a video into meaningful sections. Quora.com employs text mining algorithms to label the topics of each question, enhanc ing users’ ability to efficiently discover the answers they need.

Similarly, Yelp, the focus of our research context, implemented an AI-powered image categorization feature in August 2015 to reduce search costs for restaurant review images. This AI-powered feature automatically categorizes restaurant images by attributes (e.g., food, ambiance, etc.) and displays all images associated with a particular attribute in its gallery (Figure 1). With this new design, review images are categorized by attributes, enabling consumers to effortlessly find relevant images to evaluate a specific restaurant from various dimensions. This allows for a more efficient acquisition of visual information about a restaurant’s different attributes prior to making purchase decisions.

For rational consumers, search cost reduction fo partial information should lead to improved decision quality as it enables consumers, at least, to obtain the same amount of information as before. Prior research has also indicated that lowering information search costs can increase search intention and motivation, leading to enhanced search quality (Guo 2001, Kulviwat et al. 2004, Strebel et al. 2004). From this standpoint, the reduction in search costs for partial information is expected to improve consumer decision quality.

## 3.2. Cognitive Miser Behavior

According to behavioral economics research, consumer decision making may not always be fully rational (Stanovich 2009, Risko and Gilbert 2016). The introduction of search cost reduction tools by online platforms, which usually reduce the search cost for partial information (e.g., texts, images, videos, etc.), might induce cognitive miser behavior among consumers. Cognitive miser theory suggests that individuals, regardless of their intelligence, are more likely to think and solve problems in ways that minimize cognitive effort (Stanovich 2009).<sup>4</sup> That is, when making decisions, consumers are more inclined to acquire and process easy-to-process information (Zipf 1949, Risko and Gilbert 2016). In the context of product information searches, consumers might attempt to minimize two significant factors associated with the search action: time costs (Siegler and Lemaire 1997, Gray and Boehm-Davis 2000, Gray et al. 2006) and cognitive demands (Kool et al. 2010, Kurzban et al. 2013, Westbrook et al. 2013). First, consumers are more likely to opt for actions that are less timeconsuming to reduce the time cost associated with processing information (Gray et al. 2006). Suppose the search cost reduction tool has lowered the access or process time to certain types of product information. In that case, consumers may correspondingly lessen their search actions for other, more time-consuming information. Second, consumers are inclined to select activities that require comparatively less cognitive demands: the mental effort and energy required to process information (Kool et al. 2010). Hence, compared with consuming information from more cognitively demanding sources, consumers may prefer processing information produced by the search cost reduction tool and thus shift their attention toward the tool. As information from different sources depicts different facets of the product, the search cost reduction tool may result in consumers’ reduced awareness of or overlooking other types of product information prior to purchase, leading to lower decision quality.

Take the AI-powered image categorization feature on Yelp, our research context, as an example. According to the theory of cognitive miser, the reduced search costs for images may lead consumers to shift their attention toward categorized images and, more importantly, to neglect other information sources, such as review texts, when searching for production information. First, the AI-powered image categorization feature offers advantages in terms of time costs. On Yelp, review texts are not categorized or grouped by topics, making it a time-consuming process for consumers to find relevant details on specific attributes of a restaurant, say ambiance, which is dispersed throughout texts. In contrast, consumers can more easily gauge a restaurant’s ambiance by viewing all photos tagged with ambiance. Second, research indicates that images can convey information understood and absorbed more quickly than text (Gerrig et al. 2011). Categorized images, as opposed to review texts, present product attributes in a direct and visual manner, bypassing the need for consumers to mentally process information from unstructured texts. This makes the processing of images less cognitively demanding for consumers (Nelson 1974, Jiang and Benbasat 2007, De Langhe et al. 2016). Prior research has already shown that when there is a mixture of text and image information, consumers tend to skip over parts of the text and focus on the image information (Zinko et al. 2020). Prior to the implementation of the new feature, locating an image in a specific category, such as ambiance, was timeconsuming (i.e., the time cost to find the image was high). Despite the fact that the processing cost for images is generally lower than for the text, the high time costs prevent consumers from disproportionally shifting their attention toward images. With the introduction of the new feature, the time cost for locating the relevant images has decreased significantly. Therefore, it is more likely that consumers would disproportionately shift their attention toward images over text.

However, the feature change could introduce new complications, as the role of images in conveying prod uct information has the following limitations. First, although images can capture snapshots in time, they often fall short of providing the full context of a situation. This lack of background information can lead to the misinterpretation of events or subjects depicted. Second, the static nature of images means that they cannot convey the progression over time or the dynamics within a scenario. Third, images are unable to transmit sensory experiences such as smells, tastes, and textures. As a result, although images can depict tangible attributes and static information, such as the visual appeal of products, they struggle to present intangible attributes and dynamic information, such as service quality and product reliability (Lin et al. 2012). The decreased search costs of review images for restaurants can lead consumers to underutilize review texts due to cognitive miser. However, consumers might not notice that some restaurant attributes cannot be conveyed through review images but can be detailed in review texts, such as service quality. This oversight could heighten the risk of consumers making lower-quality decisions.

Given the conflicting theoretical predictions mentioned above, the impact of reduced search costs for partial information on online platforms on consumer decision quality remains ambiguous. Our study is among the first to provide a negative case to this puzzle by examining how the exogenous introduction of an AI-powered search cost reduction tool of review images on Yelp could influence consumer decision quality and uncover the underlying mechanism.

## 4. Research Methodology 4.1. Research Context

We investigated our research question using data sets from two leading restaurant review platforms: Yelp and TripAdvisor. These platforms are among the most popular and trusted review sites for consumers in the United States (Murphy 2017) and have been extensively used in the information systems (IS) literature to understand consumer decisions and user-generated content (Hong et al. 2016, Huang et al. 2017).

On August 27, 2015, Yelp implemented AI technologies through neural network models to automatically categorize review images into five distinct sections: food, inside, menu, outside, and drink. These categories are showcased on each restaurant’s Yelp page, as depicted in Figure 2.<sup>5</sup> Previously, review images were consolidated into a single gallery for each restaurant’s profile, resulting in a jumbled mix of photos representing various aspects of the restaurant, such as the cuisine, ambiance, and menu. The introduction of this AI-powered feature, which classifies images into specific categories, aims to diminish the search effort required by consumers to locate images pertinent to each category, thereby streamlining the process for consumers to evaluate specific attributes of a restau rant. As Yelp mentioned in the news announcement, “Sometimes the exact photo you need gets buried under several screenfuls of other photos (I’m looking at you Food).”<sup>6</sup> With the new feature, consumers wishing to gauge the ambiance or exterior of a restaurant can now quickly find relevant images under the Inside or Outside categories. The goal of this feature is “to help users find just the right information they need within the vast collection of photos our users have submitted.”<sup>7</sup> Detailed information about Yelp’s new AI-powered image categorization feature is available in Online Appendix A. In contrast, TripAdvisor’s display of review images has remained unchanged. The exogenous introduction of the AI-powered image categorization feature on Yelp, serving as a tool to reduce search costs for review images, enables us to investigate its causal impact on consumer decision quality, with TripAdvisor acting as a control group.

## 4.2. Observational Data and Measurement

We obtained Yelp’s reviews from the Yelp data set, a subset of Yelp’s review data published by Yelp for personal, educational, and academic use. The data set contains restaurant reviews in 10 representative U.S. cities, including Las Vegas, Philadelphia, Phoenix, Charlotte, Cleveland, Tempe, Madison, Scottsdale, Gilbert, and Henderson. These cities are located in different regions of the United States and have diverse cultural backgrounds. For each restaurant in the data set, we matched it with the same restaurant on TripAdvisor (if available) using the business name, address, and zip code. Our sample consists of 4,156 restaurants with accounts on both platforms and at least two reviews before and after the introduction of the review AI-powered image categorization feature on Yelp. Because each treated-control pair represents the same restaurant, the potential confounding effects of unobserved restaurant changes are eliminated.

We collected restaurant review data from both platforms in a six-month window prior to the treatment (i.e., from February 2015 to July 2015) and after the treatment $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ from September 2015 to February 2016). We also performed a systematic search of announcements on both platforms to ensure that no other confounding feature changes occurred on either platform during our observation window. The feature change occurred midmonth in August 2016, which comprises both pretreatment and posttreatment observations. Consequently, we excluded the data from our study for this month. We selected the six-month pre- and posttreatment observational window because a longer window may be more susceptible to confounding factors, and a shorter window may lead to a lack of power due to insufficient observations. In additional analyses reported in Table C1 of Online Appendix $C ,$ we confirmed the robustness of our main results using a shortened two-month window to reduce concerns about potential confounding factors. The unit of analysis is at the restaurant level, and we aggregated each restaurant’s data to the monthly level.

Our primary dependent variable of interest is consumer decision quality. Consistent with prior studies that use consumers’ postpurchase assessment of their purchase decisions as a measure of decision quality (Payne et al. 1993, Haubl and Trifts 2000), we measured consumer decision quality through their postvisit evaluations. Holding a restaurant’s quality constant, the better a restaurant matches a consumer’s needs and preferences, the better the decision to visit the restaurant is. In the context of online reviews, the rating given by a consumer reflects both the restaurant’s quality and the extent of match between the consumer’s needs and the product (Sun 2012, Kwark et al. 2014). Therefore, by comparing consumer review ratings of the same restaurant on the two platforms before and after the treatment, the relative change in consumer ratings provides a proxy for change in consumer decision quality. Each online rating is represented by a numerical score on a scale from one (most negative) to five (most positive). To gain deeper insights into consumer decision quality, we also analyzed the review texts to examine the likelihood of consumer complaints regarding four key attributes of a restaurant. These measures are detailed in Section 5.3. To control for restaurant tenure, we included the logarithm of the number of years the restaurant has been listed on Yelp (up to the focal month). Tables 1 and 2 present the main variables and summary statistics.

## 4.3. Empirical Estimation

Difference-in-differences (DID) estimation is utilized to estimate the causal effect of adding the AI-powered image categorization feature on review platforms on consumer decision quality. Specifically, DID makes use of longitudinal data from the treatment and control groups to estimate the causal effect of a treatment by comparing the changes in outcomes over time between the treatment group and the control group. Formally, our main estimation model is presented by the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{r} y _ {i t p} = \beta_ {1} P o s t _ {t} \times T r e a t m e n t _ {p} + \beta_ {2} L n (B u s i \_ a g e) _ {i t p} + \alpha_ {i p} \\ + \gamma_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t p}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where i indicates the restaurant, t denotes the month, and $p$ denotes the platform $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , Yelp or TripAdvisor); $y _ { i t p }$ represents the outcome of interest (i.e., decision quality) for consumers visiting restaurant i as observed in month t on platform $p ;$ Treatment is a dummy variable to indicate the treatment group (i.e., Yelp); and $P o s t _ { t }$ is a dummy variable that is set to one if t is after the Yelp platform change and zero otherwise. The restaurant × platform (i.e., Yelp or TripAdvisor) fixed effect $\alpha _ { i p }$ is included to control for the effects of timeinvariant characteristics of restaurants listed on Yelp and TripAdvisor, and the year-month time fixed effect $\gamma _ { t }$ is included to control for any seasonality effect. We also control for the tenure of a restaurant on the platform (Busi $\underline { { a g e } } _ { i t p } ) .$ , as more seasoned restaurants may have learned to better cater to consumers’ needs. Because restaurants on the treated platform and restaurants on the control platform are identical, any unobserved time-varying changes in a restaurant (e.g., menu change, price change, etc.) are controlled. It is also pertinent to note that the main effect of the treatment dummy variable is absorbed by the restaurant-platform fixed effect, and the main effect of Post dummy variable is absorbed by the year-month fixed effect.

5.3. Investigating the Underlying Mechanisms 5.3.1. Impact of the AI-Powered Image Categorization Feature on Reducing Search Costs in Review Images. Our study is based on the assumption that the AI-powered image categorization feature can significantly ease the effort required to navigate through various categories of images. To quantify the impact of this feature on search costs, we first conducted a descriptive analysis comparing the effort required to locate the 1st, 5th, and 10th images in each category before and after the categorization was implemented. On Yelp, users are presented with 10 images per page; viewing more requires scrolling with the mouse wheel. Prior to the feature change, users had to scroll multiple times to find additional images within a specific category, which could be a tedious process. With the feature enhancement, users can now click on a category button and instantly access 10 relevant images without any need for additional scrolling. To perform this analysis, we first ranked pictures based on their posting time from latest to oldest for each category, such as inside, outside, and drink. We then calculated the average number of scrolls required to view the 1st, 5th, and 10th images within each category, both preand postfeature change. The results are summarized in Table C2 of Online Appendix C.

Our analysis reveals that, on average, users had to perform multiple mouse scrolls to access additional photos within a category—for example, the 5th and 10th images. With the introduction of the AI-powered image categorization feature, this process has been simplified to a single click. For example, to view the 5th and 10th interior shots of a restaurant, users previously needed to scroll an average of 4.44 and 8.63 times, respectively. Postimplementation, one click on the Inside category button suffices. These results provide strong evidence that categorized images enabled by the new feature substantially reduce the search costs associated with locating images in each category.

Moreover, we conducted an additional test to examine the influence of the AI-powered image categorization feature on consumer attention to review texts. The results are presented in Table 5 below. As we do not have access to user clickstream data, we used the review helpfulness votes as an indirect measure of the attention consumers give to review texts. Our primary objective was to use these helpful votes as an indicator of the level of attention that consumers pay to the review texts. We reason that if consumers pay less attention to review texts due to the newly introduced AI-powered image categorization feature, then there should be a decrease in helpfulness votes for reviews. We normalized the helpful votes for reviews of a particular restaurant each month by dividing them by the total number of reviews for that restaurant to avoid any potential skewing of our results due to the review volume. This was necessary because the helpful votes on reviews are consumer-driven, and a large consumer base could potentially inflate the votes for reviews. The findings of our study showed a decrease in review helpful votes following the introduction of the AI-powered image categorization feature, supporting our theory that users tend to pay less attention to review texts after the implementation of this feature.

Building on these findings, we sought to understand how the introduction of an AI-powered image categorization feature affects consumer decision quality. We scrutinized postvisit review comments, focusing on complaints related to four key restaurant attributes: service, food, ambiance, and worthiness. Specifically, we examined the impact of adding the AI-powered image categorization feature on consumer complaints related to four major restaurant attributes: (1) service (e.g., waiter attitude, wait time, etc.), (2) food (e.g., food appearance, food taste, food hygiene, food quality, etc.), (3) ambiance (e.g., lighting, crowdedness, decorations, etc.), and (4) worthiness (e.g., price, food portion, etc.). We focused on these four major attributes for two reasons. First, prior research has examined and verified the validity of these attributes (Panchendrarajan et al. 2016, 2017). Second, major platforms such as TripAdvisor have also identified and adopted them as major attributes of a restaurant. In the following sections, we will explain our conjecture in the presence of cognitive miser behavior before detailing the analyses.

On Yelp, both review texts and review images constitute critical sources of information for a consumer to assess a restaurant prior to a visit. However, their effectiveness in conveying information differs for different types of restaurant attributes. Images can better depict tangible attributes such as the visual appeal of the interior, exterior, and food display (Nelson 1974, Jiang and Benbasat 2007). Although categorized images provide consumers with a more efficient way to visually survey restaurant features, they may not be suitable for presenting the intangible aspects of a restaurant, such as customer service and food taste. Such information is better reflected in review texts, where prior consumers describe their experience (e.g., with customer service) in detail.

We illustrate our conjuncture based on the cognitive miser theory using the service attribute of restaurants as an example. Suppose prospective consumers excessively rely on categorized images while paying insufficient attention to review texts, as predicted by the cognitive miser theory. In this case, they are unlikely to develop an accurate understanding of the restaurant’s service quality. Subsequently, they may decide to visit a restaurant that does not fully meet their service needs and preferences. Therefore, we anticipate a greater likelihood of consumer service complaints after the introduction of the AI-powered image categorization feature on Yelp. By comparison, review images effectively communicate a restaurant’s ambiance and worthiness attributes. For example, price information in menu photos and food portions in food photos convey a restaurant’s worthiness attribute. Lighting, crowdedness, and decoration in interior and exterior images depict the ambiance attribute. Thus, the likelihood of consumers complaining about the ambiance, or the worthiness attributes of a res taurant postpurchase is not expected to increase significantly,<sup>8</sup> as review images help them learn these restaurant attributes prior to their visit, and consumers choose the restaurants that will most likely match their needs.

In summary, if the AI-powered image categorization feature causes cognitive miser behavior in consumers and influences their decision making negatively, we should observe a significant increase in the likelihood of complaints from consumers about restaurant attributes that are difficult to communicate through images (e.g., service), but not in complaints about attributes that can be captured well by images (e.g., ambiance or worthiness).

5.3.2. Effect on Customer Complaints. To test the cognitive miser mechanism outlined above, we identified and extracted complaints about the four primary restaurant attributes (i.e., service, food, ambiance, and worthiness) using natural language processing (NLP) on the review texts. As an initial step, we manually labeled 4,800 randomly selected reviews from the sam ple. To develop the guidelines for manual coding, two coauthors independently coded 60 reviews, reaching an interrater reliability of 0.8602 as measured by Cohen’s kappa. After resolving any discrepancies and reaching a consensus on coding of all 60 reviews, we formulated a set of guidelines for identifying complaints related to each attribute. Subsequently, one graduate research assistant (RA) who was blind to the purpose of this study was hired and trained to code the 4,800 reviews. The RA manually labeled each review and noted whether a review contained consumer complaints about any of the four primary attributes of a restaurant. Afterward, two coauthors manually checked the RA’s manual coding results to ensure the RA followed the developed coding guidelines. Online Appendix B presents examples of consumer complaints related to each attribute.

Using the manually labeled data, we trained and evaluated four deep learning models to identify complaints, including a sequential model with a regular densely connected neural network layer, a sequential model with an extra embedding layer, a sequential model with a convolutional layer, and a deep learning model with hyperparameter optimization. The sequential model with an extra embedding layer using the Maxpooling option outperformed the others, yielding a minimum of 88% accuracy in predicting whether a review contains consumer complaints related to each attribute in the test set. The embedding layer took vocabulary size as the tokenized size of our training data set, and we created an embedding layer for a sequential model that mapped words from a vocabulary to 100-dimensional vectors. Additionally, we incorporated a one-dimensional convolutional layer. In the end, activation functions rectified linear unit (ReLU) and sigmoid (logistic) were added.

The trained model was employed to analyze all the reviews in our sample and predict the probability of complaints on each restaurant attribute for every review, with values ranging from zero to one. Then, the likelihood of service complaints from consumers for a restaurant in a given month was measured as the average probability of service complaints for all received reviews in that month. The likelihood of complaints related to every other restaurant’s attribute was calculated in a similar manner.

We then examined the impact of the AI-powered image categorization feature on complaints related to each attribute. The result in column 1 of Table 6 shows a significant increase in the likelihood of service complaints on Yelp after implementing the AI-powered image categorization feature $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The result suggests that the inferior decision quality of consumers is partly due to selecting restaurants that fail to meet their service needs and preferences. Column 2 of Table 6 shows that the likelihood of food complaints also marginally increases $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 3 , p < 0 . 1 )$ . Although images can convey certain aspects about food, such as food appearance, other aspects may not be captured well by images, such as food tastes, the ingredients being used, and so on. Therefore, if the AI-powered image categorization feature induces significant cognitive miser behavior in consumers, an increase in complaints about the food would be expected as well. The result on food complaints provides additional supporting evidence that adding the AI-powered image categorization feature leads to lower consumer decision quality because of cognitive miser bias. In contrast, columns 3 and 4 of Table 6 show that the treatment does not affect the likelihood of ambiance and worthiness complaints from consumers. Given that the ambiance and worthiness can be easily reflected through images, it is not surprising that the results are insignificant.

The above results underscore the cognitive miser mechanism as previously posited: As consumers excessively rely on categorized images to evaluate a restaurant before visiting, this leads them to overlook nonvisual attributes, such as service quality and food experience. These types of information are not available in categorized review images and require more cognitive efforts to extract from review texts, resulting in a higher likelihood of mismatches in service needs and food preferences.

## 5.4. Robustness Checks

We conducted a series of additional analyses to ensure the robustness of our results and provide further support for our findings.

5.4.1. Analysis Using Alternative Measures of Consumer Decision Quality. In our main analysis, we used the overall rating that consumers give a restaurant as a proxy for consumer decision quality. For a robustness check, we separated positive ratings (four- and fivestar reviews) from negative ratings (one- and two-star reviews) and estimated them separately. Positive ratings indicate that the chosen restaurant meets the con sumer’s needs and preferences, thus serving as a proxy for good decisions. In contrast, negative ratings serve as a proxy for poor choices, reflecting a substantial mismatch between the restaurant and the consumer’s needs. As the volume of positive and negative ratings are count variables, we used a negative binomial model for our estimations. The results are shown in Table 8. We observed that the AI-powered image categorization feature does not increase the volume of negative ratings from consumers $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 0 , p > 0 . 1 )$ but decreases the volume of positive ratings $( \beta = - 0 . 1 0 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ These results are consistent with our earlier findings that the AI-powered image categorization feature may lead to lower consumer decision quality.

5.4.2. Analysis Exploring the Moderating Effect of a Restaurant’s Prior Average Rating. Prior studies have shown that consumers often use products’ average ratings to form prepurchase expectations (Engler et al. 2015). Suppose consumers become less aware of a restaurant’s service or food quality after introducing the AI-powered image categorization feature due to cognitive miser behavior. In this case, they may take the easy route of forming a prepurchase perception of service or food quality based on a restaurant’s average rating due to the “halo effect” (Perrow 1961, Holbrook 1983, Sahoo et al. 2012). However, not all restaurants with a good average rating excel in the service or food dimension; a good overall rating may be driven by providing a great dining atmosphere, even when a restaurant’s service or food dimension is subpar. In this case, there is a high likelihood of a mismatch between the consumer’s service or food expectation and the restaurant’s actual service or food level. Therefore, we expect that after introducing the AI-powered image categorization feature, consumers visiting a restaurant with a higher average rating are more likely to find that the restaurant does not meet their service or food needs and that they have made an inferior decision. To empirically test this conjecture, we first created a dummy variable, high\_prior\_r, to distinguish restaurants with high prior average ratings from those with low prior average ratings based on the median value at the treatment time, and then investigated its moderating effect. ## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key Findings

Leveraging an exogenous design change on the Yelp platform, this study examines the effect of search cost reduction for partial information on consumer decision quality. We found that the introduction of Yelp’s AI-powered image categorization feature, aimed at lowering the search costs associated with review images, actually decreased consumer decision quality. This finding is at odds with the prevailing view that introducing search cost reduction tools in online platforms typically yields improved search outcomes and augments consumer information, thereby enhancing decision making (Guo 2001, Kulviwat et al. 2004, Strebel et al. 2004). We argue that this counterintuitive outcome stems from the cognitive miser behavior induced by the AI-powered image categorization feature: consumers have an overreliance on the readily available categorized images but diminish their efforts to seek out information in the review texts, which requires more cognitive effort. As a result, consumers are not adequately informed of nonvisual information such as a restaurant’s service and food quality, leading to lower decision quality. We delved deeper into consumer comments to provide empirical support for this mechanism. Specifically, we analyzed in detail how the AI-powered image categorization feature influences a restaurant’s likelihood of receiving complaints about different attributes from consumers. Notably, we found a significant increase in the likelihood of service complaints, as well as a marginally significant increase in food complaints. The likelihood of receiving complaints related to the other two attributes (i.e., ambiance and worthiness) remains unchanged. Our findings suggest that the decline in consumer decision quality is primarily due to consumers visiting restaurants that fail to match their needs in the service dimension, an attribute of restaurants that is difficult to convey through images but better presented via review texts. This result provides supporting evidence for the cognitive miser mechanism.

We further corroborate our findings through additional analyses. First, in line with the cognitive miser mechanism, we find that the negative impact of the AI-powered image categorization feature on consumer decision quality is stronger for restaurants with low service quality and marginally stronger for restaurants with low food quality. This is likely because restaurants with low service (or food) quality are less able to match the service (or food) needs of consumers who, due to cognitive miser behavior, do not have sufficient knowledge of the restaurant’s service (or food) quality before visiting. Second, we find that, in ceteris paribus, restaurants in the high prior average rating group (i.e., with a higher overall rating visible to consumers before their visit) are more affected than those in the low prior average rating group. As consumers may form higher service and food expectations for a restaurant based on its higher average rating without a sufficient understanding of the restaurant’s service and food quality, their purchase decisions are more likely to be unsatisfactory due to a higher likelihood of the restaurant not meeting the consumer’s service and food expectations.

In summary, our findings provide consistent and robust evidence that (1) counterintuitively, the reduction of search cost for partial information on online platforms can sometimes lead to decreased decision quality for consumers; and (2) the primary driver of this negative effect is consumers’ overreliance on information for which the search cost has been lowered, coupled with fewer efforts in acquiring other critical product information necessary for developing a comprehensive understanding of the product.

## 6.2. Contributions and Implications

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to identify and document that the reduction in search costs for partial information might lead to a decrease in consumer decision quality. Prior research examining search costs has shown that reduced search costs yield various benefits for consumers, such as higher search quality (Kulviwat et al. 2004, Strebel et al. 2004), reduced price (Lynch and Ariely 2000), and more varieties of products (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Kuksov 2004). We extend this line of literature by providing an example that the search cost reduction for partial information on online platforms, such as images, may negatively affect consumer decision quality.

This study enriches the extant literature by providing theoretical insights on why search cost reduction tools for partial information, such as the AI-powered image categorization feature implemented by Yelp, may hurt consumers’ decision making. Through rigorous analyses, we show that, although search cost reduction tools make it more efficient for consumers to find specific types of product information, consumers may spend less effort in acquiring other types of product information as the search cost reduction tools may induce cognitive miser behavior in consumers. As a result, consumers may not develop sufficient knowledge of the product prior to purchase, hurting their decision quality. Our findings shed light on potentially unintended consequences of introducing search cost reduction tools for partial information on online platforms and highlight the importance of examining and addressing these unintended consequences.

Our study also contributes to the existing literature on system changes in online platforms. A growing number of studies have documented that new features, such as ranking systems and multidimensional reviews introduced by digital platforms, can provide intended benefits to digital platform users (Ghose et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2018). We add to this stream of literature by demonstrating that system changes designed to assist users may unexpectedly alter user behavior and lead to unintended consequences. Our research can be generalized under certain conditions. Online platforms typically present diverse types of product information to consumers, each offering different perspectives on the product. Yet consumers are often not aware that these types of information may not fully overlap with each other. When one type of information (especially the one that is relatively straightforward to digest) is reduced by search cost reduction tools, consumers are more likely to shift their attention due to cognitive miser behavior. This shift can result in consumers inadvertently overlooking other critical details, which they might not consciously recognize, thus leading to worse decision-making outcomes.

This study also enriches the literature on website design (Macinnis and Price 1987, Underwood and Klein 2002, Lin et al. 2012). Although prior research has highlighted the positive role of high-quality product images in shaping consumer decisions (Wansink et al. 1998, Underwood and Klein 2002), our findings contribute to this line of work by providing a surprising and counterintuitive case that enhancements in image display may not always lead to better consumer decisions. Although one might expect that sorting product images into well-defined categories would provide a better way for consumers to examine product attributes and assist their decision-making visually, our study reveals the associated downside of categorized images, such as inducing consumers to be overly focused on product images, might outweigh their benefits.

Finally, our work extends extant research on product reviews and contributes to the consumer cognition literature. Prior work has examined review texts and review images separately, as well as their relative strengths (Nelson 1974, Bickart and Schindler 2001, Jiang and Benbasat 2007, Ghose et al. 2012, De Langhe et al. 2016). Our study indicates a substitution effect between the two for consumer attention, revealing how categorized review images can divert consumer attention from review texts to images. The findings provide support for the cognitive miser theory that individuals prefer simpler, less effortful thinking and problem-solving approaches (Stanovich 2009).

The findings of the study generate several managerial implications for platform owners. The results suggest there is a need to comprehensively evaluate the impacts of search reduction tools for partial information. On the surface, offering such features seems beneficial, aiding consumers in gathering product information more efficiently. However, our results indicate that search cost reduction for partial information could negatively affect consumers’ decision quality due to the cognitive miser effect (Zipf 1949, Risko and Gilbert 2016). We recom mend that online platforms implement measures to mitigate this issue when introducing search cost reduction tools. For example, in conjunction with the image cate gorizing design, Yelp might also categorize or label textual reviews to simplify the filtering process for consumers. In addition, the platform could highlight review texts to complement categorized images, thereby enhancing consumer awareness of all a merchant’s attributes. Overall, we recommend platform designers and owners consider not only the functionality and scope of a specific search cost reduction tool but also its potential interactions with other features of the platform and the resultant implications for platform participants.

## 6.3. Limitation and Future Research

This study has several limitations. First, the results of this study are based on data from restaurants in 10 U.S. cities of various sizes and economic characteristics, which may raise questions about whether our findings could be generalized to restaurants in other cities or countries. Therefore, we recommend that future research replicates this study using different samples. Second, although the natural experiment setting, coupled with the DID design, allows us to infer causally the influence of search cost reduction for partial information via the AI-powered image categorization feature on consumer decision quality, it should be extrapolated with caution. Our findings do not suggest that all reductions in search costs for partial information may negatively affect consumer decision quality. Outcomes may vary when search cost reduction is applied to information that is more complex to process. For example, if a platform categorizes review texts, the impact might not be as strong as with the AI-powered image categorization feature since some users may be less willing to engage with texts than images. Our findings indicate that search cost reduction for partial information may negatively affect consumer decision quality due to consumer cognitive miser behavior, a risk that e-commerce retailers and platform owners should consider. Third, to minimize the confounding effect of other platform changes and strengthen causal inference, our analysis sample was restricted to six months before and after the exogenous change on Yelp. Consumers’ behavior and preferences might evolve after longer exposure to any search cost reduction tool. Future research could investigate the long-term implications of search cost reduction for partial information on platform participants. Fourth, although our observation window is pre-COVID, we believe our findings can be generalized to post-COVID settings as cognitive miser behavior is an intrinsic human characteristic. Yet, it is possible that human behavior may have changed significantly following COVID-19. We suggest that future research tests our findings in post-COVID settings. Fifth, due to data limitations, the analysis in this study relies on archived data from the Yelp and TripAdvisor platforms rather than direct observation of consumers’ real-time behavior in viewing images and texts. Future research could examine this mechanism by utilizing more comprehensive data, if available. Finally, while it is beyond the scope of this study, future research with access to detailed restaurant sales data can quantify the economic impact of the AI-powered image categorization feature on Yelp.

## 7. Concluding Remarks

Online platforms play a critical role in providing information for consumer decision making. Using a DID design in a natural experiment setting, we investigate how search cost reduction for partial information on online platforms affects the quality of consumer decisions. Our findings, derived from observational data enhanced with text analysis, provide robust evidence that search cost reduction for partial information may not necessarily lead to better consumer decisions. Surprisingly, it may undermine consumer decision quality. This occurs as reduced search cost for partial information may lead consumers to spend less effort on acquiring other product/service information that requires more cognitive effort, resulting in an insufficient understanding of the product/service before purchase. Our research provides valuable insights for designing appropriate mechanisms to reduce consumers’ search costs on online platforms and for evaluating their impacts.
