# On-Demand, Long-Term, or Hybrid? An Economic Analysis of Optimal Rental Models on Sharing Platforms

Abstract. The rapidly growing sharing economy is characterized by improved resource utilization through peer-to-peer transactions. Sharing platforms usually adopt one of three rental models, long-term, on-demand, and hybrid, where the long-term rental requires full dedication of resources to market over a predefined period, the on-demand rental allow resources to be exchanged for a fraction of the period, and the hybrid rental model pro vides both options. We develop an analytical model consisting of a monopolistic sharing platform that connects renters with owners who have heterogeneous asset utilization and incur different setup and transaction costs of participation under different rental models. We identify the conditions under which each of the three rental models can emerge as the platform’s optimal rental strategy and further examine their impacts on social welfare and consumer surplus. We find that the relative setup cost and the relative transaction cost between owners and renters play a crucial role in shaping the equilibrium market price and optimality of the three rental models, whereas the total costs determine the equilib rium transaction volume and sustainability of the three rental models. Only when the total cost is low can all three models be sustainable, and each model could emerge to be optimal in the presence of a moderately high relative transaction cost on the owners’ side. Specifi cally, when the owners’ setup cost is significantly higher than the renters’, the on-demand model is optimal. When the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than the renters’ and the renter’s setup cost is low, the long-term model is optimal. Only when their setup costs are comparable or when the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than the renters’ and the renter’s setup cost is high can the hybrid model be optimal. When the platform optimally chooses the long-term or hybrid model, it also generates the highest social welfare, and in a wide range of parameter regions, it also yields the highest consumer surplus, leading to a win–win outcome. Overall, our results offer important new insights into the platform’s optimal rental-model choice in the sharing economy and its implications fo consumers and society.

## 1. Introduction

The sharing economy is built around peer-to-peer activities of acquiring, providing, or sharing goods and services through online platforms that connect people who have something to offer (e.g., cars, spare bedrooms, parking spaces, and storage spaces) with people in need of that exact thing. The most prominent examples include Airbnb for lodging, Turo for cars, Style Lend for fashion, and TaskRabbit for labor, to name a few. The sharing economy has forever changed our daily life. It is reshaping consumer behaviors and becoming a new driver of economic growth. According to a recent PricewaterhouseCoopers report, the sharing economy was expected to increase from \$15 billion in 2015 to \$335 billion by 2025, with a global growth rate of more than 30% per year for a decade.<sup>1</sup>

Two unique features characterize the sharing economy. The first is better utilization of underused assets. For example, Getaround is an online peer-to-peer carsharing service available throughout the United States and Europe. It is revolutionizing the car rental industry by allowing car owners to monetize their personal vehicles when they are not using them. The disruptive business model not only improves the owners’ asset utilization by leasing their cars out but also gives renters the flexibility to book cars for a few miles or a few hours as needed. According to the United Nations Habitat III policy report, the sharing economy can optimize the utilization of private vehicles, which remain parked 95% of the time.<sup>2</sup> A Statista survey also reveals that over half of the participants in the car-sharing economy appreciate the benefits of flexible use, such as “saving money” or “moving more comfortably.”<sup>3</sup> With the focus on access instead of ownership, the business model is very attractive to consumers, and Getaround makes a sizable commission from each rental booked through the platform.

The second unique feature is the peer-to-peer transaction. There are several technical and economic challenges involved. In the case of Getaround, car owners need to register their vehicles on the platform and install necessary hardware and software to enable safe transfer of vehicles to renters. Car renters need to meet specific qualification requirements to be eligible for rental and install a mobile app to unlock the reserved vehicles. These constitute nonnegligible setup costs for both owners and renters to participate in the sharing economy. In addition, each transaction has associated transaction costs. For example, owners incur costs for cleaning and detailing the vehicle between rentals to maintain its cleanness and appeal. Renters are responsible for choosing the insurance coverage for each rental and picking up and dropping off the vehicle at specific locations. The setup and transaction costs lead to inherent market frictions in peer-to-peer transactions, which can directly affect consumers’ participation incentives in the car-sharing economy. Because the bilateral nature of the market critically depends on the balance between supply and demand to determine the market-clearing prices, the setup and transaction costs could play an essential role in driving the market dynamics, platform profitability, and consumer surplus.

Because sharing economies are relatively new and come with the aforementioned unique features, platforms have been experimenting with different rental models. In particular, we have witnessed three commonly used rental models in practice: long-term, ondemand, and hybrid. Under the long-term rental model, consumers can only lease or rent the product for the entire predefined period (e.g., monthly). Under the on-demand rental model, consumers can lease or rent the product as needed (e.g., hourly). Under the hybrid model, both the long-term and on-demand rental options are available to consumers. For example, two leading companies in the car-sharing market, which collectively boast a market penetration rate exceeding 50%, have each elected to adopt distinct rental strategies.

RelayRides chose the hourly rental (on-demand) when it was first launched in 2010 and rebranded to Turo in 2015 to reflect its shift to long-term rentals. This is in contrast to Getaround, which uses a hybrid model to allow consumers to rent by the hour or longer periods and claims that the flexibility of the hybrid model offers its primary competitive advantage. Similarly, Atzuche, a peer-to-peer car rental startup founded in 2014, initially adopted long-term rental and then shifted to the hybrid rental strategy. In other product categories, the fashion platform Style Lend uses long-term rentals, and the bike-sharing platform Spinlister and the parking-spacesharing platform JustPark choose hybrid rentals. Thus far, very little research has examined why the different platforms have chosen different rental models, and how the consumers’ heterogeneous asset-utilization rates and their costs of participation would affect a sharing platform’s business-model choices.

Motivated by these observations, this paper studies a sharing platform’s optimal rental-model choices by considering the two unique features of the sharing economy—asset utilization and consumers’ costs. Specifically, we study the following research questions. How do different rental models affect the bilateral market-transaction volumes, equilibrium prices, and platform profits? How do different rental models affect the consumer surplus and social welfare? Is there a win–win outcome where the platform’s optimal rentalmodel choice also maximizes consumer surplus?

To answer these questions, we develop an analytical model in which a monopolistic sharing platform connects renters with owners. We assume consumers have heterogeneous asset-utilization rates and incur different costs when participating in the sharing economy under different rental models. On the one hand, the long-term rental only partially utilizes resources due to rental commitment over the entire period, whereas the on-demand rental has the advantage of achieving full utilization of resources. On the other hand, the longterm rental incurs lower transaction costs than the on-demand rental due to lower turnover, although both the long-term and on-demand rentals incur fixed setup costs. Therefore, the resource utilization and consumers’ costs represent interesting tradeoffs that influence the platform’s optimal rental strategies.

Our analyses reveal several interesting results. First, we find that consumers’ costs play a critical role in determining the sustainability of the three business models, the equilibrium transaction volume, and the equilibrium market price. When the renters’ and owners’ total setup cost is sufficiently high, no rental market is viable in equilibrium. When the total setup cost is not too high but total cost (including transaction costs) is sufficiently high, only the long-term rental market can sustain in equilibrium. When the total cost is moderate, both the long-term and on-demand rental models are sustainable. Only when the total cost is low can all three models be viable in equilibrium. We further demonstrate that the total setup cost and the total transaction cost determine not only which model is viable, but also which model generates greater transaction volume. In contrast to the total cost, the relative setup cost and the relative transaction cost between owners and renters play a crucial role in determining which model results in a higher equilibrium price.

Second, we show that the relative setup cost and the relative transaction cost between owners and renters play a crucial role in the platform’s optimal rentalmodel choices when all three rental models are viable. Surprisingly, the hybrid model is not always superior for the platform. In the presence of a sufficiently high relative transaction cost on the owners’ side, the hybrid model can never be optimal. In contrast, in the presence of comparable transaction costs on both sides, the hybrid model is optimal unless the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than the renters’. In the presence of a moderately high relative transaction cost on the owners’ side, any of the three models could be optimal. Specifically, when the owners’ setup cost is significantly higher than the renters’, the on-demand model is optimal. When the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than the renters’ and the renter’s setup cost is low, the long-term model is optimal. Only when their setup costs are comparable or when the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than renters’ and the renter’s setup cost is high could the hybrid model be optimal. Because the hybrid model offers consumers more rental choices, we find the hybrid model generates higher total transaction volume than the other two rental models, leading to a transaction-expansion effect. Meanwhile, because the long-term and on-demand rentals are substitutable options for consumers under the hybrid model, the transaction volume in each submarket is lower than that under the corresponding on-demand or long-term rental models, resulting in a demand-cannibalization effect. The interplay of these two opposite effects determines the superiority of the hybrid model.

Third, we find that the platform’s optimal rental model may not be the most beneficial to consumers and society. Only when the hybrid model is chosen by the platform, or when the long-term model is chosen in the presence of a moderate or high total cost, does the platform’s optimal model choice also maximize social welfare. In contrast, when the on-demand model is chosen, it can never be socially optimal. Fortunately, we find that, in a wide range of parameter regions, the platform’s optimal model choice also yields the highest consumer surplus. This win–win outcome is more likely to occur when renters’ and owners’ setup costs are roughly comparable.

Overall, in the ever-evolving sharing economy, where technological advancements continually drive down costs on both sides of the market, our analysis suggests that the on-demand or hybrid model is more likely to emerge as an optimal rental-model choice for the platform than the long-term model. Furthermore, our findings indicate that the hybrid model is more beneficial to both consumers and society than the on-demand model. These results offer important new insights into the platform’s optimal pricing and rental-model choices in the sharing economy.

## 2. Literature Review

Our study relates primarily to two streams of literature: sharing economies and online platform business models.

The first stream is the fast-growing literature on the sharing economy. The sharing economy emphasizes temporary access rather than permanent ownership of resources, which has revolutionized consumers’ behaviors and made profound changes to the traditional economy. Previous studies have examined economic and behavioral factors that affect consumers’ participation incentives in the sharing economy. For example, Fraiberger and Sundararajan (2017) find that consumers’ willingness to participate in the sharing market is mainly influenced by their heterogeneous price sensitivity and resource use. Chen and Wang (2019) show that lack of trust and information efficiency in peer-topeer transactions are key factors influencing consumer behaviors in the sharing economy. Furthermore, consumers may unavoidably experience disutility of delay (Taylor 2018), incur moral hazard (Nadeem et al. 2021), or have low perceived value (Kobis et al. 2021) on sharing economy platforms. To facilitate matching and bilateral transactions, platforms usually impose strict rules and conditions on platform use and may require installation of special hardware or software for asset tracking and monitoring, resulting in nonnegligible setup (Weber 2020) and transaction costs (Pei et al. 2021). The impact of these costs is more pronounced in technology-driven markets than in traditional markets. Our study provides new insights into how both fixed setup and variable transaction costs play pivotal roles in influencing consumers’ participation incentives in different rental markets within the sharing economy.

In addition to examining consumer behaviors, recent studies have explored sharing platforms’ pricing and capacity decisions. For example, Jiang and Tian (2018) use a two-period model to investigate how a manufacturer strategically sets retail prices and product quality in response to consumer expectations of sharing. In a duopoly setting, Zhang et al. (2022) consider dynamiccommission-rate, fixed-commission-rate, and fixedwage schemes and examine their implications for platforms, consumers, and society in equilibrium. Benjaafar et al. (2022) explore car-sharing platforms’ optimal number of vehicles to achieve a specified service level by considering demand patterns, vehicle availability, and customer preferences. Different from these studies, our research explicitly characterizes the bilateral nature of peer-to-peer transactions by focusing on both renters’ and owners’ heterogeneous asset utilization and transaction costs under the different rental models and examines the platform’s equilibrium pricing and optimal rental-model choices.

Several analytical studies also focus on the socialwelfare implications of the sharing economy. For example, Jiang and Tian (2018) and Filippas et al. (2020) theoretically examine the market conditions under which the sharing economy benefits firms and consumers. They find that the sharing economy may either reduce asset ownership by offering attractive rental options or increase ownership thanks to high incomes from leasing resources out. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first analytical paper to examine the rental-model choice in the sharing economy. We not only consider heterogeneous participation incentives for both owners and renters, but also study the platform’s optimal rental strategies and welfare implications.

The second stream of literature examines digital platforms’ business models. For example, Tian et al. (2018) compare reseller and online-marketplace models for online retailers and show that the pure marketplace model is optimal when market competition is not intense. Abhishek et al. (2021) examine the effect of a peer-to-peer rental market on the business model of a durable-goods manufacturer. They identify the conditions under which the manufacturer should introduce a direct leasing option or enable peer-to-peer rental on its platform, in addition to traditional selling. Several theoretical studies have specifically examined online platforms’ revenue models and pricing strategies in the presence of bilateral-market characteristics. For example, Chen et al. (2016) compare the brokerage and advertising models for a consumer-to-consumer trading platform. They show that a brokerage model is preferable to an advertising model for online platforms when the trade-matching probability is high. Basu et al. (2019) study an online matching platform’s provision of both search and authentication services, where the latter authenticates the features and credentials of prospective matches. They show complementarity in the platform’s optimal pricing for search and authentication services. In contrast, with a focus on enhancing resource utilization and facilitating efficient matching in the new context of collaborative consumption, our study uniquely contributes to the literature on platform business models by examining the digital platform’s rental-model choices within the sharing economy.

A particularly relevant pricing strategy on digital platforms is on-demand, use-based pricing. The payper-use business model is commonly used in cloud computing or entertainment platforms where consumers can flexibly scale their use up or down according to their needs and pay for additional resources only when required. Balasubramanian et al. (2015) find that pay-per-use pricing achieves perfect discrimination along consumer use frequency, which can be cost effective for selling information goods. Ladas et al. (2022) further investigate how customer preferences, market structure, and cost structure influence the busi ness model choice between traditional selling and payper-use pricing in the duopoly setting. They find that when consumers have low use needs and the competition effect is strong, the pay-per-use model is more profitable. Our study sheds new light on this stream of literature by exploring the design and impact of payper-use strategies in the sharing economy. However, different from this stream of literature, we focus on the bilateral nature of peer-to-peer transactions, where the equilibrium price is determined by market-clearing conditions rather than the platform. We contribute to the growing literature that deals explicitly with eco nomic issues relating to peer-to-peer transactions in the sharing economy by comparing long-term, on-demand, and hybrid rental models.

## 3. Model

We consider a sharing platform on which owners of a product (e.g., a car) can lease their idle resources to potential renters. Owners and renters have heterogeneous utilization of the product in a defined period (e.g., a month or a year). We define utilization as the percentage of time the product is used within the period, which we assume to follow a uniform distribution in the respective population; that is, $\theta _ { i } \sim U [ 0 , 1 ]$ where $i = 0 ,$ , r denotes owners and renters, respectively. We further denote the maximum value the product can offer over the entire period as q. Both the owner’s and the renter’s realized valuation of the product is proportional to this maximum value based on their own utilization; that is, $\theta _ { i } q .$ . Without loss of generality, we normalize each population, owners and renters, to one.

The sharing platform can choose from three different rental models to match demand and supply: the long term, on-demand, and hybrid rental models. Under the long-term rental model, owners lease their product and renters rent the product for the entire period at a fixed fee. Under the on-demand rental model, owners lease their product when not in use and renters rent the product as needed based on a unit price. Under the hybrid rental model, the platform offers both the longterm and on-demand rental options. The platform’s profit comes from the commission it charges on transactions with a commission rate α.

We denote the rental price under the long-term rental model as $p _ { \mathrm { l } } ,$ which is the price a renter pays to use the product for the entire period. After deducting the commission payment to the platform, each owner earns the rental income $( 1 - \alpha ) p _ { \mathrm { l } }$ . To ease comparison of prices under different rental models, we denote the on-demand rental price that a renter with utilization 1 needs to pay as $p _ { \mathrm { d } } ;$ equivalently, we can interpret $p _ { \mathrm { d } }$ as the unit rental price (per the whole period considered). Accordingly, under the on-demand rental model, a renter with utilization $\theta _ { \mathrm { r } }$ pays $\theta _ { \mathrm { r } } p _ { \mathrm { d } }$ and an owner who has utilization $\theta _ { \mathrm { o } }$ earns income $( \mathrm { 1 } - \alpha ) ( 1 - \theta _ { \mathrm { o } } ) p _ { \mathrm { d } }$ by leasing out the product out when not in use.

To facilitate smooth, fast, and secure transactions, the sharing platform requires both owners and renters to take certain initial measures for participation, which incur some fixed setup costs, denoted as $f _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and $f _ { \mathrm { r } } ,$ respectively. For example, car owners need to install Internet of Things (IoT) sensors to enable real-time location tracking and use monitoring of their vehicles. Car renters must pass specific qualification requirements such as driving license and credit risk checking to guarantee safe rentals. In addition, both renters and owners incur variable transaction costs associated with each transaction, denoted as $c _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and $c _ { \mathrm { r } } ,$ respectively. For example, car renters incur inconvenience costs by spending time and effort to pick up and drop off cars to specific locations for each use, and car owners incur costs of cleaning and maintaining their vehicles after each use. We normalize the transaction cost under the long-term model to zero because of the significantly lower frequency of transactions associated with each product compared with the on-demand rental. For on-demand rentals (under either the on-demand or the hybrid model), the expected number of transactions generally increases in the percentage of time an owner leases her product or a renter rents products over the predefined period. Accordingly, we assume that an owner incurs $( 1 - \theta _ { \mathrm { o } } ) c _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and a renter incurs $\theta _ { \mathrm { r } } c _ { \mathrm { r } }$ total transaction costs, respectively. Furthermore, for ease of exposition, we assume $c _ { \mathrm { o } } \geq c _ { \mathrm { r } }$ . This assumption is sensible because the cleaning and maintaining burden is often born by the owners rather than renters after each use, as typical on the car-sharing and property-sharing platforms. When $c _ { \mathrm { o } } = c _ { \mathrm { r } } = 0$ , the hybrid model reduces to the on-demand model (as implied by Lemma 3)

because the long-term rental option offers no advantage against the on-demand option. To eliminate this trivial case, we assume $c _ { \mathrm { o } } > 0$

(i) Self use: The owner does not lease the product and derives the expected utility $\theta _ { \mathrm { o } } q$ from her own use.

## 4.3. Hybrid Rental Model

Owners and renters can choose their preferred rental options based on their utilization. Intuitively, we would expect low-utilization owners to prefer the long-term option because of the low utility from their own use and high-utilization owners to prefer to keep the product for self-use because of the high utility. The moderate-utilization owners might prefer the on-demand option, so they can use the product when needed and rent it out for income when the product is not in use. Similar reasoning applies to renters.

It is worth noting that the equilibrium long-term market price also depends on the transaction costs $( c _ { \mathrm { r } }$ and $c _ { \mathrm { o } } )$ associated with the on-demand rental. This is because, under the hybrid model, consumers can choose between on-demand and long-term rentals, and the balance of supply and demand in the on-demand rental submarket affects the long-term rental submarket. Whether the long-term rental price is higher than the on-demand rental price depends on both the setup cost difference and the transaction cost difference between renters and owners. For instance, if the owners’ transaction cost is significantly higher than the renters’, the owners’ relatively larger disincentive to lease in the on-demand market would shift supply to the long-term rental market, resulting in a lower long-term rental price. Similarly, the setup cost difference between renters and owners has a varying impact on the balance of supply and demand in the on-demand and long-term markets, resulting in different rental prices.

Transactions in the long-term submarket are contrib uted by the low-utilization owners (high-utilization renters), who make their submarket participation decisions by comparing the leasing (rental) benefit in the long-term submarket with that in the on-demand submarket. Because the setup costs are incurred in either submarket participation, they play no role in determining the transaction volume in the long-term submarket. Meanwhile, an increase in the transaction costs associ ated with on-demand rental increases the transaction volume in the long-term submarket because of the reduced attractiveness of the on-demand submarket. As under the on-demand model, an increase in the setup costs or in the transaction costs reduces the equilibrium transaction volume in the on-demand submarket because these costs crucially affect both owners’ and renters’ participation in this submarket. As a result, an increase in the setup costs or in the transaction costs also lowers the social welfare. Because of the complex dynamics involved when balancing the demand and supply in the two submarkets, the effects of setup and transaction costs on the platform’s profit and consumer surplus are no longer monotonic.

This is because the hybrid model offers consumers two options. Compared with the long-term model, its additional on-demand option can attract some consumers who otherwise would not have participated in the market, contributing to the total transaction volume. Compared with the on-demand model, its additional long-term option can attract some fully dedicated resources to be traded, enhancing the total transaction volume. As a result, the hybrid model generates greater transaction volume than the long-term and on-demand models. We call this the transactionexpansion effect.

Meanwhile, the transaction volume in each submarket is lower than that under their corresponding rental models. This is because the long-term and on-demand rentals are substitutable options for consumers, and the demand for one cannibalizes the demand for the other. As a result, the transaction volume in the on-demand submarket under the hybrid model is not as high as that under the on-demand model, and the transaction volume in the long-term submarket is not as high as that under the long-term model. We call this the demand-cannibalization effect.

In contrast to the transaction volume comparison (under which the total setup cost or the total transaction cost plays a critical role), the relative setup cost or relative transaction cost between owners and renters plays a crucial role in determining which model results in a higher equilibrium price. This is because the relative costs dictate the owners’ and renters’ relative incentives to participate in the market and the relative strengths of supply and demand, which in turn shape the equilibrium price.

When both the on-demand and the long-term models are viable (under a moderate or low total cost), as suggested by Lemmas 1 and 2, the setup costs have distinct effects on the equilibrium market-clearing price: An increase in the owners’ cost discourages the supply and, thus, increases the price, whereas an increase in the renters’ cost weakens the demand and, thus, raises the price. Although the setup costs play a similar role in consumers’ participation decisions under both models, their influence under the on-demand market is more pronounced because, under this model, the product is only leased or rented a fraction of time. As a result, when the owners’ setup cost is relatively high (i.e., higher than $f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { l d } } )$ , the equilibrium price under the on-demand model is higher than that under the longterm model. Two observations are worth highlighting about the threshold $f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { l d } }$ . First, as illustrated in Figure 4, the threshold is generically small in our setting because we assume a higher transaction cost for owners than for renters, which tends to drive up the price under the on-demand model. Second, the threshold of the owners’ setup cost relative to the renters’ decreases as the total cost increases. This is because, in the presence of a higher total cost, the marginal consumers trade a greater fraction of time under the on-demand model, reducing the difference in the effects of setup costs under the two models. While conventional wisdom suggests that the long-term rental could be a cheaper option for consumers than on-demand rental, our result indicates that this is not always true—when the owners’ setup cost is very low, the equilibrium price under the on-demand model could be lower, as implied by Proposition 2(a).

When the total cost is low and all three rental models are viable, as illustrated in the bottom-left region in Figure 4(b), Proposition 2(b) shows several important observations. First, interestingly, the on-demand rental price under the hybrid model is the same as that under the on-demand model. The main reason is that, under the hybrid model, the marginal consumers who are indifferent between joining the markets and not are the consumers of the on-demand option (i.e., $\theta _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { d } }$ and $\theta _ { \mathrm { r } } ^ { \mathrm { d } }$ characterized in Equations (4) and (5)). Meanwhile, in the long-term submarket the demand from renters who have high utilization matches the supply from owners who have low utilization in equilibrium. Therefore, there exists a unique on-demand submarket price that balances the total supply and demand on the platform, which is the same equilibrium price that clears the market under the on-demand model. Therefore, the on-demand market price is the same under the two models.

Second, under the hybrid model, the price in the long-term submarket could be higher than that in the on-demand submarket. Under the hybrid model, consumers have two rental options—long-term and on-demand rentals. When the owners’ setup cost becomes lower, more owners participate in the markets, which drives the market prices down. Because the setup cost does not directly affect owners’ choices between the on-demand and long-term submarkets, these additional owners are primarily active in the on-demand submarket and thus have a more significant effect on the on-demand submarket price. As a result, when the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than the renters’ $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { ~ - } } < f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { h } } )$ , the on-demand submarket price becomes lower than that of the long term submarket. Furthermore, because of the external ity between the on-demand and long-term submarkets, the price in the long-term submarket is dragged down and becomes lower than that under the long-term model. This explains the bottom-left region in Figure 4(b). This intuition, together with that for $p _ { \mathrm { d } } ^ { * } < p _ { \mathrm { l } } ^ { * } ,$ , explain the $f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { h } } \leq f _ { \mathrm { o } } < f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { l d } }$ region in Figure 4(b).

In contrast, when the owners’ setup cost is higher than the renters’ $( \mathrm { i . e . , } f _ { 0 } \ge f _ { \mathrm { r } } )$ , both higher setup and transaction costs on the renters’ side discourage their participation, which makes the price in the on-demand submarket the highest. Because of its externality imposed on the longterm submarket, the high on-demand submarket price also boosts the long-term submarket price. As a result, the price under the long-term model is the lowest, which explains the top-left region in Figure 4(b).

Third, it is worth highlighting the region $f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { l d } } \leq f _ { \mathrm { o } } < f _ { \mathrm { r } } ,$ in which the price difference between the on-demand and long-term submarkets under the hybrid model is even greater than the price difference between the on-demand and long-term models. In this case, the owners’ setup cost is lower but not significantly lower than the renters’, which makes the on-demand (sub)- market price higher than both the long-term submarket price and the price under the long-term model. Furthermore, compared with the mild setup cost difference (which affects consumers’ participation similarly in both long-term and hybrid models), the relative transaction cost in the on-demand submarket under the hybrid model is critical in determining its price ranking relative to the long-term model. In particular, the owners’ relatively high transaction cost compared with the renters’ provides participating owners with greater incentives toward the long-term submarket on the supply side, driving down its price and making it even lower than the price under the long-term model.

Our findings have important practical implications. In reality, the owners’ and renters’ setup costs and transaction costs can be different in different markets. For example, in the accommodation-sharing market, renters can easily set up an account and conveniently plan their stay with the help of Airbnb’s advanced reputation system, so they likely incur lower setup and transaction costs than owners who need to take care of the house registration, cleaning, and maintenance. Our model predicts that the on-demand rental could be more expensive than the long-term rental in such markets. This is consistent with Airbnb’s practice of offering weekly and monthly discounts that signal significant savings from the long-term rental. The discounted prices are highlighted next to the original prices in consumers’ search results. As another example, on the low-end car-sharing platform Atzuche in

China, renters need to pay large security deposits and may incur various fees such as cancellation, claim administration, and improper returns, which bring higher setup and transaction costs for renters than for owners. Thus, consistent with our model prediction, the on-demand rental is less expensive than the longterm rental. For example, a Honda Odyssey minivan can be rented at RMB 210–230 per day but RMB 7,260 per month.

## 5.3. Social Welfare and Consumer Surplus

Proposition 3 presents the platform’s optimal rental model choices concerning the maximization of the platform’s profit. Another important consideration is social welfare—the total benefit derived by the platform and consumers. Social planners would prefer the rental model that maximizes the social welfare. Proposition 4 presents the conditions under which each rental model leads to the highest social welfare, and Figure $7 ( \mathrm { a } )$ further illustrates the equilibrium regions.

Proposition 4. (a) When $\begin{array} { r } { q - \frac { ( 1 - \alpha ) f _ { t } c _ { t } } { q } \le f _ { t } + c _ { t } < q , } \end{array}$ , the long-term rental model generates the highest social welfare.

(b) When $\begin{array} { r } { f _ { t } + c _ { t } < q - \frac { ( 1 - \alpha ) f _ { t } c _ { t } } { q } , } \end{array}$ , the hybrid rental model generates the highest social welfare.

Social welfare concerns resource reallocation and utilization via the market. Although the prices and commissions could influence consumers’ participation, they are internal transfers in the social system and do not affect social welfare. Specifically, in this sharing economy, three factors affect the social welfare. First, as in a typical exchange market, the number of participating consumers and the number of resulting transactions are critical to social welfare. After all, resources are reallocated via market transactions. Different rental models might result in different market prices, leading to different levels of consumer participation and resource reallocation. Second, resource utilization is also key to social welfare. Under the longterm model, a consumer who rents the product will be its sole user and the resource might stay idle when the renter does not use it, whereas the idle resource is better used under the on-demand model. Third, the setup and transaction costs, in addition to affecting the equi librium prices and resource reallocation, are the costs associated with the related trades and thus the direct social loss. Different rental models result in different transaction costs.

Two observations suggested by Proposition 4 are worth noting. First, Proposition 4 shows that the on-demand model cannot be superior from the social-welfare perspective. On the one hand, when the setup and transaction costs are high, only high-utilization renters transact with low-utilization owners, and participants under the on-demand model would incur large transaction costs because of the volume traded. The transaction costs not only discourage some consumers from participating, but also represent a social loss for participating consumers. Both hurt social welfare and cannot be compensated by the potential benefit from the better resource utilization compared with the long-term model. As a result, the longterm model outperforms the on-demand model in generating social welfare. On the other hand, when the setup and transaction costs are low, both the on-demand model and the hybrid model can attract a comparable number of participating consumers. The advantage of the hybrid model over the on-demand model is that, by offering two submarkets, consumers self-select between the submarkets considering the transaction costs and their potential resource utilization. In equilibrium, the trades occurring in the long-term submarket are between low-utilization owners and high-utilization renters, which reduces the hybrid model’s transaction costs without losing much utilization benefit compared with the on-demand model. As a result, the hybrid model outperforms the on-demand model in generating social welfare.

In this case, although consumers enjoy an additional on-demand option offered by the hybrid model, the price in the on-demand submarket is high because of the limited supply and strong demand, hurting consumer surplus.

When the win–win outcome occurs, the platform’s optimal choice is also the one that maximizes social welfare. However, the rental model that maximizes social welfare does not necessarily render a win–win outcome. For example, when the hybrid rental model is optimally chosen by the platform but $f _ { \mathrm { o } } \geq f _ { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \mathrm { l h } }$ , the social welfare-maximizing model is the hybrid model, which leads to the highest platform profit at the expense of consumers, as illustrated in Figures 5 and 7(b). In such a case, if social planners are concerned about consume surplus, some incentive mechanisms or policy interventions should be implemented.

On the positive side, Corollary 1 reveals that it is not rare that the platform’s optimal rental-model choice yields the highest consumer surplus. The win–win outcome is likely to arise in equilibrium when the total cost is low and the renters’ and owners’ setup costs are comparable or when the total cost is moderate or high. In particular, when the total cost is moderate or high and the long-term model is optimally chosen by a platform, the long-term rental model is also the most beneficial for consumers. When the total cost is low and the hybrid rental model is optimally chosen by a platform, very likely it is also the best for consumers. We observe that both the long-term and hybrid rental models are popular business models adopted by sharing platforms. Our analysis helps justify platforms’ choices of these models over the on-demand model from the social-welfare and consumer-surplus perspectives.

## 6. Conclusion

In this paper, we develop an analytical model to study three popular rental models in the sharing economy: the long-term, on-demand, and hybrid rental models. We examine how consumers’ costs (including setup and transaction costs), resource utilization, and the bilateral nature of the two-sided market affect the equilibrium transaction volumes and market-clearing prices on the platform. We further examine the implications of the different rental models on platform profit, social welfare, and consumer surplus. Our findings provide important managerial insights for different stakeholders in the sharing economy.

First, our analysis demonstrates that the platform’s optimal rental-model choices critically depend on the consumers’ costs. When the renters’ and owners’ total setup cost is sufficiently high, no rental market is viable in equilibrium. When the total setup cost is not too high but total cost is sufficiently high, only the longterm rental market is sustainable in equilibrium. When the total cost is moderate, both the long-term and on-demand rental models are sustainable. Only when the total cost is low are all three models viable in equilibrium. Therefore, prohibitively high costs could be the major barriers for the on-demand and hybrid rental models to be sustainable. For example, China has seen a boom in its car-sharing economy in recent years. However, the lack of trust among consumers and no clear definition of legal responsibilities lead to relatively high setup and transaction costs among consumers. Therefore, many of China’s car-sharing startups (e.g., Baojia) adopt the long-term rental as their primary rental model.

Second, our results show that consumers’ setup and transaction costs play a critical role in the platform’s optimal rental-model choices. In the presence of a moderately high relative transaction cost on the owners side, when the owners’ setup cost is significantly higher than the renters’, the on-demand model is optimal. When the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than the renters’ and the renters’ setup cost is low, the longterm model is optimal. Only when their setup costs are comparable or when the owners’ setup cost is significantly lower than the renters’ and the renters’ setup cost is high do consumers enjoy the rental flexibility brought by the hybrid model, and the platform benefits from the positive transaction-expansion effect. In contrast, when the owners’ transaction cost relative to the renters becomes sufficiently high, the strong disincentive for the owners in the on-demand submarket under the hybrid model drives up the equilibrium on-demand price. The negative demand-cannibalization effect cannot be outweighed by the positive transaction-expansion effect, making the hybrid model not as good as the on-demand model. In this case, despite the flexibility offered by the two rental choices, the hybrid model can never be optimal. Therefore, we caution the platform that adopts the hybrid rental model about the two opposing effects. The platform should carefully manage consumers’ participation incentives by assessing the consumers’ setup and transaction costs involved on the sharing platform.

Third, we find that the platform’s optimal rental model may not be the most beneficial to consumers and society. Only when the hybrid model is chosen by the platform, or when the long-term model is chosen in the presence of a moderate or high total cost, can a profitmaximizing platform’s and a welfare-maximizing social planner’s objectives be well aligned. Fortunately, our findings suggest that it is not rare for the platform’s optimal rental model choice to also yield the highest consumer surplus. The win–win outcome is more likely to occur when the disparity between the renters’ and own ers’ setup costs is small. Therefore, policymakers may offer favorable policies or even subsidize the disadvantaged side to balance consumers’ participation incentives on both sides of the market, leading to mutually beneficial social outcomes.

Overall, this study offers several new insights regarding the platform’s optimal pricing strategies and rental models in the sharing economy. Although conventional wisdom suggests that the long-term rental model may offer a lower price than the on-demand rental model due to resource commitment, our results suggest that it could be either way. Moreover, one may conjecture that the hybrid rental model that takes advantage of both the full resource commitment of the long-term rental and the high resource utilization of the on-demand rental should naturally be preferred by the platform. However, our analysis shows that it does not need to be the case. These new findings are driven by the bilateral nature of the two-sided markets on the sharing platform, which is fundamentally different from the traditional posted price retail markets.

Furthermore, as the advancement of mobile and Internet technologies continues to lower the technologi cal barriers to consumers’ participation in the sharing economy, our analysis informs policymakers to encourage the hybrid rental model on sharing platforms. On the one hand, the hybrid model provides renters with more rental options to satisfy their diverse consumption needs. On the other hand, it provides owners with more flexible leasing options to increase their resource utilization. In an online environment that lacks consumer trust and seller reputation, the government may actively introduce legal and regulatory requirements to facilitate the peer-to-peer transactions within well defined boundaries, ensure compliance of market participants, and orchestrate the sustainable development of platform business models in the sharing economy.

Several potential directions for future research remain. First, our stylized model focuses on a relatively homogeneous product (e.g., a car) such that the incurred transaction cost associated with each transaction is the same across all owners (or all renters). In reality, there are scenarios where the incurred transaction costs could depend on the type of the products. For example, cleaning a luxury car (or clothes that require dedicated care) may incur a higher cost than cleaning an economy car (or basic clothes). Future work may extend our framework to consider heterogeneous products and heterogeneous transaction costs on the sharing platform. Second, although our study does not delve into endogenizing commission rates, we recognize commission optimization as a compelling avenue for future research. Exploring the intricacies of commission rate dynamics could offer valuable insights and contribute to the ongoing discourse in our field.
