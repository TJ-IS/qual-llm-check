# To Partner or Not to Partner? The Partnership Between Platforms and Data Brokers in Two-Sided Markets

Abstract. Data have become an important competitive asset, especially for online advertising platforms, leading to the emergence of a new data broker industry. Platforms can partner with data brokers to acquire external data to enhance their targeting capabilities, but this practice has stoked growing consumer privacy concerns. This study develops a gametheoretic model to examine the economic mechanism underlying the partnership between competing platforms and a data broker in a two-sided market. Interestingly, our analysis shows that increasing consumer privacy concerns caused by the data broker may encourage platforms to partner with the data broker rather than discourage them. The driving force is that, because of negative cross-side network effects from advertisers to consumers, increased privacy concerns can serve as a strategic lever to soften price competition on the advertiser side. However, when both platforms partner with the data broker, a prisoner’s dilemma may arise in equilibrium even when the data broker can greatly improve the targeting capabilities. We find that the platform-data broker partnership hurts consumer surplus when platforms adopt a pure ad-sponsored model without charging consumers, but it may benefit consumer surplus when platforms adopt a mixed model with ad-sponsored and subscription-based revenue. Moreover, our analysis indicates that platforms may not have incentives to adopt high levels of privacy protection and may instead adopt asymmetric privacy protection strategies, with one platform adopting high privacy protection and the other opting for low protection. A series of model extensions confirm the robustness of our key findings. We conclude with a discussion of managerial and policy implications.

## 1. Introduction

Data have become a strategic asset for many businesses and industries (DalleMule and Davenport 2017). The emerging data broker industry, established to meet the demand for data, was valued at USD 389 billion in 2024 and is expected to reach USD 561 billion in 2029.<sup>1</sup> Major data brokers in the market, including Acxiom, Experian, Oracle, and Lotame, actively collect consumer data from various online and offline sources (e.g., social media, web history, and offline purchase history). Data brokers aggregate and process the collected data to generate consumer insights, which are then sold to other companies for purposes such as targeted advertising and personalized pricing.

Given the importance of data, digital platforms (e.g., Facebook, TikTok, and publisher websites) have begun to partner with data brokers (Venkatadri et al. 2019). These platforms are funded by digital advertising in a two-sided market, in which they provide online services to consumers while collecting consumer data for selling targeted ads to advertisers. By partnering with data brokers, platforms can improve their targeting capabilities by acquiring external data from data brokers. For instance, Facebook used to partner with several data brokers (e.g., Acxiom, Experion), allowing for the data collected online by Facebook to be merged with data collected offline by data brokers. This enables platforms to refine their targeting based on information compiled by these partners, such as offline demographic and behavioral information like addresses, income, and purchase history. Other examples include the partnership between Snapchat and Oracle, where

Snapchat can gain better user insights by leveraging Oracle’s offline data (Wilson 2017).

The platform-data broker partnership, although beneficial to advertisers, may hurt consumers. According to a survey by eMarketer (2019), 77% of U.S. Internet users are concerned that targeted advertising based on their personal data may invade privacy. Recently, the emergence of platform-data broker partnerships has exacerbated consumer privacy concerns because the linking of the data from two parties may leak more sensitive information about users to advertisers (Venkatadri et al. 2018). In line with recent privacy regulations such as the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), platforms must disclose detailed information about data transactions with third parties. Hence, platformdata broker partnerships are much more visible to consumers than ever before. However, because of the untransparent operations of the data broker industry, platform-data broker partnerships have triggered greater concerns over possible invasions of privacy (Ram and Murgia 2019, Sherman 2020). Moreover, increasingly common data breach incidents have also heightened privacy concerns about the platform-data broker partnership. One of the most notorious examples is the Facebook Cambridge Analytica Scandal. Typically, when platforms collect extensive data to enable more targeted advertising, users may indeed become more vulnerable to privacy invasion (i.e., negative cross-side network effects). The increased privacy concern may lead to loss of users and reduced platform usage, which indirectly affect the platforms’ competitiveness on the advertiser side. As a consequence, platforms’ ad pricing and advertiser demands may also be affected.

Faced with tremendous pressure from consumer privacy groups and regulatory authorities, some platforms have stopped partnering with data brokers (Tau 2020). For instance, Facebook announced it would terminate its partnership with data brokers in 2018 after the Cambridge Analytica Scandal (Ingram and Fioretti 2018). Evidently, such a decision is made to save the firm’s image and reputation in reaction to a wave of criticism. Nevertheless, when surveying the market, we find that many other platforms are still closely partnering with data brokers to leverage external data to enhance their targeting capabilities. For instance, Snapchat has partnered with Oracle to improve its ad targeting since 2017 (Wilson 2017). Besides these examples, many publisher websites have partnered with Lotame to enhance targeting capabilities. These diverse partnership strategies motivate our investigation of platforms’ incentives to partner with the data broker, given the potential merits (i.e., improved targeting level) and demerits (i.e., increased privacy concerns) in a twosided market. Moreover, in view of the ongoing debate about the data broker industry, it is worthwhile to examine the welfare implications of platform-data broker partnerships.

Given the diverse strategies adopted by competing platforms, understanding platform competition is integral to our study as it significantly impacts consumer choice, advertiser demand, platform strategies, and market outcomes. In a highly competitive landscape, platforms are compelled to seek partnerships with data brokers to gain a competitive edge through superior targeting capabilities, which has become a pivotal strategy in the advertising market (Lotame 2020). However, this also raises important questions about the tradeoffs between improved targeting and increased privacy concerns. Because of growing privacy concerns, platforms have started to implement measures such as using firewalls and enhancing encryption to protect consumers’ personal data and reduce their privacy concerns. Additionally, recent privacy regulations like GDPR and CCPA have pressured firms to prioritize consumer privacy protection. However, it is unclear whether competing platforms have incentives to adopt high levels of privacy protection or if competitive pressures lead them to compromise on consumer privacy. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of platform competition is essential for analyzing the strategic decisions of platforms and their welfare implications.

In particular, we address the following research questions in this study. (i) How does the partnership with a data broker affect platforms’ pricing strategies and profits? (ii) When should competing platforms partner with a data broker? (iii) How does the partnership between platforms and the data broker affect the consumer surplus, advertiser surplus, and social welfare? (iv) What are competing platforms’ privacy protection strategies?

To answer these questions, we build a game-theoretic model in which two competing platforms provide services to consumers while selling targeted ads to advertisers. We consider two types of advertisers, that is, multihoming advertisers that display ads on both platforms and single-homing advertisers that display ads on one platform. Partnering with the data broker can improve platforms’ targeting capabilities, but it also invokes greater consumer privacy concerns, and platforms need to pay a fee to the data broker for consumer data.

Our analysis yields several interesting results. First, we find that the increase in consumer privacy concerns caused by the data broker may incentivize rather than discourage platforms from partnering with the data broker. This result can be explained from the perspective of the negative cross-side network effect in twosided markets. Specifically, when partnering with the data broker amplifies greater privacy concerns related to targeted advertising, platforms, in order to prevent consumer churn, exhibit reduced incentives to compete for advertisers. Consequently, both platforms are inclined to charge a higher ad price to control the number of advertisers. In equilibrium, the price competition would be softened, leading to higher profits. This result reveals an important implication: increased privacy concerns caused by platform-data broker partnerships can be a strategic lever to soften price competition on the advertiser side. This privacy concern-induced higher price is consistent with trends observed in the industry. In practice, many publishers are trying to increase ad prices and reduce the number of ads in response to consumers’ growing privacy concerns about targeted ads, which has proven to boost revenue for some publishers (Marshall 2017). As competition among platforms on the consumer side intensifies, we find that platforms are more likely to actively pursue partnerships with data brokers. Our research provides a possible explanation of why platform-data broker partnerships still prevail in the market despite growing consumer privacy concerns about data brokers.

Another key factor motivating the platforms to partner with the data broker is that the latter can help platforms improve targeting capabilities, which aligns with our expectations. However, it is intriguing to find that partnering with the data broker may lead to a prisoner’s dilemma for platforms; that is, platforms may be worse off than if they both commit not to partner with the data broker. This happens when the data broker induces high, but not too high, privacy concerns. In this case, platform competition will be mitigated, but not substantially, so the incremental revenue from partnering with the data broker cannot compensate for the data fee paid to the data broker. This result resonates well with recent field studies and industrial reports, which indicate that although partnering with a data broker can increase platforms’ competitive advantage in targeting capabilities, their profits may decrease because the data broker charges a high data price (Neumann et al. 2019, CMA 2020). Overall, our findings highlight that platforms should carefully assess the increased privacy concerns and the targeting improvement brought by data brokers before making such partnership decisions.

Our results also reveal important welfare implications by examining consumer surplus, advertiser surplus, and social welfare. We find that the platform’s business model plays a crucial role in whether partnering with the data broker benefits or hurts consumers. When platforms adopt a pure ad-sponsored model, partnering with a data broker always hurts consumers because of the increased privacy concerns. However, when platforms adopt a mixed model of subscriptionbased and ad-sponsored revenue, a platform-data broker partnership can benefit consumers because it will intensify the price competition on the consumer side, resulting in a lower consumer price and thus leading to a higher consumer surplus. The caveat here is that, although the ad-sponsored model does not charge consumers, it can harm them in the context of platformdata broker partnerships. Hence, policymakers should take into account the platform’s business model when scrutinizing the impact of platform-data broker partnerships on consumer surplus. Looking at advertiser surplus and social welfare, we show that advertisers and society can be better off when the improvement in targeting capabilities outweighs the increase in consumer privacy concerns.

In response to increasing privacy concerns, platforms have initiated measures to safeguard consumers personal data and mitigate privacy issues. Our findings suggest that platforms do not always have incentives to adopt stringent privacy protections. Specifically, implementing high privacy protection levels can allevi ate consumers’ privacy concerns about the data broker and targeted advertising, which in turn intensifies platform competition on the advertiser side. We find that competing platforms may adopt asymmetric privacy protection strategies, where one platform enforces high privacy protection, whereas the other opts for a lower level. Indeed, such asymmetric privacy protection strategies are evident in real-world market practices. For example, in the social media sector, Snapchat has imple mented stronger privacy protections than Instagram. Snapchat uses end-to-end encryption on photos and videos shared between its users to better protect user privacy (Arpit 2023).

Finally, we conduct several extensions to demonstrate the robustness of key findings and derive some new insights. For example, we extend the model by considering advertisers’ endogenous multihoming decisions and con sumers’ opt-out options of targeted ads. We also examine the correlation between the targeting improvement and the increased privacy concerns and the scenario where the two platforms with different baseline targeting capabilities. Moreover, we investigate platforms’ endogenous decisions on targeting improvement. Our analysis shows that the qualitative insights still hold in these extensions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We review the relevant literature in Section 2. In Sec tion 3, we describe our model framework, followed by the model analysis and equilibrium results in Section 4. Section 5 extends the model with several alternative model setups. Finally, the paper concludes in Section 6 with a discussion of managerial implications, limitations of the study, and future research directions.

## 2. Literature Review

This study relates to several streams of literature. First, it is relevant to the growing literature on two-sided platform markets. Second, it contributes to the literature on consumer privacy concerns. Finally, we contribute to the emerging literature on data broker markets. Below, we describe the relevant studies within each stream and highlight the gap between previous studies and ours.

## 2.1. Two-Sided Platform Markets

Our study contributes to the growing literature on two-sided platform markets. Starting with pioneering works by Caillaud and Jullien (2003) and Rochet and Tirole (2003), subsequent studies have investigated pricing strategies for two-sided platforms (Armstrong 2006, Rochet and Tirole 2006, Hagiu 2009). Given the presence of cross-side network effects, an important implication from these studies is that platforms can optimize profits by subsidizing one side and charging a high price on the other side. The existing literature has primarily focused on single-homing agents on both sides, or the “competitive bottlenecks” setting with multihoming on one side of the market. However, when users on both sides can decide to single-home or multihome endogenously, Bakos and Halaburda (2020) show that it may not be optimal for platforms to subsidize one side because there is no strategic interdependence between the two sides in this case.

Other studies in this stream examine the implications of nonprice factors on platform competition under cross-side network effects (Hagiu and Spulber 2013, Anderson et al. 2014, Dou and Wu 2021). For instance, Hagiu and Spulber (2013) examine platform incentives to invest in first-party content as a strategic nonprice control and how such content interacts with the platforms’ pricing strategies. Feng et al. (2020) explore how cross-side network effects influence the optimal market entry time of competing platforms. Dou and Wu (2021) investigate how piggybacking, in which platforms can import exclusive external users, affects platforms’ optimal pricing and subsidization strategies. Moreover, Chellappa and Mukherjee (2021) examine the strategic role of information in two-sided platform competition by investigating competing platforms’ preannouncement strategies. More recently, Sun et al. (2023) examine how competing platforms’ service quality is affected by the strength of cross-side networks in online food delivery industry.

Our study contributes to this stream of literature in two important ways. First, most of this research assumes that both sides of the market have positive cross-side network effects on the other side. In our context, crossside network effects are positive in one direction (i.e., consumers to advertisers) but negative in the other because of privacy concerns (i.e., advertisers and data brokers to consumers). We examine how the strengthened negative cross-side network effect caused by data brokers would affect platform competition. Second, this study is among the first to introduce the partnership with data brokers as a nonprice control for platforms and investigate the interplay between cross-side network effects and price competition.

## 2.2. Consumer Privacy Concerns

This study is also related to the literature on consumer privacy issues (see Acquisti et al. (2016) for a comprehensive literature survey). A recent strand of literature examines the implications of consumer privacy in the context of price discrimination (Conitzer et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2020) and personalized service provision (Chellappa and Shivendu 2010). Close to our context, several papers examine the impact of privacy concerns when platforms utilize consumer data for targeted advertising. For instance, Gal-Or et al. (2018) study the role of privacy concerns on platform competition, finding that a higher privacy concern can intensify platform competition because it reduces platforms’ incentive to invest in targeting capability, thereby reducing the targeting differentiation of competing platforms. Dimakopoulos and Sudaric (2018) examine how privacy concerns affect competing platforms’ decisions on how much data to collect, finding that the data collection level can be inefficiently high if the competition is weak. Further, Casadesus-Masanell and Hervas-Drane (2015) analyze the implications of consumer privacy for competition in a marketplace where firms can obtain profits from consumer purchases and from selling consumer information to third parties.

Our study is more relevant to Gopal et al. (2018), which examines the impact of privacy concerns on publisher websites’ incentives to share user information with third parties (e.g., advertisers). In their model, third parties generate negative network effects on the consumer side because of user privacy concerns. Although we also consider privacy concerns in consumer utility functions, our study departs from theirs in that we focus on the partnership between platforms and the data broker as a strategic choice variable for platforms. We characterize the conditions when partnering with the data broker can improve platform profits and when it leads to a prisoner’s dilemma outcome for platforms. Moreover, in Gopal et al. (2018), platforms charge prices from both sides (advertisers and consumers), and there is no competition on the adver tiser side. In contrast, in our setting, we explicitly model the platform competition on the advertiser side.

Overall, our study contributes to this growing literature by examining how consumer privacy concerns affect the partnership between platforms and the data broker, which is a growing trend in practice but rarely studied in the literature. Our analysis provides insights into the economic mechanisms that explain the platform-data broker partnership.

## 2.3. Data Broker Markets

This study also contributes to the emerging literature on online data markets (Bergemann and Bonatti 2019), in which data brokers play a strategic role in selling data to downstream firms (e.g., advertising platforms in this study). Given the emergence of the data broker market, the research on this topic is currently in its early stages. The existing studies investigate the data brokers’ data selling strategies in different contexts, where data can be used to price discriminate (Montes et al. 2019, Bounie et al. 2021), improve consumer targeting (Bergemann and Bonatti 2019, Zhang et al. 2020), and predict market demand (Bimpikis et al. 2019). For example, Neumann et al. (2019) empirically examine the effectiveness of third-party data from data brokers in improving consumer profiling, and find that, on average, it can increase the identification of a user by 0%–77%. Mehta et al. (2021) investigate the pricing policies for data brokers and find that the pricequantity schedule is optimal for the data broker. Zhang et al. (2023) investigate the impact of data brokers on downstream firm competition, where competing firms can buy data from the data broker to improve service quality. Furthermore, Zhang et al. (2024) propose a novel model to examine the data broker’s optimal strategy of selling different types of data insights to downstream firms.

Different from the above studies that primarily focus on a single-sided market that involves the interactions between firms and consumers, we focus on a twosided market where the platform-data broker partnership benefits the advertiser side but hurts the consumer side. Our study sheds light on the impacts of the platform-data broker partnership on different stakeholders, including platforms, advertisers, consumers, and the data broker. We contribute to the ongoing debate about the data broker industry by showing that the platform-data broker partnership does not necessarily hurt consumer surplus or social welfare.

## 3. Model

We consider a two-sided market consisting of three types of players, that is, two symmetric competing platforms (denoted by $i = A , B ) ,$ a unit mass of consumers, and a unit mass of advertisers.<sup>2</sup> Platforms provide content services to consumers while collecting their data and selling targeted ads to advertisers. Thus, platforms compete for both the consumer side (called the X side) and the advertiser side (called the Y side). Following the prior literature (Dimakopoulos and Sudaric 2018, Bakos and Halaburda 2020), we employ a two-sided Hotelling model to capture the consumers’ and advertisers’ horizontal preferences over two platforms. Typically, consumers have different preferences about platforms’ content services, while advertisers have different preferences about platforms’ ad formats and user demographics given their different marketing goals and target audiences. Specifically, we assume consumers and advertisers are uniformly distributed along two Hotelling lines, that is, $x \sim U [ 0 , { \dot { 1 } } ]$ for the X side, y \~

U[0; 1] for the Y side. Platforms are located at the endpoints of the two Hotelling lines, with Platform A being at zero and Platform B being at one. Below, we describe the platform profits, consumer utility, and advertiser payoff.

## 3.1. Platforms

By collecting and analyzing consumer data, platforms can learn consumer preferences and build consumer profiles. These consumer insights enable platforms to develop targeting capabilities, which refers to its ability to deliver personalized and relevant advertisements to the right users based on their interests, behaviors, and demographics. A high targeting capability allows advertisers to reach their desired audience more effectively and thus can attract advertisers to purchase ad slots on platforms. We denote platforms’ baseline targeting capabilities based on their own user data as $\beta .$ . In the main model, we assume platforms adopt a pure ad-sponsored business model without charging consumers, which is commonly adopted by digital platforms (e.g., Facebook and YouTube). In Section 5.1.1, we also consider platforms adopting a mixed business model with both ad-sponsored and consumer subscriptionbased revenue.

To enhance their targeting capabilities, platforms can partner with data brokers who collect a wide variety of consumer data from online and offline sources, including public records, online activities, purchase histories, and loyalty programs. For instance, Facebook used to partner with several data brokers (e.g., Acxiom, Experion, and Oracle) to gain access to an even broader range of data. Data brokers do not directly interact with users but can provide the platform with additional information to create more robust and accurate user profiles. Therefore, this partnership enables platforms to enhance their tar geting capabilities. We denote the enhanced targeting capabilities as $\beta + \delta ,$ , where δ is the improvement in targeting capabilities enabled by the data broker. This additive functional form has been widely adopted by prior literature to capture the enhanced value gain (Mantovani and Ruiz-Aliseda 2016, Chen and Guo 2022). Without loss of generality, we normalize $\beta$ and δ to range from zero to one, that is, $0 < \beta < 1 , 0 < \delta < 1$ . In Section 5.2.2, we also consider two alternative multiplicative functional forms for enhanced targeting capabilities: β(1 + δ) and $\beta + ( 1 - \beta ) \delta$ . Our analysis shows that the key insights remain qualitatively valid.

When platforms partner with the data broker to acquire additional consumer data, they need to pay the data broker a fee for access to consumer data. Media articles and industry reports (Moore 2016, MMR 2023) show that charging based on the number of consumer records (per-record pricing) is a common pricing model employed by data brokers (White 2019, Klimas 2022, Farrell 2023). For example, Acxiom, a well-known data broker specializing in digital marketing and targeted advertising, charges a data price of \$79 per 1,000 users (Klimas 2022). Similarly, Experian charges a data price from \$0.007 per record to over \$0.2 per record (Kaplan et al. 2021). The above evidence shows that the platform’s payment to the data broker is proportional to the consumer demand of the focal platform. Hence, the platform’s payment can be denoted by $\gamma x _ { i } ,$ where $x _ { i }$ is the consumer demand of platform $i ,$ and $\gamma$ represents the unit price per consumer record. In Online Appendix A7, we consider an alternative payment structure between platforms and the data broker: one-time payment, where platforms pay a fixed fee for accessing the data broker’s data. Our result reveals that this payment structure change does not affect the main findings qualitatively.

Platforms can choose whether to partner with the data broker, denoted by Y (partner) or N (do not partner). Table 1 summarizes the platforms’ decision matrix. There are four different partnership configurations between platforms and the data broker. The first (second) argument in each configuration represents platform A’s (B’s) decision.

The platforms’ profit functions are as follows: (i) when platform i does not partner with the data broker, $\pi _ { i } = p _ { i } y _ { i } ;$ (ii) when the platform i partners with the data broker, $\pi _ { i } = p _ { i } y _ { i } - \gamma x _ { i } ,$ , where $p _ { i }$ is the ad price charged for advertisers, $y _ { i }$ is platform $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ advertiser demand, and $x _ { i }$ is the consumer demand. In the main model, we assume the unit data price $\gamma$ is exogenous. In Online Appendix A8, we consider the case where the data broker decides the unit data price endogenously.

## 3.2. Consumers

Consumers derive a basic value (denoted by $v _ { c } )$ from consuming the platforms’ content services. In this process, platforms collect consumer data such as names, contact details, search history, IP addresses, and number of clicks. Platforms utilize consumer information to help advertisers deliver targeted ads to consumers, which has infringed on consumer privacy (Mayer and Mitchell 2012). A recent survey indicates that most respondents resent targeted ads because of perceived privacy intrusions.<sup>3</sup> Thus, although the service is free of charge, consumers incur privacy costs when using the platforms’ service.

Following the prior literature (Lin 2022), we consider two components of a consumer’s privacy cost: (i) intrinsic privacy cost (denoted by κ), which reflects a consumer’s intrinsic preference for $\mathrm { \dot { \ p r i v a c y } } , ^ { 4 }$ and (ii) variable privacy cost, which depends on the number of targeted ads on the platform (Tucker 2012, King 2019). Typically, the number of targeted ads positively correlates with the number of advertisers. Given that advertisers’ ad intensity is not the focus of this study, we consider it to be exogenous and assume that each advertiser purchases the same and fixed number of ad impressions $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , one thousand impressions). Following the prior literature (Reisinger 2012, Gopal et al. 2018, Lin 2020), we normalize the fixed number of ad impressions placed by each advertiser to one. In this setting, as the number of advertisers increases, there might be more targeted ads for consumers, which would hurt consumer privacy, leading to a decrease in consumer utility. Hence, we model the consumers’ variable privacy cost αy , where α denotes the consumer privacy concerns about targeted ads, and $y _ { i }$ represents the number of advertisers served by platform i.

## 3.3. Advertisers

We consider an advertising market consisting of both single-homing and multihoming advertisers. In practice, some advertisers may have a limited budget for advertising (Tarver 2022, Konstantinovic 2023), so these advertisers can advertise on one platform only, which is called single-homing. In contrast, advertisers with sufficient budgets may advertise on both platforms $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ multihoming). Therefore, to better capture the practice, following Doganoglu and Wright (2006), Kaiser and Wright (2006), and Park et al. (2021), we assume a proportion of multihoming advertisers, denoted as m, which display ads on both platforms. The remaining 1 � m proportion of advertisers are single-homing advertisers that will compare their utility from two platforms and choose one that gives them a higher utility. We assume these two groups of advertisers are uniformly distributed on the Hotelling line. In the main model, for ease of exposition, we consider that the number of multihoming advertisers is exogenously given. In

Section 5.2.1, we consider the case where the m proportion of advertisers makes their homing decisions (singlehoming versus multihoming) endogenously. Moreover, in the main model, we assume two groups of advertisers purchase the same amount of ad impressions. We consider an extended model where single-homing advertisers and multihoming advertisers purchase different amounts of ad impressions in Online Appendix A10. Our analysis demonstrates the key insights remain qualitatively consistent with these model extensions.

Advertisers derive benefits from displaying ads on platforms and pay the ad price $p _ { i }$ to platforms. Consistent with previous studies (Dimakopoulos and Sudaric 2018), we adopt the Hotelling model to capture advertisers’ preferences between the two platforms. Formally, an advertiser located at y will obtain the following payoff $W _ { i } ( y )$ when displaying an ad on platform i:

$$
\begin{array}{l} W _ {A} (y) = v _ {s} + (\beta + \mathbb {I} _ {A} \delta) x _ {A} - p _ {A} - \theta y, \\ W _ {B} (y) = v _ {s} + (\beta + \mathbb {I} _ {B} \delta) x _ {B} - p _ {B} - \theta (1 - y), \end{array}
$$

where $v _ { s }$ represents the basic value that advertisers can derive from advertising on the platform using the basic functionality offered by platforms. For example, an advertiser may create and promote a page on a social media platform to build a social presence and connect with customers. In this process, advertisers can gain value from the platform through increasing consumer awareness. For ease of exposition, we follow the prior literature to assume that the basic value $v _ { s }$ is the same for both platforms (Anderson et al. 2014, Chellappa and Mukherjee 2021, Dou and Wu 2021). In the main model, we assume v is sufficiently large so that the advertiser market is fully covered. We also consider the case where $v _ { s }$ is relatively low and the advertiser market is not fully covered in Online Appendix A11.

The second component $\left( \beta + \mathbb { I } _ { i } \delta \right) x _ { i }$ is the additional value (targeting value) that advertisers derive from utilizing the targeting features offered by the platforms. This component measures the effectiveness of targeting in identifying the relevant consumers, which depends on the platforms’ targeting capabilities $\left( \beta + \mathbb { I } _ { i } \delta \right)$ and the number of consumers $x _ { i } .$ . Platforms leverage their targeting capabilities to deliver the ads to the right consumers. By partnering with the data broker, platforms can improve their targeting capabilities from β to $\beta + \delta .$ Moreover, because of the network effects, a larger number of consumers enables platforms to find the targeted users more easily, which reflects the positive cross-side network effects from consumers to advertisers. This modeling approach has also been adopted by prior literature (Reisinger 2012, Dimakopoulos and Sudaric 2018, Gal-Or et al. 2018).

The third component $p _ { i }$ is the ad price charged by the platform. Following the prior literature (Reisinger 2012, Athey et al. 2018, Gal-Or et al. 2018, Kra¨mer et al. 2019), we consider the case where platforms sell their ad inventory directly to advertisers. In practice, this type of direct ad sales between platforms and advertisers can also be done programmatically through an automated system, known in the industry as programmatic direct or programmatic guaranteed sales (Dasic 2021, Matityahu 2022). For example, YouTube offers programmatic guaranteed deals to advertisers, where the price is determined by the platform. According to a recent industry report from eMarketer (Fisher 2018), programmatic direct sales account for a growing share of programmatic ad spending.

The fourth component θy or $\theta ( 1 - y )$ denotes the advertisers’ misfit cost. Typically, different platforms support various ad formats, such as image ads, carousel ads, collection ads, story ads, and video ads.<sup>7</sup> An advertiser’s misfit cost can be understood as the extent to which the platform’s ad format matches advertisers ideal ad format. It can also refer to the extent to which the user demographics of the platform align with advertisers’ ideal target audience. When the user demographics of a platform closely match the preferences and characteristics of an advertiser’s audience, the misfit cost is low. Conversely, if the user demographics of a platform differ significantly from the advertiser’s ideal audience, the misfit cost is high.

We also examine how the intensity of platform competition affects platforms’ partnership strategies. The intensity of platform competition can be measured by the misfit cost parameters t and θ, with t indicating the intensity of platform competition on the consumer side, and θ indicating the intensity of platform competition on the advertiser side. Our result suggests that when consumer-side platform competition is more intense (reflected in lower t), platforms should pursue partnerships with data brokers more actively. The is because, in a highly competitive consumer market, platforms need to increase ad prices to offset the negative impact of targeted ads on consumer utility. Platforms can leverage the partnership with the data broker to increase their prices. Additionally, we numerically find that in a more competitive advertiser market (reflected in lower θ), it is better for platforms to adopt the asymmetric strategy, that is one platform chooses to partner with the platform, while the other does not. This asymmetric (differentiation) strategy can help platforms to mitigate the competition to some extent.

Despite the growing popularity of the partnership between platforms and the data broker, a natural and relevant question is whether such a partnership increases platforms’ profits compared with the case where both platforms do not partner with the data broker. We examine this issue in the following proposition.

Proposition 2. When $\rho _ { 2 } < \rho < \rho _ { 3 } ,$ , both platforms partnering with the data broker is a prisoner’s dilemma situation, where $\begin{array} { r } { \rho _ { 3 } = \frac { t \gamma + ( 1 + m ) \alpha ( \gamma - ( 1 + m ) \delta ) } { ( 1 + m ) ( ( 1 + m ) ( \beta + \delta ) - \gamma ) } . } \end{array}$

Proposition 2 shows that when both platforms partner with the data broker, they may be worse off than if neither does, leading to a prisoner’s dilemma outcome. This result occurs when the increase in consumer privacy concerns is relatively high but not high enough to ease competition to a large extent (i.e., $\rho _ { 2 } < \rho < \rho _ { 3 } )$ When $\rho$ is relatively high $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \rho > \rho _ { 2 } ) .$ , partnership with the data broker is the dominant strategy for both platforms because the data broker can help improve the targeting capabilities, and the increase in consumer privacy concerns can ease price competition. However, when both platforms partner with the data broker, their competitive advantage in the advertiser market weakens. Besides, unlike the case where platforms do not partner with the data broker, platforms must pay a data fee to the data broker. We show that when the increase in consumer privacy concerns is not very high $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \rho < \rho _ { 3 } )$ , the incremental revenue from the partnership cannot offset the date fee. Thus, both platforms receive a lower profit. This finding echoes a recent field study showing that although it is prevalent for platforms to partner with a data broker, doing so can harm the platforms’ profit when the third-party data cost is high (Neumann et al. 2019).

Furthermore, we find that the threshold $\rho _ { 3 }$ increases with the consumer side misfit cost t and the data price $\gamma .$ . This implies that the prisoner’s dilemma is more likely to happen when the competition on the consumer side is less intense and when platforms incur a higher data acquisition cost. The reason is that weaker competition on the consumer side intensifies the price competition on the advertiser side, and this adverse effect is more prominent when both platforms partner with the data broker. Therefore, although platforms currently are keen to partner with a data broker to improve their targeting capabilities, such a partnership may hurt them, especially when there is less competition on the consumer side, and the data broker has high bargaining power over the data price. Some industrial examples confirm the potential perils of partnering with the data broker. For instance, many publishers believe that partnering with Google Analytics 360 (Google’s data service) indeed will improve their competitive advantage in targeting capabilities, but their actual profits may decrease because Google often charges a high fee for data service because of its dominant position (CMA 2020).

## 4.4. Platforms’ Privacy Protection Strategies

The previous sections have examined how platformdata broker partnerships impact platform profits and social welfare outcomes. Faced with growing privacy concerns, platforms have started to take measures (e.g., restricting access, firewalls, and encryption protection) to protect consumers’ personal data and reduce their privacy concerns. Moreover, recent privacy regulations (e.g., GDPR, CCPA) also put pressure on firms to protect consumer privacy. In this section, we examine the platforms’ motivation for privacy protection when they both establish a partnership with the data broker. In particular, we investigate whether and when competing platforms have incentives to adopt high or low levels of privacy protection.

We assume platforms can choose to adopt a high (H) or low (L) level of privacy protection, denoted by $\phi _ { j }$ $\left( j \in \{ H , \ L \} \right)$ ). In total, there are four scenarios for platforms’ privacy protection strategies. Table 3 summarizes platforms’ decision matrix on privacy protection.

When platforms adopt privacy protection, the consumer utility can be rewritten as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} U _ {A} (x) = v _ {c} - \kappa (1 - \phi_ {j}) - (\alpha + \rho) (1 - \phi_ {j}) y _ {A} - t x, \\ U _ {B} (x) = v _ {c} - \kappa (1 - \phi_ {j}) - (\alpha + \rho) (1 - \phi_ {j}) y _ {B} - t (1 - x), \end{array}
$$

where platforms’ privacy protection could reduce consumers’ intrinsic privacy cost to $\kappa ( 1 - \phi _ { j } )$ and reduce consumers’ privacy cost toward targeted advertising to $( \alpha + \rho ) ( 1 - \phi _ { j } )$ . The following proposition summarizes the platforms’ equilibrium privacy protection strategies.

i. When $0 < \rho < \rho _ { 1 } ^ { * }$ , both platforms adopt a high level of privacy protection, that is, HH is the equilibrium.

ii. ## 5. Extensions

In previous sections, we investigated the economic impact of the partnership between competing platforms and the data broker. In this section, we extend our main model from the perspective of different players, including consumers, advertisers, platforms, and the data broker. In short, our analysis demonstrates that the main insights derived from the main model still hold qualitatively under these various model extensions. We present key insights below, and the detailed analysis is provided in Online Appendix C.

## 5.1. Consumer-Side Extensions

5.1.1. Platforms Charge Prices on the Consumer Side. In this section, we extend the model to consider the scenario where platforms also charge fees on the consumer side for their content. In the real world, this corresponds to the practice of some publisher websites (e.g., New York Times, The Economist) that charge consumers a subscription price.

Letting $r _ { A } \ \left( r _ { B } \right)$ denote the consumer prices charged by platform A(B), the consumer utility functions can be rewritten as: $U _ { A } ( \boldsymbol { x } ) = v _ { c } - \kappa - ( \alpha + \mathbb { I } _ { A } \rho ) y _ { A } - t \boldsymbol { x } - \boldsymbol { r } _ { A } ,$ $U _ { B } ( \boldsymbol { x } ) = v _ { c } - \kappa - ( \alpha + \mathbb { I } _ { B } \rho ) y _ { B } - t ( 1 - \boldsymbol { x } ) - r _ { B }$ . Platforms now can obtain profits from both the consumer side and the advertiser side, resulting in the following profit functions: $\pi _ { i } = ( r _ { i } - \mathbb { I } _ { i } \gamma ) x _ { i } + p _ { i } y _ { i }$ . Following the approach in Section 4, we can derive platforms’ equilibrium prices on both sides and their profits in each subgame. Below, we highlight some key insights about platforms’ pricing strategies.

First, we find that the consumer price decreases with the platforms’ ad targeting capabilities (β). As β increases, platforms have incentives to compete for consumers more aggressively to amplify the targeting value on the advertiser side, leading to a lower consumer price. This result implies that the improvement of targeting capabilities induced by the data broker can intensify price competition on the consumer side. Second, on the advertiser side, when consumer privacy concerns increase, platforms increase their ad price. The intuition is the same as the main model. By comparing the platform profits in each subgame, we find that the equilibrium partnership strategies remain qualitatively consistent with those in the main model.

Recall that in the main model, we find that consumers are worse off when platforms partner with the data broker. We reexamine the welfare implications in the following proposition.

Proposition 5. If platforms charge prices on both sides, then compared with the scenario where platforms do not partner with the data broker, both platforms partnering with the data broker will increase consumer surplus when $\begin{array} { r } { \delta > \frac { 2 \gamma + \rho + m \rho } { 2 ( 1 + m ) } . } \end{array}$

Proposition 5 indicates that when platforms charge prices from consumers, the partnership between platforms and the data broker can benefit consumers when the improvement in targeting capabilities brought by the data broker is relatively large. The driving force is that the improvement in targeting capabilities prompts platforms to reduce prices on the consumer side. Hence, although consumers suffer a privacy loss, they can receive a monetary benefit (i.e., a lower price) as compensation. This result highlights that the impact of platformdata broker partnerships on consumers depends on the business model of the platform. The takeaway for consumers is that platform-data broker partnerships may make them better off when they pay for the platforms content, which provides implications for consumer advocates who might consider pushing to prevent this practice. As for advertiser surplus and social welfare, we show the results are similar to those for the main model.

5.1.2. Consumers Have the Opt-out Option. In this extension, we investigate the scenario where consumers have the option to opt out of targeted ads served by the data broker. We analyze two cases: one where the consumers’ opt-out decision is exogenous, and another where the decision is endogenous, influenced by their misfit with the platform and their intrinsic privacy costs. Below, we present the analysis for the exogenous scenario, whereas the analysis for the endogenous scenario is detailed in Online Appendix A9. Overall, our analysis shows that considering consumers’ opt-out behavior does not affect the qualitative results derived in the main model.

For the exogenous scenario, following Chen and Stallaert (2014), we consider that there is a proportion of consumers choosing to opt out and another proportion of consumers opting in. Typically, when the increased privacy concern $( \rho )$ caused by the data broker is higher, more consumers will opt out (Fazlioglu 2023). For analytical tractability, we denote the proportion of opt-out consumers as $\mu \rho$ . The remaining $1 - \mu \rho$ consumers opt in. The parameter $\mu$ reflects the impact of privacy concerns on consumers’ opt-out decisions. A large $\mu$ corresponds to a large proportion of opt-out consumers because of privacy concerns.

For consumers who opt out, platforms can only provide a basic targeting capability for advertisers to target these consumers. Thus, in this case, the advertisers’ payoff functions change because their ability to target consumers decreases. Consider the YY case (where both platforms partner with the data broker) as an example. We can rewrite the advertisers’ payoff functions as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} W _ {A} (y) = v _ {\mathrm{s}} + (\beta + \delta) (1 - \mu \rho) x _ {\mathrm{Aa}} + \beta \mu \rho x _ {\mathrm{Ao}} - p _ {A} - \theta y, \\ W _ {B} (y) = v _ {\mathrm{s}} + (\beta + \delta) (1 - \mu \rho) (1 - x _ {\mathrm{Aa}}) + \beta \mu \rho (1 - x _ {\mathrm{Ao}}) \\ - p _ {B} - \theta (1 - y). \end{array}
$$

In $W _ { A } ( y )$ and $W _ { B } ( y ) .$ , the second (third) term represents the targeting value derived from consumers who opt in (out). For opt-out consumers, platforms offer basic targeting capabilities $\beta ,$ while for consumers who opt in, platforms offer enhanced targeting capabilities $\beta + \delta$ . Note that $( 1 - \mu \rho ) x _ { \mathrm { A a } }$ and $\mu \rho x _ { \mathrm { A o } }$ represent the opt-out consumers and opt-in consumers on platform $A ,$ respectively.

Our analysis shows that when considering consumers’ opt-out option, the platforms’ partnership strategies remain qualitatively valid; that is, when $\rho$ is relatively high, both platforms have incentives to partner with the data broker (Figure 8). Furthermore, we find that in the YY case, when $\mu$ is relatively small $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \mu \mathrm { ~ < ~ } \frac { ( 1 + m ) ( \beta + \delta ) - \gamma } { \alpha ( \delta + m \delta - \gamma ) + 2 ( ( 1 + m ) ( \beta + \delta ) - \gamma ) \rho } ) , } \end{array}$ platforms’ ad prices increase with $\rho ,$ which is consistent with the main model. Otherwise, when $\mu$ is relatively large, platforms ad prices decrease with $\rho .$ This result can be explained as follows. From the main model, we know that when $\rho$ increases, platforms have incentives to compete less aggressively on the advertiser side, leading to higher prices. We call this the competition-mitigation effect. Now, when considering the opt-out option, there is another force at play. Specifically, when $\rho$ increases, more consumers will opt out, which hurts platforms’ targeting value, thereby forcing platforms to reduce prices. We call this the opt-out effect. Our analysis shows that when $\mu$ is relatively small, the competition effect dominates, so our main model results remain valid. However, when $\mu$ is relatively large, the opt-out effect dominates, and thus platform prices decrease with $\rho .$

5.2.1. Advertisers’ Endogenous Multihoming Decisions. In this extension, we consider the case where this m proportion of advertisers endogenously determines their homing decision (i.e., single-homing or multihoming). The other 1 � m proportion of advertisers will single-home. An advertiser located at y obtains the following utility when multihoming:

$$
\begin{array}{c} W _ {A \& B} (y) = W _ {A} (y) + W _ {B} (y) = 2 v _ {s} + (\beta + \mathbb {I} _ {A} \delta) x _ {A} \\ + (\beta + \mathbb {I} _ {B} \delta) x _ {B} - p _ {A} - p _ {B} - \theta . \end{array}

Because of analytical intractability, we numerically analyze platforms’ partnership strategies. The result shows that when m is small (Figure 10(a)), our key insights derived from the main model still hold; that is, when increased privacy concerns are high, both platforms have incentives to partner with the data broker.

In contrast, when m is large, we find that the increased privacy concerns will hurt platform profits. The intuition is that when many advertisers multihome, the inherent competition intensity between the two platforms is weak (because platforms only compete for single-homing advertisers). Thus, the effect of partnering with the data broker on mitigating platform competition is limited. Meanwhile, when $\rho$ increases, platforms increase their prices, which makes some multihoming advertisers switch to joining only one platform. Consequently, both platforms’ advertiser demand decreases, and their profits decline. Therefore, when the increased privacy concern $\rho$ is high, platforms have less incentive to partner with the data broker (Figure 10(b)). This result provides an important insight that it is more suitable for platforms to partner with the data broker when platform competition for the advertising market is intense. On the contrary, when the platform competition for the advertising market is weak, platforms are better off not partnering with the data broker.

Overall, our analysis shows that incorporating multihoming advertisers into the model does not change our key results, provided that a certain degree of platform competition exists on the advertiser side. In this vein, our key insights that increased privacy concerns would incentivize platforms to partner with the data broker still hold qualitatively, because this partnership can mitigate platform competition.

5.2.3. Negative Same-Side Network Effects for Advertisers. In practice, as more advertisers join the platform, competition among them is likely to intensify, potentially reducing each advertiser’s payoff. This phenomenon is known as the negative same-side network effect. In this extension, we examine how this effect influences platforms’ partnership strategies.

Specifically, when incorporating this effect, advertisers’ payoff functions can be rewritten as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} W _ {A} (y) = v _ {s} + (\beta + \mathbb {I} _ {A} \delta) x _ {A} - \hat {p} _ {A} - \theta y - \omega y _ {A}, \\ W _ {B} (y) = v _ {s} + (\beta + \mathbb {I} _ {B} \delta) x _ {B} - \hat {p} _ {B} - \theta (1 - y) - \omega y _ {B}, \end{array}
$$

where the last term $\omega y _ { A }$ and $\omega y _ { B }$ denote the negative same-side network effect on the advertiser payoff, $\omega$ denotes the strength of the network effect, $y _ { i }$ is the expected number of advertisers on platform $i , i = A , B$

With this new setting, we obtain the following main results. First, we find that platforms have incentives to increase their ad prices in the presence of the negative same-side network effect. This occurs because platforms aim to control the number of advertisers on their platforms to mitigate the adverse effects of overcrowding. Consequently, platforms increase their ad prices in equilibrium, leading to less intense platform competition. Second, the result suggests that platforms’ partnership strategies are qualitatively consistent with the main model, as illustrated in Figure 12. Specifically, when consumers’ increased privacy concerns are high, both platforms have incentives to partner with the data broker. Third, as the intensity of the negative network effect increases, we numerically find that platforms have greater incentives to partner with data brokers. This is because relaxed competition allows platforms to leverage improved targeting capabilities to maximize their profits. As a result, the $\boldsymbol { Y } \boldsymbol { \hat { Y } }$ scenario, where both platforms partner with the data broker, is more likely to occur in equilibrium.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions

Motivated by the growing popularity of platform-data broker partnerships, this study builds a game-theoretic model to investigate the economic mechanisms underlying the partnership between platforms and data brokers in a two-sided market. Our model incorporates the impact of the platform-data broker partnership on both the advertiser and consumer sides. We contribute to the literature by considering the partnership with the data broker as a strategic variable for competing platforms and by studying how such partnerships interact with the platforms’ pricing strategies. We also examine the welfare implications of platform-data broker partnerships.

This study provides important implications for both research and practice. Our analysis reveals that the increase in consumer privacy concerns caused by the data broker may incentivize platforms to partner with the data broker. The driving force is that when consumer privacy concerns are high, platforms compete for advertisers less aggressively, resulting in high ad prices. This result implies that partnering with the data broker can not only improve targeting capabilities but also serve as a lever for platforms to ease price competition. Therefore, platform managers should be aware that growing privacy concerns may not always hinder platforms from partnering with data brokers.

Our findings offer practical implications for industries with different competition levels. Platforms in highly competitive consumer markets, such as social media platforms like Facebook and TikTok, should actively seek partnerships with data brokers to enhance ad targeting capabilities, allowing them to increase ad prices and offset the negative impact on consumer utility. In less competitive consumer markets, such as niche professional networks like LinkedIn, platforms may focus on improving user experience over extensive data broker partnerships. For platforms facing intense competition for advertisers, such as online search engines like Google and Bing, they should adopt asymmetric strategies, with one partnering with data brokers and the other not, to mitigate competition. By tailoring their strategies according to the intensity of competition on both the consumer and advertiser sides, platforms can optimize their operations, enhance user satisfaction, and achieve better performance.

Despite the growing popularity of partnerships between platforms and data brokers, we find that platforms can be in a prisoner’s dilemma situation when deciding whether to partner with the data broker. This situation is likely to occur when consumer privacy concerns are not extremely high, the data broker’s data analytics capability is low, or the data broker charges a high data price. In this case, each platform has an incentive to partner with the data broker, but by doing so, they are worse off than if neither platform partners with the data broker. This finding echoes a recent field study showing that, although partnering with the data broker is the dominant strategy for platforms, their profits may suffer because of a high third-party data cost and low targeting improvement enabled by the data broker (Neumann et al. 2019). Moreover, some industrial reports reveal that publishers indeed face a dilemma regarding the use of third-party data services. For instance, some publishers have realized that partnering with Google Analytics 360 (Google’s data service) can improve their competitive advantage in targeting capabilities, but their profits may decrease because Google charges a high data fee because of its market power. Therefore, our analysis suggests that platforms that intend to partner with the data broker must carefully the data broker’s potential impact on consumer privacy concerns and targeting capabilities.

From the consumer welfare perspective, our results provide important insights into the recent debate about regulating the data broker industry (Sherman 2020). Contrary to the common belief that the emerging data broker industry would hurt consumer surplus because of higher privacy costs, we find that a platform-data broker partnership may make consumers better off, especially when platforms adopt a mixed model of subscription-based and ad-sponsored revenue. The intuition is that partnering with the data broker intensifies the platforms’ price competition on the consumer side, leading to a lower consumer price. However, when platforms adopt a pure ad-sponsored model without charging consumers, the platform-data broker partnership does hurt consumers. This finding provides implications for policymakers by pointing out that the business model adopted by platforms plays a crucial role in the consumer welfare implications of platform-data broker partnerships.

Regarding advertiser surplus and social welfare, we find that the platform-data broker partnership can be a welfare-enhancing practice if it does not trigger strong concerns about personal privacy. Thus, policymakers should take measures to alleviate the consumers’ pri vacy concerns about the data broker, such as making the operations of the data broker more transparent. For example, obtaining consumers’ consent before using their data has been considered the first step toward regulating data brokers (Kraus 2020). Overall, our findings highlight the positive social value of platform-data broker partnerships in leveraging multiple data sources to improve consumer targeting.

Our finding also provides insights into platforms’ privacy protection strategies, highlighting that higher levels of privacy protection are not always beneficial in a competitive environment, particularly when consumers’ intrinsic privacy concerns are low. Consumers exhibit varying levels of sensitivity to different types of data across industries; for example, they are generally more sensitive about their healthcare and financial data than their news browsing or online shopping data. Therefore, platforms handling sensitive information should invest heavily in privacy protection. In contrast, platforms like news websites may optimally invest less in privacy protection. Additionally, it may be advantageous for platforms to adopt asymmetric privacy protection strategies, where one platform invests more heavily in privacy protection while another invests less, to differentiate themselves and cater to varying consumer concerns.

This study has several limitations that provide opportunities for future research. First, this study considers competition between two symmetric firms. Future studies could extend the model to incorporate heterogeneities between the two platforms. For instance, on the consumer side, the platforms’ intrinsic service value may differ. Also, consumers may have different privacy concerns levels about the two platforms. It is worthwhile to investigate how these heterogeneities affect platforms’ partnership strategies. Second, in our model, we assume that the platforms’ baseline targeting capabilities are exogenously given. Given the growing importance of targeting technology, platforms may endogenously determine their optimal investment level in targeting technology (e.g., data analytics level). Future research could investigate the interplay between investing in targeting technology and the platforms’ optimal partnership decisions. Finally, future studies could extend the model to consider the case where platforms partner with different data brokers. In this case, the partnership may bring different targeting benefits to platforms. We leave these interesting issues to future research.
