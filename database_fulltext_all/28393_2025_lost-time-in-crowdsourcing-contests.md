Abstract. Crowdsourcing contests have become increasingly popular as a means of seeking innovative solutions to business problems. However, the abundance of concurrent contests on platforms can lead to disparities in solvers’ contest awareness, resulting in “lost time”—the time elapsed between the start of a contest and a solver’s first visit. This study investigates how lost time influences solvers’ submission behavior and its subsequent impact on matching inefficacy as measured by solver disqualification, which occurs when a solver fails to produce a submission meeting the seeker’s baseline criteria. Utilizing a proprietary data set from a European crowdsourcing platform, we employ a mediation analy sis framework to examine the relationship between lost time and two key dimensions of submission behavior: time to first submission and number of submissions. Our findings reveal that higher levels of lost time are associated with fewer submissions, indicating reduced solver persistence. However, this negative effect is partially mitigated by solvers faster initial submissions when facing lost time. Importantly, we demonstrate that changes in submission behavior fully mediate the negative impact of lost time on solver disqualification, highlighting the crucial role of submission behavior in influencing solver disqualification. Furthermore, we provide evidence supporting latecomer disadvantage as an important mechanism driving solvers’ behavioral responses to lost time. Our study contributes to the crowdsourcing literature by uncovering the impact of lost time on solvers behavior and performance, offering valuable insights for contest design. By understanding and mitigating the effects of latecomer disadvantage, platforms can enhance the effectiveness of crowdsourcing in solving problems.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdsourcing, which harnesses the collective intelligence of a diverse pool of solvers through contests, has rapidly emerged as an innovative approach to problemsolving (Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Zhang et al. 2019). The continued popularity of crowdsourcing has led to a proliferation of online platforms hosting multiple concurrent contests at any given time, often requiring solvers to visit and review several open contests before participating (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010).

This visiting activity can create disparities among solvers regarding when they become aware of an open contest, which directly affects the time available to them to participate in the contest. To capture this heterogeneity in contest awareness, we formalize the concept of lost time, defined as the time elapsed between the start of a given contest and the solver’s first visit to that contest, relative to the contest duration. A higher lost time value indicates that the solver not only has limited time to work on the contest but also is at a competitive disadvantage compared with earlier participants. Despite documented evidence regarding the prevalence of lost time in practice (Kucherbaev et al. 2014, Mo et al. 2018) and the crucial role of solver heterogeneity in determining contest outcomes (Dechenaux et al. 2015), the impact of lost time on the dynamics of solver engagement in contests remains unexplored.

To overcome the competitive disadvantage arising from lost time (hereinafter latecomer disadvantage), sol vers may adjust their submission behavior through two key dimensions: their time to first submission and number of submissions (Liu et al. 2014, Bockstedt et al. 2016, Boudreau et al. 2016, Lemus and Marshall 2021, Deodha and Gupta 2023). Time to first submission refers to the duration between a solver’s initial visit and their firs submission. This aspect of submission behavior is significant as it influences the effort dedicated to the initia submission, which can serve as a reference point for subsequent submissions by the solver (Kagan et al. 2018, Bodas Freitas and Lawson 2019). However, when solvers face lost time, it remains unclear how they wil allocate their contest time. On one hand, solvers engage in a range of problem-solving activities, including problem definition, idea generation, evaluation, analysis, and implementation of selected ideas (Agrawal et al. 2019). Having more available time allows solvers to identify promising ideas and invest in their development, resulting in higher-quality solutions. On the other hand, since seeker preferences are often unknown, solvers can benefit from refining their solutions based on feedback received on earlier submissions (Bockstedt et al. 2016). For this reason, allocating less time to the initial submission may benefit solvers by allowing them to iteratively enhance their solutions.

Additionally, lost time can also affect solvers’ persistence in problem solving as represented by their number of submissions (Lemus and Marshall 2021). Specifically, latecomer disadvantage arising from lost time can influence the number of submissions in two opposing ways. On the one hand, solvers may feel less motivated and less engaged when perceiving a disadvantage due to lost time, leading to decreased persistence and fewer submitted solutions (Deodhar and Gupta 2023). On the other hand, the disadvantage of lost time can drive solvers to compete more intensely by submitting multiple solutions to increase the odds of a successful outcome (Berger and Pope 2011, Lemus and Marshall 2021).

Ultimately, these different responses of solvers to lost time, as represented by their submission behavior, can affect the efficacy of matching contest seekers with solvers who have the appropriate skills and expertise (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010). One measure we consider in examining matching efficacy in crowdsourcing contests is solver disqualification. Solver disqualification occurs when a solver fails to produce at least one submission meeting the baseline performance or qualification criteria set by a seeker, indicating an ineffective matching process by the contest platform. Despite its significance in evaluating the effectiveness of crowdsourcing, solver disqualification as an intermediate outcome measure is often overlooked in the literature (Koh and Cheung 2022). Given the above-highlighted theoretical ambiguity in predicting solvers’ response to lost time, this study attempts to examine the following research questions:

1. How does lost time influence solvers’ submission behavior in crowdsourcing contests, specifically their time to first submission and number of submissions?

2. To what extent do changes in submission behavior, driven by lost time, mediate the relationship between lost time and matching inefficacy, as measured by solver disqualification?

To answer these questions, we obtain proprietary data from an established European crowdsourcing platform that hosts contests for marketing and advertising solutions. The data set includes detailed contest visiting and participation data from 5,859 solvers across 588 contests, resulting in 50,394 solver-contest observations. Through a mediation analysis framework, we first examine the impact of lost time on the two key dimensions of submission behavior. We subsequently examine the effect of lost time on solver disqualification, with the two key dimensions of submission behavior as the mediators. After controlling for unobserved heterogeneity across contests and solvers and employing various model specifications, our findings reveal a negative relationship between lost time and number of submissions, indicating that solvers are less persistent in submitting more solutions when they face a high level of lost time. However, solvers facing lost time may also make their first submission more quickly, thus creating room for subsequent submissions and thereby partially mitigating the overall negative effect of lost time on the number of submissions. Additionally, the mediation analysis demonstrates that while the net effect of lost time on solver disqualification is negative, the effect is fully mediated through submission behavior with no direct observable link between lost time and solver disqualifi cation. This finding underscores the importance of influencing submission behavior to mitigate lost time’s overall impact on matching inefficacy.

To investigate the existence of latecomer disadvantage as a key mechanism behind solvers’ adjustments to their submission behavior, we carry out two additional checks. First, we address the possibility that lost time may be merely a proxy for time insufficiency. Employ ing a matching and subsampling approach, we demonstrate that with similar time availability, solvers with more lost time exhibit different submission behavio than those with less lost time, suggesting that the nega tive effects of lost time are not solely attributable to time insufficiency. Second, we demonstrate that the negative effects of lost time can be mitigated, or even reversed to positive, when solvers are granted additional contest time to alleviate their initial disadvantage. This finding is consistent with existing literature, which suggests that solvers are likely to respond negatively when they perceive a disadvantage to be difficult to overcome, whereas they respond more positively when they deem it surmountable.

Findings from our study have important theoretical implications in three key areas. First, given the prevalence of lost time in practice (Kucherbaev et al. 2014, Mo et al. 2018), it is surprising that prior studies have overlooked its impact on contest dynamics and outcomes. Although prior research has examined solvers’ performance and its correlation with heterogeneity between solvers (e.g., in terms of ability, Zhang et al. (2019); cultural background, Bockstedt et al. (2015); and experience, Bayus (2013)), we expand on this stream of research by showing how lost time, as another form of heterogeneity, affects contest matching inefficacy—specifically solver disqualification, an area that has received little attention in the literature (Koh and Cheung 2022). Importantly, we demonstrate that adjustments in submission behavior constitute the primary pathway through which lost time influences solver performance, highlighting the importance of studying submission behavior to observe how solvers respond to lost time. Second, our study makes an important theoretical contribution by isolating the effect of latecomer disadvantage from the impact of time insufficiency in crowdsourcing contests. Previous literature primarily focuses on time availability, showing that individuals problem-solving behavior is affected by time pressure (Zur and Breznitz 1981, Kagan et al. 2018) and that more creative solutions can be generated with adequate time (Chen et al. 2021, Korpeoglu et al. 2021). However, in crowdsourcing contests, contest time is not merely a constraint but also a competitive advantage. To the best of our knowledge, prior work has not differentiated between the effects of time insufficiency and the perceived disadvantage of being a latecomer. By disentangling these effects, we highlight the importance of latecomer disadvantage as a distinct mechanism influencing solvers’ behavior and performance. Third, our study contributes to the growing research on inefficacies in the matching process within crowdsourcing contests by focusing on solver disqualification as a performance metric. Drawing on the concept of market failure in two-sided markets (Rochet and Tirole 2003, Armstrong 2006), we conceptualize solver disqualification as a form of matching inefficacy in crowdsourcing contests. Our study demonstrates the role of lost time in influencing solver disqualification, thereby extending our knowledge of the factors that contribute to ineffective matching outcomes in crowdsourcing platforms.

Taken together, our study findings shed light on the significant impact of lost time on solver behavior and performance in crowdsourcing contests, contributing to a better understanding of the theoretical tension related to the submission behavior dimensions and the mechanisms influencing matching inefficacies in these platforms. By offering insights for seekers and platform managers on managing the adverse effects of lost time, we aim to help mitigate latecomer disadvantages and improve overall contest outcomes.

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1. Prior Literature

Our study builds on two interrelated streams of research on crowdsourcing contests. One stream focuses on solver characteristics (e.g., prior experience and ability) and examines their implications for problem solving in a contest (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010, Dissanayake et al. 2015, Zhang et al. 2019). The other stream focuses on contest characteristics (e.g., prize amount, contest duration, parallel contests) that can be managed by contest seekers, often in coordination with platform managers, and examines how these characteristics affect problem solving (Huang et al. 2012, Chen et al. 2021, Mo et al. 2021, Ko¨rpeog˘ lu et al. 2022).

As discussed by Dechenaux et al. (2015, p. 622), tournament theory suggests that solver heterogeneity typically has a negative effect in a contest because it may lead to a lower aggregate effort by solvers. When solvers are highly heterogeneous in their abilities or costs of effort, weaker solvers may be discouraged from exerting effort if they perceive a low probability of winning against stronger competitors. Building on this logic, the crowdsourcing literature has primarily focused on understanding the effect of heterogeneity in solver characteristics on the dynamics of contest participation and outcomes. For instance, Jeppesen and Lakhani (2010) use data collected from Innocentive, a science-focused innovation contest platform, to examine how solvers characteristics affect their chances of winning. They find that solvers offering a unique view of a problem based on expertise from a different field have a higher chance of winning. Relatedly, Huang et al. (2014) examine how solvers’ prior experience affects their participation, while Boudreau et al. (2016) investigate solvers responses to the number of participants in a contest. Meanwhile, Zhang et al. (2019) focus on ability heterogeneity among solvers, examining how solvers react when they see a superstar participant. Additionally, Liu et al. (2014) and Deodhar and Gupta (2023) demonstrate that solvers may be discouraged from participating when they observe high-quality solutions submitted by others before their own participation.

Our focus goes beyond previous studies, which have primarily examined the implications of solver characteristics and submission behavior for contest outcomes without explicitly considering the role of solver awareness in the process of contest engagement. We argue that solver awareness of open contests, as reflected by their visiting activity on the platform, represents a distinct type of heterogeneity that can significantly influence submission behavior and performance, beyond the effects of expertise, experience, and ability that have been the focus of prior work. Specifically, we propose that the extent of lost time—the elapsed time between a solver’s first visit to a contest and their submission— captures variations in solvers’ awareness and attention allocation that shape their participation decisions and outcomes.

Prior studies have identified various contest characteristics, such as prizes and contest duration, that can enhance crowdsourcing efficacy (Liu et al. 2014, Ales et al. 2017, Lee et al. 2018, Chen et al. 2021, Korpeoglu et al. 2021, Deodhar and Gupta 2023). Among these aspects, contest duration is particularly relevant to our study, as it is closely related to lost time. Research has shown that contest duration can have significant effects on solver participation and performance. For example, Chen et al. (2021) find that increased contest duration enhances overall solver participation but lowers the participation of high-ability solvers. Using a game-theoretic model, Korpeoglu et al. (2021) demonstrate that extended contest durations may drive more innovative solutions. Research in the related domain of online labor markets corroborates these findings, showing that auction duration in outsourcing platforms can affect labor participation decisions and lead to lower completion rates by online workers (Liang et al. 2022). The collective evidence from these studies suggests that contest timing can influence solvers’ expected payoffs and, consequently, their participation behavior. We extend this line of work by introducing the concept of lost time as a latecomer disadvantage rather than a proxy for time insufficiency. We examine how differences in contest entry time affect solvers’ submission behavior and, subsequently, matching efficacy. By revealing that the effect of lost time on contest performance is fully mediated by submission behavior, our mediation analysis provides a nuanced understanding of the mechanisms through which contest timing influences solver disqualification as a matching outcome. Furthermore, our study diverges from prior research, which often focuses on the final outcomes of contests—that is, whether a given solver won a given contest or not (Boudreau et al. 2011, Bockstedt et al. 2016, Lee et al. 2018)—by instead emphasizing the importance of solver disqualification as a crucial indicator of platform matching efficacy. Because a key goal of crowdsourcing platforms is to match contest seekers with solvers who possess the necessary skills and expertise (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010), the effectiveness of this matching process is crucial to platform success (Liu et al. 2014, Koh and Cheung 2022). By examining the factors that influence solver disqualification, our approach offers insights into how platforms can enhance their matching efficacy.

## 2.2. Lost Time as a Latecomer Disadvantage: Effects on Submission Behavior

Time availability is a fundamental consideration in problem solving (Kagan et al. 2018, Korpeoglu et al. 2021), as it sets an upper boundary on the effort individuals can expend during problem solving and how much individuals can learn from and improve upon their initial attempts. Solvers who visit a contest after its starting date face two primary challenges associated with lost time. The first of these is time insufficiency, referring to the lack of adequate time to complete a task within the limited timeframe allocated for it, as mentioned earlier. The second challenge is the latecomer disadvantage, which occurs when solvers perceive themselves as being at a competitive disadvantage compared with early participants due to having less time to understand the seeker’s preferences and work iteratively on developing solutions, potentially affecting their motivation to engage in the competition.

Although both time insufficiency and latecomer disadvantage are expected to influence solvers’ submission behavior, our study emphasizes the latter due to its more complex implications. If time insufficiency were the only factor, a straightforward solution for seekers or the platform would be to extend the contest duration, thereby ensuring that all solvers have enough time to complete their tasks despite lost time. However, the dynamics change when latecomer disadvantage comes into play: As the crowdsourcing literature (Boudreau et al. 2016, Lemus and Marshall 2021) indicates, solvers submission behavior can be significantly influenced by their perceived competitive disadvantage in a contest. More importantly, latecomer disadvantage is not alleviated by the contest duration—latecomers still perceive themselves as disadvantaged relative to early participants, even in longer contests.

Therefore, our study focuses on how lost time might be perceived as a latecomer disadvantage, subsequently affecting solver submission behavior and solver disqualification. The notion of lost time is especially relevant in our research context, which is a blind contest setting where solvers cannot observe others’ submissions. In this setting, latecomers lacking the strategic advantage of viewing others’ submissions (Bockstedt et al. 2016) may primarily rely on the lost time information for their decision making. In subsequent sections, we examine how lost time, viewed as latecomer disadvantage, influences the dynamics of solver participation in a contest by analyzing two dimensions of submission behavior: the time to the first submission and the num ber of submissions.

2.2.1. Lost Time Effect on Time to First Submission. The timing of an initial submission can significantly influence subsequent solutions (Kagan et al. 2018, Bodas Freitas and Lawson 2019). The role of time to first submission is of particular interest, as it can have opposing effects. On one hand, solvers engage in multiple problem-solving activities during contests, such as problem definition, idea generation, idea evaluation, in-depth analysis, and implementation of chosen ideas (Jiang et al. 2021, Koh and Cheung 2022). As such, a longer timeframe enables solvers to better identify promising ideas that align with the problem context and selectively advance their development (Jiang et al. 2021). Thus, investing more time in the initial submission can increase the likelihood that the submission will meet the seeker’s preferences. On the other hand, solvers frequently participate without a precise understanding of the seeker’s preferences. For this reason, feedback received during the submission process can offer invaluable insights into the gap between the solver’s solution and the seeker’s expectations, facilitating revisions of the initial solution. Therefore, a faster initia submission can facilitate an iterative improvement process, ultimately leading to a solution that more closely matches the seeker’s needs.

In situations where solvers face a latecomer disadvantage due to lost time, the preferred strategy remains unclear. When dealing with greater amounts of lost time, solvers might adopt a “solution crafting” strategy that entails devoting a significant portion of their time to creating a comprehensive first submission, especially if they anticipate a lack of time for extensive revisions later. The quality of the first solution during ideation is crucial, as it sets the foundation for the entire problemsolving process (Kagan et al. 2018). When solvers face a latecomer disadvantage, they may be more inclined to limit their interactions with the contest environment and focus intently on crafting a high-quality initial solution. This approach can help minimize distractions and enable solvers to immerse themselves in the problemsolving process, leveraging their expertise and creativity without external interference.

However, although this focused approach may seem efficient, spending extra time on the initial solution is also risky due to unclear seeker preferences, and this risk could be further exacerbated by the latecomer disadvantage (Becker and Huselid 1992). Without a clear understanding of the seeker’s expectations, solvers may invest a significant amount of time and effort into creating a solution that ultimately fails to align with the seeker’s needs, resulting in a higher likelihood of solver disqualification. This risk is particularly pronounced for latecomers, as they have less time to course-correct and adapt their solutions based on feedback.

Because an excessive focus on the initial solution could limit the opportunity to receive timely feedback regarding the seeker’s preferences, thus increasing the risk of disqualification, solvers might instead opt for an early submission to obtain such feedback, allowing them to gauge the alignment of their solution with the seeker’s expectations and decide whether further investment is warranted. Unlike the solution crafting strategy, the benefits of this early submission approach do not necessarily diminish for latecomers. If the required revisions are significantly more extensive than they expected, solvers can choose to cease participation to avoid further time wastage; thus, making an early initial submission can serve as a strategic lever for latecomers, enabling them to offset potential disadvantages they might otherwise encounter.

Ultimately, whether solvers opt for an early submission depends on their individual strategic inclinations and the perceived tradeoffs concerning contest participation. Latecomers must carefully balance the benefits of focusing on a high-quality initial solution with the potential risks associated with unclear seeker preferences and the advantages of early submission for obtaining valuable feedback. Investigating how solvers adjust their time to first submission in response to lost time—and how this adjustment, in turn, influences contest outcomes—can help shed light on the impact of lost time on solver performance.

2.2.2. Lost Time Effect on Number of Submissions. Many crowdsourcing platforms offer solvers the opportunity to submit multiple entries into a contest beyond their initial submission, highlighting another crucial facet of submission behavior—the number of submissions. The number of submissions reflects the solver’s persistence and intention to craft new solutions. Generating additional submissions enables solvers to revisit and reevaluate their existing problem-solving strategies, revise their initial understanding of the problem, and adapt their problem-solving approach to generate alternative solutions that better align with their revised understanding. Solvers may need to leverage input from the seeker and experiment with new ideas to craft one or more additional submissions that are distinct from the initial submission (Wooten and Ulrich 2017, Sanyal and Ye 2023). Thus, by making additional submissions, solvers can enhance their competitiveness in the contest and reduce their likelihood of disqualification (Lemus and Marshall 2021).

This study postulates that lost time affects not only how solvers allocate their time but also their number of submissions. Although the impact of lost time on submission behavior remains unexplored, researchers have studied how the number of submissions changes in response to the competitive landscape. Specifically, the literature suggests that solvers change the number of submissions as a reaction to the competitive environment and that this reaction can be either negative or positive. On the negative side, recent studies demonstrate that solvers may become less productive and engaged when they perceive themselves to be at a disadvantage (Liu et al. 2014, Bockstedt et al. 2022, Deodhar and Gupta 2023), leading to fewer submissions. Solvers who realize that their chance of winning is slim due to the presence of top performers/solutions may become demotivated and limit their interaction with the contest, thus restricting their opportunities for refinement and resubmissions. Similarly, if the latecomer disadvantage due to lost time demotivates solvers, they will be less likely than early participants to revisit their solutions, thus lowering their likelihood of refinement and resubmissions.

However, a contest disadvantage might not always negatively impact solvers’ motivation; instead, it could spur them to enhance their competitiveness. For instance, both Boudreau et al. (2016) and Lemus and Marshall (2021) found that information about the sol ver’s competitive disadvantage can have a positive effect. Although low-ability solvers may respond negatively by investing less effort in the contest, high-ability solvers may respond positively by exerting additional effort to maintain their high chances of winning. Furthermore, the time pressure from lost time can motivate solvers to compete more rigorously within a competitive setting (Malhotra 2010). That is, solvers may strive to maintain their competitive edge by submitting more solutions, ensuring that the seekers fully consider their ideas.

The literature does not clearly delineate how solvers perceive and react to the latecomer disadvantage—that is, whether they are more proactive in or more discouraged from making additional submissions. Often, researchers focus on broader aspects of crowdsourcing contests, such as incentive structures (Ales et al. 2017) or contest platform designs (Mo et al. 2018, Ko¨rpeog˘ lu et al. 2022). However, a fundamental concern within the crowdsourcing platform is how solvers perceive time as a resource and adjust their level of engagement with the contest accordingly. Therefore, the theoretical tensions surrounding the effect of lost time on the number of submissions and its subsequent outcome need further examination.

## 3. Data

## 3.1. Platform Background

We analyze data from a well-established European online platform hosting crowdsourcing contests for advertising and creative content. The platform has been in operation for more than a decade and has a user base exceeding 500,000 users from 192 countries. Clients range from nonprofits to Fortune 500 companies, and the platform collaborates with them to set contest parameters like prize amount and duration. Solvers participate by browsing contests and submitting solutions, which can range from generating advertising ideas to creating graphics and videos.<sup>1</sup>

To minimize the evaluation workload of the seekers and maximize the effectiveness of the contests, the platform has a team of platform managers responsible for overseeing solver participation and providing feedback and evaluation. Each instance of solver participation in a contest is randomly assigned to a manager, meaning that a single contest can be facilitated by multiple managers. With the assigned manager, each submission goes through a two-step evaluation process: manager evaluation and seeker evaluation. During the manager evaluation phase, managers, after communicating with the seekers, screen out solutions that do not meet the baseline performance criteria set by the seeker and provide private feedback to guide solvers through the participation process. This feedback is given within a timeframe ranging from a few hours to two days after the submission. If a solution requires significant modification, a grace period may be granted at the discretion of the manager and seeker, allowing the solver extra time to work on the solution. Solvers can also submit additional solutions to address the feedback or offer variants of their ideas.

After the contest is over, the qualified submissions proceed to the seeker evaluation phase, where the solutions labeled as qualified are evaluated as candidates for the final winners. The chosen winners then transfer the intellectual property of their idea to the seeker in exchange for the agreed-upon prize. To date, the platform has awarded more than e9 million in prize money across nearly 1,500 contests.

For this study, we obtained proprietary data on visiting and participation activity<sup>2</sup> from 588 crowdsourcing contests hosted on the platform between January 2013 and March 2017, involving 5,859 solvers and 50,394 observations. These contests are blind in that a participating solver cannot observe any submission information of other participating solvers or their identities. In addition, a given contest homepage also does not show the current number of participating solvers or the number of submissions received during the contest. The average contest duration in our data set was 23 days, with an average of 86 solvers and 147 submissions per contest. Regarding the prize structure, the total average prize amount per contest was e8,259.95, and the average number of prizes per contest was 3.63. Finally, regard ing the platform environment, the number of ongoing contests that overlapped with a focal contest ranged from 1 to 23, with an average of 11.

Our data set includes the details of completed contests and participating solvers. In addition, the data set includes timestamp information on the solvers’ first visit to the contest homepage, as well as when they entered their contest submissions. The availability of timestamp information regarding contest homepage visits and submissions is a unique feature of our data set. Regardless of whether a solver submitted solutions to a contest, the timestamp of the solver’s first visit to the contest homepage is recorded by the platform. Our data set includes timestamp information on 307,638 visits to contest homepages, of which 50,394 visits (i.e., 16%) resulted in at least one submission (i.e., contest participation). Therefore, the sample of 50,394 visits serves as the primary sample for our main analysis.

To accurately interpret the effects of lost time, we need to tackle two key empirical issues: selection bias connected with contest participation and the endogeneity of lost time. Selection bias stems from the fact that solvers may opt to join a contest selectively, allowing us only to observe the submission behavior and outcomes of solvers who participate. In contrast, the endogeneity of lost time arises from variables omitted from Models (1) and (2) that could causally influence both the outcome and an independent variable simultaneously, thus introducing bias into the estimates of the effect of the independent variable (Wooldridge 2002).

To correct for selection bias, we employ a two-step procedure suggested by Heckman (1979). Initially, we estimate a probit model to predict a solver’s likelihood of submitting a solution after visiting a contest. In addition to the independent variables $Z _ { i j }$ and LostTime , we include the number of ongoing contests (NumberofOngoingContests ) and the average lost time associated with all ongoing contests (LostTimeOngoingContests<sub>ij</sub>) at the solver’s first visit to a focal contest. These variables account for the competition among contests on the platform during the solver’s visit—A factor previously highlighted by Korpeuglu et al. (2021). We argue that these competition variables are likely to influence whether solvers are able to visit and join a focal contest but are less likely to directly affect their performance in the focal contest, thereby addressing the exclusion restriction.

Additionally, we also consider the leniency of the platform manager assigned to a solver in their previous contest as a factor affecting participation. On the studied platform, each solver is randomly assigned a contest manager who initially evaluates their entries before they are reviewed by the seekers. As discussed in Section 3 (and later Section 5.2), these managers may also give solvers the opportunity to revise their solutions by granting extra time, thereby motivating further refinement. Given that solvers’ motivation to participate in future contests can be influenced by their prior experience (Franke et al. 2013, Huang et al. 2014), we conjecture that a solver’s experience with a “lenient” manager (i.e., one who allowed extra time) in a previous contest could increase their willingness to engage in a subsequent contest. We therefore include a measure, ManagerLenien-$c y _ { i j - 1 } ,$ , in the model predicting solver participation.<sup>5</sup> Given the random assignment of managers to solvers in a contest, the measure of manager leniency is likely to demonstrate exogenous variation and satisfy the exclusion restriction assumption. The first-step probit model results are presented in Online Appendix 5. We use the predicted probabilities from this model to calculate the inverse Mills ratio $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { i j } } )$ , which is included in Models (1) and (2), to assess the impact of lost time on submission behavior measures.

To address the endogeneity of the lost time measure, we use the instrumental variable (IV) approach to estimate our models. We seek an instrument that will satisfy the assumptions of instrument relevance (i.e., the instrument is correlated with lost time) and instrument exogeneity (i.e., the instrument is unlikely to be correlated with unobserved factors that may affect submission behavior). We focus on using a solver’s pattern of prior visiting activity on the platform as a source of exogenous variation in lost time. If solvers visit a contest at a given time, they are more likely to be aware of newly available contests or of contests that are about to begin. Therefore, solvers’ visiting activity on the platform prior to the start of a focal contest may indicate how quickly they became aware of a focal contest, affecting their lost time in the contest. However, prior visiting activity is less likely to be correlated with specific characteristics of a focal (future) contest or with the submission behavior of the solver in the focal contest. This is because the starting date for a focal contest is separately determined by the platform and the seeker, independent of the submission behavior or prior visiting activity of any specific solver. Thus, solvers’ prior visiting activity represents a pattern of their prior engagement on the platform, which is likely determined independent of the focal contest.<sup>6</sup>

We construct two measures to represent a solver’s prior visiting activity on the platform: visiting frequency, defined as the number of contest visits in the last four weeks<sup>7</sup> before the starting date of the focal contest, and prior visiting time difference, which is the time difference between the starting date of the focal contest and the last visit date to another contest prior to the starting date of the focal contest. As the visiting frequency of a solver in a timeframe that is close to the start of a focal contest increases, the solver is more likely to become aware of the platform environment and the contests that are scheduled to begin, thereby reducing the extent of lost time associated with the focal contest.<sup>8</sup> With regard to prior visiting time difference, a solver’s last visit date to a contest prior to the starting date of the focal contest indicates the recentness of the solver’s engagement on the platform. Thus, the more distant a solver’s last contest visit is in time from the focal contest’s starting time, the less likely it is that the solver is aware of the focal contest, thereby increasing the potential for lost time in the focal contest.

In addition to the conceptual justification of the instruments, we perform statistical tests to check their validity, which are detailed in Online Appendix 8. These tests—including the Lagrange multiplier test for underidentification, the F-statistic for joint significance of the coefficients of the instrumental variables, and the overidentification test—collectively support the validity of the instruments. Nonetheless, in Section 6, we conduct a further check related to instrumental variables and also use an instrument-free identification approach to assess the robustness of our results.

## 5. Additional Analyses

We have already shown how lost time impacts solver performance by affecting submission behavior. This section delves deeper into the role of latecomer disadvantage as a mechanism behind this impact. First, we explore the alternative explanation that lost time is merely a proxy for time insufficiency. We do so by studying whether the lost time effect remains even when solvers have ample time or similar time availability, using a subsample and matching approach. Second, we explore whether granting solvers extra time $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \mathrm { a }$ grace period) lessens or changes the direction of the lost time effect. Third, we perform a detailed analysis at the submission level to understand how lost time influences solver persistence.

## 5.1. Beyond Time Insufficiency: Isolating the Latecomer Disadvantage

Although we propose that lost time can induce the latecomer disadvantage, one alternative explanation for our findings is that lost time may merely proxy for time insufficiency; that is, lost time simply reduces the available time for solvers to participate in a contest. To further explore this alternative explanation, we compare two scenarios:

Scenario A: A solver participates in a 10-day contest with 50% lost time (i.e., 5 days remaining).

Scenario B: A solver participates in a 20-day contest with 75% lost time (i.e., 5 days remaining).

If lost time acts solely as a proxy for time insufficiency, then, holding all else constant, the effect of lost time on submission behavior should be similar when solvers have the same amount of time remaining across the two scenarios. In other words, a solver in Scenario A would exhibit a similar time to first submission and number of submissions as a solver in Scenario B. However, if the lost time effect reflects factors beyond time insufficiency, we would expect the solver in Scenario B to show a shorter time to first submission and fewer submissions despite having the same time remaining.

To account for time insufficiency in our analysis, we include two additional control variables in our analysis. The first variable, TimeRemaining , captures the degree of time insufficiency using the number of days remaining after joining the contest. This variable, however, does not account for the task complexity. Although we have already controlled for the contest fixed effect to capture time-invariant contests factors (e.g., task complexity and contest duration), to more precisely tease out the effect of time insufficiency from lost time we create a binary variable for time sufficiency (TimeSufficien-$t _ { i j } ) .$ , indicating whether solver i’s time remaining after visiting contest j is at least as much as the average submission time for this contest among solvers who do not face any lost time.<sup>11</sup>

To further ensure comparable task complexity among solvers, we employ a coarsened exact matching (CEM) approach to create a matched sample. This analysis, discussed in detail in Online Appendix 9, matches solvers with similar task complexity, thus minimizing the possibility that observed effects are driven solely by time constraints.

Table 4 demonstrates that the effect of lost time persists even when accounting for the possibility of time insufficiency as an alternative explanation on the matched sam ple. Specifically, columns (1) and (3) include analyses on the matched sample using time remaining and time sufficiency as control variables, whereas columns (2) and (4) focus only on the subsample of solvers with sufficient time (i.e., TimeSufficient<sub>ij</sub> � 1). All results indicate that our findings remain consistent, confirming the persistence of the lost time effect even when the impact of time insufficiency is minimized.

## 5.2. Mitigating the Negative Impact of Lost Time: Role of a Grace Period

To examine how the lost time effect due to latecomer disadvantage can be alleviated, we conducted an analysis that incorporates the concept of a “grace period” offered by platform managers during contests. Specifically, after receiving a solution from a solver, platform managers have several options: they can provide feedback to the solvers, approve or disqualify the solution, or grant a “grace period” with the seeker’s approval. This grace period gives solvers who have submitted solutions that require further refinement additional time beyond the original contest duration to better align their solutions to the seeker’s requirements.<sup>13</sup>

To test whether the provision of a grace period can alleviate the negative lost time effect, we analyze its moderating impact. Specifically, in addition to the key variables used in the main models (Models (2) and (3)), we include the variable Grace that captures whether solver i is granted a grace period in contest j, along with its interaction term with $L o s t T i m e _ { i j }$ to examine the potential alleviation of the lost time effect. Because the grace period is only granted on our platform after solvers have submitted at least one solution, we adjust one of the dependent variables. Specifically, instead of using the time to first submission, which is not impacted by the grace period, we utilize the average time spent on subsequent solutions (SubsequentSubmissionTime<sub>ij</sub>) as a measure of how quickly solvers submitted additional solutions after the first solution. This adjustment allows us to capture the lost time effect on the time spent on subsequent submissions. To that end, Model (4) explores the effect of lost time on the number of submissions, whereas Model (5) investigates the effect on the average time spent on subsequent submissions.

## 6. Robustness Checks

Although we have presented conceptual arguments and empirical tests for the instruments used to account for the endogeneity of lost time, we want to further mitigate the risk of unobservable time-varying factors affecting our analysis. Specifically, one assumption about the instruments, which represent the prior visiting activity of a solver on the platform, is that such activity is not affected by unobserved time-varying confounders associated with the solver (e.g., free time available to a solver). We relax this assumption in additional analyses, which are available in Online Appendix 12, and the results continue to demonstrate the consistency of our main findings. Additionally, we employ an instrument-free identification method, namely the Gaussian copula approach (Park and Gupta 2012), which models the correlation between the endogenous variable and the error term. This approach is elaborated in Online Appendix 13, and the results show signs, significance levels, and coefficient sizes consistent with our main analysis. To further strengthen our estimation, we employ several additional measures. Specifically, we utilize a more detailed, hourly level of lost time as an alternative metric in Online Appendix 14; address the nonlinearity in our dependent variable, the number of submissions, in Online Appendix 15; and mitigate censoring bias in the time to first submission using a Tobit model in Online Appendix 16. We also consider the binary nature of solver disqualification and employ alternative measures for contest outcomes, such as the solution disqualification rate and the likelihood of winning, in Online Appendix 17. The consistency of results across all these analyses reaffirms the robustness of our main findings.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion

This study examines the largely overlooked issue of lost time as a latecomer disadvantage in crowdsourcing contests. Our analysis reveals significant relationships among lost time, submission behavior, and the likelihood of solver disqualification in a contest. Specifically, our findings demonstrate that solvers who join a contest later tend to submit their solutions more quickly, thereby reducing the time to first submission. This reduction in time to first submission suggests a shift in strategy: Rather than spending extensive time crafting a polished solution from the outset, late-joining participants appear to prioritize getting an initial submission in quickly, likely focusing on subsequent iterative refinements to improve their entry. However, the accelerated initial submission does not sufficiently compensate for the negative impact of the lost time, as these solvers still show fewer subsequent submissions and an increased likelihood of disqualification. Through subsample and matching analysis, we explore the role of latecomer disadvantage as a mechanism behind the effects of lost time. When we compare solvers with high vs. low levels of lost time, the adverse effects persist for the former group even when they have similar time availability to the latter. This finding indicates that the negative impact of lost time is not simply a result of contest time insufficiency and that solvers with higher levels of lost time face a significant competitive disadvantage that affects their submission behavior as well as the likelihood of their disqualification.

Collectively, the findings from our study have important implications for research on crowdsourcing. First, building on Dechenaux et al. (2015), who recognized that greater heterogeneity among players leads to lower aggregate effort in contests, we emphasize the importance of understanding solver heterogeneity and its role in solvers’ performance for researchers proposing potential remedies. Previous studies have examined how solvers’ performance is affected by various types of heterogeneity, such as ability (Zhang et al. 2019), cultural background (Bockstedt et al. 2015), and experience (Bayus 2013). Our study builds on this stream of work, responding to recent calls for more research into crowdsourcing platforms and heterogeneity in lost time (Mo et al. 2018, Chen et al. 2020). In contrast to prior studies (Huang et al. 2014, Zhang et al. 2019) that ignored heterogeneity among solvers in terms of lost time, our study demonstrates that such heterogeneity cannot only create differences in submission behavior among solvers but also affect the extent to which solvers are able to submit one or more solutions that may meet or exceed the baseline performance criteria set by the seeker and the platform manager. Thus, at a broader level, our focus on lost time allows us to unpack the dynamics of contest participation and understand how visiting activity on a contest page prior to participation can explain solvers’ behavior in the contest. Additionally, our findings shed light on how visiting activity influences the effectiveness of solvers’ problem-solving efforts in the contest.

The second contribution of our study stems from our empirical examination identifying latecomer disadvantage as a mechanism underlying the effects of lost time on submission behavior. While the crowdsourcing literature has noted that solvers might be discouraged by competitive disadvantage due to the competitive environment—such as when they observe a high quality solution submitted by a competitor (Liu et al. 2014, Deodhar and Gupta 2023)—to our knowledge no study has examined the disadvantage related to lost time. Our work contributes to the literature by showing that latecomer disadvantage is one critical mechanism behind the lost time effect. We show this disadvantage in two steps. First, we show that the lost time effect still persists even after accounting for time insufficiency, suggesting that the influence of lost time on solvers submission behavior extends beyond the lack of avail able contest time. Second, by examining the grace period policy implemented by the studied platform, we find that the negative lost time effect on the number of submissions can turn positive when solvers are given extra time (i.e., a grace period). This finding aligns with existing crowdsourcing literature (Boudreau et al. 2016, Lemus and Marshall 2021) indicating that solvers can respond positively to a contest disadvantage if they perceive it as surmountable.

Our study’s third contribution lies in our focus on solver disqualification as a performance metric, through which we add to the growing research on inefficacies in the matching process within crowdsourcing contests (Liu et al. 2014, Koh and Cheung 2022). This focus diverges from prior studies that primarily examine solvers’ final performance outcomes, such as winning (Boudreau et al. 2011, Bockstedt et al. 2016, Lee et al. 2018). Drawing from the concept of market failure in two-sided markets (Rochet and Tirole 2003, Armstrong 2006), we conceptualize solver disqualification as a form of matching inefficacy in crowdsourcing contests. In two-sided markets, inefficacies can arise due to information asymmetry, search costs, or externalities (Rysman 2009). In crowdsourcing contests, late-joining solvers may face information asymmetry regarding problem requirements or solution quality, leading to a higher likelihood of disqualification (Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Boudreau et al. 2011). Moreover, search costs associated with finding a suitable contest may increase as time passes, further contributing to the risk of disqualification (Mo et al. 2018, Koh and Cheung 2022). Our study demonstrates the significant role of lost time in influencing solver disqualification, highlighting the temporal dimension of matching inefficacy in crowdsourcing contests. Interestingly, our mediation analysis reveals that lost time primarily impacts solvers through changes in submission behavior rather than by having a substantial direct effect on disqualification. This finding underscores the importance of understanding lost time’s indirect effects on solver performance through submission behavior, drawing attention to the behavioral mechanisms that contribute to matching inefficacies (Liu et al. 2014, Bockstedt et al. 2016).

From a practical perspective, our study underscores the importance of strategically designing contests, as lost time significantly influences not only contest participation but also submission behavior and, consequently, contest outcomes. Contest design parameters are typically established through consultations between seekers and platform managers on crowdsourcing platforms. As such, it is crucial for both seekers and platform managers to understand how contest design choices impact solvers’ perception of lost time. For instance, because lost time is influenced by the contests that are concurrently available, platform managers and seekers may need to diligently balance the effectiveness of each contest by minimizing the overall lost time through contest scheduling.

Given that lost time cannot be completely eliminated, our findings suggest that focusing on strategies to modify submission behavior may be more effective than solely attempting to reduce lost time. Platform managers could explore and implement various incentive structures and feedback mechanisms to encourage early and consistent submissions. This approach could include developing analytics tools to track and analyze submission patterns. The crowdsourcing literature has suggested that incentive structures (Jiang and Wang 2020) and feedback mechanisms (Lemus and Marshall 2021) play important roles in altering submission behavior. Seekers can contribute to this effort by designing contest structures that incentivize early submissions, such as early bird bonuses, and by providing regular, constructive feedback to maintain solver engagement throughout the contest.

Lastly, our findings highlight the complexity involved in mitigating the effects of lost time. While extending contest duration can minimize time insufficiency, it does not necessarily eliminate the latecomer disadvantage that arises from inherent differences in lost time among sol vers within a crowdsourcing contest. Seekers and platform managers can alleviate the effects of lost time by addressing the latecomer disadvantage, and our research demonstrates that offering a grace period to solvers can be an effective way to do so. Although solvers may not anticipate this extension initially, providing a grace period to solvers who face the latecomer disadvantage and are seeking additional time to refine or develop thei solutions can ultimately help seekers maximize their yield of qualified solutions.

Our work is subject to some limitations, which can also serve as opportunities for future research. While our paper primarily investigates lost time’s impact on submission behavior and outcomes within a contest, it does not examine the dynamics of cross-contest competition on crowdsourcing platforms. Crowdsourcing platforms share similarities with online auction platforms like eBay, where multiple contests or products simultaneously compete for users’ attention and participation (Simonsohn 2010).<sup>15</sup> Building on prior work on auctions, future research could explore how the presence and characteristics of concurrent contests affect solver behavior and outcomes, providing valuable insights into the broader competitive landscape of crowdsourcing. Furthermore, our study does not investigate the antecedents of lost time, such as geographical, region-specific, or socio-cultural heterogeneity among solvers. For example, cultural differences in holiday schedules (e.g., Christmas in Western countries and Lunar New Year in East Asian countries) may influence solver engagement and lost time. Exploring these antecedents can provide insights into how to create effective contest designs that alleviate lost time and enhance seekers’ chances of success in crowdsourcing by capturing solvers’ attention and participation.

Additionally, although our study focuses on blind contests, which do not allow solvers to observe any contest information, future research could investigate the generalizability of our findings to other types of crowdsourcing platforms with different characteristics, such as nonblind contests, varying screening processes, and diverse reward structures. Finally, our unique context includes the involvement of platform managers, who may not perfectly reflect the seekers’ preferences. Although we expect the managers’ impact to be minimal because they evaluate submissions based on baseline performance criteria set by the seeker independent of when solutions are submitted, we encourage researchers to further investigate the effects of lost time in scenarios where seekers directly evaluate the solutions. By addressing these limitations and exploring the suggested avenues, future research can develop a more comprehensive understanding of the role of lost time in crowdsourcing and its implications for contest design and solver behavior across diverse platform contexts, considering both within-contest and cross-contest dynamics.
