# To Split or to Merge? How Partitioning Affects Consumption and Engagement with Digital Content

Abstract. Despite the rising popularity of serialized digital content on online platforms, authors and publishers currently lack a comprehensive understanding of the economic implications associated with content partitioning. This research investigated how content partitioning affects the consumption patterns, engagement activities, and subsequent economic behavior of consumers in the context of serialized e-books. Identical e-book titles were partitioned into two formats: small partitioning (SP), where extended narratives are split into numerous short episodes per installment, and large partitioning (LP), where stories are divided into a limited number of episodes, each delivered through more extensive storytelling. Drawing on the literature on resource partitioning and cognitive processing, we formulated hypotheses exploring how these partitioning structures influence consumption quantity (i.e., the total number of words read) and progression rate (i.e., how far a consumer progresses into an entire serialized book). We then assessed how content characteristics moderate the relationship between partitioning structures and those consumption patterns. Finally, attention was directed toward how partitioning structures influence engagement activities, such as consumption intensity (i.e., the use of textual annotations and highlights), review characteristics (i.e., submission, length, informativeness, and valence), and subsequent purchase behavior. For empirical validations, we col laborated with a partner company to develop a consumption-tracing scheme, which keeps track of individual users’ consumption of and engagement with serialized content. The findings revealed that SP structures more effectively increase consumption quantity (measured by the number of words read) compared with LP formats. However, LP outcompetes SP in elevating progression rate. Notably, LP is more effective than SP in inducing higher levels of engagement as well as a predisposition to submit high-quality book reviews and make subsequent purchases. Furthermore, the positive effects of LP over SP reinforce as book popularity and quality increase. This research offers both scholarly and practical implications for how the partitioning of serialized content influences consump tion and engagement patterns. These insights are invaluable for stakeholders seeking to ensure the sustained growth and viability of digital content platforms.

## 1. Introduction

Human consumption behaviors, such as eating, spending, watching, and reading, are often influenced by the way that resources are partitioned into smaller units. This phenomenon is evident in various aspects of daily life; for instance, in the culinary domain, experts contend that how cooking ingredients (e.g., vegetables and meats) are chopped can alter their perceived flavor and taste, ultimately influencing the amount of food consumed. Similarly, partitioning a mass of indulgent food into constituent pieces can effectively reduce the quantity consumed and the rate of consumption (Cheema and Soman 2008, Kleef 2014). Scholars have explained these effects by emphasizing the role of increased decision-making points and reflection oppor tunities facilitated by partitioning. These actions enable individuals to regulate their subsequent consumption. To illustrate, consider the scenario of consuming chocolates individually wrapped in small packages. In such cases, conscientious consumers seeking to limit their intake may face continuous choice decision points: whether to unwrap another unit either after or during current consumption. This “partitioning effect” can, therefore, minimize impulsive indulgence, acting as a deterrent to excessive eating (Kerameas et al. 2015).

Our research builds upon the aforementioned discussion, exploring their application in the digital content market. Specifically, we investigate how partitioning long, hedonic, serialized digital content products (i.e., fiction and fantasy e-books) into same-sized episodes affects consumers’ consumption and engagement behaviors. Although the effects of partitioning physical goods are well documented in the literature, the impacts on digital products remain underexplored. This research gap is significant because of the inherent differences between physical and digital products. For instance, digital content possesses two distinct characteristics that differentiate it from physical content: intangibility and accessibility. Unlike physical books or magazines, digital content is not tangible, which may diminish the natural unit bias—the tendency to consider a single unit as an appropriate amount to consume— and lead to potentially different consumption behaviors. Moreover, digital content facilitates rapid and convenient access and transition to subsequent episodes, potentially reducing the significance of decision points that typically control consumption in partitioned physical resources. These unique attributes of digital content necessitate a re-evaluation of traditional theories and findings related to content partitioning and consumption.

Granular segmentation in serialized digital content has emerged as prevailing trend, increasingly becoming the standard in the publishing and entertainment industries and in online education. Major players in the industry, such as Netflix, have adopted this approach by offering season-long movies or dramas as serialized episodes rather than stand-alone films. A similar strategy is observed on YouTube, where many users upload single-topic content in separate short-form installments (Oladipo 2022). Online education platforms, including Coursera, have launched “clips” to enhance learning by segmenting lengthy educational videos into numerous shorter videos (Belsky 2022). Digital content creators recognize granular segmentation as a valuable adjustment for attracting users, aligning well with shortened consumers’ attention spans.<sup>1</sup> The resurgence of serialization has significantly impacted the dynamics of digital publishing, with content platforms leveraging this structure to release leisure and amusement products (e.g., fiction and cartoons). This approach aims to attract new subscribers and engage existing customers more actively with their services. Amazon’s Kindle Vella, introduced in April 2021, providing authors with a platform to share serialized stories with readers while generating revenues through the platform.

Although serialized digital content has opened innovative avenues for revenue generation and sustained membership, there exists a noticeable gap in research regarding the segmentation effects of serialized formats. This study aims to address this void by investigating the impacts of partitioning serialized digital content on content consumption and engagement. The focus of our exploration is the market of serialized entertainment e-books, which has become a crucial component of plat form owners’ digital content strategies for consumer attraction. Serialized digital content serves as an ideal context for this research because of several reasons. First, in practice, the number of episodes in an e-book and the length of each episode vary widely. Determining the optimal partitioning strategy for lengthy digita content, with significant implications for user consump tion and engagement, remains unclear for content plat forms and creators. For example, in our sample of 940 serialized e-books, the average number of episodes was 255 for products with small partitions, contrasting sharply with only 12 for books with large partitions. This contrast indicates that books offered through smal partitions, on average, contain 21 times more episodes than those with large partitions. Considerable variations also exist in the length of episodes, ranging from as few as 200 words to nearly 100,000 words per installment.<sup>2</sup> Second, serialized digital content is typically lengthy, posing challenges for individuals to maintain engagement throughout an entire series (Voyles 2018). Many consumers abandon book consumption well before reaching the final sections, raising concerns among authors and platform owners (Online Appendix A). Lit tle is known about how much of each episode consu mers complete and how far they progress through an entire serialized product. Existing literature suggests that the segmentation of content significantly influences consumer perception and cognition. Therefore, a well thought-out partitioning strategy is crucial for increasing consumer propensity and user engagement, ulti mately translating into long-term business profits.

In consideration of these developments, our focus turns to understanding how content partitioning structures are linked to the consumption patterns, engagement dynamics (Zhang et al. 2019), and subsequent purchasing behaviors of consumers as they engage with identical hedonic-oriented<sup>3</sup> serialized e-books (e.g., romance, fantasy, and chivalrous fictions) partitioned into numerous episodes, each containing a short story (e.g., 200 episodes). This scenario is contrasted with the consumption of minimal installments, each featuring a long narrative (e.g., 20 episodes). For clarity, we refer to the former structure as “small partitioning” (SP) and the latter as “large partitioning” (LP). More specifically, our investigation delves into how variations in partitioning structures affect the amount of consumption assessed through two criteria: (1) the total consumption rate based on word counts (i.e., consumption rate) and (2) the total number of episodes read (i.e., progression rate).

Furthermore, we extend these analyses by examining the moderating effects of book characteristics (e.g., popularity, quality, and length) on the relationship between partitioning structure and consumption quantity. Finally, beyond scrutinizing consumption quantity, we assess the qualitative aspect of consumption. This involves examining how content-partitioning structures influence consumption intensity (i.e., the use of text annotations and highlights), engagement behavior (i.e., the submission of book reviews and review characteristics) (Pu et al. 2020, Liu and Feng 2021), and the decision for subsequent purchases.

Our study navigates a distinctive context, setting it apart from prior research that primarily examined the effects of physical resource partitioning on consumption. Unlike physical resources, digital content is intangible, which reduces the tendency to view a single unit as an appropriate amount to consume, leading to potentially different consumption behaviors. Moreover, digital content enables swift and convenient access to the next episode, lessening the importance of decision points that usually govern consumption in partitioned physical resources. Next, prior research has largely concentrated on the effects of content partitioning within cognitive contexts (e.g., educational materials), finding that partitioning can enhance learning performance by reducing cognitive load and facilitating systematic engagement. However, it is unclear whether these benefits extend to hedonic content, where unstructured and emotionally driven consumption patterns are more prevalent. The segmenting barriers might rather hinder the desired free and active hedonic-oriented consumption behaviors, such as skipping boredom and extracting excitement.

Finally, quantitatively measuring actual consumption at granular levels is challenging given the difficulties associated with tracing usage after purchase. Sellers typically remain unaware of what buyers do with purchased products and the extent of their consumption. Many studies on resource partitioning and segmentation kept track of consumption in laboratory environments—a setting that may have brought about inaccurate assessments because of possible Hawthorne effects. To resolve this issue, we established a partnership with a major e-book platform in Asia to develop and implement an innovative consumption-tracking system. This system automatically and continuously stores and updates individuals’ reading activities related to serialized e-books. Because consumers must use e-book reader apps connected to the company’s servers, every consumption activity within the app, such as closing a book and highlighting keywords, can be accurately traced and recorded with precise time stamps.

A preview of our results reveals intriguing insights regarding identical serialized e-books presented through SP and LP structures. Notably, the SP structure surpasses the LP counterpart in terms of consumption quantity, measured by the total number of words read. However, an interesting nuance emerges, indicating that individuals engaging with LP-structured content progress further into a serialized book compared with those consuming SP equivalents. In other words, whereas LP consumers exhibit a lower consumption rate, they read more episodes than their SP counterparts. These findings collectively suggest that the larger content size offered by the LP structure induces more strategic and efficient consumption behaviors than those prompted by SP offerings. Some interesting patterns also emerged from the engagement and economic effects of partitioning. LP consumers demonstrate more active engagement with serialized content, evident in their increased use of text annotations and highlights. Furthermore, they submit more book reviews, characterized by higher quality in terms of length and informative, compared with their SP counterparts. Moreover, consumers experiencing LP exhibit higher consumption satisfaction as reflected in more positive review valence and display a greater inclination for additional purchases of other serialized books compared with those engaging with SP. These nuanced patterns illuminate the multifaceted impact of partitioning structures on consumer behavior and economic outcomes in the context of serialized digital content.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Consumption and Engagement with Digital Content

Over the past few years, information systems researchers have explored various facets of digital content consumption and engagement, encompassing diverse products, such as e-books, video series, games, and songs (Zhang et al. 2019, Zhou et al. 2021).<sup>4</sup> Approaching the consumption of digital content from a rational addiction perspective, Kwon et al. (2016) demonstrated how rational social app users strategically adjust their app consumption for optimal utility. Examining consumer engagement with advertising content on social media, Lee et al. (2018) found that incorporating ad content reflecting brand personality (i.e., human emotion) potentially enhances engagement. Additionally, Huang et al. (2019) investigated postpurchase engagement behaviors of online game players, unveiling the motivational factors (i.e., effectance, challenge, and curiosity) that sustain engagement over time through hidden Markov approaches.

Although these studies concentrated on the diverse engagement and consumption patterns across various digital content types, they collectively overlooked the aspect of product structure. To the best of our knowledge, no academic research has investigated the effects of digital content structure on consumption or engagement patterns. The current study addresses this gap, offering a novel perspective by examining the relationship between digital content partitioning and postpurchase behaviors of consumers (e.g., consumption, engagement, and repurchase activities), particularly concerning lengthy serialized e-books.

## 2.2. Effects of Segmentation on Cognitive Performance

Given the inherent cognitive limitations in human comprehension and perception, researchers have extensively examined how the division of learning material affects individuals’ cognitive capabilities and learning performance.<sup>5</sup> A consensus has emerged in these studies with respect to the positive effects of segmentation on mental competence and cognitive processing. Hasler et al. (2007) and Spanjers et al. (2011) demonstrated that breaking down learning material into smaller units reduces the mental effort required for engagement and enhances attention, facilitated by pauses or temporal cues inherent in segmented formats. Mayer and Moreno (2003) proposed that segmentation provides pauses between mental efforts, affording individuals additional time and mental breaks crucial for effective cognitive processing. Further investigations have deepened our understanding of segmentation outcomes on performance, uncovering an array of moderating factors. For instance, the cognitive advantages derived from breaking down learning content may be more pronounced under complex tasks and visual processing (Hassanabadi et al. 2011), which demand higher cognitive load and mental investment compared with simple tasks and audio-based processing (Wong et al. 2012).

Our thorough survey of the literature reveals common elements in studies on the effects of segmentation on cognitive performance. Many of these studies focused on exploring the positive segmentation effects on the cognitive capability and learning performance of students, particularly under complex tasks (i.e., problem-solving instructions). Most of these studies carried out laboratory experiments for short durations, utilizing both segmented and unsegmented materials to validate segmentation effects. Our work distinguishes itself by focusing on how partitioning influences the consumption patterns and engagement behaviors of consumers in the realm of hedonic-oriented content, such as fantasy, romance and fiction, prevalent in digital content markets.

This shift in focus is significant as the effects of partitioning may differ substantially in hedonic-centered consumption compared with the previously explored cognitive-driven reading. The latter, geared toward acquiring information and solving complex problems, typically involves systematic thinking, high concentration, working memory, and goal-oriented reading tendencies. The existing literature has established that in this context, partitioning positively influences cognitive capabilities and learning performance by reducing mental effort (Hasler et al. 2007, Wong et al. 2012). In contrast, hedonic-focused consumption, driven by the pursuit of pleasure and emotional satisfaction, entails more unstructured and emotionally driven reading pat terns (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982). Readers in this context may choose to reread certain parts, selectively skip introductions, or engage freely based on emotional reactions. The partitioning in this scenario might hinder desired free and active hedonic-oriented consumption behaviors, such as extracting excitement through strategic reading, thereby revealing potential side effects.

## 2.3. Partitioning and Consumption Contro

Another pertinent body of research explores how the parti tioning of resources (i.e., food and money) influences indi viduals’ consumption behaviors. Cheema and Soman (2008) demonstrated that under resource partitioning, the total consumption of indulgent food items (e.g., chocolates and cookies) decreases, and the consumption rate slows down. They averred that resource partitions create addi tional “decision points” for consumers, prompting them to decide whether to continue with consumption at each juncture. Similarly, Dhar et al. (2007) found that using sep arate mental payment accounts (e.g., envelopes containing money) can effectively disrupt shopping momentum, pre venting impulsive subsequent purchases after an initial buy. These psychological barriers encourage individuals to pause, contemplate future consequences, and shift from automatic spontaneous thinking modes to deliberative rational modes. Scholars postulated that the frequent pauses and separate mental accounts arising from parti tioning enable consumers to exert self-regulation and restrain consumption. This reasoning draws on the work of Wertenbroch (1998), who discovered that customers strategically self-impose constraints on themselves when purchasing vice products (e.g., cigarettes). Smokers, for instance, tend to buy single packs instead of more costeffective 10-pack cartons to monitor their consumption of indulgent items, despite the volume discounts offered by the latter. Opting for single packs creates more decision points for “rational smokers” to reduce indulgence and mitigate health risks consciously.

Consumer psychology suggests that individuals tend to overestimate consumption when food is partitioned into small units (Pelham et al. 1994). Health scientists and medical professionals stressed the importance of unit size in regulating food intake, asserting that larger food portions often lead to overeating. Kleef (2014) dem onstrated that approximately 23% less chocolate is consumed when presented in small unit sizes compared with when served in large unit sizes. Similarly, Marchiori et al. (2011) found that cutting unpacked candies in half results in a 50% reduction in consumption. Researchers (e.g., Geier et al. 2006, Raynor and Wing 2007) distinguish unit size from portion size, clarifying that the former denotes the total number of units into which a portion of food is divided. For example, two slices of pizza may be equivalent in portion contained (i.e., total calories and weights) to one slice, but they differ in terms of units (two units versus one unit).

Two categories of human heuristics and biases are pertinent in understanding partitioning effects. The first relates to the unit bias, wherein individuals, particularly in terms of food or energy intake, perceive a single unit an appropriate (optimal) amount to eat. Consequently, they tend to consume larger quantities when presented with a single large unit compared with smaller units (Geier et al. 2006). According to the unit bias, individuals offered a large (unpartitioned) pizza slice of the same quantity and calories are likely to eat more in tota quantity than those given two small (partitioned) slices. The second category involves the numerosity bias, which pertains to people’s tendency to use numerosity as a cue for assessing size and quantity (Pelham et al. 1994). Consumers often overestimate or overinfer the size of resources when presented with high numerical figures. For instance, a pizza with eight slices is perceived to be greater in quantity than one sliced six ways, despite the actual size remaining the same. In the same vein, consumers may perceive 1,000 loyalty points to be greater and more beneficial than 100 points, even though both levels correspond to an identical proportion of financial rewards (say, \$10) (Nejad and Onay 2014). A consumer’s sense of possession utility increases with degrees of partitioning for identical resources. From the perspectives of consumption and monitoring, consumers undergo moments of reflection on their behaviors when initiating the consumption of a new unit. Frequent pauses resulting from the consumption of smaller units lead consumers to perceive that they have consumed enough, encouraging them to stop the behavior (Cheema and Soman 2008). In summary, this stream of research has primarily focused on the role of partitions in monitoring and constraining the consumption of unhealthy food products. Our work deviates from this line of investigation in that although these studies looked into partitions as a means of controlling the quantity of consumed offline physical resources (i.e., food), where units are directly touchable and “decision points” between segments are tangible during the consumption process, the present research evaluates the effects of partitions on the consumption and evaluation of serialized digital content. In this context, the content episode is intangible, and the transition to next episode is digitally seamless (Koiso-Kanttila 2004).

## 3. Hypotheses Development

## 3.1. Partitioning, Cognitive Processing, and Serialized Content Consumption

The consumption of books involves human cognition and mental processing as reading entails constantly making meaning from texts (Woolley 2011) (Figure 1). Although variations exist across genres and degrees of sophistication, effectively bringing texts to the forefront of human attention and ensuring comprehension require an understanding of the intricate cognitive operations at play in mental processing and representation. These operations encompass inferencing, analysis, structuring, and reflection (Coiro 2011). The capacity of readers to extract meaning from texts and congruently grasp writers’ interpretations is enhanced when the cognitive load on their working memory is minimized (Daneman and Green 1986, Manset-Williamson and Nelson 2005, Pressley and Harris 2006). There exists a limitation to our cog nitive processing resources (Sweller 1988, Just and Carpenter 1992) and working memory capacity (Cowan 2008), posing a barrier to comprehension and sustained attention. In the processing of text-based information, the mental representations and understood concepts stored in working memory decay rapidly unless adequately refreshed (Baddeley et al. 1986). Given these cognitive constraints, humans develop a hierarchical structure to organize vast amounts of information and representations. They prioritize and retain key elements as retrieval cues to sustain the optimal functioning of working memory (Cowan 2008).

Consumers of digital content dynamically manage their mental resources and adjust their consumption strategies by leveraging the cognitive architecture established through partitioning, influencing the level of attention and enthusiasm devoted to product consump tion (Wong et al. 2012). The partitioning of lengthy content into shorter episodes serves to regularly rejuvenate working memory, allowing consumers to reset their attention, mindset, and cognition. In situations where all other factors are equal, individuals exhibit peak attention levels when cognitive processing initiates, and this awareness undergoes periodic refreshment with each new processing cycle (Ahn et al. 2018). The narrative structure plays a pivotal role as a plot introduced at the outset of each episode tends to garner more attention compared with those embedded in the middle of the installment. Essentially, partitioning influences the mental cognitive structures of individuals (Mogilner et al. 2008), leading to varied perceptions of the same stories and narrations based on their placement within an episode. For instance, cliffhangers in a brief episode may be perceived as routine plot developments if posi tioned in the middle of a lengthy installment. Moreover, the recognition of short length, signaled through sen sory cues, encourages consumers to fully devote cogni tive resources to the consumption of each episode. Consequently, small partitions are more likely than their large counterparts to evoke heightened cognitive concentration on the ongoing consumption experience.

In a digital context, the unit of an episode and its distinctive book cover cannot be physically detected (Koiso-Kanttila 2004). This intangibility poses a potential challenge to the unit bias, which typically denotes the tendency to consider a single unit as an appropriate amount for consumption. The inability to physically discern a unit may disrupt the natural tendency to overestimate consumption when content is partitioned into smal units. Moreover, the digital setting facilitates a seamless transition to the next episode in a serialized digital book with minimal friction and without imposing a significant cognitive burden (Zu¨ ndel 2019). This effortless accessibility diminishes the impact of the “decision points,” which would otherwise disrupt continuous consumption momentum. In conclusion, the absence of sensory distinction resulting from intangibility and accessibility in the digital setting may obscure the division (Breivik et al. 1998, Laroche et al. 2001), weakening the expected positive effects of granular partitioning on consumption control. This contradicts prior literature focused on partitioning and consumption control in offline resource contexts (Cheema and Soman 2008).

Conversely, if individuals do perceive the length of each episode, large partitions may psychologically overwhelm consumers because of their presentation of voluminous texts, making careful, thoughtful consumption challenging (Friedlander 2018). These substantial installments may prompt consumers to adopt a strategic, expeditious consumption approach, focusing on main ideas and narratives while skimming content to reduce cognitive exhaustion. The issue is that these calculated actions tend to adversely affect the consumption rate measured by the number of words read. Given that small partitions more effectively alleviate cognitive load and enhance interaction with texts compared with large installments, the former more effectively increases consumption quantity (i.e., consumption by word count). In alignment with the discussion above, we proposed the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1a. Serialized e-books characterized by large partition structures deteriorate consumption quantity (i.e., total quantity in terms of words read) compared with those presented through small partitions.

Although small partitions are considered more effective than large counterparts in enhancing the consumption rate as measured by the number of words read, the former may be structurally disadvantaged when it comes to how far consumers progress into a serialized book. Despite enabling strong concentration on individ ual episodes, small partitions create more decisionmaking situations at the conclusion of each episode (Cheema and Soman 2008), potentially discouraging further consumption. Moreover, the heightened withinconcentration engagement (i.e., a consumer’s heightened attention within an episode) driven by small partitions may not seamlessly translate into across-concentration engagement (i.e., a consumer’s heightened attention across episodes). The depletion of cognitive resources because of careful, thorough consumption reduces one’s willingness to carry on consumption (Chen et al. 2018).

Serialized books, characterized by small partitions, offer readers a heightened sense of personal accomplishment upon completing each episode, leading to immediate satisfaction (Atkinson 1953). Unlike large installments, content delivered in small increments provides consumers with more frequent opportunities to experience the rewards of accomplishment. This aligns with the numerosity bias, where individuals tend to overemphasize quantity in favor of larger numeric values or large figures (Pelham et al. 1994). To illustrate, consider the perception of a pizza sliced into eight pieces versus six pieces. The product appears larger when divided into eight, disregarding the size of each slice (Pelham et al. 1994). Similarly, readers may focus solely on the number of book episodes completed, overlooking the size of each episode. This perceptual bias, however, can potentially hinder continued engagement with the reading process. The augmentation in perceived achievement may inadvertently discourage consumers from progressing further. The satisfaction derived from perceived success might intensify a sense of satiety toward current action, ultimately diminishing the desire for continued consumption (Galak et al. 2013).

In contrast, consumers adapt their consumption approaches through the mental accounting facilitated by partitioning (Wong et al. 2012), Large partitioning (i.e., small number of episodes) may elicit a sense of selfefficacy (Bandura 1986), motivating individuals to consume the entire story rather than focusing on completing each episode. This stems from the belief that a smaller number of episodes (e.g., three) requires less mental effort to comprehend the complete narrative compared with a larger number (e.g., eight) (Pelham et al. 1994). Essentially, in a digital setting, the quantity of content partitions serves as a crucial cue signaling the content volume, with a smaller number of episodes instigating a sense of self-efficacy, propelling individuals to complete the content. Consumers engaged in larger partitions, driven by self-efficacy in their consumption motivation, may strategically concentrate on core stories and skim peripheral narratives to digest the entire story. This inclination might be further strengthened by the digitalization of content, ensuring swift accessibility for navigating multiple episodes, in contrast to the more intricate process associated with physical printed books. Consequently, for identical serialized e-books, large partitioning proves more effective in increasing progression rates compared with small alternatives. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1b. Serialized e-books characterized by large partition structures enhance progression rates (i.e., how far consumers progress into an initiated book) compared with those presented through small partitions.

## 3.2. Moderating Effects of Book Length, Popularity, and Quality on Consumption Patterns

The popularity and quality of content can significantly boost self-efficacy, thereby amplifying the impact of content partitioning on consumer behaviors. In the context of experiential consumption of digital content, achieving a high-quality consumption experience is the ultimate goal for consumers. Consequently, positive perceptions regarding the popularity and quality of content can create elevated expectations for the utility of the consumption experience (Amaldoss and He 2018). This heightened expectation, in turn, may lead consumers to believe in their ability to successfully complete the consumption process. In other words, a stronger belief in successfully finishing the content consumption can empower consumers to develop strategic consumption strategies based on the mental accounting established through partitioning (Wong et al. 2012). This, in effect, contributes to an increase in progression rates but a decrease in consumption quantity.

Within the realm of serialized digital fiction, the qual ity or excitement of each episode can vary across partitions. The narrative structure of serialized fiction typically places the most exhilarating events or climactic content in middle to late episodes, aligning with the recognized “exposition–rising action–climax–resolution” narrative arc prevalent in novels and films (Cervantes 2023). Consequently, when readers grapple with the cognitive demands of LP while holding heightened expectations induced by the popularity and quality of the book, the initial episodes may appear comparatively mundane. This perception arises as early episodes often focus on establishing background or introducing characters. The interplay between heightened beliefs induced by book popularity and quality coupled with the perceived initial dullness under an LP structure serves as a catalyst for strategic consumption. Readers, aiming to uncover the most exciting elements, engage in strategic consumption practices, resulting in a significant reduction in consumption quantity.

Moreover, the length of content impacts the cognitive load and user experience (Singh and Cole 1993, Ricketson 2016). The size of a book may, therefore, moderate the effects of partition structures on consumption quantity and progression rate. While formulating the hypoth eses, we posited that, all else being equal, small partitions of serialized books can effectively prompt greater consumption quantity than large installments. However, the positive effect of small partitions may diminish as their size increases because of the expansion of book length. In essence, delivering a lengthy book in small partitions highlights the limited effects of such structures on consumption quantity as the compactness of an episode depends on the total size of the overarching narrative. The larger the book, the larger a “small”

episode becomes when compared with a short installment under a small-scale narrative.

In this scenario, the refreshing of cognitive architectures (i.e., working memory) unfolds less frequently and at a slower pace. This hinders consumers from heightening their attention and expectations at the onset of each episode. Additionally, the positive sensory cues and perceptions derived from short episodes, where consumers fully engage their cognitive resources, may dissipate, no longer influencing consumption. As the length of episodes increases, the probability of incomplete consumption (i.e., abandonment of reading midsession) escalates accordingly (Lee et al. 2021). This, in turn, diminishes the positive effects of small partitions on consumption quantity. Furthermore, the advantages of large partitions in terms of progression rates may become less apparent with increasing book length. In such cases, additional time and perceptual cognitive loads are required to complete a book, potentially hindering further advancement into the content. With these considerations, we propose the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 2a. The negative effects of large partitions on consumption quantity are accentuated with book popularity and quality.

Hypothesis 2b. The positive effects of large segmentation on progression rate are accentuated with book popularity and quality.

Hypothesis 2c. The negative effects of large partitions on consumption quantity are attenuated with the length of a book.

Hypothesis 2d. The positive effects of large partitions on progress rates are attenuated with the length of a book.

## 3.3. Partitioning, Consumer Engagement, and Purchase

Partition structures may play a crucial role in shaping how consumers interact with texts and engage with narrations, particularly in the context of serialized e-books. Two key aspects of consumer engagement are explored: in-depth consumption behaviors facilitated by digital marking schemes (i.e., annotations and highlights) and the influence of online book reviews. Marking functions, including annotations and highlights, serve as tools for consumers to enhance their understanding and connection with books. They help organize principal ideas, enable the recollection of favorite passages, and allow for the insertion of comments and questions (Glover et al. 2007). Such annotations and highlighting also guide attention, facilitate quick navigation for review, and reveal contextual cues (Li et al. 2014). These interactions elevate the quality of consumption experience, performance, and satisfaction (Johnson et al. 2010). Moreover, text marking assists platform administrators, owners, and authors in identifying key narrations within a book, offering valuable insights into consumer preferences and engagement, as these reflections are electronically transmitted to servers. Notably, digital annotations have gained widespread adoption for Netflix movies and YouTube content, empowering users to identify and capture memorable moments and share specific locations and time frames with their social networks (Profy 2019).

Interestingly, large partitions are more likely to evoke user engagement through annotations and highlights compared with small partitions. The substantial cognitive load imposed by extensive content segments encourages consumers to employ in-depth consumption strategies and establish contextual cues (i.e., favorite passages). Consumers engaged with larger partitions, driven by high self-efficacy, are predisposed to leverage these marking schemes. As episode length increases, comprehension requires the integration of more stories, potentially dividing attention because of the subsequent rise in cognitive load (Sweller et al. 1990). In response to this expanded narrative space and divided attention, consumers judiciously utilize annotation and highlighting tools to minimize cognitive load and integrate disparate information for enhanced comprehension. Conversely, the utility of such tools may not be as pronounced for small partitions given the lower cognitive load associated with consuming shorter content.

Furthermore, serialized e-books featuring large partitions are expected to surpass those with smaller installments in terms of generating online peer reviews, characterized by higher quality (e.g., length and informativeness). Consumers who prefer larger episodes progress farther into a book, gaining a more comprehensive understanding of storylines and acquiring additional information to share with others. Applying social cogni tive theory, which underscores the influence of self efficacy—an individual’s belief in their capability to successfully execute a particular task (Bandura 1986)—on intention and behavior, previous research (e.g., Kankanhalli et al. 2005, Cheung and Lee 2012) has demonstrated that perceived knowledge self-efficacy is a critical motivational factor influencing individuals’ intentions to provide online reviews based on word of mouth. Holding other factors constant, consumers engaged with large partitions perceive a relatively higher level of knowledge acquisition about a book because of their further progression compared with consumers of small episodes. Conse quently, those engaging with larger partitions are more likely to submit high-quality reviews (Lee et al. 2021), contribute valuable information, and assist others. Fur thermore, they are more inclined to provide high ratings driven by their self-efficacy heightened by the experience of large partitioning.

When considering experienced goods, such as fiction, customers derive entertainment value from noninstru mental aspects, such as suspense, surprise (Ely et al. 2015), fantasy, emotional stimulation, and enjoyment (Holbrook and Hirschman 1982). The incomplete partial consumption associated with small partitions hinders consumers from experiencing the full range of emotional stimulation and intensified excitement. Additionally, as consumers only partially engage with a book, they are unable to unfold specific details regarding characters and narratives (Lee et al. 2021). In contrast, consumers comprehensively understanding a serialized story under large partitions fully experience a spectrum of features, ensuring suspense and surprise from the beginning to the end of the book. Consequently, they become more deeply immersed in the book and more willing to express their feelings and emotions about its characters and narratives (Lee et al. 2021).

Finally, our attention shifts to another postconsumption behavior exhibited by consumers: the additional purchase of other serialized books. The extension of conversion, indicating a high level of satisfaction with the consumer’s prior experience with serialized books, is immensely beneficial for platform managers in terms of profit and loyalty. Consumers engaging with large partitions strategically exhibit a more comprehensive understanding of the entire storyline of serialized books. This deeper engagement may lead to heightened consumption self-efficacy, satisfaction, and an increased likelihood of making additional purchases compared with those engaging with smaller segments. From a self-efficacy perspective, cognitive satisfaction is primarily derived from completing the entire experience rather than a partial one (Bandura 1986). Therefore, the more extensive consumption facilitated by large partitioning, as opposed to small partitioning, is expected to more effectively trigger subsequent purchases. Based on these considerations, we formulate the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 3a. Serialized e-books characterized by large partitions enhance consumer engagement in annotating and highlighting compared with those presented through small partitions.

Hypothesis 3b. Serialized e-books characterized by large partitions enhance online review submission, review length, informativeness, and valence compared with those characterized by small partitions.

Hypothesis 3c. Serialized e-books characterized by large partitions accelerate subsequent purchases compared with those characterized by small partitions.

## 4. Research Setting and Data Description 4.1. Empirical Setting

To validate our hypotheses, we collaborated with one of the major online e-book platforms in Asia (ABC Books<sup>7</sup> hereafter), which specializes exclusively in electronic fiction since its inception in 2008. The platform offers a diverse array of fiction genres, including romance and fantasy, exclusively in electronic format without offering printed versions. To utilize the e-book service that allows browsing, purchase, and consumption, consumers must install e-book reader programs accessible through both mobile applications and personal computers, similar to Kindle. The reader application, connected to the company’ server, meticulously records and tracks consumption patterns of users. The purchase and consumption processes of the company align with those of other reputable e-book platforms, such as Amazon and Jellybooks. The platform sells numerous e-books whose formats vary across products, with some sold as nonserial, independent offerings and others released in serialized structures comprising multiple episodes that are presented in sequential order. To examine the effects of partitioning on consumption patterns, we focused exclusively on fiction-based hedonic oriented electronic content available in serialized format (i.e., romance, fantasy, and chivalrous fiction<sup>8</sup>). The data utilized for our study included detailed information on individual purchase transactions and consumption patterns derived from this specific genre of serialized electronic content.

During the study period, the company was actively experimenting with two different content-partitioning structures to better understand consumers’ preferences (Online Appendix D). This unique setting enabled us to examine how variations in content-partitioning structures affect consumption and engagement behaviors. Importantly, it allowed us to control for potential confounding effects arising from both observable and unob servable content characteristics. The study involved identical e-book titles partitioned into a number of epi sodes, with the serialized content offered in two structures: a small partition format and a large partition alternative depending on the length of each episode. For the same e-book titles, the SP structure divided the entire series content into numerous episodes, each con taining a short story. In contrast, the LP structure divided the content into a considerably smaller number of installments, each featuring a longer narrative. To illustrate, for each serialized e-book title in our sample, the average number of episodes available through an SP structure was 255, whereas the LP arrangement offered only 12. It is important to note that the serialized products themselves were exactly the same, differing only in the number of partitioned episodes presented. Furthermore, because of the varying amount of content in each episode of LP and SP, the price of each episode in both formats may differ. However, the total price to purchase the entire series remained identical between the two formats. For example, consider serialized content partitioned into 10 episodes in LP format and 50 episodes in SP format. In LP format, each episode costs 1 dollar, resulting in a total of 10 dollars when a consumer purchases the entire series. Conversely, in SP format, the price of each episode for the same title might be 20 cents, leading to a total cost of 10 dollars when a consumer purchases the entire series in SP format. Therefore, consumers’ choice between LP and SP formats may be influenced by the price of each episode, potentially introducing selection bias in our analyses. To address this concern, we implemented procedures in the succeeding section to ensure the validity of our findings against possible endogeneity. Additionally, we conducted numerous robustness checks to confirm the robustness of our findings.

## 4.2. Data

To investigate the effects of content partitioning on consumption patterns and engagement behaviors, we gathered comprehensive data on consumer transactions, content consumption, and e-book releases from ABC Books’ server-stored logs spanning from August 1, 2017 to May 17, 2018. To compare and contrast the individual consumption patterns driven by SP and LP structures for identical e-books, we concentrated on serialized titles that were available in the two formats (Online Appendix D).

Initially, we compiled data on 1,617 serialized e-book titles that were accessible in both LP and SP formats during the data collection period. Subsequently, we identified consumers who not only purchased but also consumed e-books available in both LP and SP structures within our data collection time frame. This ensured that our sample had exposure to both LP and SP content, indicating no inherent preference for a specific partitioning arrangement. To minimize potential confounding effects arising from repeated consumption of the same content in different formats, we excluded consumers who read identical e-book titles under both LP and SP arrangements. As a result, our final data set consisted of 70,391 individual- and serialized contentlevel (i and j, respectively) observations from 15,943 distinct consumers and 1,617 unique serialized books available in both LP and SP formats.

The company stores content consumption data, with the tracking program recording consumers’ identification codes, e-book identifiers, consumption time stamps, and completion rates each time that a consumer closes the e-book reader app. Following previous studies (e.g., Schweidel and Moe 2016), we defined an individual’s reading session as the period during which the same serialized content is consumed in either LP or SP format separated by one or more hours of inactivity. Details on the key variables are as follows.

Consumption variables. We leveraged consumption data to capture diverse reading patterns exhibited by consumers. We first accessed data on consumption amount (ConsumptionQuantity) and the ratio of episodes that the consumer read (ProgressionRates) to comprehend consumption patterns. Specifically, ConsumptionQuantity is a consumption variable determined by word count, representing the total number of words read by each consumer from a given e-book. Our tracking system was designed to monitor the number of words on each page and episode, allowing us to calculate the words read based on completed pages and episodes. Thus, ConsumptionQuantity allows us to measure and compare the extent to which a consumer has consumed the con tent of a given book between LP and SP structures in terms of word count. Next, ProgressionRates is a percentage-based variable (i.e., range from zero to one) driven by episode count, quantifying how far a consumer progressed into an entire serialized book in terms of the number of episodes consumed. To account for varying episode numbers across serialized e-books because of partition structures, we normalized this measure by the total number of episodes in a given serialized content. Thus, this variable enables us to capture the proportion of the content that a consumer has consumed within a specific book. It is essential to note that ConsumptionQuantity indicates the volume of content consumed in terms of words read, whereas ProgressionRates represents the relative completeness of episode consumption, which is important for understanding how partitioning strategies affect the likelihood of consumers continuing through and completing the serialized content for comprehensive understanding. These complementary insights allow us to draw nuanced conclusions about the impact of partitioning on both the depth (volume of words) and breadth (progression through episodes) of consumption.

Engagement variables. In addition to consumption patterns, we gathered information on consumption intensity to capture the engagement behaviors of consumers. Consumption intensity (ConsumptionIntensity) was measured based on the number of highlights and annotations that each consumer leveraged during consumption. The more frequently these engagement behaviors occur during consumption, the greater the likelihood that the consumer is deeply engrossed in the content (Liu 2005). Within-consumption (e.g., annotating and highlighting) engagement activities can serve as quality signals for products, enabling content creators to make informed decisions. Annotations and highlights provide valuable feedback that book authors can leverage to identify story sections perceived as interesting by readers, facilitating the creation of more engaging subsequent narratives. To explore postconsumption behaviors, we collected data on individuals review entries after consumption (Reviewing). The review submission data included identification codes of reviewers and e-books, time stamps of review entries, and textual commentary. Additionally, we obtained the length of reviews generated by each consumer measured as the number of words (ReviewLength). To assess the informa tiveness of reviews (ReviewInformativeness), we utilized a topic-modeling approach to identify main themes in the review content (Puranam et al. 2017, Khern-Am-Nuai et al. 2018). Two topics (i.e., sentiment and product specifics) were identified as the optimal number of topics in our review data set, with topics related to product specifics containing more useful information to pinpoint product quality (Khern-Am-Nuai et al. 2018). Lastly, we collected the valence of each review (ReviewValence) entered by a consumer using the linguistic inquiry and word count dictionary. A detailed explanation of how we measured the review content and valence is provided in Online Appendix E.

Purchase variables. To assess the time elapsed until a consumer makes subsequent content purchases after consuming focal content (Purchase), we gathered information on individual transactions following the consumption of the focal content. This data set includes the consumer identification code, transaction time stamps, and the product identification code.

Content format variable. In order to investigate the effects of content partitions on consumption patterns, we collected data on variables related to, namely, LargePartitioning. As previously mentioned, the platform provides serialized content in both LP and SP structures for the same titles, resulting in varying numbers of episodes. We used a binary variable (LargePartitioning) to indicate whether content was partitioned via the LP format. If LargePartitioning equaled one, the content was divided using LP, featuring fewer episodes but a greater amount of content per installment.

Content characteristic variables. To explore the moderation effect of content characteristics, we gathered information on online consumer reviews and the length of content. Specifically, we collected data on review volume (ReviewVolume) and the average review valence (ReviewRating) for each content when a consumer purchased the content. Additionally, we obtained information on content length measured by the total number of words in a serialized book (Length).

Control variables. Several variables were included in the analysis to control for their potential effects on consumption patterns. These include the sales price of the entire content (Price), the discount rate (Discount), the publish date of the content (PublishDate), and whether a consumer ordered the entire series (SeriesOrder). A detailed description and summary statistics of these variables are provided in Table 1.

Although our empirical setting provided an opportunity for counterfactual analyses on the effects of content partitioning, potential selection issues may have persisted. Specifically, unobservable individual- or productspecific factors could have influenced consumers’ decision to read e-books in either LP or SP structure, possibly because of personal preferences. Differences in pricing schemes between LP and SP products may have also induced a selection problem. To address potential selection bias stemming from the nonrandomly selected sample between LP versus SP content, we employed an instrumental variable approach (Angrist and Krueger 1994). A valid instrumental variable should be correlated with the endogenous variable (LargePartitioning) but directly uncorrelated with the dependent variables of consumption patterns (Wooldridge 2010). We proposed individuals’ historical consumption of LP (HistLargePartitioning) as an instrumental variable to mitigate endogeneity in our model. The consumption of LP in the past is likely correlated with the current choice of LP format but uncorrelated with unobservable factors influencing different consumption patterns in the current serialized content. To justify exogeneity, we highlight that our sample comprises individuals who purchased and consumed e-books available in both LP and SP structures within our data collection period. This indicates an absence of individual preference for a particular partitioning arrangement, supporting the argument that the instrument variable (IV) (HistLargePartitioning) is unlikely to be contingent on individuals’ intrinsic characteristics.

## 5.2. Moderating Effects of Content Characteristics on Consumption Patterns

To obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of content partitioning on consumption patterns, we investigated potential moderating effects influenced by the content characteristics of serialized books.<sup>9</sup> Specifically, we employed the number of reviews (ReviewVolume) to gauge product popularity (Lin et al. 2019), where a higher review volume represents more discussion about products and may contribute to an increased likelihood of product awareness among consumers (Forman et al. 2008). Additionally, we used the rating of reviews (ReviewRating) to assess product quality, reflecting the evaluations of other consumers (Wu et al. 2021). We operationalized review rating as the average of review points generated by other consumers. Lastly, we considered the length of a book (Length) measured by the total number of words in serialized content. To examine these moderating effects, we included interaction terms of LargePartitioning and content character istics variables (ReviewVolume, ReviewRating, and Length) in Equation (1). The results are presented in Table 3. Regarding consumption quantity in the first column in Table 3, the interaction terms between LargePartitioning and online reviews (ReviewVolume and ReviewRating) are negative and significant at the 0.01 level. In terms of progression rate, the estimated coefficients of the interaction terms between LargePartitioning and online reviews (ReviewVolume and ReviewRating) are positive and significant at the 0.01 level, indicating that the positive effect of LargePartitioning is more pronounced with popular or high-quality products. As for episode progression rates (ProgressionRates), its interaction term with length is negative and significant at the 0.01 level, revealing that the positive effect of LP on consumption progression is undermined by the length of a book. In summary, the efficacy of LargePartitioning on consumption quantity and progression rates becomes more salient with review volume and review valence, whereas these effects are alleviated with book length, thereby supporting Hypotheses 2a, 2b, 2c, and 2d.

Reviewing behavior can represent the overall assessment of the consumption experience and serve as a reflection of product quality (Lee et al. 2021). With these considerations in mind, we operationalized four dependent variables related to reviewing behavior—whether a consumer decides to generate a review (Reviewing), the length of reviews generated by a consumer (ReviewLength), the informativeness of reviews generated by a consumer (ReviewInformativeness), and a rating given by a consumer (ReviewValence).

We first look into the relationship between content partitioning and review intention by employing a random utility choice framework (McFadden 1974). The dependent variable is the observed binary choice of consumers regarding whether to leave a review after consuming a serialized content. ## 7. Implications

Scholars have examined how resource partitioning influences cognitive learning, consumption constraints, and advertisement responses. To extend the literature, we assessed how splitting lengthy serialized hedonicoriented e-books into smaller episodes affects the con sumption patterns, engagement intensities, and subsequent purchase of e-book consumers. We focused on two partitioning structures (LP and SP) and their differential effects on consumption that was measured on the basis of total word count and episodes consumed. In addition to exploring partitioning effects on quantitative consumption, we assessed the qualitative aspects of consumption (i.e., annotations and highlights), engagement (i.e., online reviews), and subsequent economic behavior (i.e., purchase). We also looked into how content characteristics moderate the relationship between partitioning and consumption.

Recognizing that the effects of partitioning may differ significantly in hedonic-centered consumption compared with cognitive-driven reading for learning and educational purposes, we underscore the need for nuanced exploration. The partitioning in hedonic-focused digital content may induce systematic reading patterns with high concentration from the beginning of the content by prompting consumers to reset their attention, mindset, and cognition before each episode segment; it could hinder the desired free and active hedonic-oriented consumption behaviors (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982), such as extracting excitement through selective skipping. Our findings reveal that small partitioning in serialized digital fiction inhibits individuals’ selective and strategic consumption behavior, thereby negatively impacting engagement activities and subsequent purchase decisions. These results contradict previous literature that emphasized the favorable effects of partitioning on cognitive performance.

Unit bias has been employed to account for the greater consumption observed when food is presented in large units than in small alternatives (Geier et al. 2006). Contrastingly, the segmentation effect lessens overall consumption quantity because it engenders more decision-making hurdles that induce rational behaviors (Cheema and Soman 2008, Kleef 2014). The prior research has primarily focused on the positive role of partitions in monitoring and constraining the consumption of unhealthy food products. These contexts differ from that explored in our work as the focal product that we examined is serialized digital content. The absence of sensory distinction resulting from intangibility and accessibility in the digital setting may obscure the division (Breivik et al. 1998, Laroche et al. 2001), weakening the expected positive effects of granular partitioning on consumption control. This contradicts prior literature focused on partitioning and consumption control in offline resource contexts (Cheema and Soman 2008), highlighting a novel contribution of this study.

Our findings offer important theoretical insights into the value of consumption information that are not easily found in other academic works. Researchers have long considered the act of buying as reflective of consumer preference and satisfaction, whereas little attention has been paid to what consumers actually do with a product after purchase. We attempted to fill this void by exploring consumers’ postpurchase behaviors: namely, consumption, engagement, and subsequent purchase. For digital content products that are available online or through apps (e.g., movies, e-books, and games), information on consumption patterns (e.g., quantity, speed, and frequency) can be obtained in real time and serve as a crucial indicator of consumer preference, product quality, and future demand propensities.

Our results also provide content creators and platform operators with a number of actionable recommendations related to the effective partitioning and monetization of serialized e-books. Many digital content creators have preferred granular serialization as a strategic adjustment to attract more users, catering to shortened attention spans. The initial impression may suggest that partitioning always yields positive effects on consumer engagement and attention, but we found that these implementations might inadvertently contribute to the further shortening of individuals’ attention spans. Such segmentation could potentially impede consumers’ active and in-depth consumption behaviors, especially revolving around the progression rates and engagement activities. These findings underscore the importance of considering managerial implications for industries that might be indiscriminately adopting content segmentation, including subscription or free-based serialized digital services. Digital platforms must gain a precise understanding of the relative effects of content partitioning, considering the potential side effects.

For revenue enhancement, platforms may consider using different pricing strategies depending on the structure of serialized content. The findings revealed that individuals consuming small partitions exhibit a higher total consumption amount than that registered by consumers of long segments as determined using word count. However, readers who favor short narratives progress far less into serialized books that they initiate than do consumers of lengthy episodes; the former, therefore, consumes fewer episodes. Pay-per-episode models can be more beneficial to vendors of e-books parceled out in large segments, whereas pay-per-wordcount schemes would work more effectively for products delivered in small portions. We provide an in-depth discussion of determining the optimal partitioning strategy in Online Appendix S. Nonetheless, we advise managers against aggressively partitioning serialized content because doing so may cause satiety and burnout among consumers, which may motivate the early termination of consumption. Finally, the effectiveness of partitioning structures in ensuring consumption and engagement is significantly moderated by book characteristics, such as book popularity, quality, and length over episodes. This means that a partitioning structure does not always universally dominate another and that publishing constituencies must thus take these intervening patterns into account when segmenting and promoting serialized products.

We also highlight that the manner in which serialized stories are divided also affects the engagement and participation of consumers. Consumers occupied with large partitions more frequently annotate text and highlight narrative elements than those consuming small partitions—activities that are generally considered to reflect deeper engagement with stories. Alternative lengthy episodes may induce consumers to use these marks to organize and memorize reading material and efficiently interact with texts. In addition, individuals consuming products that are offered in large installments more actively participate in the consumption process through high-quality (e.g., lengthy and informative) review submissions. Finally, to rejuvenate the e-book industry, which has suffered a sharp decline in recent sales, platforms and publishers may consider the strategic adjustment of content structure. Consumers experienced with large segments are more likely to repurchase other serialized books than those consuming small splits. Repurchase activities are generally considered to be an extension of consumer satisfaction with the reading experience.

## 8. Conclusion

This study was intended to investigate how content partitioning affects users’ postpurchase behaviors (i.e., consumption, engagement, and purchase) in relation to serialized e-books that unfold plots and narratives through sequential episode-by-episode structures. The findings revealed that partitioning episodic stories significantly influences consumption patterns, engagement, and subsequent purchase decision. This research is not without limitations. Although similar serialization schemes have been adopted for various categories o digital content goods, we focused only on the partitioning effects of hedonic-oriented fiction e-books (e.g., written content, such as blog posts, newsletters, and social media contents) that differ characteristically from cognitive-focused content (e.g., educational purpose books) or more attention-grabbing watching content (e.g., movies, dramas, and other visual storytelling modes of entertainment). We, therefore, advise against generalizing our results to other forms of digital content and recommend an expansion of the current work to fil the void just acknowledged (Online Appendix T). Simi larly, our attention was devoted only to digital serial ized books, but printed forms of these products may stimulate different consumption and engagement pat terns. Another deficiency is related to the lack of incorporation of content stories into the analysis. We used book fixed effects to control for possible observable and unobservable heterogeneities in the sample, but factors other than partitions may have influenced consumption and engagement in our context. Additionally, the observational nature of the data used in this study presents potential limitations. Although we have employed sta tistical methods to control for confounding factors and ensure the robustness of our results, many underlying psychological factors that inform our hypotheses cannot be directly measured. Constructs, such as consume satisfaction, perceived enjoyment, and psychological responses to partitioning, are complex and multifaceted, often requiring experimental or longitudinal data to capture comprehensively. Finally, we used text annotations and highlights as proxies for levels of engagement with books. Although many studies have demonstrated the strong relationship between the use of these mechanisms and degrees of engagement, arguably, these standards simply indicate individual preferences and do not necessarily represent consumer engagement. Subsequent studies should endeavor to identify additional measures that can thoroughly represent consumer engagement.

Despite these caveats, our findings provide platform administrators and content creators with a number of useful strategic insights into the consumption and engagement of serialized content. Our research setting was unique in that identical e-books were serialized in two variant structures (large and small partitions), and their consumption quantity and engagement were precisely measured through a tracking system. The hope is for these newly crafted approaches to advance our understanding of how resource partitioning influences the consumption of and engagement with digital content, which serve as essential resources for the growth and sustainability of platforms.
