# How Product Display Orientation Affects Customers’ Choice Satisfaction in Online Purchase: A Choice Closure Perspective

Abstract. Online retailers often confront the problem of order cancellation due to customers’ poor satisfaction with their online purchase decision, termed as choice satisfaction in this study. However, very little e-commerce literature has addressed customer choice satisfaction, and none, to our knowledge, has investigated how to design product display interfaces to achieve it. Drawing from choice closure theory and eye and vision research, we examine how product display orientation of an online shopping web page affects customers’ choice satisfaction upon purchase. We propose that a horizontal (versus vertical) display of comparable products on an e-commerce website is more positively related to customer choice satisfaction by promoting a higher level of choice closure (a psychological process by which online customers come to perceive a decision as completed and settled). Through five carefully designed experiments (including one using an eye-tracking device), we find that online customers achieve a higher level of choice satisfaction from an assort ment of comparable products displayed horizontally than vertically on e-commerce websites. This effect results from the fact that a horizontal product display increases the amount of comparisons customers make between product options prior to making a purchase decision and consequential sense of choice closure after the decision. We also find that a cue of finality (e.g., adding a textual note “The end” to the product display) can largely attenuate this effect. The implications for online retailers’ product showcase strate gies are discussed, along with future research directions.

## Introduction

Over the past two decades, information systems (IS) research has extensively studied how to design e-commerce interfaces for online retailers to promote customer online purchase (e.g., Jiang and Benbasat 2007a, Wells et al. 2011, Yi et al. 2015, Yuan and Dennis 2019). Much less attention is paid to customer satisfaction with their purchase decision, referred to as choice satisfaction (Gu et al. 2013, 2018) in this study. Customer choice satisfaction is crucial for online retailers, given that customer order cancellation before actually experiencing a product often results from customer dissatisfaction with the purchase decision, and the cancellation process incurs huge labor and logistic costs that online retailers must shoulder. For example, to make the earliest possible delivery (e.g., same-day or next-day delivery), when customers place an order, online retailers (e.g., Amazon, JD.com, etc.) almost immediately pack and dispatch the chosen product to subwarehouses. If the order is canceled, online retailers may have to contact the logistic agent to ship the item back and then decide whether to return it to inventory or discard it completely, depend ing on its condition. According to software provider nShift, online cancellation and return account for an average 20% of online purchases, costing online retailers \$642 billion annually (Ludmir 2023), which underscores the economic cruciality of ensuring customer choice satisfaction. However, choice satisfaction is challenging to achieve in practice. A recent survey commissioned by the online shopping platform Slickdeals found that 74% of U.S. adults experienced instant dissatisfaction with their online purchase (Payne 2022). It is therefore crucial for online retailers to ascertain how to help online customers achieve choice satisfaction after making their purchase decision.

Customer choice is often influenced by the choice context, defined as the decision context in which a choice is made (e.g., Simonson and Tversky 1992, Houghton et al. 1999, Suk et al. 2012). Product display features that constitute the main decision context are supposed to have a meaningful influence on online customers’ choice satisfaction. The present study therefore attempts to understand how to achieve choice satisfaction by investigating an important, but subtle, structural feature of an online retailer’s product display web page: product display orientation. This refers to the horizontal or vertical direction of an online product assortment displayed on an e-commerce website from which customers make a purchase decision (Deng et al. 2016) (Online Appendix A).<sup>1</sup> Although horizontal product display has seen a surge in popularity in recent years with the wide use of the sideway-swiping gesture on touchscreens (Robson 2022), vertical product display is still widely adopted in e-commerce. For example, out of the top 100 e-commerce websites worldwide ranked by Automizely in 2023,<sup>2</sup> over 40% use vertical orientation to display products. This tendency toward vertical product display is even more obvious in mobile e-commerce, probably due to vertically oriented phone screens: 48.6% of all sites visited on tablet computers are browsed using a vertical orientation, with the number increasing to 91.7% for mobile phones (ScientiaMobile 2018). Despite the prevalence of vertical product display used in e-commerce, it remains unclear whether a vertical product display benefits online customer choice satisfaction compared with a horizontal one.

The extant IS literature has not shed light on the relationship between product display orientation and online customer choice satisfaction at the postpurchase stage. Numerous researchers have investigated various aspects of interface designs for product display (e.g., Jiang and Benbasat 2007a, b; Cyr et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2019; Yang and Xiong 2019; Yuan and Dennis 2019; Kinzinger et al. 2022), but not product display orientation. Moreover, they have looked into the decisionmaking processes that lead to the purchase decision (e.g., product attitude, purchase intention, willingness to pay, etc.; Jiang and Benbasat 2007a, Yuan and Dennis 2019, Kinzinger et al. 2022), but not to choice satisfaction after purchase decision. Although some notable exceptions (e.g., De et al. 2013, Yang and Xiong 2019) do address customer satisfaction, they mainly focus on examining customer satisfaction with product usage, which we denote as use satisfaction to differentiate from choice satisfaction. Although much is known about use satisfaction in the IS literature (e.g., De et al. 2013, Sahoo et al. 2018, Yang and Xiong 2019, Wang et al. 2021), the theoretical explanations derived from the literature may not apply to choice satisfaction, in which the product is not yet used and experienced.

Specifically, the dominant underlying theory for explaining use satisfaction is expectation confirmation theory, which posits that customers’ use satisfaction results from a positive confirmation between their expected product performance and actual use experience (Oliver 1977, 1980). Choice satisfaction, however, generates from counterfactual thinking about what customers might have accomplished had they chosen alternatives that they gave up, a comparison process made between chosen and forgone alternatives (Inman et al. 1997, Tsiros 1998, LeBoeuf et al. 2010). As this comparison process can occur even before the chosen product is used and experienced (e.g., Tsiros 2008; Gu et al. 2013, 2018), expectation confirmation theory that delineates the formation of use satisfaction upon actual usage cannot be leveraged in our study to explain and predict choice satisfaction.

Marketing research has examined choice satisfaction primarily through the theory of choice closure (Gu et al. 2013, 2018), which posits that once customers experience choice closure after making a purchase decision, they will stop making comparisons between the chosen and foregone options and thus feel more satisfied with their choice compared with those who do not experience such finality. This literature has identified triggers of choice closure based on a metaphorical linkage between behavioral/visual closure and experienced choice closure after the choice is made, such as performing acts of closure (e.g., putting a lid on after choosing one chocolate from a box of chocolates) (Gu et al. 2013) or seeing a textual note of “selected” after making a choice (Gu et al. 2018). These studies did not consider how the decision context prior to the purchase decision can be designed to promote choice satisfaction after purchase. Notably, no prior research has considered the impact of product display interface design on choice satisfaction. Motivated by this important research gap, we put forward the first research question: To what extent and how does product display orientation affect online customers’ choice satisfaction on e-commerce websites?

Despite the desire of online retailers to facilitate customer choice satisfaction, product display orientation is costly to change when an existing display orientation is deemed less advantageous. Indeed, a small change in website design may incur tremendous cost. For example, the average cost of a website redesign for a small- to medium-sized business is between \$15,000 and \$80,000; this cost can dramatically increase to over \$100,000 for a larger business, according to a study by HubSpot (Vettorino 2023). The remarkable cost leads to the second research question more on the practical front: How can a website interface be designed to mitigate the impact of a less advantageous product display orientation on customer choice satisfaction?

We address these research questions by building on the influential theory of choice closure, as developed by Gu and colleagues (Gu et al. 2013, 2018), and synthesizing it with the eye and vision research that human beings’ view field is much wider in the horizontal direction than in the vertical direction (i.e., the horizontal display advantage; Ojanpa¨a ¨ et al. 2002, Strasburger and Po¨ppel 2002). We propose that instead of the metaphorical triggers of choice closure already documented in the existing literature (e.g., closing behaviors or visual signals; Gu et al. 2013, 2018), a horizontal (versus vertical) product display leads people to make more adequate product comparisons before making a choice and thus to experience choice closure and feel more satisfied with the chosen option once the choice is made. Furthermore, we propose that a cue of finality, referred to as an online textual note that signals no more options that follow, facilitates customers’ product comparisons by focusing their attention on the currently presented products, which also renders perceived choice closure and neutralizes the otherwise disadvantaged effect of vertical product display. Our theoretical hypotheses are empirically verified through five randomized experiments (including an eye-tracking study).

Our research has both theoretical and practical significance. First, it extends prior IS literature on e-commerce—particularly the topic of e-commerce interface design—by examining the impact of the important, but understudied, design feature of product display—namely, product display orientation— on customer choice satisfaction after online purchase decision. Second, it distinguishes choice satisfaction from use satisfaction and squarely addresses the former, which has been largely neglected in the extant IS research, and builds on the novel theory of choice closure and the literature on eye and vision (rather than expectation confirmation theory) to theorize the linkage between product display orientation and choice satisfaction. Third, it adds to the extant literature on choice closure by identifying product display orientation as a novel trigger of choice closure via a prechoice product-comparison process, thereby adding to the literature that primarily addressed behavioral or visual triggers that operate through a postchoice metaphorical process (Gu et al. 2013, 2018). Taking a step further, it also suggests the cue of finality in product display as an effective moderator for the impact of product display orientation on choice closure, which can alternatively facilitate product comparisons via attention focusing. Finally, the current research provides managerial implications to online retailers on how to display products to improve choice satisfaction and use the cue of finality to neutralize the undesirable effects of (vertical) product display orientation on choice satisfaction.

## Theoretical Background Product Display and Customer Choice Satisfaction in E-Commerce

Despite the practical importance of choice satisfaction, very few investigations on e-commerce have studied what factors might facilitate customers’ formation of such satisfaction. Although some research (e.g., De et al. 2013, Yang and Xiong 2019) has addressed customer satisfaction, it mainly focused on examining customer satisfaction after experiencing the products purchased online (termed as use satisfaction in our study), and much less has been done to understand choice satisfaction.

To explain choice satisfaction, we focus on a nuanced structure feature of product display—that is, horizontal versus vertical display orientation of a product assortment, from which online customers make product comparisons and form a purchase decision. Such a particular focus results from a very practical consideration—that is, to cater to customers’ idiosyncratic needs, online retailers often provide various product options even for a single product (e.g., Apple’s new iPhone 15 series that has four different models). Horizontal and vertical displays thus arise to visually direct customers to navigate through product options and to facilitate their purchase decisions (Deng et al. 2016). As a result, online customers more often confront the decision context in which they need to choose one option from multiple alternatives, rather than to evaluate a single available option and decide whether to choose it or not.

Examining the impact of display orientation for multiple products on choice satisfaction in the e-commerce setting is not only practically relevant, but also theoretically significant. Prior e-commerce research (summarized in Table 1) has primarily investigated interface designs in three important domains: product display, website/application, and online decision aid. Whereas the latter two research streams focus on the design features of IT artifacts, the first stream of literature is more germane to the current work. To the best of our knowledge, however, the literature has investigated various display formats of a single product, rather than the display the orientation of multiple products or a product assortment for online customers to make a purchase decision. Moreover, although the literature has focused on diverse decision-making processes that lead to a purchase decision, choice satisfaction at the postpurchase stage has been largely neglected. For example, one stream of research has shown that product display can influence product evaluations such as customer’s product attitudes (Jiang and Benbasat 2007a, Kinzinger et al. 2022), willingness to pay (Yuan and Dennis 2019), purchase intentions (Wells et al. 2011, Yi et al. 2015), or actual purchase (Yang and Xiong 2019). Another stream of research has emphasized product display empowered by new technologies—such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and the virtual fitting room— and investigated how they can improve customers product understanding (Jiang and Benbasat 2004, Suh and Lee 2005, Jiang and Benbasat 2007b) and reduce product uncertainty (Liu et al. 2019). A third stream of research has looked into the impact of product display on a wide variety of product perceptions, such as perceptions of product price (Huang et al. 2019b), product quality (Wells et al. 2011), local presence (a sense of a product being present with a consumer in his own environment; Vonkeman et al. 2017), and social presence (Cyr et al. 2009). Given the obvious literature gap in addressing horizontal versus vertical layout of a product assortment in e-commerce websites and the neglect of choice satisfaction as outcome variables in e-commerce literature, it is important to theorize the relationship between product display orientation and customer choice satisfaction.

Unfortunately, a theoretical account explaining the above relationship is still lacking in the IS literature. In a related domain of customer (use) satisfaction, prior research has built upon expectation confirmation theory (Oliver 1977, 1980) to extensively investigate its driving factors. The theory proposes that the gap between expected performance and actual use experience determines use satisfaction. A wealth of extant research has suggested that online retailers can improve use satisfaction by providing sufficient product details and helping online customers establish a reasonable expectation before purchase. This is often achieved through the use of various IT artifacts—such as the Zoom function and AR/VR (e.g., De et al. 2013, Yang and Xiong 2019)—or the provision of adequate online product reviews (e.g., Sahoo et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2021). By contrast, setting a high, but unrealistic, expectation (such as by providing customer-generated images with high review ratings) can decrease product use satisfaction by creating a negative disconfirmation (which occurs when performance does not meet expectation) (Guan et al. 2023).

Notably, expectation confirmation theory involves the comparison between experienced and expected product performance and can therefore only predict satisfaction upon actual use (i.e., use satisfaction). On the contrary, as choice satisfaction results from the comparison between the chosen and the forgone alternatives that can happen before customers experience the chosen product, expectation confirmation theory becomes less applicable in predicting the linkage between product display orientation and choice satisfaction. A more suitable theoretical lens is therefore needed.

In this study, we draw on the theory of choice closure as our core theoretical foundation to explain this linkage. Recent investigations (e.g., Gu et al. 2013, 2018) have shown that customers often feel dissatisfied about a choice they already made because they have not psychologically closed or finalized it. In this case, they may continue comparing the chosen and forgone options and imagining what their present situation would be like if they had chosen differently. Given that product display orientation matters to how a product assortment is displayed online to facilitate customers’ navigation through comparable products, we build on this theory and extend it by elaborating on how product display orientation might vary the amount of product comparisons being made before making a purchase decision, which, in turn, render the perception of choice closure and choice satisfaction after the purchase decision.

## Choice Closure Theory

Choice closure refers to a psychological state characterized by perceiving the act of choosing as being completed, resolved, and settled (Gu et al. 2013, 2018). The concept of choice closure was developed based on the concept of psychological closure, defined as a state in which a person perceives that an event is complete and achieves a feeling of “pastness” when thinking of it (Kivetz et al. 2006, Nunes and Dre\`ze 2006, Sevilla and Kahn 2014). Whereas psychological closure refers broadly to a closure of life events in nonchoice domains, choice closure is a context-specific concept particularly relevant to a choicemaking process (Gu et al. 2013, Ilyuk and Block 2015). We therefore review prior work on both psychological closure and choice closure.

The related literature has proposed that when people achieve psychological closure with an event, they are likely to suppress the cognitive thoughts associated with the event, subsequently decreasing the “accessibility” of the event in memory (Li et al. 2010, Gu et al. 2013). For example, a sense of psychological closure about a negative experience can relieve the negative feelings associated with the event (Li et al. 2010), and the feeling of psychological closure after consuming a pharmaceutical product can prevent customers from taking additional doses (Ilyuk and Block 2015).

More germane to the present investigation, however, is evidence that the experience of choice closure can suppress cognitive thoughts associated with the choice and deter further comparisons between the chosen and foregone options (Gu et al. 2013, 2018). Note that customers do not automatically perceive their choice (once made) as psychologically closed, and this is especially the case in the decision context of our interest that requires customers to make a choice from a set of comparable alternatives. In such a case, the comparability among the alternatives renders choice more difficult (Tversky and Shafir 1992, Dhar 1997), which stimulates customers to revisit their choice and ponder forgone alternatives at the postchoice stage. Corroborating this, a sizable body of extant literature has indicated that customers tend to revisit their choice, especially when the choice is difficult, and compare it with other alternatives after purchase (Zeelenberg 1999; Carmon et al. 2003; Gu et al. 2013, 2018; Ma and Roese 2014).

Choice closure and the deterred comparison process that follows can subsequently facilitate choice satisfaction. Specifically, any comparisons across products with both meaningful advantages and disadvantages are found to reduce the attractiveness of the chosen option. This is so because of “negativity bias”—the cognitive dominance of negative over positive thoughts or memories—where the relative disadvantages loom larger than the relative advantages (Kahneman and Tversky 1979, Wright 1991, Carmon and Ariely 2000, Baumeister et al. 2001). Corroborating this line of research, the literature on counterfactual thinking also found that the generation of counterfactuals to the forgone options (i.e., what if I chose the option that I gave up?) produces more negative than positive experience (Kahneman and Miller 1986, Roese 1997). Taken together, such research confirms that choice closure can prevent customers from making further comparisons and thus render greater choice satisfaction after the purchase decision.

Moreover, Gu and colleagues (Gu et al. 2013, 2018) have identified certain triggers of choice closure. Specifically, they found that either the act of closing the box lid after choosing one chocolate (a behavioral trigger) or seeing an online textual note “selected” attached to a chosen option (a visual trigger) can lead people to feel a sense of choice finality. Notably, these identified triggers operate through metaphorical thinking, a process in which people attempt to understand abstract concepts based on behavioral or sensory experience (Lakoff 1993, Landau et al. 2010). We reason, however, that these metaphorical processes cannot explain the relationship between product display orientation and choice closure because either the act of closing a lid or seeing the text note of “selected” happens after customers make a choice, therefore directly marking the choice as “closed” through the metaphorical processes. In contrast, product display orientation is delivered during customers decision-making process before a choice is made, therefore making the aforementioned metaphorical linkage difficult to establish. As such, we extend the extant choice closure literature by proposing that in a choice domain that requires online customers to choose one option from other comparable alternatives via comparisons, the adequacy of such comparisons prior to a purchase decision improves their understanding about their chosen option once the choice is made. This perception leads the postchoice product comparisons to be unnecessary and subsequently nudges choice closure. We further theorize that horizontal and vertical product display orientations influence people’s ability to effectively compare products before making a choice, drawing insights from eye and vision research.

## Product Display Orientation: Horizonta Display Advantage

The eye and vision research has suggested that different orientations render different areas of view fields, which refers to the number of degrees a visual angle extends during a stable fixation of the eyes (Strasburger and Po¨ppel 2002). The general findings from this research stream, which we use as a basis for our theorizing, propose the horizontal display advantage: the human field of view is much wider in the horizontal direction than in the vertical direction. Specifically, whereas humans have a forward-facing horizontal field of view (without any eye movements) of slightly over 210 degrees, the vertical range of their view field is only 150 degrees (Dagnelie 2011). Because the shape of our vision field is more easily extended in the horizontal direction than in the vertical direction, people tend to estimate a vertical line as being longer than an equally long horizontal line (a phenomenon called vertical-horizontal illusion; Ku¨ nnapas 1957).

Corroborating the horizontal display advantage, several streams of research found that the wider view field in the horizontal versus vertical direction can enhance people’s capacities of information scanning, which allows them to more efficiently recognize a target in the information if the information is arranged horizontally rather than vertically. For instance, Williams (1966) in his seminal work showed that people can recognize a target number more quickly when the array that contains the number is presented horizontally compared with vertically. Likewise, people can recognize more character spaces in horizontal lists (i.e., up to 10 charac ter spaces) than in vertical lists (i.e., 4–5 characte spaces) of the same length during a single eye fixation (Ojanpa¨a ¨ et al. 2002). Extending this theory to marketing, Deng et al. (2016) showed that people can scan an assortment of product options more easily when the assortment is displayed in a horizontal (versus vertical) orientation, allowing them to recognize more product options in a given time and thus not only perceive, but also choose more variety from the assortment.

Although Deng et al. (2016) extended the investiga tion to product arrays, their primary focus was on choice variety—the choice of multiple items from a product array in an offline context—through the mechanism of visually scanning and recognizing the available product options. In one supplementary study (i.e., their study 5), they also explored choice satisfaction when customers had to choose a single item from multiple alternatives, similar to our research context. However, their aim was to delineate a boundary condition of their focal mechanism by empirically demonstrating that the differential effect between horizontal and vertical orientation was attenuated in such a singlechoice context under the circumstance that customers already have clear preferences (thus allowing participants to make a choice directly based on the existing preferences without needing to compare products). Nonetheless, the single-choice context was not their primary research focus, and, more importantly, their work did not address product comparisons as the key underlying mechanism through which customers choose one product out of multiple comparable ones. In contrast, our research focuses on an e-commerce choice context that requires people to select one product from other comparable ones without clear preferences, making product comparisons both important and necessary for making a choice. Because the theoretical mechanism of information recognition discussed by Deng et al. (2016) is not necessarily applicable to the choice context of the current study, we offer distinct theoretical reasoning in our hypothesis development.

## Hypotheses Development Product Display Orientation and Choice Satisfaction

We propose that customers will experience a higher level of satisfaction with a chosen option if they choose from a horizontally displayed product assortment than from a vertically displayed one on e-commerce websites. The rationale for this proposition is developed based on integration of the theory of horizontal display advantage (Ojanpa¨a ¨ et al. 2002, Dagnelie 2011) with the theory of choice closure (Gu et al. 2013, 2018). Specifically, the theory of horizontal display advantage suggests that people’s view field is wider in the horizontal direction than in the vertical one. Consequently, even if the same product assortment is provided, customers can scan more product options from this assortment during each stable eye fixation when it’s displayed horizontally than vertically.

We argue that the capability to scan more product options during each stable eye fixation can afford more product comparisons. Extant studies on decision making have concluded that as the number of options scanned increases, people make more pairwise comparisons (e.g., Tversky and Shafir 1992, Iyengar and Lepper 2000). For example, if an individual can scan only three options (option A, B, or C) in the vision field, this individual would make pairwise product comparisons only three times: between options A and B, between options A and C, and between options B and C. If four options are scanned, however, this individual would make product comparisons six times to fully examine these options. By making more product comparisons, people can better discern the differences between each option and better understand the option they eventually choose. Consequently, after they make a choice, the improved understanding of the chosen option will make it unnecessary to further compare it with other forgone ones and will thus elicit a feeling of choice closure at the postchoice stage.

Putting these predictions together, we suggest that a horizontal display better triggers a sense of finality than a vertical display because the increased number of options scanned in the former situation leads online customers to make more product comparisons prior to choice, which improves their understanding of the chosen option and perception that the choice is resolved upon adequate product comparisons once the choice is made (Inbar et al. 2011). In contrast, because a vertical display leads to fewer options to be scanned during each stable eye fixation, customers make fewer compar isons in this case. As a result, they may experience less choice closure and resist moving on at the postchoice stage. Our proposition that more product comparisons made within a horizontally displayed assortment prior to a purchase decision increases the perception of such a decision as settled and finalized after the decision, along with that perceived choice closure positively influences choice satisfaction (Gu et al. 2013, 2018), suggests that a higher level of choice satisfaction can be achieved when the product options are presented hori zontally (versus vertically). Formally stated:

Hypothesis 1. Online customers will achieve higher choice satisfaction when they choose among an assortment displayed horizontally than vertically on e-commerce websites.

Hypothesis 2. The effect of product display orientation on choice satisfaction results from a higher level of choice closure triggered by a horizontal (versus vertical) product display.

## Cue of Finality Mitigates Vertical Disadvantage via Attention Focusing

Our theorizing has suggested that presenting product alternatives in a horizontal direction would facilitate product comparisons prior to a purchase decision and, once the decision is made, generate more feelings of choice closure, leading online customers to feel more satisfied with the chosen option compared with that in a vertical direction. However, changing an established display orientation can be costly for online retailers, as even a small change in web design can incur significant expenses. Therefore, it is crucial to understand how to mitigate the negative impact of vertical displays on choice satisfaction if this orientation has already been adopted for certain reasons.

Given that focused attention facilitates decision makers’ information processing (including comparisons) (Atkinson and Shiffrin 1968, Greenwald and Leavitt 1984), we propose that the cue of finality may mitigate the disadvantage of vertical product display orientation in promoting choice satisfaction by fostering attention to product comparisons prior to choice and inducing a sense of choice closure after the choice. The cue of finality refers to a visual sign or verbal note— such as the grayed arrow or a text noting “the end” on a product display page—that indicates the finality of the product options available in e-commerce. Although research has addressed behavioral or visual triggers of closure delivered after people make a choice via the metaphorical linkage between these triggers for closure and choice closure (Gu et al. 2013, 2018), the cue of finality is provided during a customer’s decision-making process that cannot function through the metaphorical process, but through fostering attention.

Specifically, the literature on cognitive resources (e.g., Payne 1982, Russo and Dosher 1983) has consistently shown that as people have limited cognitive resources, they allocate cognitive resources judiciously to different tasks at hand. To this end, we reason that a cue of finality indicates to the customer that no more options follow, therefore allowing them to focus their attentional resources solely on the set of currently presented options. Otherwise, they may reserve their cognitive resources for processing other alternatives that may be presented on the next page and even imagining what these alternatives would be like (Mogilner et al. 2012). Such focused attention can help decision makers better perceive and elaborate on the details associated with the attended options via neural excitation (Janiszewsk et al. 2012), thereby enabling them to compare options to a larger extent before making a choice than otherwise. Increased focused attention that aids product comparisons can lead customers to believe that once their decision is made, they should close the decision-making process and stop making further product comparisons. We therefore posit that the differential effect of horizontal versus vertical display orientations on choice satisfaction would be weakened or reduced when the cue of finality is available. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. The disadvantaged effect of vertical product display on choice satisfaction in comparison with horizontal product display is weakened when a cue of finality is available in product display.

## Study Overview

To examine our hypotheses, we conducted five studies either through an online panel on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) or in a laboratory setting. In Study 1, we provided direct evidence for Hypotheses 1 and 2, proposing that participants were more likely to perceive their choice as closed and thus felt more satisfied with their chosen product when the choice was made from a horizontal product array than a vertical one on e-commerce websites. In Study 2, we further validated the underlying mechanism of the focal effect by showing a sequential link from product display orientation to the number of product comparisons, to feelings of choice closure, and to behavioral evidence of choice satisfaction (i.e., actual product switch). In doing so, we offered eye-tracking evidence for the number of product comparisons and found that participants did make a greater number of comparisons, as indicated by a larger number of between-option eye saccades (rapid movements that abruptly change the point of fixation between each product option) in the horizontal than in the vertical condition. In Study 3, we affirmed the underlying mechanism via a moderation approach by showing that the focal direct effect of product display orientation on choice satisfaction would be removed if a finality cue was provided to trigger participants’ product comparisons and then choice closure (Hypothesis 3). In Study 4, we replicated the focal effect while controlling for a set of variables that are closely related to prior purchase experience (e.g., frequency of past purchases for hand creams, frequency of past e-commerce purchases, and frequency of past purchases on mobile devices) to show the robustness of our finding. In the final Study 5, we significantly increased the number of options provided to simulate a real online setting and used a binary behavioral indicator of choice satisfaction (i.e., an actual choice of cancellation opportunity) to show the ecological validity of our finding.

In all five studies, participants were randomly assigned either to the vertical or horizontal display condition and were asked to make a choice from a set of comparable (equally attractive) product options. The product information was exactly the same in the two conditions, except for product alternatives displayed either in a horizontal or vertical orientation (see Figure 1 for the specific stimuli used in each study). Moreover, because individuals exercise more effort for the horizontal scrolling than the vertical scrolling, we presented the product alternatives on a single page, so that partici pants did not have to scroll left-right or up-down to view all items and thus their physical effort was held constant across conditions.

We recruited 236 participants for a two-condition design in Study 1 and 387 participants for a fourcondition design in Study 3, both of which ran on MTurk. We recruited 88 participants for a two-condition design in Study 2, 82 participants for a two-condition design in Study 4, and 155 participants for a two-condition design in Study 5, all of which were conducted in the laboratory setting. All the samples exceeded the standard suggested by Simmons et al. (2011), which posits at least 30 observations per condition in laboratory studies and 50 observations per condition in online studies.

## Study 1: The Focal Effect

In the first study, we aim to provide evidence for the main hypothesis that a horizontal display of product alternatives can increase customers’ choice satisfaction more than a vertical product display, and customers perceived choice closure in their purchase decision underlies the effect. Moreover, we attempt to rule out the alternative explanation associated with perceived variety. Prior research has found that a horizontal product display leads customers to perceive more variety in the product alternatives than a vertical product display (Deng et al. 2016). This can render the illusion that the choice is made from a larger choice set (Mogilner et al. 2008), thus leading customers to feel more satisfied with the choice. We therefore seek to evaluate this possibility in our research as well.

## Method

A total of 236 participants (52.97% male; mean age � 38) were recruited through MTurk to take part in the experiment and were given compensation of U.S. dollars (USD) 0.60. (2016), we excluded 23 participants who failed the attention check question: “This question aims to examine your attention paid to the survey. Please click ‘4’ on the scale items that are labeled from 1 to 9.” This left 213 participants (53.52% male; mean age � 39) for data analysis. Participants were randomly assigned either to the horizontal or to the vertical display conditions.

For the first step, participants were asked to imagine that they wanted to buy a mouse pad from an online retailer and needed to search product information on its e-commerce website. After that, participants in the horizontal condition saw five equally attractive mouse pads displayed horizontally on the web page, whereas those in the vertical condition saw the same five mouse pads displayed vertically. They were then asked to evaluate the mouse pads at their own pace and to indi cate the one they liked best. In order to make their choice more consequential, we told participants that they would have a 1/50 chance of receiving the mouse pad they actually chose as extra compensation.<sup>3</sup> After making a choice, participants indicated their choice satisfaction by responding to a single-item scale adopted from prior literature (Mogilner et al. 2008, Gu et al. 2013) based on a nine-point Likert scale: “How satisfied are you with the mouse pad that you chose?” (1 � not at all, 9 � very much).

After that, participants reported the extent to which they felt closure toward their choice decision by responding to three items validated by Beike and Wirth-Beaumont (2005): “After choosing the mouse pad, to what extent do you feel you have reached closure about your choice?”; “After choosing the mouse pad, to what extent do you perceive that decision as settled?”; and “After choosing the mouse pad, to what extent do you think of that decision as behind you?” (1 � not at all, 9 � very much). These three items were averaged to form a composite index of participants’ perceived choice closure (α � 0.83). Finally, we asked participants to indicate how much variety/distinctiveness there was in the product display (Deng et al. 2016) from 1 (very little) to 9 (very much) as a measure of perceived variety (r � 0.51). We summarized all the measuring scales used in Studies 1–5 in Online Appendix C and reported the measurement statistics (e.g., average (AVE), composite reliability (CR), correlation, etc.) in Online Appendix D.

## Discussion

Study 1 provides direct evidence to support Hypotheses 1 and 2: that a horizontal (versus vertical) product display increased participants’ perceived choice closure in decision making and subsequently enhanced their satisfaction with the chosen option. ## Study 2: Eye-Tracking Evidence for Underlying Mechanism

Using eye-tracking techniques, the primary goal of Study 2 is to provide more objective evidence in support of our proposed underlying mechanism: that customers make more sufficient product comparisons before making a choice and subsequently feel a greater sense of choice closure and more satisfied with their chosen option after making the choice when choosing from a product array displayed horizontally than vertically. Given that human visual comparisons are invariably accompanied by eye jumps and movements (e.g., Yarbus 1967, Russo and Leclerc 1994, Zelinsky 2001), previous literature (e.g., Huang et al. 2019a) measured the eye jumps between different alternatives to trace participants’ product comparison processes. Following the literature, we measure participants’ eye saccades between product alternatives—jumps of their visual focus from one alternative to another—at the time of evaluation as an indicator of the number of product comparisons they made.

Moreover, given that people’s intention and actual behavior are not always consistent (Ajzen 1985), we also use participants’ actual product switch as a behavioral indicator of choice satisfaction (Mogilner et al. 2012, Gu et al. 2013, Ma and Roese 2014). The rationale is that if a horizontal (versus vertical) product display leads to more choice satisfaction, participants in the horizontal condition should stick to their originally chosen product, rather than switch to other alternatives.

Finally, the evidence for a sequential link from product display orientation to number of product comparisons, to perceived choice closure, and finally to actual product switching is still lacking. Study 2 therefore aims to provide empirical evidence for such a link.

## Method

A total of 88 students (45.45% male; mean age � 21) from a local university in South China participated in the laboratory study and were compensated with a small gift (a coffee mug). Participants were randomly assigned to the horizontal condition or the vertical condition.

Apparatus and Materials. Participants were asked to imagine that they were going to buy a coffee mug by choosing from five products on an e-commerce website. As in Study 1, participants in the horizontal condition saw five equally attractive mugs displayed in a horizontal orientation, whereas those in the vertical condition saw the mugs displayed vertically. In order to increase their involvement and make subsequent decisions more consequential, participants were told that they could keep the coffee mug they selected at the end of the experiment as the gift. Eye movements were recorded using the Gazepoint GP3 eye tracker with no head or chin gear (for the validity of using Gazepoint GP3 in recording eye movement, see Zugal and Pinggera 2014, Cuve et al. 2022). Eye positions were sampled at 60 hertz with a 0.5–1 degree of visual angle accuracy.

Procedure. Participants were tested individually. Upon arriving, they were told that they would evaluate several coffee mugs on the computer and that a built-in camera on the computer monitor would record their behaviors to ensure the quality of data collected. They were instructed not to move around in their seat and to keep their eyes on the screen once the experiment began. The eye-tracking device was calibrated by asking participants to focus on five red dots that were presented sequentially on different areas of the computer screen (for the mechanism behind the eye-tracking calibration, see Pfeiffer et al. 2020). After the calibration, participants began to evaluate the five coffee mugs shown on a 15-inch computer screen.<sup>6</sup> They were allowed to control the time they spent on examining the coffee mugs and were asked to indicate which coffee mug they would like to choose. We recorded their actual eye movement before they indicated their choice.

We then measured participants’ perceived choice closure by asking them to respond to the same scale used in Study 1. These three items were averaged to form a single index of perceived choice closure (α � 0.68). Finally, instead of using the self-reported choice satisfaction scales employed in Study 1, we assessed whether participants were satisfied with their chosen option by designing a scenario to observe their actual switching behaviors toward an alternative equivalent option they were offered. This approach is based on the common knowledge that customers who feel less satis fied are more likely to withdraw from the original choice and to switch to other alternative options (Mogil ner et al. 2012, Gu et al. 2013, Ma and Roese 2014). Specifically, at the end of the experiment, participants were asked to wait for the experimenter to get them the coffee mug they had selected. After a moment, the experimenter returned and told them that the coffee mug they had selected happened to be out of stock, and they would have to wait for several days to receive it. Partici pants could then choose between receiving the amount of money equivalent to the coffee mug cost and waiting for their chosen coffee mug to be delivered. Participants who chose the second option were then asked to leave their addresses and told that their selected coffee mugs would be delivered to them upon availability.

Scoring. The eye tracker recorded participants’ eye movements while viewing the coffee mugs. The eye movements were categorized into five areas of interest (AOIs) and defined a priori, with each AOI associated with a single coffee mug. Eye saccades (the eye jumps made during the product-evaluation process) was recorded by the eye-tracking device. Specifically, we followed prior research on eye movements (e.g., Shi et al. 2013, Bera et al. 2019, Huang et al. 2019a) to distinguish three saccadic metrics: (1) the eye jumps made between product options (i.e., between-AOIs saccades), (2) the eye jumps made within a specific product option (i.e., within-AOIs saccades), and (3) the eye jumps made between product options and the empty space (i.e., run saccades).

Whereas researchers view the first type of saccadic movements as related to information comparison, they consider the other two as related to “information integration” (e.g., Johnson and Mayer 2012, Lin and Lin 2014). To this end, our theory would only predict the effect of product display orientation on the first type of saccadic movements that better represent product comparisons, but suggest a null effect on the other two types of saccadic metrics.

## Discussion

Results of Study 2 provide more objective eye-tracking evidence for the underlying mechanism of adequate comparisons and, subsequently, perceived choice closure. Specifically, the results show that participants more frequently shifted their eyes between product alternatives (while holding their fixation duration constant) when the products were displayed horizontally versus vertically. This suggests that they could make more sufficient comparisons before making a choice and, subsequently, perceive a higher level of choice closure after choosing in the horizontal condition, rather than in the vertical condition. Moreover, as participants in the horizontal condition performed fewer switching behaviors than those in the vertical condition, the observed results in Study 1 are replicated: horizontal versus vertical product display led to greater choice satisfaction, albeit using an actual-behavior indicator. By constructing an actual decision-making scenario, Study 2 also demonstrates the external validity of our findings. Finally, by demonstrating a sequential mediation chain, this study offers more solid evidence that horizontal (versus vertical) product display facilitated participants’ comparisons between product alternatives before a choice. This led them to feel that their chosen option was made based on sufficient comparisons and should thus be closed without further reconsideration after the choice. Consequently, participants felt more satisfied with their chosen option and were less likely to withdraw from the original option when presented with an alternative option.

## Study 3: Does a Cue of Finality Matter?

The purposes of Study 3 are twofold. First, we aim to provide further evidence for the underlying process associated with choice closure via a moderation approach. Specifically, our theory suggests that a horizontal (versus vertical) display of product alternatives can enhance customers’ choice satisfaction via psychologically closing their purchase decision (Hypotheses 1 and 2). In Study 3, we examine whether the effect of product display orientation on choice satisfaction is attenuated when customers can achieve choice closure in some other way (e.g., providing a text note “The end”) (Hypothesis 3). Moreover, by identifying a boundary condition of the focal effect, we also attempt to figure out how to alleviate the less advantageous effects of a vertical display if it has already been adopted for practical reasons and, thus, provide valuable managerial implications for online retailers on website design. We return to this implication in detail in General Discussion.

## Method

A total of 387 participants (56.07% male; mean age � 39) were again recruited through MTurk to take part in the experiment, with compensation of USD 0.60. A total of 36 participants who failed the same attention check questions as in Study 1 were excluded, leaving 351 participants (56.13% male; mean age � 39). They were randomly assigned to cells of a two (product display: horizontal versus vertical) × two (ending mark: present versus absent) between-subjects design.

The procedure of Study 3 was similar to that of Study 1. Participants were asked to imagine that they wanted to buy a mouse pad from an online retailer. They searched product information on the retailers’ e-commerce website and found five mouse pad options. Participants in the horizontal condition viewed the product alternatives in a horizontal display, whereas those in the vertical condition saw the same set of product alternatives presented vertically. Where the ending mark was present, participants saw the product alternatives end with a text note: “The end.” As in Study 1, participants were asked to indicate a choice they liked best and were given the incentive of having a 1/50 chance to receive the product they actually chose after the experiment.<sup>7</sup> They then indicated their choice satisfaction by responding to the items adopted from Gu et al. (2018) based on a nine-point Likert scale (1 � not at all, 9 � very much; r � 0.53).

The planned contrast confirms that when an ending mark was absent, participants were more satisfied with the chosen option if the products were displayed in a horizontal orientation $( M = 7 . 5 6 ,$ $S D = 1 . 0 \dot { 2 } )$ compared with a vertical orientation (M � $6 . 9 9 , S D = 1 . 3 3 ; \bar { F } \left( { 1 , 3 4 7 } \right) = 1 1 . 2 9 , p = 0 . 0 0 1 ;$ Cohen’s d � 0.486), thus replicating the results of prior studies. However, when the ending mark was present, the difference between the two conditions did not reach a level of significance (M � 7.57, SD � 1.12 versus $M _ { \mathrm { v e r t i c a l } } { = } 7 . 4 8 , S D = 1 . 0 3 ; F < 1 )$

## Discussion

Study 3 provides further evidence for the underlying mechanisms of choice closure via a moderation approach (Spencer et al. 2005). Specifically, our first two studies confirm that a horizontal (versus vertical) product display led customers to experience a higher level of choice closure; they thus felt more satisfaction with their chosen option made from a horizontal array rather than from a vertical one. However, the current study implies that merely providing an ending mark in the product display can help customers conduct more efficient product comparisons before making a choice and perceive the choice to be closed afterward, which subsequently improves their choice satisfaction over and above the impact of product display orientation. Moreover, this study also suggests valuable practical implications. That is, when online retailers have to display products vertically in some situations (e.g., to fit a portrait screen size or for aesthetic purposes), they can at least provide a cue of finality to minimize the less advantageous consequences of vertical display in choice satisfaction.

## Study 4: Choice Satisfaction Differs from Use Satisfaction

Study 4 aims to show the robustness of the empirical findings for Hypothesis 1 (the effect of display orientation on choice satisfaction) by controlling for variables closely related to prior purchase experience, including frequency of past purchases, frequency of past e-commerce purchases, and frequency of past purchases on mobile devices (Houdeshel and Watson 1987, Shiv and Huber 2000, Palmer 2002). It also aims to rule out two alternative explanations associated with feelings of being overwhelmed and perceived enjoyment. First, studies have found that a horizontal product display is easier to process than a vertical display (Deng et al. 2016). As a result, online customers may be less overwhelmed in processing product information and thus feel more satisfied with the chosen option in the first situation than in the second one.

Second, ease of information processing may also render a higher level of enjoyment in product evaluations (Im et al. 2010, Mosteller et al. 2014). Subsequently, customers may misattribute the positive experience generated from processing fluency to the product they chose (Lee and Aaker 2004, Kim et al. 2009, Lee and Higgins 2009, Lee et al. 2010, Zhao and Xie 2011, Huang et al. 2017) and feel more satisfied with their decision. We rule out these alternative possibilities in Study 4.

By implementing Study 4, we also seek to empirically demonstrate the differential effects of product display orientation on choice satisfaction and on user satisfaction, affirming that product display orientation affects choice satisfaction, but not user satisfaction. Finally, Study 4 generalizes the findings from the first three studies, which use search goods (mouse pads and coffee mugs), by testing the hypotheses with experience goods (hand creams).

## Method

A total of 82 students (68.29% male; mean age � 19) from a local university in South China participated in the study for a small monetary reward (RMB 5). As in prior studies, participants were randomly assigned to the horizontal condition or the vertical condition. Unlike the previous studies, however, Study 4 adopted a two-stage experimental design. In the first stage, participants were asked to imagine that they would like to buy a hand cream and to search product information on an e-commerce website via a tablet. They were then provided with an iPad, on which they found five equally attractive hand cream options presented, each of which included a product picture along with the product name. In the horizontal condition, participants saw the hand cream options presented in a horizontal orientation, whereas in the vertical condition, they saw the hand cream options presented in a vertical orientation.

After reading the product web page, participants were asked to evaluate the hand cream options at their own pace and to indicate the one they liked best. After making a choice, participants reported their choice satisfaction by responding to three seven-point items used by Ma and Roese (2014) (α � 0.89): “How satisfied are you with your choice?”; “How much do you like your choice?”; and “How regretful do you feel about your choice?” (reverse coded). They also reported anticipated product performance by responding to four seven-point items (α � 0.84; adopted from Guan et al. 2023): “I have a high expectation of the quality of the selected hand cream”; “I expect things would not go wrong if I choose this hand cream”; “I expect I would like to recommend this hand cream to my friends”; and “I expect this hand cream fits my need well.”

In the second stage, participants received their chosen hand cream and were invited to try it in another room. Two participants who chose not to try the hand cream product were excluded, leaving 80 participants (67.50% male; mean age � 19) for data analysis. The remaining 80 participants indicated their perceived product performance after experiencing it by responding to four seven-point items $( \alpha = 0 . 9 4 ;$ adopted from Guan et al. 2023): “After using the hand cream, I think it has a high quality”; “After using the hand cream, I think choosing it is not a wrong choice”; “After using the hand cream, I will recommend it to my friends”; and “After using the hand cream, I think it fits my needs well.” Based on previous literature (Diehl and Poynor 2010, Lin et al. 2018, Guan et al. 2023), participants’ use satisfaction can be measured by the difference between anticipated product performance before usage and perceived product performance after usage.

In addition to the three main constructs, participants also rated several other perceptions when they evaluated the products, including feelings of being overwhelmed (“How difficult/distracted did you feel when evaluating the products?”; r � 0.71; Jia et al. 2017) and perceived enjoyment (“How enjoyable/ pleasurable/interesting did you feel when evaluating the products?”; α � 0.81; Iyengar and Lepper 2000). Finally, participants reported their past purchase experience, including their frequency of past purchases for hand cream products, frequency of past e-commerce purchases, and frequency of past purchases on mobile devices. Their responses to these questions are included in data analysis as covariates.

## Results

Choice Satisfaction. A one-way ANCOVA was conducted on participants’ choice satisfaction as a function of product display orientation with their frequency of past purchases, frequency of past e-commerce purchases, and frequency of past purchases on mobile devices as covariates. We conducted a similar one-way ANCOVA on participants’ use satisfaction while controlling for the frequency of past purchases for hand creams, the frequency of past e-commerce purchases, and the frequency of past purchases on mobile devices. In addition, although the impact of past pur chases on mobile devices was significant $( F ( 1 , 7 5 ) = { \bar { 4 } } . 5 4 ,$ p < 0.05), neither the impact of past purchases for hand creams (F < 1) nor that of past e-commerce purchases $( F ( 1 , 7 5 ) = 2 . 5 9 , p = 0 . 1 1 )$ was significant.

## Discussion

In conclusion, Study 4 reaffirms the effect of display orientation on choice satisfaction after controlling for their frequency of past purchases for hand creams, frequency of past e-commerce purchases, and frequency of past purchases on mobile devices. Meanwhile, the study rules out two alternative mechanisms: feelings of being overwhelmed and perceived enjoyment. The results of Study 4 also provide empirical evidence for the divergent effects of product display orientation on choice satisfaction and on use satisfaction. The results show that participants felt more satisfied with a chosen hand cream even before use when they chose it from a horizontally displayed product assortment rather than a vertically displayed one, which replicates the results from prior studies; however, they did not feel more satisfied with the use of the product in the first situation than in the second one. Finally, the focal effect can be applied to both search goods and experience goods, therefore providing more direct evidence for its generalization across different types of products.

## Study 5: Ecological Validity of the Focal Effect

The final Study 5 attempts to strengthen the ecological validity of the focal effect from three perspectives. First, in all prior studies, we asked participants to choose from a relatively small product assortment (i.e., five product options). One might wonder whether the focal effect can be generalized to a more realistic e-commerce setting, where there are always a large number of product options available. In Study 5, we therefore offer a much larger product assortment for participants to choose from (i.e., 24 product options) in order to simulate the real e-commerce setting. Research (e.g., Iyengar and Lepper 2000, Chernev 2003, Whitley et al. 2018) has shown that 24 options represents a relatively large assortment size.

Moreover, in all prior studies, product options were displayed in a single line (either horizontally or vertically) due to the small assortment size. Once the size becomes larger, it is impossible to display all products in a single line on one web page. Perhaps for this reason, an increasing number of online retailers (e.g., Amazon) have begun to adopt the grid format for product display (i.e., product matrix), a display format that has also been employed in existing literature (e.g., Deng et al. 2016). We therefore display products in a grid format in Study 5.

Finally, all prior studies, except Study 2, relied on the self-reported item scales to measure choice satisfaction, which are intentional in nature. Given that people’s intention and actual behavior are not always consistent (Ajzen 1985), Study 5 attempts to evaluate choice satisfaction using a binary behavioral measure to show the generalizability of the focal effect to actual behavior. Specifically, participants would indicate whether they would like to pay for a nominal cost to obtain the opportunity for canceling their choice. To reiterate, our theory suggests that a horizontal display leads customers to feel more satisfied with their choice than a vertical one. If so, we expect customers who make a choice from a horizontally displayed product assortment to be less willing to obtain the opportunity for choice cancellation (because they feel more satisfied with their selection) than those who choose from a vertically displayed product assortment.

## Method

A total of 155 participants (43.23% male; mean age � 19) from a local university in South China participated in exchange for a small gift (a fridge magnet priced at RMB 10, equivalent to USD 1.4), plus an extra nominal payment (RMB 1). As in prior studies, participants were randomly assigned to the horizontal condition or the vertical condition. They were asked to imagine that they would like to buy a fridge magnet and search product information on an online retailer’s website using an iPad. They then viewed 24 equally attractive fridge magnets displayed on the iPad. In line with the procedure used by Deng et al. (2016), the 24 fridge magnets were arranged in a matrix of three rows × eight columns in the horizontal condition, whereas they were arranged in a matrix of eight rows × three columns in the vertical condition.

After viewing the products, participants were asked to choose the one they liked best and were informed that they would actually receive the chosen product shipped directly from the online retailer several days later. Such a design resembles the typical online shop ping procedure, in which customers place an order online, but receive the product at a later time. We then assessed participants’ satisfaction with their chosen option by creating a scenario to observe their willingness to retain the opportunity for choice cancellation. Specifically, each participant was provided with an opportunity to cancel and change their selection upon receiving the product, albeit at a nominal cost (50% of their extra payment). Participants were asked to indicate whether they would like to retain the cancellation opportunity by responding to a binary scale (1 � yes, 0 � no). Their responses served as a behavioral measure of choice satisfaction.

## Results

Choice Satisfaction. Our descriptive statistics suggest that participants in the horizontal condition were less likely to retain the opportunity for cancellation (12 out of 79, or 15.19%) than were those in the vertical condition (22 out of 76, or 28.95%).<sup>8</sup>

Following established guidelines (Torres-Reyna 2014, Leeper 2017, Schuetz et al. 2020), we ran a binary logistic regression model with product display orientation (1 � horizontal, 0 � vertical) as the independent variable and actual selection of cancellation opportunity as the dependent variable (1 � retain the cancellation opportunity, 0 � do not retain the cancellation opportunity), while controlling for participant gender (1 � male, 0 � female) and age. We assessed goodness-of-fit through the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test. The H-L test yielded a χ<sup>2</sup> (7) of 1.02 and was insignificant (p � 0.99), indicating that the model fits the data well, as there were no significant differences between the observed and predicated outcomes. Moreover, we found that product display orientation had a negative impact on participants selection of the cancellation opportunity (β � �0.81, SE � 0.40, Wald χ<sup>2</sup> � 4.00, p < 0.05; Cohen’s d � 0.445). That is, a one-unit increase in the standardized horizontal displays decreased selection of the cancellation opportunity by 44.61% (odds ratio � 0.45). The probability indicated that participants in the horizontal condition had a 30.84% probability of selecting the opportunity for cancellation. We also found a discrete decrease of 13.40% in the probability of selecting the opportunity for cancellation when including this condition (average marginal effect of the treatment). Collectively, these results suggest that participants were less likely to retain the opportunity to cancel their chosen product in the horizontal condition than in the vertical condition, thus suggesting a higher level of choice satisfaction in the first situation than in the second one.

## Discussion

The results of Study 5 again support Hypothesis 1 that a horizontal display increases customer choice satisfaction compared with a vertical display. These results therefore replicate previous findings, using an increased number of product options that more closely resemble a real e-commerce setting and a more consequential behavioral measure of choice satisfaction. This, in turn, enhances our confidence in the ecological validity of the focal effect.

## General Discussion

Drawing from eye and vision research and the theory of choice closure, this paper offers a new and needed treatment of whether, how, and why product display orientation can impact customers’ choice satisfaction on e-commerce websites. The results from Study 1 show that participants were more likely to feel satisfied with a chosen product if they chose it from a product array displayed horizontally than vertically, and this effect was driven by the greater feelings of choice closure brought by the horizontal product display. Moreover, Study 2 provides more objective eye-tracking evidence that participants did make more product comparisons before making a choice (as indicated by a greater number of between-option eye jumps) and were more likely to perceive their choice to be closed and to feel greater choice satisfaction (as indicated by the lower rate of actual switching). Study 3 reveals that the effect of display orientation on choice satisfaction was attenuated when a cue of finality was provided to draw attention to the currently presented options and then facilitate product comparisons to reach choice closure. Study 4 supports the robustness of the focal effect while controlling for variables closely related to prior purchase experience and ruling out several alternative explanations. The final Study 5 validates the ecological validity of the focal effect by showing that it can hold in a context that more closely resembles the real e-commerce setting (with a larger product assortment) and with a more consequential behavioral measure of choice satisfaction.

## Theoretical Implications

Our research contributes to the literature in several key aspects. First, it contributes to our understanding of online customer choice satisfaction—an important, but overlooked, topic of IS research—by studying what interface design features might facilitate customers’ formation of such satisfaction. Specifically, prior IS research (e.g., De et al. 2013, Yang and Xiong 2019) has focused on customer satisfaction upon actual usage of a purchased product (termed as use satisfaction in our study). This prior research has relied on expectation confirmation theory (Oliver 1977, 1980) to extensively investigate how to design e-commerce interfaces for online retailers to improve use satisfaction. Our research, however, focuses on what we term as choice satisfaction rather than use satisfaction. Moreover, drawing from the novel theoretical lens of choice closure theory and the eye and vision research, our results confirm that a horizontal (versus vertical) display leads customers to make more adequate product comparisons before a choice, which can render the experience of choice closure and increase choice satisfaction after the choice. The current study, to the best of our knowledge, is among the first to systematically investigate choice satisfaction in e-commerce and, in doing so, extends the theory of choice closure to the IS field.

Moreover, our research adds to the prior literature on interface design (especially in the domain of product display), an increasingly important stream of IS research, by examining a common, but understudied, design feature of product display—product display orientation. Although this stream of research (e.g., Liu et al. 2019, Yang and Xiong 2019, Yuan and Dennis 2019) has documented the effect of various interface designs in product display on customers’ decision-making processes that lead to a purchase decision, none of them have focused on the impact of product display orientation on customers’ decision-making process that determines their satisfaction with the purchase decision, especially in the e-commerce context that allows one to choose among multiple comparable products. Ou research is therefore the first to examine product display orientation and its impact on customer choice satisfaction at the postdecision stage.

Our study also contributes to the theory of choice closure by establishing customers’ decision-making process (i.e., product comparisons in our study) before a purchase decision as a meaningful mechanism underlying the impact of product display orientation on choice closure in the online purchase context. Prior research (Gu et al. 2013, 2018) has shown that choice closure can be directly marked and elicited by either behavioral or visual triggers of closure after a purchase decision is made and suggested that metaphorical thinking underlies the linkage between behavioral/sensory triggers of closure and choice closure. Different from this well established perspective, we identify product display orientation as a nuanced factor affecting choice closure through product comparisons, a decision-making process before the purchase decision. In addition, our research extends the theory of choice closure by establishing the cue of finality as an effective moderator for the impact of product display orientation on choice closure and revealing the underlying mechanism of fostering attention that also strengthens product comparisons and then choice closure.

Finally, our research extends the applicability of the horizontal display advantage from the eye and vision research to the e-commerce setting and goes beyond the most related investigation by Deng et al. (2016). Whereas Deng et al. (2016; except study 5, which will be detailed below) mainly focused on choice variety of multiple options chosen from an assortment, we focus on choice satisfaction when people can only choose a single option in an assortment, a more practical and common e-commerce purchasing scenario. Furthermore, whereas Deng et al. (2016) drew on horizontal display advantage theory to suggest that consumers recognition of more options in the horizontal (versus vertical) display contributes to their perceived variety and consequential choice variety, our study posits product comparisons as the unique mechanism explaining perceived closure with a single choice that eventually leads to choice satisfaction. Taken together, although a horizontal (versus vertical) display of a product assortment allows people to recognize more options in the work of Deng et al. (2016), it functions by facilitating product comparisons and perceived choice closure in our research, thus shedding light on a theoretically distinctive logic.

Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that study 5 in the work of Deng et al. (2016), which we mentioned earlier in the paper, reveals a null effect of display orientation on customer satisfaction with a single choice. This is interesting, as the result is in contrast to our findings at the first glance. However, a careful analysis of that study suggests a very important circumstance for the null effect—that is, participants in that study had a clear prior preference for the product options. In such a circumstance, we reason that product options appear less comparable (i.e., one option is clearly preferred over the others; Tversky and Shafir 1992, Dhar 1997), allowing participants to make a choice directly based on the existing preferences without needing to compare products. By contrast, our work addresses a preference-neutral context, in which customers need to choose one product out of multiple comparable ones, thus making product comparisons not only an important, but also a necessary step for choosing. As such, a horizontal display that better facilitates the productcomparison process leads to a higher level of choice closure and eventually greater choice satisfaction in our case, but does not have an impact on choice satisfaction in the context of Deng et al. (2016). To this end, we believe that customers’ prior preference may serve as a plausible boundary condition of our focal effect, and we leave future research to formally examine it.

## Practical Implications

Our current findings offer actionable guidance for e-commerce websites on how to display products in different orientation patterns to improve customers choice satisfaction via choice closure prompts and to decrease order cancellation, a challenge that has long perplexed online retailers. Our research suggests that if e-commerce platforms are designing a new interface, they may consider adopting a horizontal display as a starting point.

Nevertheless, when a vertical design has been adopted for certain reasons, online retailers should at least know how to minimize the less advantageous impacts caused by that design. For example, in some situations, online retailers may need to consider the tremendous cost of even a minimal change in display formats—including financial investment, time cost, customers’ confusion caused by the change, and so forth—if a vertical display has already been adopted. In other cases, online retailers, such as Amazon and JD.com, display products horizontally on their desktop websites, but turn to vertical display on mobile websites to fit the portrait mobile screen. In these situations, online retailers can use the cue of finality to help customers finalize their choice and achieve choice satisfaction. Per common usage, they can put a “the end” mark at the end of a product display or use the grayed right arrow key at the rightmost point for a similar effect. But if the product display is so long that the message of finality cannot be effectively delivered to customers, online retailers can consider delivering othe cues such as showing the “selected” label after online customers make their choice or encouraging the act of closing the lid of a box after product sampling, as sug gested by previous literature (Gu et al. 2013, 2018).

## Limitations and Future Research

We acknowledge certain limitations that might inspire future research directions. First, in all five experiments, participants chose from the domain of low-priced products (coffee mugs, mouse pads, hand creams, and fridge magnets). Online customers may sometimes need to make a choice of high-priced products, such as a desktop computer, a camera, or even a car and a house. Our results might not be generalizable to choices where individuals can be expected to make suf ficient product comparisons or exhaustively evaluate all possible alternatives, regardless of display orienta tions. Despite this limitation, our results have impor tant implications for a variety of products, ranging from search goods (e.g., mugs, mouse pads, etc.) to experience goods (e.g., hand creams). We therefore do not consider product categories as a serious limitation on the applicability of our findings. Future research might, nonetheless, test these findings in other product categories.

Moreover, we included only vertical and horizontal conditions in the experimental design, without involving a pure control condition. From the perspective of ecological validity, as mentioned above, we searched the top 100 e-commerce websites worldwide and found that either a vertical display or a horizontal display is used in practice. We aimed to find out which display is relatively better and, therefore, did not consider a control condition that does not exist in real-world business. Probably for this reason, numerous existing studies (e.g., Williams 1966, Deng et al. 2016, Kim et al. 2019) likewise did not include a control group. Despite such ecological considerations, it is worth noting that without a pure control condition, it is unclear whether a horizontal display increases choice satisfaction or a vertical display decreases it (Cook et al. 2002). Although this question is out of the scope of the current research, we call for future research to tease apart these two processes.

Our theory assumes that people are equally accustomed to both display orientations. However, this assumption may not always hold. For example, people may get used to a vertical display on cell phones, but be more habituated to a horizontal display on tablets/ desktops. In these situations, the effect of display orientation on choice satisfaction may be attenuated or strengthened, depending on the specific display direction to which they have been habituated. Specifically, whereas habituation to a horizontal display may polarize the horizontal display advantage that ultimately strengthens the focal effect, habituation to a vertical display may weaken the horizontal display advantage and subsequently attenuate the effect. Future research might consider this possibility and explore whether this is the case empirically.

Although we purposely conducted experiments on different devices to show generalization of our findings (i.e., Studies 1–3 on desktop and Studies 4 and 5 on tablet), it is noteworthy that the current findings may offer limited practical implications for mobile purchasing, given the difficulty of implementing a horizontal design on a portrait phone screen. However, prior literature (e.g., Van Kerckhove and Pandelaere 2018) suggests an alternative approach for online retailers to adopt a quasi-horizontal display with new technologies on mobile screens. As an example, they might display products in a manner that allows customers to swipe left and right and direct them to check product options horizontally on the portrait mobile screens. Future research may consider providing more direct evidence for this possibility.

Our results from Study 2 showed that online retailers can reduce the rate of cancellation from 80.95% to 58.7% by merely changing the product display format from a vertical direction to a horizontal one. Despite these encouraging results, it is worth noting that the results emerged in a well-controlled laboratory setting, which may differ from a real-world business setting in terms of product categories, interface complexity, and other factors. To this end, it would be worthwhile to investigate whether the focal effect can be applied in a much more realistic field setting in the future research.

Although the results of Study 5 have demonstrated that the impact of product display orientation on choice satisfaction in a more realistic setting with a large assortment of 24 options, it would be intriguing to extend the current research to determine the threshold where the number of options becomes overwhelming. Prior research has suggested that as the number of options in an assortment exceeds a certain threshold, choice overload may occur (e.g., Chernev et al. 2015), leading the relationship between the number of options and choice satisfaction to be complex. For instance, whereas some studies report a consistent positive effect (Scheibehenne et al. 2010), others suggest an inverted-U curve, indicating a point of diminishing returns if choice overload occurs (McShane and Bo¨ckenholt 2018, Simonsohn 2018). Therefore, it would be interesting to investigate when the choice overload effect would emerge as the number of displayed options go beyond a threshold and, if so, how it might interact with display orientation, potentially either mitigating or exacerbating the impact of display orientation on choice satisfaction. Indeed, this line of research raises intriguing questions regarding the optimal choice architecture (i.e., display options and orientation) and its impact on choice satisfaction. These questions could provide valuable insights into how the structure and presentation of product choices influence user experience and decision making and therefore offer deeper implications for design strategies in various online contexts. We call on future research to explore these promising directions and possibilities.

Following the procedure suggested by Deng et al. (2016), we manipulated product display orientation using a grid display format in Study 5 and found that the focal effect can be generalized to this format as well. Although the current research focuses on the impact of product display orientation on choice satisfaction regardless of display formats, one might still wonder whether the grid display format outperforms the single-line format or vice versa. A closer analysis of our data revealed that the effect size (measured by Cohen’s d) in Study 5 (i.e., grid display format) was comparable to that in other studies (i.e., single-line display format), suggesting that the focal effect was prominent in the grid format as well as that in the single-line format. However, exploring the differential effects of display formats (grid versus single-line) on choice satisfaction seems to be a promising direction for future research.

Finally, it is important to note that we measured participants’ choice satisfaction immediately after they made their selection, making this a state-based assessment. A future extension could be more of a longitudinal design to examine the enduring aspect of the phenomenon. Additionally, although the controlled laboratory experiments chosen in the current research has its strength in establishing internal validity and have been widely adopted in extant e-commerce research, future research could consider employing field experiments to provide a more natural testing ground.

## Conclusion

In investigating how and in what ways product display orientation can impact customers’ choice satisfaction on e-commerce websites, we show that a horizontal (versus vertical) product display can allow customers to conduct more adequate product comparisons before making a purchase decision and subsequently render a higher level of choice closure and lead to more satisfaction about the chosen option after making the decision. In terms of theorical contributions, our research adds to the literature on interface design, choice satisfaction, and choice closure. In practical terms, it provides actionable guidance for e-commerce websites on how to display products in different orientations to enhance customers’ choice satisfaction and reduce the high percentage of order cancellations, a serious challenge for many online retailers.
