# Navigating Platform-Led Affiliate Marketing: Implications for Content Creation and Platform Profitability

Abstract. Recently, some user-generated content (UGC) platforms have introduced shopping features to generate additional commission-based revenue from in-app transactions. These platforms allow creators to produce shoppable content, where promoted products are tagged with affiliate links, and in return, they earn a percentage of the commission the creators receive from merchants (such a new business model is referred to as platform-led affiliate marketing). Despite its growing popularity, the impact of this business model on the key stakeholders (i.e., UGC platforms, content creators, and content consumers) has yet to be systematically analyzed in the existing literature. This paper aims to address this significant literature gap by employing a game-theoretic model. Our findings demonstrate that platform-led affiliate marketing can create a win-win situation for UGC platforms, creators participating in affiliate marketing, and content consumers. Furthermore, we find that even creators not participating in affiliate marketing can sometimes benefit indirectly from other creators’ participation. On the other hand, surprisingly, we find that UGC platforms may not necessarily benefit from adopting this emerging business model because of potential losses in traffic revenue. These results carry significant managerial implications for UGC platforms regarding whether and how to leverage affiliate marketing. Moreover, our findings suggest that creators may benefit from reducing their production efforts in the face of intensified competition. More importantly, optimal production decisions for creators can vary significantly, depending on specific factors (e.g., an increase in the number of creators or content substitutability) contributing to heightened competition. These findings offer valuable insights that can guide creators in refining their production strategies.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, the number of Internet users has witnessed a dramatic surge. By the end of 2021, there were more than 5.2 billion Internet users worldwide, accounting for more than 66.2% of the global population (InternetWorldStats 2022). Simultaneously, the usage of mobile devices has been increasing at an astonishing rate. With an annual growth rate of 10.4% over the past five years, the global mobile user count was projected to reach 7.49 billion in 2025 (Oberlo 2022, Statista 2023). This combined growth in Internet and mobile users has led to an upswing in voluntary content creators and a recent surge in user-generated content (UGC) (Arpit 2021, Kumar et al. 2022). Consequently, there has been a rapid proliferation of UGC platforms in recent years. For instance, TikTok, a prominent short-video UGC platform, topped the list as the most downloaded app in the nongame category during the first two quarters of 2021 (Dean 2022). In the meantime, UGC has significantly influenced consumers’ purchase behavior (Li and Li 2019, Feng et al. 2022) and is widely touted as one of the most effective means of improving online conversion rates and sales performance (Pham 2020). Notably, studies by Taggbox reveal that approximately 50% of consumers find new products through UGC, and as many as 84% believe that UGC impacts their shopping decisions (Taggbox 2021). Given their capacity to inspire users to purchase, UGC platforms are widely regarded as having enormous commercial potential (McCracken 2020, Mallipeddi et al. 2022).

However, users who are inspired by UGC to make a purchase often find it inconvenient to do so; when content on a platform sparks their interest in buying a product, users usually have to leave the platform, search for the product elsewhere, and then make the purchase on a third-party e-commerce site (Tipser 2022). Consequently, the platform cannot directly generate revenue from content consumers’ purchases. In response to this challenge, some UGC platforms (e.g., Instagram) have recently introduced in-app shopping features (AboutInstagram 2021). These platforms allow eligible content creators to produce shoppable content, where promoted products are displayed and tagged with an affiliate link, often in the form of a purchase button. This seamless integration enables consumers to engage in online shopping directly from where they consume content. The trackability of affiliate links further enables content creators to generate commission revenue from each transaction, with the platform retaining a percentage of the earnings (McCracken 2020). This innovative business model can actually be viewed as a partnership between UGC platforms and content creators. Specifically, these platforms provide creators with in-app shopping features that enable them to generate commissions from the sales they drive. In return, the platforms have found a new revenue source through creators’ participation in affiliate marketing.

The effectiveness of such affiliate-marketing endeavors led by UGC platforms (referred to as platform-led affiliate marketing) has proven to be remarkable. A prime example of this success is Douyin (the Chinese version of TikTok), which generated more than 1.2 million affiliate-link-enabled transactions in a single day on December 12, 2018 (MarketingToChina 2019). Encouraged by Douyin’s success, various other UGC platforms, including Instagram, TikTok, and Pinterest, have recently embraced this innovative business model (Liffreing 2021). Despite its growing popularity, there have been few studies to date that systematically examine this novel approach. Particularly, it remains unclear whether and how the key stakeholders (i.e., UGC platforms, content creators, and content consumers) can benefit from the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing. This research aims to address this critical gap in the literature by investigating the impact of this business model on the key stakeholders.

## 1.1. Motivation

Platform-led affiliate marketing represents an innovative variation of the traditional affiliate marketing model (MarketingToChina 2019). This emerging business model involves content creators on the platform serving as “affiliates” for third-party merchants. Their role is to produce compelling content that effectively promotes the products of these merchants. When content consumers are interested, they can effortlessly make a purchase by clicking on the affiliate link integrated with the content. Consequently, for each successful transaction, the merchant rewards the affiliates with a commission fee (Enfroy 2021). The unique feature of platform-led affiliate marketing lies in the direct involvement of UGC platforms in the affiliate marketing process. These platforms enable the seamless integration of shopping features and, in return, take a percentage of the commission that affiliates receive from the merchants (Sil berling 2021).

As a result, platform-led affiliate marketing has emerged as a highly lucrative revenue stream for major UGC platforms (e.g., Instagram). Traditionally, these platforms generate revenue primarily from content consumer traffic (hereafter referred to as traffic revenue), using methods like advertising and branding. For instance, Instagram’s annual advertising revenue alone exceeds \$20 billion, and as of May 2022, it was estimated to be valued at more than \$110 billion with more than 1 billion monthly users (Bleznak 2022). The advent of platform-led affiliate marketing introduced a new source of revenue for UGC platforms. Specifically, the trackability of affiliate links allows these platforms to earn additional commissions (hereafter referred to as commission-based revenue) from affiliate-link-enabled transactions (McCracken 2020).

This emerging business practice exerts a profound impact on content creators as well. In June 2021, Instagram made a significant move by adopting platform-led affiliate marketing, recognizing it as one of the “new ways for creators to make a living” (AboutInstagram 2021). This new business model provides eligible con tent creators with an effective means of monetizing thei content. For instance, on Instagram, qualifying creators can select from a range of products available for promotion, create shoppable content (see the middle panel of Figure 1), and earn commissions on the purchases they drive (see the right panel of Figure 1).

At first glance, platform-led affiliate marketing appears to create a win-win situation for all key stakeholders involved in the UGC ecosystem. However, a closer examination reveals potential challenges to content quality and the long-term sustainability of UGC platforms. For instance, integrating a purchase button into content can reveal creators’ selling intentions and negatively affect consumers’ perception of content quality (Wojdynski and Evans 2016). Prior studies have also suggested that commercial posts may lead to consumer annoyance, potentially reducing content consumption (Kumar 2015, Chatterjee and Zhou 2021). This decline in consumption can have a significant, negative impact on creators’ utilities and, consequently, the platform’s revenue (Mallipeddi et al. 2021). Despite the importance of these implications, the actual impact of emerging platform-led affiliate marketing on all key stakeholders remains unclear, and formal analysis in the existing literature is notably lacking. Our study aims to fill this critical gap by conducting an in-depth economic analysis of this new business model. Specifically, we develop a game-theoretic model to investigate the scenario where a UGC platform offers affiliate-marketing opportunities to its content creators, who can strategically decide on their production efforts. In the following, we delve into the specific research questions and highlight the key findings of our study.

## 1.2. Research Questions and Key Findings

Under platform-led affiliate marketing, shoppable content has the potential to generate commissions for both the platform and content creators. However, it also reveals creators’ selling intentions, which can be perceived as a nuisance and distraction to content consumers (Admin 2019). Concerns have been raised that Instagram, for example, might be shifting from a content platform to more of a marketplace, potentially hurting consumers’ content consumption experience (Hawke-Media 2020). On the other hand, an opposing view suggests that allowing creators to monetize their content could actually enhance content quality, thus benefiting consumers (Lopez 2019). To gain an in-depth understanding of the impact of platform-led affiliate marketing on content quality of UGC platforms, we ask, Will the content quality of the platform deteriorate if platform-led affiliate marketing is adopted? Intuitively, one may expect a decline in content quality because of the abundance of distracting shoppable content under affiliate marketing. However, based on our findings, this is not necessarily the case. In fact, adopting platform-led affiliate marketing may actually lead to higher content quality. This happens because, despite generating a nuisance for consumers, affiliate marketing can effectively incentivize creators’ production, thereby enhancing the overall content quality. Consequently, consumers can rest assured that they may enjoy even better-quality content when the platform adopts this emerging business model. As for UGC platforms, they should not hesitate to embrace this business model because of concerns about its implications for content quality.

Moreover, although it is well recognized that UGC platforms have found a new revenue source, the true benefits of this new business model remain uncertain. Concerns have arisen that consumers might disengage from these platforms because of an overwhelming amount of shoppable content (McCoy 2019). The debate surrounding LinkedIn’s decision to allow creators to monetize their videos through product promotion exemplifies this predicament. One perspective advocates that LinkedIn should “absolutely” permit creators to monetize content to incentivize their content production. However, an opposing view argues that in-video endorsements could lead to a poor experience for consumers, prompting them to abandon the platform (Lopez 2019). Hence, a crucial question naturally arises: Are UGC platforms better off adopting platform-led affiliate marketing? In practice, the adoption of affiliate marketing has gradually become a trend among UGC platforms, with major platforms like Instagram expressing optimism about its potential. However, our findings indicate that UGC platforms may not always benefit from adopting affiliate marketing because of the potential decline in traffic revenue. This finding holds significant implications for UGC platforms considering the adoption of affiliate marketing. They must exercise caution and refrain from hastily following the trend, because generating additional commission-based revenue through platform-led affiliate marketing, albeit tempting, may sometimes have an adverse impact on their profitability.

Regarding content creators, those engaged in affiliate marketing (hereafter referred to as affiliated creators) can produce shoppable content and generate commission revenue, but they may face challenges because of the nuisance associated with such content. This nuisance can negatively impact consumers’ perception of the content’s quality (Wojdynski and Evans 2016), potentially leading to a decline in content consumption. In light of this situation, some creators are concerned about the actual benefits they can derive from this new business model (HawkeMedia 2020). Furthermore, creators who are not involved in affiliate marketing (hereafter referred to as nonaffiliated creators), especially those ineligible to participate, may feel left behind and worry about missing out on the opportunity to generate commission revenue. Thus, we ask: Are content creators better off with platform-led affiliate marketing? Surprisingly, our results reveal that affiliate marketing can benefit both affiliated and nonaffiliated creators. Specifically, we find that affiliated creators are always better off, and interestingly, nonaffiliated creators can sometimes be better off because of the spillover effect from other creators’ participation in affiliate marketing. In such cases, platform-led affiliate marketing essentially serves as an effective means of differentiation among creators; affiliated creators focus on the commissions generated from product promotion, whereas nonaffiliated creators focus on the benefits derived from increased content consumption. This finding also holds meaningful implications for creators deciding which platform to join. Joining platforms with affiliate marketing can be a good strategy even for creators who are ineligible to produce shoppable content on those platforms.

Additionally, in platform-led affiliate marketing, affiliated creators receive a commission from merchants for each transaction facilitated through affiliate links, whereas the platform itself retains a percentage of this commission (such a percentage is hereafter referred to as the commission-split rate) (Silberling 2021). Although the commission provided by merchants usually aligns with industry standards (Bradley 2021), the commission-split rate may vary significantly among platforms. For instance, the commission-split rate is 10% on Douyin<sup>1</sup> but more than 25% on Instagram (Bradley 2021, Warren 2021). This observed disparity potentially arises from the inherent trade-off that the platform faces when setting the commission-split rate. A higher rate may yield larger commission-based revenue, but it might also lead to reduced effort from creators in content production, potentially resulting in a loss in traffic revenue for the platform. Despite the substantial impact of the commission-split rate on platform revenue, no existing literature examines how to determine the opti mal rate. Our study aims to fill this literature gap.

In fact, the emerging “creator economy” on UGC platforms can be regarded as a gig economy for digital content, where creators are precarious workers relying on the platforms for content distribution (Chayka 2021). The immense power imbalance between platforms and creators puts the latter at risk for being exploited. This issue is further exacerbated by intensified competition among creators, which may lead them to increase their production efforts in the competition for consumers (Binod 2021). Thus, one might anticipate platforms to reduce monetary rewards provided to content creators. However, there are opposing views that suggest that intensified competition can actually discourage creators from contributing content (Zhao et al. 2023). This prompts platforms to consider the need to increase compensation to better incentivize content production. Therefore, as competition among creators intensifies, platforms face an increasing challenge of how to prop erly compensate creators, which boils down to the problem of setting the optimal commission-split rate in our context. Although important, guidelines for platforms to address this challenge are still lacking. Thus, our study aims to address the following question: Should the platform set a higher commission-split rate as competition among creators intensifies? Our results suggest that in such a case, higher commission-split rates may not benefit platforms. This is because although they may generate more commission-based revenue, traffic revenue would decrease substantially, leaving platforms worse off.

Moreover, in the presence of increasingly fierce competition, content creators face the need to produce highly engaging content to compete for audiences, which typically incurs relatively high production costs (Kumar and Qiu 2021, Mills 2021). This situation is further complicated by platforms’ adoption of affiliate marketing. On the one hand, some creators can now participate in affiliate marketing to receive additional monetary rewards, which incentivizes them to put in more production efforts. However, on the other hand, their effectiveness in boosting demand through production efforts decreases because of the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing, potentially leading them to cut down on their production efforts. Thus, it is not immediately clear whether increasing production efforts to cope with the intensified competition is the optimal strategy for creators. To provide guidelines for creators regarding their production strategies, we ask: Should creators devote more efforts to content production as competition intensifies under platform-led affiliate marketing? With creators increasingly relying on content production for monetary rewards, they often exert more efforts when faced with heightened competition (Binod 2021). However, our findings suggest that in the context of platform-led affiliate marketing, as competition among creators intensifies, whereas nonaffiliated creators should invest more effort in production, affiliated creators may not choose the same strategy. In particular, it is sometimes more advantageous for affiliated creators to cut back on their production efforts, even though doing so may cause them to lose some audience. This is because as competition among creators intensifies, the platform may set a higher commission-split rate, reducing the marginal benefit of the affiliated creators’ production efforts. Furthermore, our results show that when adjusting their production strategies, affiliated creators should consider the specific reasons (whether it be the increase in the number of content creators or the content substitutability) that have boosted competition; the intensified competition because of different reasons may have divergent impacts on affiliated creators’ production strategies.

Our research makes substantial contributions from both theoretical and practical perspectives. First, this paper stands as one of the earliest attempts to study platform-led affiliate marketing, an emerging business model that blends content with e-commerce on UGC platforms. Our findings provide important guidelines for UGC platforms on whether and how to leverage affiliate marketing to monetize their traffic. Second, our study contributes to the growing literature on revenue management of UGC platforms by demonstrating that platform-led affiliate marketing can not only provide platforms with an additional revenue source but also effectively incentivize creators’ content production. Third, our findings challenge the commonly held belief that enabling shopping features would negatively impact content quality. Instead, we demonstrate that content quality of UGC platforms may even improve with the adoption of affiliate marketing. Lastly, our findings shed light on creators’ optimal production strategies. Specifically, when facing intensified competition, creators should exercise caution and refrain from hastily exerting more efforts to attract consumers. Instead, they should carefully consider the specific reasons that have caused the intensified competition and tailor their production strategies accordingly.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature. Section 3 introduces the modeling framework, and Section 4 presents the models analyzing the regimes without and with platform-led affiliate marketing, respectively. Section 5 examines the impact of affiliate marketing on the key stakeholders. Section 6 conducts comparative analyses. Additionally, Section 7 presents four model extensions, and in Section 8, we conclude the paper with discussions on research contributions and managerial implications.

## 2. Literature Review

Our study relates mainly to two streams of literature: (a) affiliate marketing and (b) revenue management on UGC platforms. Next, we briefly review the related research and highlight our contribution to each stream.

## 2.1. Affiliate Marketing

The extant literature on affiliate marketing has focused primarily on various aspects such as the benefits of this new mechanism (Duffy 2005), merchants’ choice of payment schemes (Libai et al. 2003), and fraud detection and prevention (Edelman and Brandi 2015), as well as affiliates’ advertising disclosure behavior on social media platforms (Mathur et al. 2018) and their strategic decisions regarding product production (Suryanarayana et al. 2019). Recently, some studies have delved deeper into affiliate marketing. For example, Ho et al. (2017) analytically investigated cashback-based affiliate marketing (i.e., consumers can be reimbursed with a certain portion of the transactional amount when purchasing through an affiliate site), revealing a cash-back paradox where all consumers may ultimately face higher prices. Sun et al. (2020) empirically compared the effectiveness of affiliate marketing on social media platforms with that of sponsored search on e-commerce platforms in increasing traffic and sales for sellers, finding the latter to be more effective. Cao et al. (2021) empirically studied the effect of referral affiliate marketing on an e-commerce platform, noting its attenuated effect on sales when combined with price promotions.

Our study diverges from prior literature on affiliate marketing in several significant ways. First, most previous studies have examined affiliate marketing without considering the involvement of a UGC platform in the value exchange process. In contrast, our study analyzes a novel platform-led affiliate-marketing business model, where UGC platforms are directly involved by provid ing integrated shopping features and strategically deciding the percentage they retain from the commission generated through affiliate-link-enabled transactions. By capturing this unique structure of platform-led affiliate marketing, our model delves into the UGC platform’s strategies, that is, optimal decisions on affiliate marketing adoption and commission-split rates. Second, unlike most previous studies that focus on a sole affiliate, our research embraces the reality of platformled affiliate marketing, where both affiliated and nonaf filiated creators coexist and compete on the platform.

Moreover, these two types of creators may experience different influences from affiliate marketing and the platform’s decisions. Our study captures this uniqueness by modeling the competition between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators and also their optimal strategies, providing valuable insights into the impact of affiliate marketing on different types of creators. In essence, the inclusion of UGC platforms significantly alters the traditional affiliate marketing landscape. Our study aims to shed light on the vast business potential of affiliate marketing when adopted by UGC platforms, unraveling its implications and paving the way for future research and industry adoption.

## 2.2. Revenue Management on UGC Platforms

UGC platforms primarily generate revenue through the traffic generated by users who engage with high-quality content created by content creators. Existing studies in this stream focus mainly on how UGC platforms incentivize content creators to produce more high-quality content, thereby generating additional revenue from consumer traffic. One widely studied mechanism is ad revenuesharing. Jain and Qian (2021) analytically investigated the optimal design of a revenue-sharing scheme and how it is affected by creators’ competition and customer types. Bhargava (2022) employed a game-theoretical modeling framework to examine how platforms can motivate contributors through revenue-sharing of ad payments when the level of market concentration among creators is considered. Liu and Feng (2021) explored the impact of monetary incentives on UGC contributors and identified two crowding-out effects, namely motivation-crowding-out and competition-crowding-out, affecting creators’ participation. Ren (2023) investigated the interplay between a platform’s advertising policy and creators’ production strategies. Additionally, the literature has explored other mechanisms to incentivize content creators. For example, Ma et al. (2009) analyzed contributors’ production decisions under different quality control policies implemented by platforms. Chen et al. (2011) examined the influence of moderation on users’ incentives to generate content and found that appropriate moderation can help improve content quality. Ghosh and McAfee (2011) addressed the challenge of incentivizing high-quality content using a rating mechanism. Huang et al. (2015) investigated the content creation and consumption behavior of employees within an enterprise and found that the creation of leisure-related content can stimulate work-related content creation. More recently, Pu et al. (2020) investigated the impact of identity disclosure on creators’ content generation. Deng et al. (2021) investigated the influence of editorial reviews on subsequent users’ review contributions on a UGC platform and found that editoria reviews not only increased the number of user reviews but also enhanced their quality. Burtch et al. (2022)

analyzed the impact of peer awards on the volume and novelty of creators’ content.

Our study is closely related to the literature on the ad revenue-sharing business model (see, e.g., Jain and Qian 2021 and Bhargava 2022); however, it distinguishes itself in several significant ways. First, whereas the exist ing literature has focused primarily on incentivizing creators to produce high-quality content and thus generate greater traffic revenue, it has often overlooked the potential for these platforms to generate revenue from other sources. Under platform-led affiliate marketing, alongside traffic revenue, the platform can create a new revenue stream through commissions from affiliate link-enabled transactions. To capture this uniqueness of platform-led affiliate marketing, we model the interplay between the UGC platform’s two revenue sources— traffic revenue and commission-based revenue—and how it influences the platform’s optimal strategies. Furthermore, in Section 7.2, we delve into the platform’s optimal decision on the monetary incentive provided to content creators following the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing. By doing so, our research contri butes to the literature by unveiling how the platform should adjust its revenue-sharing schemes after adopting platform-led affiliate marketing. Second, under the ad-revenue-sharing business model, creators do not blend their content with ads, and hence, their ability to attract consumers’ consumption remains unaffected by ads. However, under platform-led affiliate marketing, creators participating in affiliate marketing may experi ence reduced effectiveness in attracting consumers because of the potential nuisance caused by affiliate marketing, which in turn influences their production strategies as well as the platform’s decisions. To capture this, we model the negative impact of the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing on content quality of affili ated creators. Third, whereas the existing literature has typically investigated the scenario where all creators participate in ad revenue-sharing, our model captures the coexistence and competition of two distinct creator types in the platform-led affiliate marketing landscape: affiliated creators and nonaffiliated creators.

## 3. Model Setup

We consider a UGC platform that hosts n > 2 independent content creators who produce content (such as photos, videos, and live-streaming) to compete for consumers’ attention.<sup>2</sup> A typical example of such a UGC platform is Instagram, where more than 40 billion photos are produced by independent creators, and content consumers spend an average of 53minutes daily consuming content (Smperth 2022). In the following subsections, we describe the problems for the key stakeholders (i.e., content consumers, creators, and the platform) and the game sequence.

## 3.1. The Problem of Content Consumers

Consumers on UGC platforms typically value content quality and derive utility from the consumption of multiple creators’ content (Mathews 2021), which has also been widely studied in the literature (see, e.g., Ghosh and McAfee 2011 and Jain and Qian 2021). In addition, consumers usually derive more utility by spending more time consuming content (and hence, having more content consumption), and this process typically features diminishing returns (see, e.g., Amaldoss et al. 2021). As a result, we consider that consumers consume the content from n creators, and their utility derived from consuming creator $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ content (also referred to as content i) is $q _ { i } t _ { i } - \frac { t _ { i } ^ { 2 } } { 2 } , i \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , n \}$ . Here, $q _ { i }$ denotes consumers’ perceived content quality (also referred to as content quality) of content $i ,$ and a larger $q _ { i }$ indicates higher utility generated per unit consumption of content i. In addition, $t _ { i }$ denotes the amount of time that consumers spend consuming creator $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ content (hereafter referred to as creator i’s demand).

In addition to content quality, the degree of substitutability among creators’ content is another important factor in this context because it affects consumers’ content consumption and hence, creators’ competition intensity (Mehrotra 2021). In particular, consumers would consume less if the degree of substitutability were to increase. To capture such an impact, we consider that consumers derive less consumption utility when consuming content with a higher degree of substitutability. In particular, we consider that the substitutability between content i and j results in a disutility of $\theta t _ { i } t _ { j }$ for consumers if they spend time $t _ { i }$ and $t _ { j }$ consuming content i and $j ,$ respectively. Here, $\theta \left( 0 < \overset { \cdot } { \theta } < 1 \right)$ denotes the extent to which the content produced by each creator is substitutable for that of his or her competitors (hereafter referred to as content substitutability).

Considering the complexity of the ecosystem on the UGC platforms, to focus on the main trade-offs in our study, we use aggregate demand to capture consumers consumption. Specifically, we consider a mass of content consumers as a whole instead of focusing on individual consumers’ content preferences. Such treatment has been widely adopted in the content-creator economy literature (see, e.g., Godes et al. 2009 and de Cornie\`re and Sarvary 2023).

Then, we can formulate consumers’ problems as follows. Consumers decide their optimal time spent on each creator’s content by solving

$$
\max _ {t _ {1}, t _ {2}, \dots , t _ {n}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(q _ {i} t _ {i} - \frac {t _ {i} ^ {2}}{2}\right) - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n - 1} \sum_ {j = i + 1} ^ {n} \theta t _ {i} t _ {j}\tag{1}
$$

The above setup that captures consumers’ optimal strategies of multiple-good consumption is based on the classic works of Dixit (1979) and Singh and Vives (1984).

Here, the first term $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \bigg ( q _ { i } t _ { i } - \frac { t _ { i } ^ { 2 } } { 2 } \bigg ) } \end{array}$ calculates consu mers’ accumulated utility generated from consuming content produced by all the creators, and the second term $\textstyle \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n - 1 } \sum _ { j = i + 1 } ^ { n } \theta t _ { i } t _ { j }$ calculates the disutility caused by the content substitutability. Note that because creators compete for consumers’ attention, both the number of creators $( \mathrm { i . e . , } n )$ and the content substitutability $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \theta )$ have impacts on consumers’ consumption decisions and thus the intensity of competition among creators.

## 3.2. The Problem of Content Creators

The surge of content creation training programs and creator toolkits has greatly improved novice creators’ production capability (Lahey 2020). Such a phenomenon has made creators more competitive and homogeneous (Bhargava 2022). In line with the practice, we consider the scenario where the platform hosts n symmetric independent content creators in the main model, which has been commonly examined in the literature (see, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ Amaldoss et al. 2021).<sup>3</sup> Content creators typically derive base utilities (e.g., enjoyment, reputation, interpersonal ties, and a sense of altruism) from contributing content (Zhang and Zhu 2011, Krishnamurthy et al. 2014), and such utilities have been considered to be directly proportional to the creators’ audience sizes (see, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , Liu and Feng 2021). Therefore, we consider that the base utility generated by a content creator with demand $t _ { i }$ is $\beta t _ { i }$ , where $\beta > 0$ represents the base utility derived from consumers’ unit consumption. Additionally, content creators may also receive monetary incentives from the platform for their content production. For example, certain UGC platforms (e.g., Snapchat) compensate creators based on the number of views their content generates (Whateley 2023). Hence, we consider that the platform provides a monetary incentive $\xi \ge 0$ to creators for each unit of consumers’ consumption that they generate.<sup>4</sup> Taking these two aspects into account, we consider that the total utility generated by a content creator with demand t is represented as $( \beta + \xi ) t _ { i }$

To enhance content quality, creators typically must increase their production efforts, for instance, by dedicating more time to improving content design (Binod 2021). In line with this practice, the existing literature (see, e.g., Jain and Qian 2021) has considered that the quality of creators’ content is directly proportional to (and, for simplicity, is equal to) their production efforts. Therefore, we consider that for creator $i ,$ the quality of content, $q _ { i } ,$ is equal to his or her effort in content production, $e _ { i } .$ . Furthermore, content production is generally subject to diminishing marginal return, and a quadratic form for the production cost is commonly adopted in the literature (see, e.g., de Cornie\`re and Sarvary 2023). Hence, we consider that for creator i dedicating effort $e _ { i } ,$ the production cost is ${ \frac { c e _ { i } ^ { 2 } } { 2 } } ,$ where c denotes the contentproduction cost coefficient for creators.

Then, when the platform does not adopt affiliate marketing (hereafter referred to as the regime without affiliate marketing), creator i maximizes his or her utility from production, $u _ { i } ,$ by solving

$$
\max _ {e _ {i}} \left(\beta + \xi\right) t _ {i} - \frac {c e _ {i} ^ {2}}{2},\tag{2}
$$

where the first term $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } ( \beta + \xi ) t _ { i } )$ calculates the base utility and the monetary incentive creator i can derive from content production, and the second term $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { c e _ { i } ^ { z } } { 2 } )$ calculates his or her production cost.

When the UGC platform adopts affiliate marketing (hereafter referred to as the regime with affiliate marketing), creators are provided with a way to generate commission revenue by producing shoppable content (Enfroy 2021). However, such opportunities are usually not available to all creators. For example, to date, Instagram allows only U.S.-based creators to produce shoppable content (AboutInstagram 2021).<sup>5</sup> Hence, we classify creators on the platform into two types. Specifically, we consider that m $( 0 < m \leq n )$ creators are both eligible and willing to produce shoppable content. These creators are referred to as affiliated creators because they participate in affiliate marketing. On the other hand, the remaining $n - m$ creators, referred to as nonaffiliated creators, either do not meet the eligibility criteria or choose not to engage in affiliate marketing.<sup>6</sup> We use M and M to denote the sets of affiliated and nonaffiliated creators, respectively.

As for affiliated creators, they can earn additional commission revenue, the value of which is influenced by many factors. Among these factors, product attributes are widely recognized as a crucial determinant because they directly impact consumers’ needs and, consequently, their willingness to make a purchase through affiliate links (Kumar 2021, Buildd 2023). Based on the above discussion, we denote the total commission generated from consumers’ unit consumption of shoppable content as $\omega + \lambda \eta$ (hereafter referred to as the marginal commission revenue).<sup>7</sup> Here, ω $\left( \omega > 0 \right)$ represents the base marginal commission revenue generated from consumers’ shoppable content consumption, $\eta \left( 0 < \eta < 1 \right)$ represents consumers’ valuation of the product attributes, and $\lambda \left( \lambda > 0 \right)$ represents the coefficient for the correlation between the marginal commission revenue and the valuation of product attributes. Thus, λη represents the additional marginal commission revenue attributed to the consideration of product attributes. In addition, we use α to denote the percentage of the marginal commission revenue claimed by the platform (hereafter referred to as the commission-split rate). Thus, the commission revenue generated by an affiliated creator with demand $t _ { i }$ is represented as $( 1 { \dot { - } } \alpha ) ( \omega + \lambda \eta ) t _ { i }$

Although affiliated creators can generate additional commission revenue through shoppable content, such content may be perceived as a nuisance by consumers, given its inclusion of product promotion elements (Goldstein et al. 2014). In particular, the extant literature (see, e.g., Wojdynski and Evans 2016) suggests that the integration of product promotion activities within content adversely affects consumers’ perceived content quality, especially when these promotions are recognized by consumers. Moreover, this negative effect is more pronounced when promotions are included in high-quality content rather than in low-quality content (Bachmann et al. 2019). Thus, we consider that for affiliated creator $i , i \in M ,$ producing shoppable content will result in a decline in the perceived quality of the content, given the effort put into content production. Specifically, we let $q _ { i \in M } \dot { = } \delta e _ { i \in M } . ^ { 8 }$ Here, $\delta ( \bar { 0 } < \delta < 1 )$ is an inverse measure of the adverse effect of affiliate marketing on content quality, with a smaller $\delta$ indicating a greater negative impact and a lower tolerance from consumers toward shoppable content. For nonaffiliated creators, who do not engage in affiliate marketing, as in the regime without affiliate marketing, we consider that consumers’ perceived quality of their content is equal to their production effort, that is, $q _ { i \in \overline { { { M } } } } = e _ { i \in \overline { { { M } } } } ,$

Based on the discussion above, the impact of platformled affiliate marketing on affiliated creators can be summarized into two key points. First, participating in affiliate marketing generates additional commission revenue for affiliated creators. Second, affiliate marketing can create a nuisance, thereby negatively affecting how consumers perceive the quality of content produced by affiliated creators, given their efforts in content production. As for nonaffiliated creators, although they are not directly affected by platform-led affiliate marketing, their production strategies and utilities are indirectly influenced through competition. In the following, we describe the problems of affiliated and nonaffiliated creators, respectively. The affiliated creator $i \in M$ maximizes his or her utility from content production by solving

$$
\max _ {e _ {i}} \big (\beta + \xi + (1 - \alpha) (\omega + \lambda \eta) \big) t _ {i} - \frac {c e _ {i} ^ {2}}{2},\tag{3}
$$

where α denotes the commission-split rate and hence, $( 1 - \alpha ) ( \omega + \lambda \eta )$ represents the commission that creator i can receive from consumers’ unit time of content consumption. Then, the first term $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } \left( \beta + \xi + ( 1 - \alpha ) \right.$ $( \omega + \lambda \eta ) ) t _ { i } )$ calculates the sum of the base utility, the monetary incentive provided by the platform, and the commission revenue that creator i can derive from content production, and the second term $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { c e _ { i } ^ { . } } { 2 } )$ calculates his or her production cost. Without loss of generality, we normalize $\beta$ to one in the model.

As in the regime without affiliate marketing, the nonaffiliated creator $i \in \overline { { M } }$ maximizes his or her utility from content production by solving

$$
\max _ {e _ {i}} (\beta + \xi) t _ {i} - \frac {c e _ {i} ^ {2}}{2}.\tag{4}
$$

## 3.3. The Problem of UGC Platform

Under the regime without affiliate marketing, the platform derives revenue solely from consumers’ total consumption of content, known as traffic revenue. Using $\gamma , \gamma > \xi ,$ to denote the platform’s marginal traffic revenue, we can express its revenue as $\overset { \smile } { \pi } = ( \gamma - \xi ) \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i } ,$ where $\textstyle \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i }$ represents the total consumption of all creators’ content by consumers. Here, the marginal traffic revenue refers to the revenue generated by the platform from each additional unit of consumer consumption. For instance, if consumers spend an extra unit of time on the platform, the marginal traffic revenue reflects the revenue generated from that specific period.

As discussed in Section 1.1, the impact of platformled affiliate marketing on the platform arises from the additional revenue source and the decision on the commission-split rate, both brought about by creators participation in affiliate marketing. Specifically, when the platform adopts affiliate marketing, it not only earns traffic revenue but also generates commission-based revenue from transactions enabled by affiliate links by taking a percentage of the commission paid by thirdparty merchants. In particular, merchants compensate creators with a commission for each transaction facilitated by an affiliate link, and the platform decides on the percentage it charges (denoted by α), with content creators receiving the remainder. Thus, under the regime with affiliate marketing, the platform maximizes its revenue by solving

$$
\max _ {\alpha} \gamma \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i} + \alpha (\omega + \lambda \eta) \sum_ {i \in M} t _ {i} - \xi \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i},\tag{5}
$$

where $\textstyle \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i }$ denotes the consumers’ total consumption of all creators’ content and $\textstyle \sum _ { i \in M } t _ { i }$ the consumers’ total consumption of content by affiliated creators. The first term $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \gamma \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i } )$ represents the traffic revenue generated by all content creators on the platform, the second term (i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { \alpha ( \omega + \lambda \eta ) \sum _ { i \in M } t _ { i } ) } \end{array}$ calculates the commissionbased revenue generated by affiliated creators through transactions enabled by affiliate links, and the third term $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \xi \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i } )$ represents the monetary incentive 9 provided by the platform to content creators.

## 5.1. Will Content Quality of the Platform Deteriorate If Affiliate Marketing Is Adopted? Deteriorate If Affiliate Marketing Is Adopted?

Maintaining high-quality content is crucial to a UGC platform’s long-term success; however, the commercial activities brought about by the adoption of affiliate marketing may actually hurt content quality. In fact, there have been concerns that instead of being a content platform, Instagram is too much of a marketplace, which may negatively impact users’ content consumption experience (HawkeMedia 2020). To gain a deeper understanding of this issue, we proceed to examine the impact of affiliate marketing on the platform’s content quality and summarize the findings in the following proposition.

Proposition 1. Under the regime with platform-led affiliate marketing, compared with the regime without it:

• If the base marginal commission revenue exceeds a certain threshold $( i . e . , \omega > \omega _ { 1 } ) _ { . }$ , the platform’s content quality actually improves $( i . e . , \overline { { q } } > \overline { { q } } ^ { o } )$ , leading to an increase in consumer welfare.

• Conversely, if the base marginal commission revenue falls below this threshold $( i . e . , \omega < \omega _ { 1 } ) .$ , both the platform’s content quality and the consumer welfare decrease.

$$
\text { Here }, \omega_ {1} = \frac {(2 + (n - m - 2) \theta) (1 + \xi) - \delta^ {2} (\gamma (1 - \theta) + (\theta (n - m - 1) + 1) (1 + \eta \lambda) + (n - m) \theta \xi)}{\delta^ {2} (1 + (n - m - 1) \theta)}.
$$

Because the adoption of affiliate marketing will generate a considerable amount of shoppable content that may distract content consumers, there has been concern that content quality of the platform may deteriorate in the long run (HawkeMedia 2020). However, interestingly, we find that the adoption of affiliate marketing may actually improve the platform’s content quality. This result can be explained as follows.

Content quality of the platform critically depends on how much effort creators devote to production (Liu and Feng 2021). When platform-led affiliate marketing is adopted, the production of shoppable content brings nuisance to content consumers and lowers the affiliated creators’ content quality (i.e., $q _ { i \in M } = \delta e _ { i \in M } < e _ { i \in M } )$ . On the other hand, the commission generated from affiliate marketing $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } ( 1 - \alpha ) ( \omega + \lambda \eta ) t _ { i \in M } )$ provides affiliated creators with an additional monetary incentive to devote production efforts. Thus, if the additional production efforts devoted by affiliated creators are large enough, the increase in content quality can outweigh the nuisance caused by shoppable content production, leading to improved content quality of the platform. This happens when the additional monetary incentive $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } ( 1 - \alpha ) ( \omega + \lambda \eta ) t _ { i \in M } )$ for affiliated creators is relatively large. In particular, if the base marginal commission revenue is above a given threshold $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \omega > \omega _ { 1 } )$ affiliated creators can generate substantial commission revenue by devoting significant efforts to production and boosting consumers’ content consumption. As a result, content quality of the platform is improved.

Proposition 1 yields meaningful implications for the UGC platforms that are still hesitant about adopting affiliate marketing. UGC platforms have long been criticized for having excessive ads. For example, as previously stated, many Instagram users have complained that instead of being a content platform, Instagram is now too much of a marketplace (HawkeMedia 2020). Thus, some UGC platforms may be cautious about adopting affiliate marketing because of concerns over the nuisance caused by shoppable content. However, our results show that UGC platforms should not be overconcerned with the adverse impact of affiliate marketing on content quality. Instead, they should be fully aware that platform-led affiliate marketing can effectively incentivize creators to produce high-quality content, and as a result, the platform’s content quality may actually improve under this new business model. This finding also provides meaningful implications for consumers who have long been concerned about the nuisance caused by excessive advertising content on UGC platforms and thus worry that the adoption of platformled affiliate marketing may aggravate this problem. Specifically, consumers should be aware that despite the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing, they may actually benefit from this business model because of the potential content quality improvement.

This finding contributes to the growing body of literature that examines how to better incentivize content creators to produce high-quality content. The extant literature on this topic focuses mainly on platforms’ quality-control policy (see, e.g., Ma et al. 2009), rating mechanisms (see, e.g., Ghosh and McAfee 2011), and ad-revenue-sharing mechanisms (see, e.g., Bhargava 2022). Our study contributes to this stream of literature by showing that platform-led affiliate marketing can effectively incentivize content creators’ high-quality content creation by providing them with the opportunities to promote products and generate commission revenue. In addition, this finding also contributes to the literature on affiliate market ing. As discussed in Section 2.1, the extant literature in this stream (see, e.g., Sun et al. 2020) does not examine the impact of platform-led affiliate marketing. Such a literature gap exists mainly because UGC platforms usually do not play a role in traditional affiliate marketing. Even worse, before the emergence of platform-led affiliate marketing, some UGC platforms (e.g., Instagram and TikTok) did not allow creators to post clickable links in an attempt to prohibit them from participating in affiliate market ing (Waters 2021). However, as UGC platforms increasingly realize the importance of generating additional commission-based revenue from affiliate-link-enabled transactions, some of them have become directly involved in affiliate marketing. Our study adds to the literature by further unveiling the business potential of affiliate marketing when it is adopted by UGC platforms. Specifically, we show that by actively initiating an affiliate marketing program and turning content creators into affiliates, UGC platforms can effectively incen tivize creators and thus improve their content quality.

## 5.2. Are UGC Platforms Better Off Adopting Affiliate Marketing?

It is well recognized that platform-led affiliate marketing provides UGC platforms with a new revenue source (McCracken 2020). However, the production of shoppable content also brings nuisance to content consumers, which may drive them to leave the platforms (McCoy 2019) and potentially lead to a loss in their traffic revenue. Therefore, it remains unclear whether UGC platforms can indeed benefit from affiliate marketing. In Proposition 2, we attempt to provide guidelines on the adoption of this emerging business model by examining how affiliate marketing affects the revenue of platforms.

Proposition 2. Compared with the regime without affiliate marketing:

• If the base marginal commission revenue exceeds a certain threshold $( i . e . , \ \omega > \omega _ { 2 } )$ , the platform benefits from adopting platform-led affiliate marketing $( i . e . , \pi ^ { * } > \pi ^ { o } )$

• Conversely, if the base marginal commission revenue falls below the threshold $( i . e . , \omega < \omega _ { 2 } )$ , the platform does not adopt affiliate marketing.

For brevity, the expression of ω<sub>2</sub> is provided in Online Appendix A.

Because the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing can help monetize consumers’ traffic on the platform and additionally generate commission-based revenue, one might expect that UGC platforms would be better off adopting the new business model (McCracken 2020). However, our results show that this is not necessarily the case. Specifically, the platform is better off only when the base marginal commission revenue is above a given threshold $\left( \mathrm { i . e . , } \omega > \omega _ { 2 } \right)$ . This result can be explained as follows.

As discussed in Section 3.3, the adoption of affiliate marketing has a positive effect on the platform’s revenue by generating additional commission-based revenue $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \alpha ( \omega + \lambda \eta ) \sum _ { i \in M } t _ { i } } \end{array}$ in Equation (5) in Section 3.3). In the meantime, as shown in Proposition 1, when the base marginal commission revenue is relatively large $\left( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \omega > \omega _ { 1 } \right)$ , the platform’s content quality will be higher under affiliate marketing $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \overline { { q } } > \overline { { q } } ^ { o } )$ . The improved content quality in turn boosts consumers total consumption $( \mathrm { { i . e . } } , \ \dot { \sum } _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i }$ in Equation (5) in Section 3.3), leading to an increase in the platform’s traffic revenue $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \gamma \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i } )$ . Thus, when $\omega > \omega _ { 1 }$ , with the increased traffic revenue and the additional commission-based revenue, the platform will be better off adopting affiliate marketing. On the other hand, when $\omega \leq \omega _ { 1 }$ , the average content quality decreases $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \overline { { q } } \leq \overline { { q } } ^ { o } )$ , leading to a decline in consumers’ total consumption $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i } )$ and hence, the platform’s traffic revenue $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \gamma \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i } )$ . In such a case, whether the platform will be better off adopting is determined by the trade-off of two opposing effects. One is the aforementioned positive effect due to the additional commission-based revenue, and the other is the negative effect due to the decrease in the platform’s traffic revenue. The value of the base marginal commission revenue $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \omega )$ affects the magnitude of both effects.

Specifically, as explained below, a larger ω leads to a strengthened positive effect but a weakened negative effect.

Under the regime of affiliate marketing, consumers demand for affiliated creators’ content $( t _ { i \in M } )$ increases with the base marginal commission revenue because a higher ω incentivizes affiliated creators to devote more effort to content creation. In the meantime, consumers demand for nonaffiliated creators’ content $( t _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } )$ decreases with the base marginal commission revenue $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { \mathcal { I } t _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } } { \partial \omega } < 0 ) } \end{array}$ because of the competition between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators. When the base marginal commission revenue ω increases, the increase in the total demand for affiliated creators’ content (i.e., $\textstyle \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i \in M } )$ outweighs the decrease in that of nonaffiliated creators’ content $\textstyle ( { \mathrm { i . e . , } } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { i \in { \overline { { M } } } } )$ , leading the consumers consumption on the platform $( \mathrm { i . e . } , \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { \tilde { n } } t _ { i } )$ to increase. Thus, the decrease in the traffic revenue due to the adoption of affiliate marketing becomes smaller as ω increases, weakening the negative effect of the adoption. Furthermore, because consumers’ demand for affiliated creators $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , t _ { i \in M } )$ increases with the base marginal commission revenue $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \frac { \partial t _ { i \in M } } { \partial \omega } > 0 ) } \end{array}$ , the commission-based revenue generated by the platform $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \alpha ( \omega + \lambda \eta )$ $\textstyle \sum _ { i \in M } t _ { i } )$ increases with ω, which strengthens the positive effect of the adoption. When the base marginal commission revenue ω exceeds a certain threshold $\left( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \omega > \omega _ { 2 } \right)$ the positive effect dominates the negative one, leaving the platform better off adopting the new business model.

Proposition 2 provides important implications for UGC platforms seeking to adopt affiliate marketing. Because many major UGC platforms $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Instagram) have begun to adopt platform-led affiliate marketing, monetizing consumers’ traffic through shoppable content is becoming a trend. Our finding sends an important message to UGC platforms that they should be cautious about rushing to follow, because having an additional commission-based revenue source is not necessarily beneficial. Furthermore, our result suggests that the best timing for platforms to adopt is when the affiliate-marketing business model becomes relatively mature and can generate relatively high commissionbased revenue from consumers’ unit shoppable content consumption. Our results can also provide a plausible explanation for the strategic decisions of Instagram regarding the introduction of platform-led affiliate marketing. Before adopting affiliate marketing, in 2019, Instagram launched a feature called “checkout,” allowing consumers to purchase from a brand’s shoppable post (AboutInstagram 2019). Two years later, Instagram introduced platform-led affiliate marketing to allow content creators to produce shoppable content (AboutInstagram 2021). The “checkout” feature may have helped Instagram consumers become more customized to purchasing through affiliate links, which in turn increases the commission generated from each shoppable content. Thus, UGC platforms expecting to follow the trend can learn from this example by cultivating consumers’ purchase habits before adopting affiliate marketing.

Our finding contributes to the literature on the revenue-management strategies of UGC platforms by investigating the scenario where UGC platforms can generate a new revenue source, that is, commissionbased revenue, by adopting platform-led affiliate marketing. As discussed in Section 2.2., the extant literature in this stream has focused primarily on platforms quality control policies (see, e.g., Ma et al. 2009), rating mechanisms (see, e.g., Ghosh and McAfee 2011), adrevenue-sharing schemes (see, e.g., Jain and Qian 2021), and optimal advertising level design (see, e.g., Ren 2023). Although affiliate marketing has now become an important revenue source for UGC platforms, there is scant literature to date that has systematically examined this business model. Our study fills this critical literature gap by exploring the interplay between platforms’ two revenue sources (i.e., traffic revenue and commission-based revenue) and identifying whether and when affiliate marketing can benefit UGC platforms.

## 5.3. Are Content Creators Better Off With Platform-Led Affiliate Marketing?

Many UGC platforms adopting platform-led affiliate marketing (e.g., TikTok) have claimed that this new business model can help creators better monetize their content by providing them with one more revenue source (Waters 2021). However, some creators have raised concerns about whether it is indeed beneficial (HawkeMedia 2020). In addition, in practice, not all creators are eligible to participate in affiliate marketing. For example, as previously stated, Instagram’s current policy is to only allow U.S.-based creators to produce shoppable content (AboutInstagram 2021). In such a case, creators ineligible for producing shoppable content may feel left behind and become concerned about how they would be impacted by the new business model. Thus, to provide implications for content creators, we next investigate how platform-led affiliate marketing impacts creators’ utilities and summarize our findings in the following proposition.

Proposition 3. Under the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing, compared with the regime without affiliate marketing:

a. Nonaffiliated creators are actually better off $( i . e . , u _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } ^ { * }$ $> u _ { i } ^ { o * } )$ if the base marginal commission revenue falls below a certain threshold (i.e., $\omega < \omega _ { 1 } )$ . Conversely, if the base marginal commission revenue exceeds the threshold (i.e., $\omega > \omega _ { 1 } )$ , then nonaffiliated creators are worse off.

b. Affiliated creators are always better off $( i . e . , u _ { i \in M } ^ { * }$ $> u _ { i } ^ { o * } )$ , regardless of the value of the base marginal commis sion revenue.

Platform-led affiliate marketing is commonly regarded as an effective tool to facilitate creators’ content production (Waters 2021). In this case, one may expect creators ineligible for participating in affiliate marketing to be worse off when some of their competitors have an additional means of monetizing content. However, interestingly, we find that nonaffiliated creators may sometimes be better off when other creators participate in affiliate marketing. This result can be explained as follows.

Recall that, as shown in Lemma 3, under the regime with affiliate marketing, nonaffiliated creators’ content quality is the same as that under the regime without affiliate marketing $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } q _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } = q _ { i } ^ { o } )$ ). As for affiliated creators, based on Proposition 1, when the base marginal commission revenue is relatively small $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \omega < \omega _ { 1 } )$ their content quality is lower than that under the regime without affiliate marketing $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } q _ { i \in M } < q _ { i } ^ { o } )$ . Thus, in such a case, nonaffiliated creators can generate more content consumption than in the regime without affiliate marketing $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } t _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } > t _ { i } ^ { o } )$ because their content con sumption decreases with affiliated creators’ content quality $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { \mathcal { I } t _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } } { \partial q _ { i \in M } } < 0 ) } \end{array}$ . As a result, with the same production strategies $( \mathrm { i . e . , } e _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } ^ { \ast } = e _ { i } ^ { o \ast } )$ , nonaffiliated creators can generate a larger utility $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } u _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } ^ { \ast } > u _ { i } ^ { o \ast } )$ because of the increased demand, which indicates that nonaffiliated creators can enjoy the spillover effect from other creators’ participation in affiliate marketing. As for affiliated creators, although they may experience a reduction in the consumers’ consumption, leading to a lower base utility and monetary incentive provided by the platform, the additional commission revenue (i.e., $\bar { ( 1 - \alpha ) } ( \omega + \lambda \eta ) t _ { i \in M } )$ always outweighs the potential loss, leaving them better off with affiliate marketing. In such a case, platform-led affiliate marketing serves as an effective means of market differentiation among content creators. Specifically, under platform-led affiliate marketing, the affiliated creators focus on the commissions generated from affiliate marketing, whereas the nonaffiliated creators focus on the benefits of providing highquality content.

Proposition 3 yields important implications for content creators in the presence of platform-led affiliate marketing. It is reported that many creators have increasingly relied on affiliate marketing for monetary rewards. For example, an affiliated creator on Instagram once claimed to earn more than \$500 monthly from shoppable content production (Cooper 2021). However, the opportunities to generate commission revenue by producing shoppable content are available only for selected creators on Instagram (AboutInstagram 2021). Given that their competitors can participate in affiliate marketing and receive additional monetary rewards from content production, creators ineligible for shoppable content production may worry about their situations. Our finding delivers an important message to content creators. All creators can benefit from the platform’s adoption of affiliate marketing, even those ineligible to participate. Furthermore, this finding can have significant implications for creators who are deciding which platform to join. Specifically, our result suggests that even if creators are ineligible for producing shoppable content on certain platforms, they can still benefit from joining the platforms that adopt affiliate marketing. Therefore, opting for platforms with affiliate marketing could be a promising strategy for creators.

Our finding contributes to the body of literature analyzing content creators’ production utilities. Specifically, we contribute to this stream of literature by demonstrating that platform-led affiliate marketing can serve as an effective means of market differentiation among content creators. This result is absent in the extant literature (see, e.g., Ren 2023) primarily because previous studies typically focus on the scenario where a UGC platform incorporates third-party advertisements into consumers’ viewing experience (e.g., TikTok can insert ads into the “For You” feed). In such cases, the nuisance caused by these ads tends to reduce consumers’ time spent on all creators’ content. In contrast, in the platform-led affiliate marketing that we study, the promotional activity is embedded only in affiliated creators content, and nonaffiliated creators are therefore not negatively influenced by the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing. Consequently, affiliated creators’ participation in affiliate marketing may potentially provide a competitive advantage for nonaffiliate creators, leading to the market differentiation outcome.

## 6. Comparative Static Analysis

In this section, our objective is to provide guidelines for the optimal strategies of UGC platforms and content creators by addressing the last two research questions presented in Section 1.2. Subsequently, we delve into the analysis of content creators’ optimal production strategies in Section 6.2.

## 6.1. Should the Platform Set a Higher Commission-Split Rate as Competition Among Creators Intensifies?

As discussed earlier, the emerging “creator economy” on UGC platforms can be regarded as a gig economy for digital content, and the immense power imbalance between platforms and creators puts the latter at risk for being exploited (Chayka 2021). Such an issue is further exacerbated by the increasingly intense competition among creators, potentially leading creators to devote more effort in order to retain the audience (Binod 2021). Thus, as competition among creators intensifies, it seems that the platforms can further extract creators surplus by setting a higher commission-split rate. However, an overly high commission-split rate would reduce creators’ production incentives, which would in turn lower platforms’ content quality and hence, the traffic revenue. Therefore, as competition among creators intensifies, platforms are increasingly facing the challenge of how to compensate creators properly. Unfortunately, there are currently no guidelines for platforms on how to tackle such a challenge. To address this issue, we investigate how platforms should set the optimal commission-split rate as competition among creators intensifies and summarize the findings in the following proposition.

Proposition 4. Under the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing, as competition among creators intensifies (i.e., with an increase in n or θ):

• If the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing is relatively large (i.e., $\delta < \sqrt { ( 1 + \xi ) / ( \gamma - \xi ) } )$ , the platform actually benefits from setting a lower commission-split rate (i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial \alpha ^ { * } } { \partial n } < 0 \ a n d \frac { \partial \alpha ^ { * } } { \partial \theta } < 0 ) } \end{array}$

Our findings provide important managerial implications for UGC platforms. Many UGC platforms (e.g., TikTok) have been providing monetary rewards to incentivize content creators to exert more production efforts (Hutchinson 2020). However, creators tend to devote more production efforts to competing for consumers as competition intensifies (Binod 2021). Thus, in such a case, one may expect the platforms to reduce the monetary rewards provided to content creators. However, our results in Proposition 4 suggest that with the additional commission-based revenue source provided by affiliate marketing, as competition among creators intensifies, UGC platforms should compensate creators more. Specifically, they should set a lower commissionsplit rate to incentivize creators’ production and thus generate more content consumption. In addition, there has been a growing trend in recent years where UGC platforms increase the monetary rewards for their content creators, and such a practice is widely believed to serve as an effective means of competing with other platforms (Malik 2021). Our results provide an alternative explanation of this phenomenon by suggesting that UGC platforms still have incentives to provide more compensation to their creators even in the absence of cross-platform competition.

Our results contribute to the literature on the strategies of UGC platforms by investigating how the platforms adopting affiliate marketing should set their commission-split rates optimally. To the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to investigate the optimal commission-split-rate decision of platforms that can generate additional commission-based revenue through affiliate-link-enabled transactions. Such an analysis is absent in the extant literature (see, e.g., Bhargava 2022) mainly because the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing is still in a nascent stage, although it has been increasingly leveraged by UGC platforms to help realize their enormous e-commerce potential. The extant literature has focused mainly on studying how UGC platforms that adopt the ad-revenue-sharing business model should optimally share their revenue with content creators. For example, Jain and Qian (2021) examined how a platform should share its advertising revenue with content creators and found that it should share more as the number of creators increases.

Unlike Jain and Qian (2021), our results suggest that as the number of creators increases, the platforms that adopt the affiliate-marketing business model may sometimes be better off providing less compensation to creators. This result is driven mainly by the unique characteristics of platform-led-affiliate-marketing business models. First, unlike the ad-revenue-sharing business model, the platform has two revenue sources (i.e., traffic and commission-based) under affiliate marketing. With the additional revenue source (i.e., commission-based), the platforms rely less on consumers’ total consumption and hence, are less incentivized to compensate creators. Second, because of the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing, affiliated creators are less effective in increasing consumers’ consumption through devoting production efforts than nonaffiliated creators. As a result, platforms incentives to compensate affiliated creators may be lower under affiliate marketing. Thus, our study also contributes to the literature on the strategies of UGC platforms by studying platform-led affiliate marketing, a novel business model whose underlying mechanism significantly differs from that of the ad-revenue-sharing business model.

In practice, the platform’s decision on the commissionsplit rate is influenced by important context-specific parameters, namely the monetary incentive provided by the platform and the additional marginal commission resulting from the consideration of product attributes. In such cases, as $\overset { \cdot } { \lambda }$ or $\eta$ increases, the increase in the demand-decreasing effect outweighs that in the margin-increasing effect, prompting the platform to lower the commission-split rate.

## 6.2. Should Creators Devote More Effort to Production as Competition Intensifies Under Platform-Led Affiliate Marketing?

According to Mark Zuckerberg, the cofounder, chairman, and CEO of Facebook, the adoption of platformled affiliate marketing on Instagram aims to empower more creators to make a living from the platforms (Silberling 2021). This brings exciting opportunities for creators, because they can now monetize their content and generate additional commission revenue through the production of shoppable content. Conversely, however, consumers under platform-led affiliate marketing may consume less content because of the nuisance caused by product promotion (Admin 2019), which may, in turn, hurt the creators and lower their effectiveness in boosting content consumption. Thus, under affiliate marketing, creators need to develop an optimal production strategy, which is especially important considering that they are facing increasingly fierce competi tion nowadays (Wallace 2021). Despite its importance, there are currently no proper guidelines regarding the development of an optimal production strategy in the face of intensified competition. Thus, we next explore how competition intensity impacts creators’ optimal decisions on production efforts and present the results in the following proposition.

Proposition 5. Under the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing:

a. Affiliated creators may not necessarily increase their efforts toward content production as competition intensifies. Specifically,

• their optimal production efforts actually decrease with the number of creators on the platform $( i . e .$ $\frac { \partial e _ { i \in M } ^ { * } } { \partial n } < 0 )$ if the marginal traffic revenue exceeds a certain threshold $( i . e . , \gamma > \gamma _ { 2 } )$ . Otherwise, $e _ { i \in M } ^ { * }$ increases with n.

• their optimal production efforts actually decrease with the substitutability among creators $( i . e . , \frac { \partial e _ { i \in M } ^ { * } } { \partial \theta } < 0 )$ if the marginal traffic revenue exceeds a certain threshold $( i . e . , \gamma > \gamma _ { 3 } )$ and the number of creators producing shoppable content falls below a certain threshold $( i . e .$ $m < m _ { 1 } )$ . b. In contrast, nonaffiliated creators always increase their efforts toward content production as competition intensifies $\begin{array} { r } { ( i . e . , \frac { \partial e _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } ^ { * } } { \partial n } > 0 a n d \frac { \partial e _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } ^ { * } } { \partial \theta } > 0 ) } \end{array}$

Proposition 5 yields important implications for content creators on UGC platforms. As previously stated, content creators face increasingly fierce competition and are striving to retain their audience by providing more engaging content (Binod 2021). This is especially true for those who rely on content production for monetary benefits. However, based on our results, creators participating in affiliate marketing should be fully aware that it is not always beneficial for them to increase their production efforts in the face of intensified competition. Instead, cutting back on their production efforts may prove to be an effective strategy, although it may lead to a decline in the consumption of their content. In the meantime, when adjusting their production strategies, creators should take into consideration the specific reasons causing the intensified competition. For example, many UGC platforms nowadays offer compensation (e.g., Creator Fund provided by TikTok) to attract creators, resulting in a large number of creators and hence, intensified competition on the platforms. In such a case, affiliated creators should reduce their production efforts, especially when the platform can generate relatively large revenue from consumers’ traffic. By contrast, in another scenario where a particular trending topic on the UGC platform causes a sensational effect, many creators on the platform tend to create their content based on the same topic, leading to higher content substitutability and hence, intensified competition (Burtch et al. 2022). In such a case, when deciding the production strategies, affiliated creators should also take into account the number of creators participating in affiliate marketing. In practice, the platform can provide creators with analytical tools (e.g., Instagram competitive analysis) to help them identify the specific causes of the intensified competition, and then creators can adjust their production strategies accordingly.

The findings in Proposition 5 contribute to the literature on creators’ production strategies. To the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to investigate creators’ optimal production decisions in the scenario wherein some can generate commission revenue by producing shoppable content. In addition, most of the extant literature (see, e.g., Jain and Qian 2021) shows that the increase in the number of creators intensifies competition, leading them to devote more effort to production. Our finding contributes to the existing literature by showing that creators participating in affiliate marketing may sometimes be better off cutting back on their production efforts as the number of creators increases. The reason that our result differs from that in the literature is that we capture platforms’ strategic decisions on the commission-split rate. Specifically, under platform-led affiliate marketing, platforms may set a larger commission-split rate as competition among creators intensifies, leaving affiliated creators worse off devoting more production efforts to competing for consumers. In addition, the extant literature usually investigates the impact of competition intensity captured by the number of creators on the platform (see, e.g., Jain and Qian 2021), the proportion of time-constrained consumers (see, e.g., Jain and Qian 2021), or the level of heterogeneity among creators (see, e.g., Bhargava 2022). Our study also adds to the existing literature by considering creators’ competition arising from their content substitutability.

To examine the influence of the monetary incentive provided by the platform and the additional marginal commission brought by the consideration of product attributes on creators’ production strategies, we conduct comparative static analyses and summarize our findings in Corollary 2.

Corollary 2. Under the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing:

a. As the monetary incentive provided by the platform (ξ) increases, creators increase their efforts toward content production.

b. As the additional marginal commission resulting from the consideration of product attributes increases (i.e., λ or η increases), affiliated creators increase their efforts toward content production.

As shown in Corollary 2(a), creators’ production efforts increase with the monetary incentive provided by the platform (ξ). This is because as ξ increases, creators can derive higher utility from consumers’ unit consumption, which incentivizes them to increase their demand by devoting more effort to content production. Additionally, we find that affiliated creators devote more effort to content production as λ or η increases (as shown in Corollary 2(b)). This occurs because as λ or η increases, the marginal commission revenue generated by consumers’ shoppable content consumption also increases, thereby encouraging affiliated creators to attract consumers’ consumption and devote more effort to production.

Furthermore, based on Proposition 5, nonaffiliated and affiliated creators adopt distinct production strategies, which may result in variations in their content quality. To gain a deeper understanding of these variations in content quality, we conduct comparative static analyses and summarize our findings in the following corollary.

Corollary 3. Under the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing:

a. As the platform’s marginal traffic revenue γ increases, the quality difference between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators (i.e., $| q _ { i \in M } - q _ { i \in \overline { { M } } } | )$ increases if the base marginal commission revenue exceeds a certain threshold (i.e., $\omega > \omega _ { 1 } )$ . Otherwise, the quality difference decreases with γ.

b. As the creators’ production cost c increases, the quality difference between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators decreases.

As shown in Corollary 3(a), the quality difference between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators increases with the marginal traffic revenue γ if the base marginal commission revenue exceeds a certain threshold (i.e., $\omega > \omega _ { 1 } )$ . This can be explained as follows. As $\gamma$ increases, the platform sets a lower commission-split rate (see Online Appendix A), incentivizing affiliated creators to increase their efforts toward content production because they can generate higher commission revenue from consumers’ unit consumption. As a result, content quality of affiliated creators improves with an increase in $\gamma .$ On the other hand, the production efforts and content quality of nonaffiliated creators remain unchanged as $\gamma$ increases. Furthermore, as demonstrated in the proof of Proposition 1, when $\omega > \omega _ { 1 }$ , affiliated creators’ content quality is higher than that of nonaffiliated creators. Therefore, when $\omega > \omega _ { 1 }$ , as $\gamma$ increases, the quality difference between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators further expands. Additionally, as indicated in Corollary 3(b), the quality difference decreases with the creators’ production cost c. This is because as c increases, both nonaffiliated and affiliated creators are better off lowering their production efforts. Consequently, creators devoting more efforts are more significantly influenced by the increased $c ,$ resulting in a reduced difference in efforts between nonaffiliated and affiliated creators. As a result, the disparity in their content quality decreases with c.

## 7. Model Extensions

We have thus far examined the impact of platform-led affiliate marketing on key stakeholders within the UGC ecosystem. Now, we aim to expand our analysis in four distinct directions. First, in Section 7.1, we delve into a scenario where creators exhibit heterogeneity in production capabilities. Second, in Section 7.2, we investigate a scenario where the platform determines the monetary incentive provided to content creators. Third, in Section 7.3, we explore a scenario where the quality of shoppable content influences the marginal commission revenue. Lastly, in Section 7.4, we study a scenario where the platform allows all creators to produce shoppable content.

## 7.1. Heterogeneous Content Creators

In the main model, we consider that content creators are symmetric to reflect the reality that creators have become increasingly homogeneous because of the surge of content creation training programs and creator toolkits (Lahey 2020, Bhargava 2022). However, in practice, creators may sometimes be heterogeneous, and such heterogeneity may also influence the optimal decisions of the key stakeholders. Hence, in this subsection, we consider the case where creators are asymmetric and investigate the impact of creators’ heterogeneity on our main findings. Following prior literature (see, e.g., Liu and Feng 2021), we consider that the creators are heterogeneous in their capability to produce content. Specifically, we consider some creators to be more capable of producing content, incurring a production cost c for each unit effort devoted to production (hereafter referred to as low-cost creators or expert creators), whereas others are less capable and incur a marginal production cost of $\varphi c , \varphi > 1$ (hereafter referred to as high-cost creators or novice creators). Note that the value of $\varphi$ reflects the heterogeneity between the low- and high-cost creators (hereafter referred to as the heterogeneity among creators). In addition, we consider that the number of low-cost creators is $n _ { 1 } ~ ( 0 < n _ { 1 } < n )$ , and hence, the number of high-cost creators is $n - n _ { 1 }$ . Furthermore, as in the main model, we consider that when the platform adopts affiliate marketing, the number of affiliated creators is m. We consider that the number of low-cost affiliated creators is m<sub>1</sub> $( 0 < m _ { 1 } < m ) _ { \nparallel }$ ; thus, the number of low-cost affiliated creators is $m - m _ { 1 }$ . Other assumptions remain the same as those in the main model.

We conduct an analysis similar to the main model and derive equilibrium outcomes. Our analysis demonstrates that the main findings still hold when creators have heterogeneous production costs (the details are provided in Online Appendix B). Additionally, we find that when creators have heterogeneous production costs, even among affiliated creators, distinct production strategies are adopted by low- and high-cost individuals, resulting in variations in content quality. To deepen our understanding of these variations in content quality among affiliated creators, we conduct comparative static analyses and summarize our findings in the following corollary.

Corollary 4. When creators have heterogeneous production costs:

a. As the platform’s marginal traffic revenue γ increases, the quality difference between low- and high-cost affiliated creators $( i . e . , q _ { i \in M _ { L } } - q _ { i \in M _ { H } } )$ also increases.

b. As the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing decreases (i.e., δ increases), the quality difference between low- and high-cost affiliated creators increases as well.

c. As the creators’ production cost c increases, the quality difference between low- and high-cost affiliated creators decreases.

As shown in Corollary 4(a), we find that the quality difference between low- and high-cost affiliated creators becomes more pronounced as the platform’s marginal traffic revenue $\gamma$ increases. This can be explained as follows. With an increase in $\gamma ,$ the platform finds it advantageous to reduce the commission-split rate $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { \partial \alpha ^ { * } } { \partial \gamma } < 0 ) } \end{array}$ in order to incentivize creators’ content production and generate higher traffic. Consequently, both low- and high-cost affiliated creators invest more effort in content production. In such a case, with the same increased marginal benefit but lower marginal production costs, lowcost affiliated creators devote greater additional efforts to content production than high-cost ones, leading to an amplified quality disparity.

Additionally, we find that a decrease in the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing (i.e., an increase in δ) contributes to a greater quality difference between low- and high-cost affiliated creators. This occurs because as δ increases, affiliated creators become more effective at attracting consumer consumption because of the diminishing adverse effect of affiliate marketing on their content quality. Consequently, the disparity in production incentives between low- and high-cost creators is further magnified, favoring low-cost creators who can derive more benefits from the increased efforts. This results in a more pronounced variation in content quality as δ increases. Moreover, we find that as the creators production cost c increases, the quality difference between low- and high-cost affiliated creators decreases (as shown in Corollary 4(c)). This can be explained similarly to the discussion following Corollary 3(b). In particular, as c increases, both low- and high-cost affiliated creators reduce their production efforts, with low-cost creators being impacted more significantly, leading to a diminished disparity in their production efforts and, consequently, a reduced quality difference.

## 7.2. The Monetary Incentive Is Endogenously Decided by the Platform

In the main model, the monetary incentive ξ is considered as exogenously given, allowing us to focus on the platform’s decision on the commission-split rate and also the impact of platform-led affiliate marketing on key stakeholders. In this subsection, we extend our analysis to explore a scenario where the platform can determine the monetary incentive provided to content creators. In such a case, the platform can jointly decide on both the monetary incentive and the commissionsplit rate to maximize its revenue under the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing. All other assumptions remain the same as those in the main model.

Like the main model, we derive equilibrium outcomes, with specific details provided in Online Appendix B. Based on these equilibrium outcomes, we further demonstrate that our key insights still hold qualitatively when the monetary incentive is endogenously decided by the platform. Additionally, by considering the endogenous decision of the monetary incentive $\stackrel { \cup } { \xi } ,$ we generate new insights, which are summarized in the following observation.

Observation 1. When the monetary incentive $\xi$ is endogenously decided by the platform, compared with the case without affiliate marketing, the platform benefits from reducing the monetary incentive provided to content crea tors ξ after adopting platform-led affiliate marketing.

In comparing the optimal monetary incentive provided by the platform without and with platform-led affiliate marketing, we find that the platform reduces the monetary incentive after adopting affiliate market ing (as shown in Observation 1). This can be explained as follows. With the adoption of affiliate marketing, con sumers’ unit consumption of content produced by affili ated creators generates additional commission revenue for the platform. However, because of the competition between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators, the signifi cant efforts devoted by nonaffiliated creators can reduce the platform’s commission revenue. Specifically, as non affiliated creators put more efforts into content production, consumers’ consumption of affiliated creators decreases because of content substitutability among creators. This reduction in consumption lowers the com mission generated by shoppable content and negatively impacts the platform’s commission revenue. Consequently, considering the smaller value contributed by nonaffiliated creators compared with affiliated ones and also the negative effect of their content production on the platform’s commission revenue, the platform is bet ter off lowering the production incentives of nonaffi liated creators. Because the platform can compensate the affiliated creators with a greater commission, it benefits from reducing nonaffiliated creators’ production incentives by lowering the monetary incentive provided to content creators after adopting platform-led affiliate marketing.

Our findings in Observation 1 convey an important message to UGC platforms implementing platformled affiliate marketing. Specifically, they should take measures to refine the production incentives for nonaf filiated and affiliated creators by adjusting the monetary incentive offered to them. Recognizing the reduced value generated by nonaffiliated creators, platforms should lower the monetary incentive for this group. In such a case, nonaffiliated creators should be aware that, in addition to potential intensified competition, creators participating in affiliate marketing can hurt their utilities by encouraging the platform to reduce the monetary incentive.

## 7.3. The Quality of Shoppable Content Influences the Marginal Commission Revenue

In the main model, we consider product attributes as a significant factor influencing the commission revenue generated from consumers’ unit shoppable content consumption. However, content quality may also impact consumers’ purchasing behavior and, consequently, the marginal commission revenue generated by shoppable content. Hence, in this subsection, we examine a scenario where in addition to product attributes, the marginal commission revenue also depends on the quality of shoppable content. Specifically, we formulate the marginal commission revenue generated by consumers’ consumption of affiliated creator $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ content as $\omega + \lambda \eta + k q _ { i \in M }$ . Here, $q _ { i \in M }$ represents affiliated creator i’s content quality, and k $( k > 0 )$ represents the coefficient of the correlation between the marginal commission revenue and content quality. Thus, kq<sub>i∈M</sub> represents the additional marginal commission revenue resulting from the consideration of the quality of shoppable content. All other assumptions remain the same as those in the main model.

We begin by examining the impact of considering the correlation between marginal commission revenue and content quality on our key findings in the main model. Because the resulting model is not analytically tractable, we resort to numerical studies for further analysis and find that the key insights hold qualitatively under this setting (the analysis details are provided in Online Appendix B). Moreover, our analysis uncovers new insights by considering the correlation between marginal commission revenue and content quality, which are summarized in the observations below.

Observation 2. When the impact of the quality of shoppable content on the marginal commission revenue is considered, under the adoption of platform-led affiliate marketing:

a. As the coefficient of the correlation between the marginal commission revenue and content quality (k) increases, the platform sets a lower commission-split rate.

b. Affiliated creators’ utilities increase with k, whereas nonaffiliated creators’ utilities decrease with k.

As shown in Observation 2(a), when the influence of the quality of shoppable content on the marginal commission revenue is considered, the platform is better off reducing the commission-split rate $\alpha ^ { * }$ as the coefficient of the correlation between the marginal commission revenue and content quality k increases. This is because as k increases, a unit improvement in content quality generates more commission revenue for affiliated creators, thereby incentivizing them to devote more effort to content production. Meanwhile, in such a case, the demand from affiliated creators becomes more valuable for the platform, because shoppable content generates more marginal commission revenue. Consequently, the platform benefits more from further expanding the total commission by incentivizing affiliated creators’ production and increasing consumers’ shoppable content consumption, rather than extracting a higher share of the commission. Consequently, the platform is better off reducing the commission-split rate as k increases. Additionally, Observation 2(b) reveals that as k increases, although affiliated creators experience an increase in their utilities, nonaffiliated creators’ utilities decrease. This is because, as shown in Observation 1(a), the platform sets a smaller commission-split rate as k increases, benefitting affiliated creators with more commission revenue. However, the increased incentives for affiliated creators to produce high-quality content also lead to intensified competition between affiliated and nonaffiliated creators. As a result, consumers’ consumption of the content produced by nonaffiliated creators decreases, leading to reduced demand and, consequently, lower utilities for nonaffiliated creators.

Our findings in Observation 2 have significant implications for key stakeholders involved in platform-led affiliate marketing. First, with the increasing influence of shoppable content quality on consumers’ purchasing behavior, UGC platforms should consider further com pensating creators participating in affiliate marketing by reducing the commission-split rate. This adjustment can incentivize affiliated creators to produce higherquality content, benefiting both the platform and the creators. Second, affiliated content creators should recognize the positive impact of the increased influence of content quality on consumers’ purchasing behavior. This increased influence grants them greater market power in negotiating commission splits with the platform, potentially leading to higher earnings from content production. Conversely, in such a case, nonaffiliated creators need to be aware of the intensified competition they may face. As affiliated creators gain more commissions and produce higher-quality content, it could become more challenging for nonaffiliated creators to compete for consumer attention and engagement. Therefore, nonaffiliate creators may need to explore alternative strategies to maintain their market presence and appeal to content consumers.

## 7.4. The Platform Allows All Creators to Produce Shoppable Content

As discussed in Section 3.2, in the main model, we consider the scenario where platforms allow only selected creators to participate in affiliate marketing. However, there is a trend among UGC platforms to extend this opportunity to all creators. For instance, Instagram has announced its plan to offer affiliate-marketing opportunities to more creators in the future (AboutInstagram 2021). Hence, in this subsection, we explore the case where the platform allows all creators to produce shoppable content, leading the number of affiliated creators (i.e., m) to be endogenously determined by creators’ production strategies.

Because the resulting model is not analytically tractable, we resort to numerical studies for further analysis. We find that our key insights hold qualitatively under this setting (the analysis details are provided in Online Appendix B). In addition, we generate new insights by considering creators’ shoppable-content production decisions and summarize them in the observations below.

Observation 3. When the number of affiliated creators (i.e., m) is determined endogenously by creators’ production strategies:

a. Creators encounter a “cold-start” problem in shoppablecontent production, where they refrain from producing such content until a critical mass is reached.

b. Creators’ shoppable content production strategies may sometimes conflict with the platform’s best interests.

As shown in Observation 3(a), creators will produce shoppable content only when a relatively large number of affiliated creators exist. This occurs because affiliated creators are less effective than nonaffiliated ones in boosting content consumption because of the nuisance brought about by affiliate marketing. Consequently, affiliated creators face more intense competition as the number of nonaffiliated creators increases. When creators participate in affiliate marketing, they often encounter a notable decline in consumer consumption. This issue is exacerbated by heightened competition stemming from an increasing number of nonaffiliated creators. On the other hand, creators can generate additional commission revenue (i.e., $( \omega + \lambda \eta ) ( 1 - \alpha ) t _ { i \in M } ^ { * } )$ through affiliate marketing. When the number of nonaffiliated creators is relatively large (i.e., m is relatively small), the decrease in creators’ demand and utilities due to participating in affiliate marketing is so substantial that it outweighs the benefits generated from the commission, leaving creators worse off producing shoppable content

In addition, as shown in Observation 3(b), creators incentives to participate in affiliate marketing may not align with the platform’s best interests. For example, in equilibrium, all creators may participate in affiliate marketing $( \mathrm { i . e . , } m ^ { * } = n )$ . However, in this scenario, the platform would be better off allowing only a subset of creators to produce shoppable content. Hence, allowing all creators to produce shoppable content, although it could be a viable equilibrium outcome, might represent a suboptimal strategy for the platform because of the potential misalignment between creators’ production decisions and the platform’s best interests.

## 8. Discussions and Conclusions

As the marketing potential of UGC continues to grow, an increasing number of third-party merchants are leveraging creators’ content to promote their products. Simultaneously, many content creators are eager to monetize their content by endorsing these merchants products to their audience. As a result, blending content with e-commerce has become an irresistible trend for UGC platforms. Furthermore, by adopting platform-led affiliate marketing, UGC platforms can partially realize the commercial potential of their traffic by charging a commission for each transaction facilitated through affiliate links. This win-win situation seems promising for all involved stakeholders. However, the proper operation of a UGC platform requires maintaining highquality content, which can be negatively impacted by affiliate marketing in the long run. In particular, excessive shoppable content could drive some users to leave the platform. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether the emerging platform-led affiliate marketing will truly benefit all stakeholders in the UGC ecosystem. To explore this further, we conduct an economic analysis of this new business model using game-theoretic modeling.

Our research makes substantial contributions from both theoretical and practical perspectives. Theoretically, our study contributes to the existing literature in several aspects. First, this paper stands as one of the earliest attempts to explore platform-led affiliate marketing, an emerging business model that blends content with e-commerce on UGC platforms yet remains largely unexplored in the extant literature. Second, we contrib ute to the literature on affiliate marketing by examining a scenario where a UGC platform adopts affiliate marketing, enabling its creators to produce shoppable con tent in exchange for a share of the commission. Third, we contribute to the literature on revenue management on UGC platforms by analyzing the interplay between UGC platforms’ two revenue sources: traffic revenue and commission revenue from affiliate-link-enabled transactions. Moreover, our findings enrich the growing body of literature that addresses how to incentivize content creators to produce high-quality content. We demonstrate that platform-led affiliate marketing can effectively incentivize creators to produce high-quality content by providing them with opportunities to promote products and earn commission revenue. Additionally, our research contributes to the literature on creators’ production strategies by investigating thei optimal production decisions in the context wherein some creators can generate commission revenue through shoppable content but are also negatively impacted by the nuisance caused by affiliate marketing.

From a practical standpoint, our research delves into several important issues with significant managerial implications for key stakeholders within the UGC ecosystem. We conduct a comprehensive study of the impact of platform-led affiliate marketing on UGC platforms’ content quality, revenue, and creators’ utilities. Our results reveal that when affiliate marketing is employed under appropriate market conditions, it can create a winning situation all around for consumers, UGC platforms, and affiliated creators (as shown in Propositions 1–3, respectively). Furthermore, even creators who are ineligible for shoppable content production can sometimes benefit indirectly from other creators’ participation in affiliate marketing. However, UGC platforms should remain mindful that they may not necessarily be better off adopting because of the potential loss in traffic revenue. This finding holds crucial implications for UGC platforms yet to adopt affiliate marketing. Because some major UGC platforms (e.g., Instagram) have already adopted platform-led affiliate marketing, others may be tempted to follow suit, expecting similar advantages. However, it is crucial for them to recognize that having an additional commission-based revenue source does not automatically guarantee higher profits. Instead of hastily following the trend of adopting affiliate marketing, UGC platforms may need to invest effort in helping consumers become more accustomed to purchasing through affiliate links.

Furthermore, many UGC platforms hesitate to adopt the business model because of concerns about its potential adverse impact on content quality. Our results deliver a crucial message that platform-led affiliate marketing can effectively incentivize creators’ production, leading to an improvement in content quality. This finding holds significant meaning for content consumers on UGC platforms. Often, consumers express dissatisfaction with excessive advertising content and have reservations about UGC platforms’ adoption of affiliate marketing. However, they can rest assured that they may experience better-quality content under the new model.

Additionally, our study examines how the platform should set the optimal commission-split rate when implementing platform-led affiliate marketing. Surprisingly, our findings show that UGC platforms are not necessarily better off setting a higher commission-split rate as competition among creators intensifies (as shown in Proposition 4). These results send a crucial message to UGC platforms that when generating additional commission-based revenue through affiliate marketing, they should take creators’ production incentives into consideration and may even be better off charging a lower commission to encourage creators to boost content consumption. This insight is particularly noteworthy for UGC platforms, given that many of them (e.g., TikTok) aim to compete with e-commerce platforms by focusing more on commission-based revenue (Forrester 2019) and may expect to adopt a higher commission-split rate, as traditional e-commerce platforms usually do.

Another important issue we investigate is creators optimal production strategies. Our results (as shown in Proposition 5) suggest that as competition among creators intensifies, nonaffiliated creators should devote more effort to content production; however, this is not necessarily the case for affiliated creators. Additionally, the changes in creators’ production strategies with competition intensity may depend upon the specific reasons causing the heightened competition (i.e., whether it is due to an increase in the number of content creators or an increase in creators’ content substitutability). These results carry significant managerial implications for content creators, many of whom are increasingly reliant on content production for monetary rewards (Binod 2021). Our results suggest that creators should not blindly invest more effort in competing for consumers’ attention. Instead, to better cope with intensified competition, they should identify the specific causes behind it, which can be achieved by utilizing various analytical tools (e.g., Instagram competitive analysis).
