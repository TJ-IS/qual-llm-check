# The Dark Side of Technological Modularity: Opportunistic Information Hiding During Interorganizational System Adoption

Abstract. The successful adoption of interorganizational systems (IOSs), such as blockchains or electronic data interchanges, in inter<sup>fi</sup>rm networks requires timely and accurate sharing of information about these roll-outs. In reality, however, the early stages of IOS adoption are characterized by information asymmetries that can arise due to a separation between <sup>fi</sup>rms that select and develop the IOS, and <sup>fi</sup>rms that implement it. This often result in staggered and asymmetric <sup>fl</sup>ows of information that can engender strategic information withholding behaviors. Drawing upon resource and network-based views, we study how the technological modularity of networked <sup>fi</sup>rms can affect these behaviors. Interestingly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that high levels of technological modularity can render <sup>fi</sup>rms more susceptible to opportunistic information withholding by network partners. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings run counter to the traditional view of modularity as a capability that can improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of IOS adoption, or as a governance mechanism that reduces risks associated with IOS adoption. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that network density and embeddedness can mitigate the opportunistic risks of technological modularity, whereas network topology can have diverging effects. In a rapidly changing and interconnected business environment where <sup>fl</sup>exibility is key, modularity is often hailed as a foundational pillar of information technology systems of the future. As optimism and investments toward modularity grow, by identifying associated risks, our work caution managers to adopt a more quali<sup>fi</sup>ed view of this capability during technological transitions.

## 1. Introduction

Interorganizational systems (IOSs), by enabling the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information and the integration of processes across <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries, can improve the ability of networked <sup>fi</sup>rms to leverage interorganizational relationships (IORs) for strategic gains (Rai and Tang 2010, Rai and Tang 2014). Notwithstanding the potential gains that IOSs can offer, the adoption of systems such as blockchains and electronic data interchanges continues to remain a challenge (Malhotra et al. 2005, Bala and Venkatesh 2007, Chang and Shaw 2009).

A growing stream of information systems (IS) research has been dedicated to understanding the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence IOS adoption. Information sharing between trading partners, within this stream of research, has been understood to be a crucial determinant of successful IOS adoption (Nicolaou and McKnight 2006, Venkatesh and Bala 2012, Peng et al.

2014). In discussing the role of information sharing during IOS adoption, however, much of the prior literature has relied on a trust and cooperation-based perspective of IORs (Dyer and Singh 1998, Gulati and Singh 1998). Such a perspective, however, ignores a fundamental tension that exists in IORs: whereas on the one hand, <sup>fi</sup>rms are expected to cooperate to maximize the shared bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from the relationship, on the other hand, they are also expected to pursue their own interests and to maximize their individual value through the relationship (Hamel 1991, Parkhe 1993, Provan 1993, Park and Ungson 2001). Managing IORs thus involves traversing a path between cooperation and noncooperation (Gulati et al. 1994, Hwang and Burgers 1997, Gulati et al. 2000). Drawing upon interorganizational theories of strategic action, the <sup>fi</sup>rst research question this paper aims to address is: How does the fundamental tension between cooperation and noncooperation in IORs affect the strategic information sharing behaviors leading up to IOS adoption?

In this study, we focus our attention on the strategic withholding of information by trading partners prior to IOS adoption. Whereas trust and cooperation among trading partners are achieved by the timely sharing of accurate information, <sup>fi</sup>rms motivated by short-term self-interest may, in certain situations, choose to withhold information from their partners (Nooteboom 1996, Das and Teng 2001, Das 2004). Our focus is motivated by prior literature that indicates that the early stages of IOS adoption can be particularly conducive for opportunistic information withholding. First, the early stages of IOS adoption are characterized by information asymmetries that can arise due to a separation between <sup>fi</sup>rms that select an IOS and <sup>fi</sup>rms that subsequently implement it (Eisenhardt 1989, Hart and Saunders 1997). An IOS is usually selected for adoption by dominant <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network, referred to as orchestrators (Riggins and Mukhopadhyay 1994, Markus and Loebbecke 2013). Because orchestrators become aware of the IOS before other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network, they attain an informational advantage over the others in the network. For example, in 2006, the decision to adopt Amadeus Altea, a new IOS, was jointly taken by two dominant airlines in the Star Alliance network, Lufthansa and United Airlines (Dunn 2006, Lazzarini 2008, Iatrou and Oretti 2016). Lufthansa and United Airlines, in this case, were at informationally advantageous positions. Attaining an informational advantage based on early awareness of an IOS adoption, we note, is not just limited to orchestrators. A broader subset of <sup>fi</sup>rms, due to information-rich positions within the network, can also become aware of the IOS adoption before their trading partners (see online Appendix II, Table A4). Once such an informational advantage is attained, agency theory posits that <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior information would be likely to withhold that information from their partners for opportunistic gains.

Second, transaction cost economics (TCE) indicates that the risk of opportunistic information withholding, due to conditions of asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city and uncertainty, can be more pronounced prior to IOS adoption (Williamson 1991, Jones et al. 1997). When <sup>fi</sup>rms are expected to adopt a new IOS, their existing investments in partner-speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets to facilitate peer-to-peer information exchange are threatened. For example, Neumann and Bachmann (2011) describe a case study of two European airline partners and note that after the two airlines had agreed on a partnership, they invested in partnership-speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets to customize their information technology (IT) systems toward each other. The potential loss of value from such existing investments can increase the risk of opportunism (Nooteboom 1996). Further, IORs are also characterized by uncertainty arising from ambiguity surrounding the timing of IOS adop tions. This type of environmental uncertainty can arise because of technological turbulence (Autry et al. 2010), and hence it may not be possible for <sup>fi</sup>rms to infer accurately the arrival of an IOS or associated standards. Overall, we posit that information asymmetries, asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city, and uncertainty, characterizing the early stages of IOS adoption, can foster conditions for opportunistic information withholding that need further examination.

Further, we note that the resource-based view of IORs suggests that strategic decisions such as the sharing or withholding of superior information depends on <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities (Wernerfelt 1984, Gulati and Gargiulo 1999). As a key capability, technological modularity, by enabling <sup>fi</sup>rms to separate their IT systems into independent functions (see, e.g., Tafti et al. 2013), allows <sup>fi</sup>rms to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently adopt new IOSs or standards (Liu et al. 2013, Xue et al. 2013, Rai and Tang 2014, Du et al. 2019). Technological modularity, by lowering partner switching costs, can also serve as a governance mechanism that mitigates risks associated with IOS adoption (Tiwana 2008, Xue et al. 2013). Recent studies on interorganizational strategy management have shown that technological modularity can also in<sup>fl</sup>uence strategic behaviors in IORs<sup>1</sup> (Schilling 2000, Worren et al. 2002). Thus, the second research question this paper aims to address is: What effect does technological modularity have on the stra tegic information withholding behaviors leading up to IOS adoption?

This question, in particular, demonstrates the practical importance of our work. To drive competitive advantage in today’s fast-paced and disruptive business environment, <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly investing in the modularization of their technology infrastructure. Hjartar et al. (2019) cites modularity as one the three pillars of a “future proof” technology foundation for business. Gartner (2020) reveals how chief information of<sup>fi</sup>cers invested in modularity for resilience during the COVID-19 pandemic crisis. Against this environment of optimism toward technological modularization, it becomes important to ask if there are any potential downsides to modularity. Strategic alliances offer a unique context to study this question. The payoff from modularization, in this context, depends on the strategic actions of partner <sup>fi</sup>rms which, depending on the situation, can be cooperative or noncooperative. Firms, during times of cooperation, can optimistically precipitate modularization while ignoring its perils, which may be realized when noncooperation arises.

The network-based perspective of IORs suggests that strategic decisions such as the sharing or with holding of relevant information can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the characteristics of the network in which the partner <sup>fi</sup>rms are ensconced (Gulati and Gargiulo 1999, Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001, McEvily and Marcus 2005, Zaheer and Bell 2005). The strategic effects of structural and compositional characteristics of a network such as size, density, embeddedness, heterogeneity, and topology have been previously examined in the context of IOS adoption (e.g., Riggins et al. 1994, Kauffman et al. 2000, Weitzel et al. 2006). Due to the ability of these characteristics to in<sup>fl</sup>uence information sharing strategies, they are likely to moderate the effects of technological modularity on the strategic information withholding behaviors during IOS adoption. Accordingly, the third research question this paper aims to address is: What effect do network structure and composition have on the relationship between technological modularity and strategic information withholding behaviors prior to IOS adoption?

To address these research questions, we develop a two-period game that models the transition from a state of bilateral IT customization between partner <sup>fi</sup>rms to a state of standardized information exchange post IOS adoption. We consider a subset of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network, such as orchestrators, to have privileged early awareness about impending IOS adoption in the <sup>fi</sup>rst period and investigate their decisions to share this information with their network partners or strategically withhold it from them. We then perform a comparative statics analysis to study the effects of technological modularity and environmental uncertainty on the equilibrium outcome. Finally, relying on a network-level perspective, the role of heterogeneity in modularities and various structural characteristics of the network on equilibrium decisions are investigated using a novel level-k thinking approach (Stahl and Wilson 1994, 1995) with the aid of network simulations.

## 1.1. Main Contributions

Through our study, we make several contributions to the extant literature. First, by identifying how strategic noncooperation in the form of information withholding arises during IOS adoption, we augment the existing trust and cooperation-based theories of IOS adoption. Our model demonstrates that understanding the true impact of antecedents on IOS adoption requires a consideration of both cooperative and noncooperative behaviors that can arise during the adoption process.

Second, our results uncover surprising insights. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that (i) <sup>fi</sup>rms with suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high technological modularity are at a greater risk of opportunistic information withholding by their partners with early awareness of impending IOS adoption, and (ii) <sup>fi</sup>rms with lower technological modularity with early access to this information are more likely to engage in opportunistic information withholding. By demonstrating that high modularity can make <sup>fi</sup>rms susceptible to opportunism exercised by their partner <sup>fi</sup>rms, we extend the current theorization of technological modularity from being a capability that can drive IOS adoption through ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency improvements and risk reduction (Baldwin and Clark 2000, Xue et al. 2013), to also being a capability that can engender additional vulnerabilities during IOS adoption.

The challenge posed by our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the riskmitigating role of modularity, we note, is particularly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. TCE states that investments in relationship speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets increase the risk of partner opportunism. Firms must therefore implement governance mecha nisms to mitigate such risks (Parkhe 1993). Recently, IS scholars have argued that modularity, by lowering switching costs and providing strategic <sup>fl</sup>exibility, can substitute for process control as a governance mechanism (Tiwana 2008, Xue et al. 2013, Schermann et al. 2016). Our study offers an important quali<sup>fi</sup>cation for this viewpoint: modularity may no longer substitute for process control when investments in partner-speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets are threatened, as in the case of new IOS adop tion. When partner-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments are threatened, modularity itself can turn into a source of partner opportunism.

Finally, our study augments the theories on network characteristics and IOS adoption (e.g., Riggins et al. 1994, Weitzel et al. 2006, Zhu et al. 2006). Network heterogeneity, IS theories suggest, can enable IOS adoption through improved information <sup>fl</sup>ows within the network (Markus et al. 2006, Ryan and Tucker 2012). Improvements in information <sup>fl</sup>ow are expected to arise from cooperative knowledge sharing between <sup>fi</sup>rms with different capabilities. Focusing on heterogeneity in technological modularity, our study offers contrary evidence that suggests that heterogeneity can lead to greater presence of opportunistic information withholding, limiting the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information required for IOS adoption. In doing so, our study provides an alternative theorization of the role of network heterogeneity during IOS adoption.

## 2. Literature Review and Contextualization

We now elaborate on the existing literature that underpins our game-theoretic model development. We further contextually situate our contributions in relation to prior literature.

## 2.1. Stages of IOS Adoption

According to the staged model of innovation diffusion (Rogers and Beal 1957, Rogers and Shoemaker 1971), the adoption of a technological innovation such as an

IOS occurs in stages. The staged model has been widely used to study IOS adoptions by IS scholars (e.g., Premkumar et al. 1994, Fichman 2001, Bala and Venkatesh 2007). Following prior studies, three stages of IOS adoption can be conceptualized in a network setting: initiation, awareness, and implementation. Initiation refers to the stage where the network orchestrator decides to adopt a new IOS and undertakes the selection/development of the IOS. Awareness refers to the stage where the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network become aware of the new IOS. Finally, at the implementation stage, <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network decide to adopt the new IOS and begin to implement it.

The majority of studies on IOS adoption (see Online Appendix III) have focused on the implementation stage. Relatively little attention has been paid to the <sup>fi</sup>rst two stages of IOS adoption, initiation and awareness. However, the strategic behaviors undertaken by <sup>fi</sup>rms during these stages can affect the likelihood and extent of IOS implementation (Cooper and Zmud 1990). In a network setting, behaviors of the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms can also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the characteristics of the network in which the <sup>fi</sup>rms are ensconced (Gulati et al. 2000, Johnston et al. 2006). Focusing on the initiation and awareness stages of IOS adoption in a network, our model analyzes the strategic behaviors of <sup>fi</sup>rms, and studies how <sup>fi</sup>rm and network characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence these strategic behaviors.

## 2.2. Information Sharing and IOS Adoption

2.2.1. Information Sharing Strategies. In discussing the role of information sharing during IOS adoption, prior studies have assumed that the interactions between trading partners are based on trust and cooperation. Venkatesh and Bala (2012), for example, suggest that dyadic relationships are based on cooperation, which enables trading partners to share private and unpublished information with each other. Such a cooperation perspective is rooted in the relational view of dyadic relationships, which suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms would be willing to share information with their trading partners in order to create combined value that could otherwise not be created by either <sup>fi</sup>rm independently (Zajac and Olsen 1993, Dyer and Singh 1998). This view, however, has been challenged by the resource-based view, which suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms in dyadic relationships would indulge in strategic noncooperation by withholding crucial information from their trading partners in order to attain a competitive advantage. Prior studies have argued that <sup>fi</sup>rms’ strategic behaviors in inter<sup>fi</sup>rm networks can oscillate between cooperation and noncooperation (Gulati et al. 1994, Hwang and Burgers 1997, Gulati et al. 2000). Therefore, it is important to consider strategic noncooperation in modeling IOS adoption. Following these studies, our model identi<sup>fi</sup>es the presence of strategic noncooperative information withholding during IOS adoption and studies its antecedents.

2.2.2. Information Sharing Strategies and IOS Awareness. Studies on IOS adoption have primarily viewed information sharing either as an enabling mechanism for or as an outcome of IOS adoption. As an enabling mechanism, information sharing allows trading part ners to assess each other’s capabilities and to determine the likelihood of IOS adoption (e.g., Bala and Venkatesh 2007, Venkatesh and Bala 2012). Because IOS adoption, through a standardization of processes, improves information <sup>fl</sup>ows between a large number of trading partners, information sharing is also viewed as a direct outcome of IOS adoption (e.g., Zhao et al. 2011, Peng et al. 2014, Rai and Tang 2014). While employing the two perspectives, however, prior studies have focused on information sharing in the implementation stage of IOS adoption, that is, after all the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms have become aware of the IOS. The role of information sharing in driving awareness about an IOS has largely been neglected. Studies on the diffusion of innovation suggest that infor mation sharing plays a crucial role in raising awareness about an innovation (Robertson and Gatignon 1986). Because information sharing is strategic in nature, we note that networked <sup>fi</sup>rms can control the awareness of an IOS based on the strategies they adopt. Firms, for instance, can limit awareness of an IOS by withholding information about the IOS from their trading partners. This motivates us to analyze cooperative information sharing and noncooperative information withholding during the early stages of IOS adoption.

## 2.3. Synergistic Factors and IOS Adoption

The technology-organization-environment (TOE) is a popular framework for studying the synergistic factors that affect IOS adoption (Tornatzky and Fleischer 1990; Zhu et al. 2003, 2006). It posits that IOS adoption is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by three types of contextual factors: technological, organizational, and environmental (Tornatzky and Fleischer 1990). Technological factors represent the characteristics of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s existing technologies, and the new technologies being considered for adoption. Organizational factors represent the characteristics of the <sup>fi</sup>rms that are adopting the IOS. Environmental fac tors represent the characteristics of the environment in which a <sup>fi</sup>rm operates. Several factors within each type have been examined in prior studies (see online Appendix III). Following these studies, we account for the effects of all three types of contextual factors in our model. Technological modularity, an important technological driver of IOS adoption (Schilling 2000, Liu et al. 2013, Rai and Tang 2014), serves as our key variable of interest. Asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city, information asymmetry, and environmental uncertainty serve as our technological, organizational, and environmental factors, respectively.<sup>2</sup> We now examine each of these factors.

2.3.1. Technological Modularity as a Synergistic Factor. The adoption of an IOS requires substantial changes to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s existing business processes (Venkatesh and Bala 2012). Technological modularity, based on the existing literature, is primarily expected to play two roles associated with these changes: operational and governance. In the operational role, technological modularity, by providing infrastructural <sup>fl</sup>exibility, is expected to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the IOS adoption process. For example, Liu et al. (2013) note that a high degree of modularity enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to rapidly adapt new and existing IT systems with the IT systems of their trading partners. Recently, Du et al. (2019) discuss how technological modularity was leveraged to achieve operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in the Shanghai Health Information Exchange, an IOS that connects 156 electronic medical record systems operated by 69 different hospitals and clinics in China’s largest city. In the operational role, technological modularity is also expected to mitigate technological risks that arise during IOS adoption.

In the governance role, technological modularity is expected to mitigate the transactional risks that arise during the adoption of new IOSs. For example, Xue et al. (2013) note that technological modularity can provide <sup>fi</sup>rms with strategic <sup>fl</sup>exibility in forging new partnerships and reduce their dependency on speci<sup>fi</sup>c trading partners. Similarly, Tiwana (2008) <sup>fi</sup>nds that technological modularity, by lowering partner switching costs, can serve as an alternative to process control as an alliance governance mechanism. Overall, we note that whereas prior studies have examined how technological modularity affects process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, technological risks, and governance during IOS adoption, its role in shaping the information sharing strategies deployed by trading partners during IOS adoption has yet to be fully explored. This is despite the fact that the broader literature on strategic action in inter<sup>fi</sup>rm networks has established technological modularity as an important driver of strategic behaviors (Worren et al. 2002, Pil and Cohen 2006). Combining the resource-based view of <sup>fi</sup>rms and theories on inter<sup>fi</sup>rm strategic behaviors, our model examines the role of technological modularity in driving information sharing strategies during IOS adoption.

2.3.2. Conditional Factors. In discussing the role of conditional factors on IOS adoption, prior studies have relied on interorganizational theories (e.g., Hart and Saunders 1997, Bala and Venkatesh 2007, Venkatesh and Bala 2012). Consistent with prior studies, we use economic and management theories of IORs to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of three conditional factors: asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city, uncertainty, and information asymmetry on the relationship between technological modularity and information sharing in the context of IOS adoption.

Existing TCE studies have examined the role of asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city and environmental uncertainty in relation to IOS adoption (e.g., Grover and Goslar 1993, Zaheer and Venkatraman 1994). Asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city and environmental uncertainty are expected to be positively associated with the decision to adopt an IOS. Asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city, studies suggest, can drive IOS adoption by creating conditions of lock-in, which compel trading partners to cooperate and invest in relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets. Similarly, studies suggest that environmental uncertainty can drive IOS adoption by creating a need for innovation and growth through cooperation with trading partners. Interorganizational theories have argued that whereas asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city and uncertainty can result in cooperative behaviors (Heide and Miner 1992, Parkhe 1993, Dyer 1997, Luo 2008), they can also result in noncooperative behaviors (Heide and John 1988, Carson et al. 2006). De<sup>fi</sup>ning asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city as the degree to which IT systems are customized in a dyadic relationship, and environmental uncertainty as the uncertainty arising from a lack of information about an impending IOS adoption, our study extends the prior analysis on these factors by investigating their conditional effects on the information sharing strategies that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption decision.

Some prior studies have also examined the role of information asymmetry in relation to IOS adoption (e.g., Hart and Saunders 1997, Markus et al. 2006, Blind and Mangelsdorf 2016). Information asymmetry is expected to be positively associated with IOS adoption. These studies note that information asymmetries in a network arise as <sup>fi</sup>rms that initiate an IOS adoption, usually in conjunction with a subset of other <sup>fi</sup>rms, tend to possess information about the IOS that is initially not available to the entire network (reasons for this kind of uneven distribution of information are discussed in Table A4 in Online Appendix II). These studies argue that <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior information drive IOS adoption by sharing information with their trading partners to maintain openness and trust in their dyadic relationships. Although this idea of cooperative information sharing resulting from information asymmetries is supported by the relational view on dyadic relationships (Zajac and Olsen 1993, Dyer and Singh 1998), the resource- and agency-based views of dyadic relationships suggest that information asymmetries can also result in noncooperative information withholding due to a misalignment of interest (O’Donnell 2000, Meuleman et al. 2010, Mazzola et al. 2016). Incorporating both the relational and agencybased views into our model, we investigate the conditional effects of information asymmetry on information sharing strategies during IOS adoption.

## 3. Model Development and Preliminaries

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst formalize the notions for bilateral customization, IOS adoption, and technological modularity. We subsequently employ them to develop our game-theoretic model to investigate strategic information withholding by <sup>fi</sup>rms in an inter<sup>fi</sup>rm network. For the sake of clarity, we initially restrict our attention to a case in which the network consists of two representative <sup>fi</sup>rms denoted as <sup>fi</sup>rms 1 and 2. These concepts naturally generalize to a network of N <sup>fi</sup>rms with $N > 2$ , as we shall see in Section 5.

## 3.1. Bilateral Customization and IOS Adoption

3.1.1. Bilateral Customization. Bilateral or dyadic customization refers to the process of a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm developing and deploying IT capabilities to accommodate unique and idiosyncratic requirements of an individual partner <sup>fi</sup>rm for information sharing, governance, and transaction structure in an inter<sup>fi</sup>rm relationship. For example, in the case of the Nissan-Renault alliance, a network consisting of <sup>fi</sup>ve global automobile manufacturers, Nissan and Renault engaged in bilateral IT customization to align their business processes, exchange computer-aided design (CAD) data and other technical documents, and even coordinate vehicle procurement and distribution with each other (Cisco 2004).

We denote by $S = \left\{ s _ { 1 } , s _ { 2 } , \ldots \ldots , s _ { n } \right\}$ the entire set of different IT systems and processes across the entire alliance network. Bilateral customization efforts are exerted by a <sup>fi</sup>rm to align its internal systems toward its partner’s con<sup>fi</sup>guration in order to make the systems interoperable and generate a greater value. We denote the customization efforts by <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 and <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 in aligning system $s _ { k }$ toward each other by $e _ { 1 2 } ^ { k } , e _ { 2 1 } ^ { k } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ respectively. Let $\boldsymbol { e } _ { 1 2 } = \{ e _ { 1 2 } ^ { k } \} _ { s _ { k } \in S }$ and $e _ { 2 1 } = \{ e _ { 2 1 } ^ { k } \} _ { s _ { k } \in S }$ denote the vector of bilateral customization efforts exerted by <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 and <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 across all systems in S. We denote the individual costs incurred by the two <sup>fi</sup>rms in exerting bilateral customization efforts toward each other by $c _ { 1 2 } ( e _ { 1 2 } )$ and $c _ { 2 1 } ( e _ { 2 1 } )$ , respectively.<sup>3</sup>

The value derived by a <sup>fi</sup>rm from a partnership depends on the extent of efforts invested by the <sup>fi</sup>rm and its partner toward making their systems interoperable. A closer alignment between their systems leads to greater interoperability, lesser frictions while collaborating, and we therefore expect each <sup>fi</sup>rm to derive a higher value. Accordingly, we denote the value derived by <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 and <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 from the partnership by $v _ { 1 2 } ( e _ { 1 2 } , e _ { 2 1 } )$ and $v _ { 2 1 } ( e _ { 1 2 } , e _ { 2 1 } )$ , respectively. We further assume that the cocreated value derived by each <sup>fi</sup>rm is linear in the customization efforts<sup>4</sup> exerted by the two <sup>fi</sup>rms across each system and thus can be expressed as

$$
\begin{array}{r} v _ {1 2} (e _ {1 2}, e _ {2 1}) = \sum_ {k: s _ {k} \in S} \left(\frac {e _ {1 2} ^ {k} + e _ {2 1} ^ {k}}{2}\right) v _ {1 2} ^ {k} \\ v _ {2 1} (e _ {1 2}, e _ {2 1}) = \sum_ {k: s _ {k} \in S} \left(\frac {e _ {1 2} ^ {k} + e _ {2 1} ^ {k}}{2}\right) v _ {2 1} ^ {k}. \end{array}\tag{A1}
$$

For example, note that if neither <sup>fi</sup>rm exerts any customization effort toward the other, that is, if $e _ { 1 2 } ^ { k } = e _ { 2 1 } ^ { k } = 0$ across all systems $s _ { k } \in S ,$ , then the systems of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms are fully noninteroperable, and therefore, neither <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to derive any value from the partnership, $v _ { 1 2 } = v _ { 2 1 } = 0 .$

3.1.2. IOS Adoption. IOS adoption, on the other hand, can be conceptualized as a form of network-wide IT standardization that is achieved, for example, through the implementation of blockchain or advanced Enterprise resource planning (ERP) and Supply chain management (SCM) systems. It refers to the process of establishing a common set of requirements and/or interfaces for the exchange of information, governance, and transaction structure with a large number of partners, thereby reducing, or completely eliminating, the need for relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT investments in inter<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships (Malhotra et al. 2007, Rai and Tang 2014). For instance, an alliance of 45 <sup>fi</sup>rms including Westinghouse, Sears, Black & Decker, and Chemical Bank offers an example of IOS adoption where faster and low-cost electronic settlements for trade transactions were introduced across the network as a replacement for customized bilateral information exchanges (Lewis 2002).

A network standard can be interpreted as a common operating standard imposed across the network by one or more dominant <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network. We therefore model IOS adoption as a common con<sup>fi</sup>guration of an orchestrator, that is, a standard-setting <sup>fi</sup>rm z toward which all the other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network must modify their internal systems fully, thus facilitating collaboration with all other partner <sup>fi</sup>rms in the alliance seamlessly. Note that this is equivalent to each <sup>fi</sup>rm exerting the maximum possible effort in aligning its systems toward that of the standardsetting <sup>fi</sup>rm z. Therefore, the cost incurred by <sup>fi</sup>rms 1 and 2 due to IOS adoption can thus be written as $c _ { 1 } ^ { S } =$ $c _ { 1 z } ( \mathbf { 1 } )$ and $c _ { 2 } ^ { S } = c _ { 2 z } ( \mathbf { 1 } )$ , respectively, where represents <sup>1 1 1</sup>a vector of effort levels of 1 across all systems in S.

Further, because the IOS resolves all interoperabil ity frictions, the value derived by <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network across each partnership is maximized post IOS adoption. Therefore, it follows from (A1) that the values derived from the IOS by the two <sup>fi</sup>rms are given by $\begin{array} { r } { \overline { { v } } _ { 1 2 } = \sum _ { s _ { k } \in S } v _ { 1 2 } ^ { k } } \end{array}$ <sub>2</sub> and $\begin{array} { r } { \overline { { v } } _ { 2 1 } = \sum _ { s _ { k } \in S } v _ { 2 1 } ^ { k } } \end{array}$ , respectively.

We thus have a unifying framework for both bilateral customization and IOS adoption whereby IOS adoption is interpreted as standardizing informational exchange between all the <sup>fi</sup>rms in a network. This lets <sup>fi</sup>rms derive the maximum value from collaboration without any loss due to misalignment of systems.

Firms in an inter<sup>fi</sup>rm network function with a common expectation that partner <sup>fi</sup>rms do not act in ways that adversely impact the other (Barney and Hansen 1994, Krishnan et al. 2016) and periodically assess their partner’s actions for signs of opportunistic behavior (Schilke and Cook 2015, Krishnan et al. 2016). We will formalize this discussion subsequently while describing the decision problems faced by the <sup>fi</sup>rms.

As noted by Das (2004), the ambiguous nature of certain deceptive behaviors in strategic alliances, such as our current example of withholding certain critical information regarding the operating environment of the alliance in the future, renders it harder to effectively contract upon or penalize deceptive behaviors, which may result in termination or dissolution of the partnership. Our main results remain invariant to alternative speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of the penalty.<sup>10</sup> Further, hereafter in this paper, we assume that the penalty L is suf<sup>fi</sup>- ciently large because, if not, trivially, it will be always optimal for <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 to withhold information from <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 regardless of the incurred penalty.

Firm 1 takes into consideration the threat of penalty it faces when it decides, at the beginning of period 1, whether to share the information about the impending IOS adoption with <sup>fi</sup>rm 2. ## 4. Analysis of the Information Sharing Game

An equilibrium analysis of the information sharing game is now presented. Broadly, we characterize a decision condition that determines when a <sup>fi</sup>rm with early awareness of impending IOS adoption will withhold this information from its partner <sup>fi</sup>rm. We then perform comparative statics analysis to identify the role of modularities of the <sup>fi</sup>rms and the prevailing environmental uncertainty on the decision to with hold information.

Proposition 1 yields a two-regime policy whereby it is optimal for <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 to share information if its partner <sup>fi</sup>rm is of low modularity and withhold information if the partner is of suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high modularity. ## 4.3. Modularity of Focal Firm

Next, we analyze the moderating role of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm’s own modularity on its decision to withhold information about impending IOS adoption from its partner.

Proposition 2. Consider two partner firms 1 and 2 of modularities $P ^ { 1 }$ and $P ^ { 2 }$ in an interfirm network. Proposition 2 reveals that a <sup>fi</sup>rm of suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently low modularity is more likely to engage in opportunistic withholding of information from its partner <sup>fi</sup>rm. Proposition 2 therefore also quali<sup>fi</sup>es the assumption that technological modularity, in general, always complements informational superiority. To the contrary, it demonstrates that a <sup>fi</sup>rm with low modularity is better positioned to opportunistically bene<sup>fi</sup>t from having early awareness of impending IOS adoption. Qualitatively, due to its capability, a high modularity <sup>fi</sup>rm is expected to exert high levels of bilateral customization efforts, and in the absence of which, its partner <sup>fi</sup>rm shall infer opportunism. As a consequence, the effort required of a highly modular <sup>fi</sup>rm to hide its opportunism is expensive, and ultimately outweighs the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of free-riding on the partner’s efforts toward bilateral customization.

Propositions 1 and 2 delineate the parameter space with regard to modularity over which opportunistic behavior of withholding dominates. It would be important to understand how changes in modularity affect this delineation.

## 5. Network Effects and Information Withholding

In the previous section, we analyzed an information sharing game with two players and primarily examined how the technological modularity of <sup>fi</sup>rms interacts with inherent informational asymmetries to engender opportunistic information withholding in the context of IOS adoption. This entails an equilibrium analysis of the information sharing game in its fullest generality.

Consider a set of N <sup>fi</sup>rms arranged in an inter<sup>fi</sup>rm network represented by a graph, ${ \bf g } = ( N , { \bf e } )$ , where ${ \mathbf { e } } \subseteq$ $N \times N$ <sup>g e e</sup>denotes the set of links corresponding to pairs of <sup>fi</sup>rms that are partners in the inter<sup>fi</sup>rm network. That is, $j \in N ( i )$ iff $( i , j ) \in { \bf e }$ . At the beginning of <sup>e</sup>period 1, we consider an information state vector, $\sigma ,$ which denotes the private information state of each firm $i \in N$ such that $\sigma _ { i } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ . Thus, at the beginning of period one, a subset of <sup>fi</sup>rms, ${ \mathcal { O } } ,$ are privy to the information about the impending IOS adoption $( \sigma _ { i } = 1 )$ , whereas the <sup>fi</sup>rms in $N \backslash \mathcal { O }$ do not have access to such information $( \sigma _ { i } = 0 )$ . If a <sup>fi</sup>rm i is aware of impending IOS adoption, then $\sigma _ { i } = 1$ , else, $\sigma _ { i } = 0$

Further, if $\sigma _ { i } = 1 .$ , then <sup>fi</sup>rm i chooses an action, $a _ { i j } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ , with respect to each of its network partners $j \in N ( i )$ . That is, if <sup>fi</sup>rm i is aware of impending IOS adoption and decides to share its private information with a partner <sup>fi</sup>rm $j ,$ then $a _ { i j } = 1$ . If <sup>fi</sup>rm i opportunistically withholds this information from partner <sup>fi</sup>rm $j ,$ then $a _ { i j } = 0$ . Vacuously, if $\sigma _ { i } = 0$ , then $a _ { i j } = 0$ . We observe that for a <sup>fi</sup>rm $i , \sigma _ { i } = 1$ , if and only if either it is an orchestrator <sup>fi</sup>rm, $i \in { \mathcal { O } } ,$ or $a _ { j i } = 1$ for some partner <sup>fi</sup>rm $j .$ Subsequently, during period 1, all partner <sup>fi</sup>rms i and j choose their levels of bilateral customization efforts $e _ { i j }$ and $e _ { j i \cdot }$ As before, if <sup>fi</sup>rm i is unaware of impending IOS adoption, that is, if $\sigma _ { i } = 0 .$ , then it will exert customization efforts in period 1 in expectation of deriving value over all current and future periods (periods 1 and 2) with only knowledge of the prior probability $p _ { e }$ of the existing information exchange paradigm becoming obsolete in future periods. At the beginning of period 2, the IOS adoption is imposed on the <sup>fi</sup>rms. Finally, post IOS adoption, <sup>fi</sup>rms that discover opportunistic information withholding by partners impose a penalty on them.

Analyzing the equilibrium outcomes in games on networks is rendered challenging due to the multiplicity of equilibrium outcomes and the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in characterizing them (Galeotti et al. 2010). Often, even numerical studies of equilibria outcomes could be computationally intractable. Whereas traditional equilibrium analyses assume full rationality, a recent stream of economics literature has focused on developing approaches that incorporate bounded rationality of the participating players. Of these, a prominent approach is the level-k thinking model (Stahl and Wilson 1994, 1995 and Nagel 1995). The level-k thinking model develops a hierarchy of rationality by considering level-0 players to choose actions nonstrategically or using a naive strategy. Then, level-1 players choose a strategy that assumes all others players are level-0 players, and, in general, level-k players adopt a strategy pro<sup>fi</sup>le in expectation of all other players being of level- k 1 (Ho and Su 2013). Further, if for some $k ,$ the strategies adopted by level-k and level-<sub>(</sub>k <sub>−</sub> 1<sub>)</sub> players are identical, then, the strategy is in fact a Nash equilibrium outcome.

The level-k analysis lends itself to an implementation within a network simulation because it allows the iterative computation of the equilibrium outcome of our network information sharing game as follows:

Level-0 strategy: Each <sup>fi</sup>rm $i \in N$ considers the information sharing or withholding decision toward <sup>fi</sup>rm $j \in$ N i in isolation, and therefore, naively adopts the strategy derived in Section 4.

Level-k strategy: For $k \geq 1$ , each <sup>fi</sup>rm i considers the decision to share or withhold information of impending IOS adoption from <sup>fi</sup>rm j in anticipation that <sup>fi</sup>rm $j ,$ and all other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network, shall follow the information sharing strategies of level- k 1 .

The <sup>fi</sup>xed point of this iterative procedure, if one exists, shall then yield an equilibrium outcome of the information sharing game. The higher levels of analysis $( k \geq 1 )$ incorporate the role of network effects on the decision to share or withhold information about impending IOS adoption from partner <sup>fi</sup>rms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, they account for the possibilities such as it being optimal to share information with those partners who would regardless obtain the information from other players in the network. Apart from its computational ease, the presented level-k analysis can also be interpreted as an equilibrium re<sup>fi</sup>nement that selects among potentially multiple Nash equilibria and yields an arguably more natural outcome for further analysis.

We now proceed to consider the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of <sup>fi</sup>rm and network-level structural and compositional characteristics on the information sharing dynamics within inter<sup>fi</sup>rm networks. The networks in our simulations are generated using MATLAB 9.4. In each iteration, random networks of $N = 1 0 , 2 0$ , and 30 nodes are generated with a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed density of links. The modularities of the <sup>fi</sup>rms are randomly and independently assigned across a set S of 20 systems and $N / 1 0$ randomly selected <sup>fi</sup>rms are designated as orchestrators that have early awareness of impending IOS adoption. Then, the equilibrium information sharing or withholding decisions, as described earlier, are computed. The simulation process is repeated over 500 iterations before altering the parameters, such as density, heterogeneity of modularities in the network, embeddedness, and modularity of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>rm.

## 5.1. Structural Characteristics: Density, Topology, and Firm Embeddedness

The structural characteristics of a network presumably in<sup>fl</sup>uence informational <sup>fl</sup>ows between the constituent <sup>fi</sup>rms of the network. We investigate the roles of network density and topology, and <sup>fi</sup>rm embeddedness on opportunistic information withholding. Network density is formally de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ratio of existing partnerships between <sup>fi</sup>rms to the total possible num ber of partnerships in the network. We also study the role of <sup>fi</sup>rm embeddedness, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ratio of the number of existing partners of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to the total number of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network. Figures 3 and 4 present the summary of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the simulations. Finally, aside from the randomly generated networks, to investigate the role of network topology, we consider three special network structures: a fully connected network $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ when density equals one), a star network, and a ring network.

Observation 1. The number of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network that receive information about impending IOS adoption is increasing in network density.

A high level of network density is seen as being especially conducive for information sharing within the network. Dense networks present several alternate channels for the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information pertaining to impending IOS adoption. Therefore, for a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from opportunistic information withholding may not materialize and could be outweighed by the risk of being discovered and penalized in denser networks.

Observation 2. The likelihood of a <sup>fi</sup>rm receiving information about impending IOS adoption is increasing in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s embeddedness.

Firm embeddedness is a <sup>fi</sup>rm-level structural characteristic that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence its access to information in the network. Firms that occupy a relatively central position in the network by virtue of having several partners are more likely to gain early access to new information (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001). This <sup>fi</sup>nding is also supported by the results of our simulation, which show that as the embeddedness of a <sup>fi</sup>rm increases, so does the likelihood of the <sup>fi</sup>rm gaining awareness about impending IOS adoption.

Our simulations on special types of network topologies—a fully connected network, a star network, and a ring network—uncover additional key insights. First, note that a fully connected network is a special case of the left panel of Figure 3, where the density exactly equals 1 (i.e., in a fully connected network, all possible partnerships in the network exist and therefore its density is one). Fully connected networks are the most conducive for information sharing. They offer the maximum possible alternate channels of information <sup>fl</sup>ows to each <sup>fi</sup>rm, thereby disincentivizing opportunism across any individual link. From simulated networks of a star structure with N 30, and the central node being an orchestrator, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the average fraction of <sup>fi</sup>rms receiving information about impending IOS adoption is limited to 0.655. Whereas in random networks with size N 30 and of randomly chosen density \~ U 0, 1 , the average fraction of <sup>fi</sup>rms receiving the information is 0.813. Star networks structurally privilege orchestrators by providing them unilateral control over information <sup>fl</sup>ows in the network.<sup>14</sup> This results in reduced access to information to other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network. Although ring networks democratize structural control uniformly across the <sup>fi</sup>rms, they still suffer from insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient alternative channels for information <sup>fl</sup>ow in the network. Therefore, orchestrator <sup>fi</sup>rms with privileged early awareness of IOS adoption are more likely to engage in opportunistic information withholding. In fact, from our simulations on ring networks of size N 30, we obtain the average fraction of <sup>fi</sup>rms receiving the information is only 0.313, that is, less than a third of the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network gain awareness of impending IOS adoption.

## 5.2. Compositional Characteristics: Firm Modularity and Network Heterogeneity

The central objective of our analysis is to determine the role of technological modularity, a key compositional characteristic, on the decision to share or opportunistically withhold information about impending IOS adoption. Observation 3 demonstrates that <sup>fi</sup>rms with suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high modularity are potential victims of opportunistic information withholding from partner <sup>fi</sup>rms as con<sup>fi</sup>rmed through our simulation output displayed in Figure 4 (left panel). This generalizes the insights yielded from Proposition 1 in Section 4 to the network setting.

Observation 3. The likelihood of a <sup>fi</sup>rm receiving information about impending IOS adoption is decreasing in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s modularity.

Moving to the network perspective, the decision by an individual <sup>fi</sup>rm to share or withhold information about impending IOS adoption from its partner is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced not only by the modularities of the dyad but also by the decisions of other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network, which in turn will depend on their modularities. Therefore, network-level heterogeneity in modularities has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the information sharing dynamics in the network. Because the modularities of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network belong to a partially ordered set (as discussed in Section 3.3), network heterogeneity in modularities can be conceptualized as the difference between the number of modular partitions in the most and least modular <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network. Equivalently, the range of observed <sup>fi</sup>rm modularities in the network is a measure of the network-level hetero geneity in modularities.

Observation 4. The number of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network that receive information about impending IOS adoption is decreasing in network-level heterogeneity in technological modularity.

Our simulation results demonstrate that the information sharing across the network diminishes with an increase in heterogeneity in modularities. Heterogeneity in the technological modularities of networked <sup>fi</sup>rms results in partnerships between <sup>fi</sup>rms with asymmetric capabilities. Because <sup>fi</sup>rms with a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently low modularity are more likely to exploit their information superiority with partners of high modularity, a greater number of asymmetric partnerships in the network engenders higher opportunistic information withholding.

Finally, we also note that because each <sup>fi</sup>rm in the network is primarily optimizing its own welfare while deciding whether to share or withhold information from its partners, opportunistic information withholding arising from such decentralized decision making naturally results in a loss of network welfare as compared with a centralized setting in which all networked <sup>fi</sup>rms receive the information simultaneously. In our simulations, expectedly, networks in which a lower fraction of <sup>fi</sup>rms receive information about impending IOS adoption (such as at low levels of network density in the left panel of Figure 3, or at high levels of heterogeneity in modularities in the right panel of Figure 4) suffer a higher loss in network welfare.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings further reveal that network density and embeddedness can mitigate such opportunistic information withholding. The topology of the network also appears to play a key role in moderating the opportunism of member <sup>fi</sup>rms. ## 6.1. Theoretical Contributions

First, <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities such as technological modularity have been understood to be key determinants of IOS adoption. We argue that solely using a trust and cooperation-based perspective that presumes information sharing between network partners can result in an incomplete understanding of the true role of these capabilities in IOS adoption. A better approach, we suggest, requires consideration of both the positive effects created through cooperative information sharing and the potential negative effects created through noncooperative information withholding. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings thus open an opportunity to re-examine the effectiveness of <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities that have traditionally been known to drive IOS adoption.

Second, according to the existing theories on IOS adoption, the modularity of technological systems within a <sup>fi</sup>rm, and their heterogeneity at the network level, are both expected to improve the likelihood and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of IOS adoption. These improvements have been attributed to modularity’s ability to mitigate technological and transactional risk (Tiwana 2008, Xue et al. 2013), and to provide infrastructural <sup>fl</sup>exibility for the business changes that accompany adoption (Liu et al. 2013, Xue et al. 2013, Rai and Tang 2014, Du et al. 2019). Departing from this traditional view of modularity, our study demonstrates that under certain conditions, <sup>fi</sup>rm technological modularity and its heterogeneity across a network can affect IOS adoption by inducing opportunistic behaviors that limit information <sup>fl</sup>ows. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings thus call for IS researchers to adopt a more quali<sup>fi</sup>ed view of this key technological capability. Relatedly, one of our methodological contributions is a formal analytical operationalization of technological modularity, developed in Section 3.2, and introducing it to IOS literature. Ou work also opens avenues for future research into conditions that can trigger the detrimental effects of modularity, and into mechanisms that can lead to the early detection and mitigation of such effects.

Third, many of the existing models on IOS adoption assume that information about new IOSs is disseminated instantly and evenly across the network. Recent studies and real-world cases that we highlight have shown that this may not always be true. First, it is important to note that the selection and development of new IOSs is usually conducted by network orchestrators. Orchestrators, therefore, typically and certainly gain early awareness of prospective IOSs. Second, governance practices followed by orchestrators during IOS selection, development, and testing can further engender such information asymmetries (see online Appendix II). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, by demonstrating the adverse consequences of the staggered and asymmetric <sup>fl</sup>ow of information about new IOSs, create an opportunity to further investigate the role of orchestrators in governing information <sup>fl</sup>ows for IOS adoption. Orchestrators need to govern the <sup>fl</sup>ow of sensitive IOSinformation within the network. Future studies can investigate the relationship between governance mechanisms adopted by orchestrators, and the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and likelihood of IOS adoption.

## 6.2. Practical Implications

Our study also has practical implications for <sup>fi</sup>rms undertaking IOS adoption. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can serve to determine the extent to which technological systems should be modularized. Whereas undermodularization has always been known to adversely affect the likelihood and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of IOS adoption, over-modularization, our model reveals, can also engender risks during the early stages of IOS adoption. By providing a quali<sup>fi</sup>ed understanding of the overall effects of modularity on adoption, our work can help <sup>fi</sup>rms undertaking IOS adoption to <sup>fi</sup>nd a balance between under- and over-modularization.

Second, our study can guide <sup>fi</sup>rms in estimating the true cost of modularization. In estimating the overall costs of implementing modularized technological systems, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rms should account for the costs of governance mechanisms, complementary <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities, and structural changes that may be required to mitigate the potential negative effects of modularization. Firms attempting to modularize technological systems, for instance, may need to develop closer links with network orchestrators to reduce information asymmetries, invest in capabilities that better predict new technology adoptions in the network, and implement governance structures and processes that provide better monitoring of partner behaviors.

Third, our study can help <sup>fi</sup>rms identify some of the conditions on which the extent and cost of modularization should depend. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that the effects of modularity on IOS adoption are moderated by the structure and composition of the network in which <sup>fi</sup>rms are ensconced. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that <sup>fi</sup>rms operating in high levels of network density and embeddedness may face lower overall risks to modularization as these network characteristics, by mitigating opportunities for information withholding, may obviate much of the need for the capability, governance, and structure related changes discussed previously.

Fourth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can guide far-sighted network orchestrators in calibrating the extent and direction of information <sup>fl</sup>ows during the early stages of IOS adoption. Real-world cases suggest that orchestrators tend to strategically limit the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information about new IOSs during the early stages. Some orchestrators, for example, limit <sup>fi</sup>rm participation in IOS selection committees whereas others selectively pick <sup>fi</sup>rms for testing new IOSs before a network-wide rollout is undertaken. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s level of technological modularity can serve as a guide for determining whether it should receive early information about a new IOS. Our study suggests that by <sup>fi</sup>rst revealing information to <sup>fi</sup>rms with high levels of modularity, orchestrators may be able to mitigate opportunism in the network.

Overall, we believe that this study broadens our understanding of IOS adoption in networks by offering novel and counterintuitive insights on the role of information sharing behaviors and technological capabilities in the process of IOS adoption. Ultimately, we hope that the concepts introduced, and the theoretical models developed in this study will serve as a platform for launching future investigations into the nature, antecedents, and impact of <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics and behaviors during IOS adoption. From a practitioner standpoint, we also hope that our study improves awareness about the potential risks that arise during the early stages of technological transitions, and nudges practitioners to effectively allocate resources toward mitigating those risks.
