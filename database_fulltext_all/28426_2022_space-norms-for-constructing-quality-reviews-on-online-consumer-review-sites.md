# Space Norms for Constructing Quality Reviews on Online Consumer Review Sites

Jinghui (Jove) Hou,<sup>a,</sup>\* Xiao Ma<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. High-quality online consumer reviews are valuable to e-commerce stakeholders. Extant research mainly focuses on what drives consumers to submit reviews, whereas little has been done to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally theorize why review writers construct high-quality reviews versus post poor review content. To advance current theorizing on the behavior of writing quality reviews, we propose the concept of space norms, a distinct instantiation of social norms inferred from locally observing how others write reviews on the local digital space (i.e., the review site). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we theorize that the focal behavior is largely directed by space norms; space norms beget a sense of how one should do and induce conformity in the given social space. We tested the theory of space norms in a series of empirical studies, and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide converging evidence. Study 1 used experimental methods and demonstrates—with large effect sizes—that review writers tend to act in accordance with the space norm of the local site. Post hoc text analyses rule out an alternative explanation and corroborate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Study 2 used <sup>fi</sup>eld data collected from two e-commerce websites to further triangulate the space norms proposition. Another post hoc study con<sup>fi</sup>rms the robustness of the results. This paper contributes to information systems research by developing and empirically validating a theory of space norms that pinpoints the role of local digital spaces in constructing the focal normative phenomenon. It also lends insights to digital platforms and the information technology practitioners. Managers looking to elicit quality reviewing behavior should devise the local digital space to nurture the space norms for the desired behavior.

## 1. Introduction

High-quality reviews are valuable to e-commerce stakeholders. As consumer-generated online reviews have become an essential component of many e-commerce websites, the notion of quality reviews is widely embraced. Managers strive to elicit quality reviews. For example, Amazon.com publishes reviewwriting guidelines mentoring consumers to write “helpful and relevant” reviews. Many sites have followed suit: eBay.com, Target.com, Taobao.com, just to name a few. Researchers in information systems (IS) and marketing have dedicated considerable effort into studying drivers of review-writing behavior (see Online Appendix A for a literature review). Yet this research falls short of understanding what directs consumers to write quality reviews.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, extant research is concerned mostly with what affects whether a consumer writes a review or not, indiscriminating in the content quality of the reviews being written (Koh et al. 2010, Goes et al. 2014, Fradkin et al. 2018). The decision on whether to write a review is not the same as the decision on how to write it. The former is concerned with the propensity to submit a review; the latter re<sup>fl</sup>ects the effort of the reviewers and the extent to which useful content is contributed. Existing studies subject the two behaviors to the same analytical models (e.g., Burtch et al. 2017, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). However, posting a review does not mean that the reviewer has invested the effort to contribute a quality review. Indeed, most consumer reviews in the marketplace are overwhelm ingly brief, irrelevant, uninformative, and of little help to other consumers (Mudambi and Schuff 2010).

Furthermore, extant research assumes that “writing reviews is an intrinsically motivated task” (Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018, p. 871). It explains review behavior largely based on the motivation theories of word-of-mouth communication (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2004) in the sense that consumers are likelier to write reviews when they have extreme opinions (Koh et al. 2010); a desire for economic incentives (Burtch et al. 2017); and a great need for uniqueness (Cheema and Kaikati 2010), attention (Shen et al. 2015), and fame (Goes et al. 2014). Whereas this assumption may hold well for the review submission behavior, it may not hold for the behavior of writing quality reviews. For example, research consistently shows that offering monetary incentives increases review submissions (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2004, Fradkin et al. 2018), but at the same time, it is shown to reduce the overall quality of the written reviews (Burtch et al. 2017, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). Other studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that monetary rewards increase review quality (Stephen et al. 2012) or have no impact (Chen et al. 2010). These con<sup>fl</sup>icting <sup>fi</sup>ndings hint that the antecedents and impact mechanisms pertaining to the behavior of writing quality reviews may be distinct from those of the review submission behavior. Nevertheless, very little research has speci<sup>fi</sup>cally theorized why reviewers contribute quality reviews.

Research on content quality suggests that studying user-generated content should revisit the role of the information technology (IT), or digital, space wherein content generation takes place (Tilly et al. 2017). Research suggests that writing quality reviews is akin to contributing a public good (Chen et al. 2018). From this view, an online review site constitutes a social space in which reviews serve common interests; users contribute and also consume the review content. Behavioral scientists have long recognized that human acts that contribute to public goods need to be understood through social norms (Etzioni 2000, Bicchieri 2012). Norms are social expectations about the proper way to behave in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts (Bicchieri 2006). They guide behavior through a sense of how one should do and can be differentiated from attitudes or preferences, that ${ \mathrm { i } } s ,$ how people want to behave (Cialdini et al. 1990). Research on online communities implies that members contribute more or less to the communities regardless of their stated preferences; rather, the contribution behavior is determined by a sense that they should act collectively “in certain ways” and “in concert” (Tsai and Bagozzi 2014, p. 145). In research on content contribution, a growing consensus views content sites as collective spaces in which social norms emerge (Ali-Hassan and Nevo 2009, Tilly et al. 2017). Researchers also suggest that the underlying technology plays a part in the normative phenomenon (Faraj et al. 2016). Despite these insights, no speci<sup>fi</sup>c theory has been put forth to explain precisely why and how norms emerge and guide behavior or to specify the role of IT in this process.

The present work attempts to advance current theorizing on the behavior of writing quality reviews, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which review writers take effort to construct useful review content. We present a theory in which a new concept of space norms is assigned a central role to analyze the focal behavior. We contend that the act of writing quality reviews represents a normative behavior that is largely guided by norms through the social expectation that one should act a certain way. Our main proposition is that digital spaces (i.e., online review sites) serve as social environments and enable the formation of space norms, a distinct instantiation of social norms that is inferred from direct observation of how other review writers construct reviews in the local IT space. Space norms beget a sense that one should write reviews in accordance with the norm and induce the corresponding behavior on the given site. We test this proposition using multiple methods.

Our work has both theoretical and practical implications. On theory, conceptualizing IT as social spaces, we propose a concept of space norms. Departing from the general norm conceptualization, space norms are a distinct norm manifested in the local digital space. With this new concept, the proposed theory formulates how and why IT gives rise to and exerts normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the behavior of writing quality reviews. We answer calls for research to develop new modes of theorizing about the “constitutive role of technology” (Faraj et al. 2016, p. 680) in studying the social phenomena of collective action and social norms (Grover and Lyytinen 2015). At a time of increasing volume of available reviews with decreased quality (Weathers et al. 2015), this study stands to bene<sup>fi</sup>t practitioners. An understanding of the behavior of writing quality reviews as being a normative act, which hinges on the space norm of the local website, would guide managerial strategies that foster space norms for the desirable behavior.

## 2. Understanding the Act of Writing Quality Reviews as a Normative Behavior

A consumer review site is a public space in which users engage in social interaction through the production and consumption of reviews (Dellarocas 2003). The nature of writing quality reviews is akin to contributing a public good (Bolton et al. 2004, Chen et al. 2010, Chen et al. 2018). Consumers of a review site value quality reviews, the production of which requires effort from individuals. The act of writing quality reviews, thus, produces a positive externality (Bolton et al. 2004, Chen et al. 2010). As both contributors and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries of quality reviews, consumers of a review site are better off if they make contributions in the col lective interest because everyone can then enjoy the fruits of the social cooperation (Bolton et al. 2004). In such social situations, people largely cooperate despite the effort; they do what others think they should do (Willer 2009).

Such a form of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence speaks to the heart of the normative mechanism—to act in accordance with the social expectation about how one should behave, that is, the mechanism central to social norms theories (e.g., the focus theory of normative conduct, Cialdini et al. 1990; the social norms theory, Perkins and Berkowitz 1986; theory of normative social behavior, Rimal and Real 2005; theory of planned behavior, Ajzen 1991). Social norms are social expectations (i.e., beliefs of what others think) about the proper or acceptable behavior in given social contexts (Cialdini and Trost 1998, Bicchieri 2012). To the extent that a behavior is normative, social norms should uniquely predict behavior beyond that accounted for by personal attitudes or preferences (Ajzen 1991; Cialdini and Trost 1998).

We propose that the act of writing quality reviews is largely guided by social norms. Our proposition is in line with views of online communities and usergenerated content sites as social <sup>fi</sup>elds of cooperation, in which normative expectations and obligations emerge to shape communities’ social practices (von Krogh et al. 2012, Faraj et al. 2016). The literature on online consumer reviews also suggests that the socially desirable act of providing helpful reviews embodies the values of reciprocity and cooperation (Parameswaran and Whinston 2007). Empirical research further demonstrates that members’ contribution behavior in online communities is largely driven by a collective sense that they should act in certain ways (Tsai and Bagozzi 2014). Altogether, we contend that a social normative perspective could offer valuable insights into understanding the act of writing quality reviews.

## 3. Theory Development and Hypotheses

With a normative approach, we reason that the extant conceptualization of norms falls short of theorizing the focal behavior, precisely because it has not accounted for the role of IT $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ online review sites) in the normative phenomenon. In so doing, we present a theory to understand the behavior of writing quality reviews; central to the theory is the concept of space norms. This new concept allows us to theorize the constitutive role that IT plays in the process by which a norm arises and operates. The theory of space norms draws on social norms research and also underscores the distinction between space norms and the related concepts and mechanisms. Next, we present some background to the theory and elaborate on its conceptual distinctiveness. We then formally present the definitions and propositions from the theory and argue for its value and novelty. We specify the mechanism by which space norms in<sup>fl</sup>uence the focal behavior. We then form hypotheses accordingly.

## 3.1. Background: General Conception of Social Norms

The proposed concept of space norms is a distinct instantiation of social norms speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the phenomenon of writing quality reviews in the online review context. As an instantiation of social norms, the concept of space norms shares de<sup>fi</sup>nitional properties with the general conception of social norms: norms are social expectations about the proper behavior in a given context (Cialdini et al. 1990, Bicchieri 2006). The essential impetus that drives normative behavior lies in the sense that one should behave in a certain way (Bicchieri 2006). For instance, we feel that we should (or are supposed to) be silent in a library or that we should not litter in public places, and often we behave accordingly.

Social norms are different from private preferences because they inherently comprise other people’s preferences for what is proper (Bicchieri 2006). In a classic experiment by Cialdini et al. (1990), the researchers stuffed handbills into college students’ mailboxes in a public mailroom and observed what students would do with them. The mailroom was altered to be either littered with handbills or completely clean. They found that students dropped signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more handbills on the <sup>fl</sup>oor when the mailroom was littered than when it was spotless. In another experiment in a university computer room, Bator et al. (2014) found that students were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more likely to turn off their computers at logoff when all unused machines were kept off and to leave them on when the unused machines were left on. These studies demonstrate that norms are context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and can emanate from the physical environments in which the behaviors of others take place (Cialdini et al. 1990). Individuals tend to survey their social environments for what other people think and do (Bicchieri 2006). They assume people’s act is consistent with their preferences, and thus, the perception of how others behave begets the expectation that such a behavior is socially preferred in that environment, that is, a norm (Rimal and Real 2005).

## 3.2. Space Norms: Toward a Theory of Normative Influence in the Digital Space

We propose that digital spaces serving as social environments may convey norms of proper behaviors. In IS research, one growing view hints at certain technologies as collective spaces in which social norms emerge (Faraj et al. 2016, Tilly et al. 2017). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, some IT spaces in which users contribute, share, and consume user-generated content facilitates the emergence of collective action and social norms (Ali-Hassan and Nevo 2009, Tilly et al. 2017). In a review of content contribution behavior, Faraj et al. (2016) hints that content sites may shape perception of a local norm; this norm provides a “reference point” for users to “orient individual efforts” and contribute content that meet the norm (p. 677). In another review article, Tilly et al. (2017) emphasize that studying user-generated content must incorporate the properties of the content sites. They conjecture that the underlying technology could enable means by which users arrive at a normative quality standard in generating content. Despite these insights, however, no speci<sup>fi</sup>c theories specify the role of technology in constructing norms or explain the process by which normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence is exerted (Faraj et al. 2016, Tilly et al. 2017).

We present a theory that speci<sup>fi</sup>es why and how digital spaces give rise to norms and how the norms subsequently affect the behavior of writing quality reviews. In the following section, we elaborate on the conceptual distinctiveness of space norms from related concepts; the precise distinction is crucial for establishing when the theory is predicted to apply and how it is empirically operationalized.

3.2.1. Specifying the Conceptual Distinctiveness of Space Norms. Our space-norms theory argues for a mechanism different from herding. Herding is a broad phenomenon by which individuals follow the crowd and imitate the actions of others (Banerjee 1992). The mechanism that drives herding is observational learning (OL; Banerjee 1992). OL leads music consumers to choose frequently downloaded songs (Salganik et al. 2006), loan lenders to choose listings that have already attracted many lenders (Herzenstein et al. 2011), and shoppers to choose products with high past sales (Li and Wu 2018). According to OL, a Bayesian individual tends to adopt predecessors’ decisions and discount the individual’s private information because that decision is likely to yield the highest utility (Banerjee 1992). When the individual has little idea about how to choose, adopting a popular choice is rational (Greenwood and Gopal 2017). Thus, the herd appears as a convergence in choice, but its function emerges from the uncoordinated economic decisions of selfserving, utility-maximizing individuals. Accordingly, the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in OL is one-way dependent: one relies on the (morally neutral) information of others. Writing quality reviews is different. It involves a different form of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence that features interdependent cooperation among individuals. Conforming to norms as opposed to herding is typically not in the immediate interest of the individual, but rather re<sup>fl</sup>ects a collective interest.

The concept of space norms also departs from that of the general social norms.<sup>1</sup> The key difference lies in the action space—the set of possible practices—that produces the conforming behavior. Here, we use the classic antilittering norm to illustrate the point. It is straightforward to conform to the antilittering norm: all one needs to do is not to litter. On the contrary, the output of conforming to the norm of writing quality reviews is a high-quality review. To produce this output, however, it takes some ways of doing—for example, evaluating more features, providing more justi-<sup>fi</sup>ed information—which can generate a wide range of practices. Unlike antilittering, the production of the conforming behavior—writing quality reviews—is, by nature, open-ended. As such, the antilittering norm entails a conforming behavior that has a concrete, limited action space. By contrast, the norm of writing quality reviews entails an undetermined action space of in<sup>fi</sup>nite gradations.

This distinction is conceptually profound because it de<sup>fi</sup>nes how a norm is transmitted, inferred, and enacted. For the antilittering norm, the way to produce the conforming behavior (i.e., not litter) is in such a clear manner that one does not have to see others behavior to know how to conform. General norms such as this can all be unambiguously conveyed in a message (e.g., “Please do not litter,” Cialdini et al. 1990). Because the way to conform is concrete and clear, a norm can be univocally or explicitly communicated via messages. A normative message typically contains norm-conformity statistics that specify the behavior of others (e.g., “75% of hotel guests reused their towels”) and the social expectation that one should conform; such messages are used in public announcements propagated via emails, print, and mass media and can effectively exert normative impact (Cialdini and Trost 1998, Goldstein et al. 2008). Thus, in the general conceptualization, “a norm is a verbal description of a concrete course of action regarded as desirable, combined with an injunction to make certain future actions conform to this course” (Parsons 1949, p. 75, italics added).

Following this norm conceptualization, IS researchers apply normative messages to mobilize behaviors, such as the use of green technologies (Fluchter et al.¨ 2014). Related to online reviews, two IS studies adopted normative messages to stimulate review submission behavior. Chen et al. (2010) found that providing normative messages via email, which included the number of movies rated by a median (typical) user, led those who formerly rated fewer than the median to rate more. Similarly, Burtch et al. (2017) found that sending a text message<sup>2</sup> that showed the number of buyers submitting reviews increased the ratio that subsequent buyers submitted reviews of an online retailer. Overall, social norms manifest as nor mative messages that convey the norms and exert the normative impact.

Conversely, the norm of writing quality reviews does not spell out a concrete course of action. Rather, the conforming behavior has an open-ended action space in which in<sup>fi</sup>nite gradations of practices can produce a quality review. We can state the output of the conforming behavior (e.g., “Please write a quality review”). However, it is implausible to completely convey how to produce the conforming behavior with a verbal description.<sup>3</sup> Instead, we argue that one infers the norm of writing quality reviews through direct observation of how others do in the local digital space. As mentioned, norm emergence research suggests that humans have an innate tendency to survey the environment and watch out for the behavior of others (Bicchieri 2006). Observing how others behave allows one to infer what others deem as proper behavior; that is, one would infer a norm (Bicchieri 2006). Indeed, the expected ways of behaving are often embodied in the practices and routines of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c social contexts (Reno et al. 1993). The act of others exempli<sup>fi</sup>es the norm, and people make sense of the norm through local observation (Lapinski and Rimal 2005).

Research on group cognition lends further support to our argument. Cognitive psychologists demonstrate that humans have the ability to extract summary information about groups of people as wholes and form perceptions of social norms rapidly and accurately, described as people (as opposed to person) perception, ensemble coding, or summary perception (Dannals and Miller 2017, Phillips et al. 2018). They contend that humans are well-adapted to capture the distribution of how others engage in a behavior and sense how a typical member behaves in a group, which, in turn, guide their judgments and behaviors toward the group, such as <sup>fi</sup>tting in with others (Dannals and Miller 2017, Phillips et al. 2018). This line of research, thus, shows evidence for the cognitive mechanism underlying norm formation and perception in social contexts in which one directly observes the actions of an assembly of others (Phillips et al. 2018). Moreover, some research examines norm emergence among distributed individuals in a bottom-up manner, that is, how a norm arises without any prescription or centralized enforcer (Young 2015). It argues that, to realize this process, an individual must have the chance to observe or interact with others because the norms are directly inscribed in people’s practices; in so doing, one is also able to capture how to perform the normative act (Hollander and Wu 2011).

A key mechanism behind the norm emergence and perception outlined is that individuals have the opportunity to locally observe the practices of others in the same situation. In this vein, the ability of the environment to allow such local observation is crucial. Owing to the relative permanence<sup>4</sup> and accessibility of content in the digital space, one is able to fully observe how others construct review content. In this regard, a review site constitutes the social environment in which users’ review-writing practices take place and are also well-preserved. The inherent properties of the technology to preserve and exhibit the social practices, thus, allow the digital space to encode the shared understanding of the proper ways of doing in that space. This distinct instantiation of norms, labeled as space norms, stands in contrast to general norms: the space norm of writing quality reviews needs to be inferred in the given review site. In other words, the digital space enables emergence and transmission of a space norm by directly exemplifying the norm with observable practices of others in the same review site. As such, the space norms concept speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally formulates why and how IT gives rise to norms and exerts normative impacts; it allows us to analyze the focal phenomenon differently than the extant norm conceptualization. This attribute is further ana lyzed next.

3.2.2. A Theory of Space Norms. Based on the preceding analyses, we propose a theory to understand the behavior of writing quality reviews. We assume that quality reviews are a public good valued by users (Chen et al. 2018). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the concept of space norms as social expectations about the proper ways of behaving that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the common value or interest and that have to be inferred from locally observing how others do in the given digital space. The digital space is conceptualized here as the IT space (i.e., the review site) serving as a social environment in which the practices of writing quality reviews are preserved and observable to all. We use the term “space” to echo the notion that IT constitutes a “collective space” in which users’ social practices take place (Faraj et al. 2016).

We argue that the behavior of writing quality reviews is largely guided by the space norm in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital space. Review writers tend to infer the space norm of writing quality reviews via direct observation of how others construct reviews on the local site. Here, the observation of how others behave begets the perception that such a behavior is deemed proper by others, which leads to the inference of the space norm. The inferred space norm begets a sense that one should write reviews in accordance with the norm and induces the corresponding behavior. Because what others expect to be proper depends on the (practices of) others that are being referred to, the space norm of writing quality reviews varies with the local IT space. One would infer a high expectation that one should write a quality review—a strong space norm—in an IT space full of quality reviews. By contrast, one infers a low social expectation for one to write a quality review on a site with many poor-quality reviews, which is a weak space norm.

Overall, the theory of space norms explains the mechanism through which digital spaces give rise to the focal normative phenomenon. It speci<sup>fi</sup>es the indispensable role of the inherent IT properties that enable the construction, transmission, and enactment of the space norm.

3.2.2.1. Social Presence. Space norms are an instantiation of social norms. To the extent that our behavior is guided by a norm, the behavior conforms to what we believe others expect from us (Cialdini and Trost 1998). In this regard, a space norm impacts behavior in the digital space in which users have social contact with others, that is, in a social environment (as opposed to a private or empty environment). This speaks to an essential factor of the normative impact: the perceived social presence of the digital space. Social presence refers to the feeling of being there with others in a mediated environment (Short et al. 1976), that is, a sense of contact with others in a shared context (Kumar and Benbasat 2006). The space norms theory argues that the impact of a space norm on behavior varies depending on the extent to which one feels social contact with others. In a place in which one does not feel any contact or connection with others, what others think would not matter much, and one is less likely to do what others think one should do. Conversely, in a place in which one feels strongly that one is together with others, what others think matters more, and one is more likely to act in accordance with the norm. As such, social presence moderates the extent to which one conforms to space norms.

3.2.2.2. Scope. The scope of the space norms theory encompasses the behavior of writing quality reviews on e-commerce websites on which the reviews are constructed by general consumers. An important caveat is that the reviews are about products and services with core attributes or features that a general consumer is capable of assessing and writing about (e.g., price of a tablet, cleanliness of a hotel). To specify this, we use the search, experience, credence classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Ford et al. 1988). The space norms theory is predicted to apply to products and services in the search and experience domains. The theory is unlikely to apply to writing reviews for credence goods. Credence goods refer to those with attributes that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult or impossible to assess; professional services (e.g., medical, legal) fall in this domain (Darby and Karni 1973). For example, it is found that patients in general are not able to assess the core attributes of doctors’ services, such as their diagnostic skills and cost-effectiveness of the treatments; instead, reviews of doctors are mainly about patients expressing feelings of needs (Siering et al. 2013). Therefore, a general consumer does not have the capabilities to evaluate and write reviews about the core attributes of credence goods regardless of the impact of space norms.

3.2.2.3. Theoretical Novelty. The proposed theory advances IS research. Adopting the general norm conceptualization, extant norm-related IS research mainly reiterates and corroborates the predicted effects of normative messages on behavior change (e.g., Chen et al. 2010, Burtch et al. 2017). Though valuable in the applied contexts, these studies are essentially testing the established normative messages in a new context. From a theoretical perspective, little understanding was added about the role of the IT itself; extant research cannot explain how certain digital spaces give rise to norms and exert normative impacts as IS scholars are concerned (Faraj et al. 2016, Tilly et al. 2017). The space norms theory contributes to this gap of knowledge. It speci<sup>fi</sup>cally explains how and why local digital spaces (i.e., online review sites) give rise to norms and shape the behavior of writing quality reviews. Thus, the space norms theory presents an IS theory that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally addresses the constitutive role that IT plays in the focal normative phenomenon.

## 3.3. Hypotheses

We now empirically test the core propositions from the theory of space norms. We predict that the behavior of writing quality reviews is subject to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of space norms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when a reviewer sees that others write quality reviews on a site, the reviewer infers that there is a high social expectation here that the reviewer should write quality reviews, that is, a strong space norm. This expectation, thus, directs the reviewer to act accordingly: write a quality review. When others’ reviews are poorly written on a site, the reviewer is less likely to infer that other expect the reviewer to write a quality review, that is, a weak space norm. Rather, the reviewer infers that writing a poor-quality review is deemed acceptable or proper here. Thus, the reviewer has a weakened feeling that the reviewer should expend effort to write a quality review.

In addition, we predict that the impact of a space norm on behavior is stronger when a writer experiences more social presence of the review site. As noted, to the extent that one’s behavior is guided by the space norm, the behavior conforms to what one believes others expect. Online review sites enable users to experience social presence (Kumar and Benbasat 2006). As the feeling of social presence increases, one feels more strongly that one is with others in the digi tal space; thus, one is more likely to conform to what others expect. As such, social presence moderates the extent to which the space norm directs the focal behavior. Summarizing our propositions, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1. The space norm inferred from a given review site on which others contribute high (versus low)

quality reviews leads individuals to write high (versus low) quality reviews.

Hypothesis 2. The impact of space norms on the behavior of writing quality reviews is stronger for individuals who feel more social presence of the review site.

We tested the hypotheses in a set of studies (see Table 1). Study 1 tested our focal hypotheses with a laboratory experiment. A post hoc study ruled out an alternative explanation. In Study 2, the main hypothesis was triangulated with <sup>fi</sup>eld data of actual reviews from two e-commerce sites; the results also verify the external validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Another post hoc study con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the robustness of the results and ruled out a series of alternative explanations in the <sup>fi</sup>eld settings.

## 4. Study 1: Testing Main Hypotheses

Study 1 used a laboratory experiment to test the impact of space norms on the behavior of writing quality reviews, the operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition of which is the extent to which a reviewer constructs review content that is relevant, informative, and helpful to the evaluation of the focal product or service; is thoughtfully composed; and provides information about whether a product or service has certain features and how well they function.

## 4.1. Experimental Design and Materials

One hundred sixty-eight undergraduates (89 males, $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 2 0 . 0 1 , \ S D _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = \bar { 1 . 2 5 } )$ at a large U.S. university participated in the study for course credit. The study used a one-way, between-subjects design with three conditions (space norms: high versus low versus control) in which the website presented either (1) four high-quality treatment reviews, (2) four low-quality treatment reviews, or (3) no reviews (control condition). We chose one search product (calculator) and one experience product (headphone). A separate pretest with 80 undergraduates con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that our participant population was familiar with calculators and headphones. The selected products were displayed on a <sup>fi</sup>ctitious e-commerce website, TodaysDeals.com, modeled after Amazon.com (see Online Appendix B). Each web page presented two product photos, a product description, and price information followed by reviews ostensibly written by others (i.e., the treatment reviews) and a text box in which participants could write their own reviews.

4.2.1. Choice of Four Treatment Reviews. By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, to perceive a norm, one needs to perceive how some other people behave. Namely, it is a perception of a group of persons. Otherwise, if it was one person or two, how this one or two persons behave may just be seen as idiosyncratic rather than as a social expectation (i.e., a norm). Thus, the perceptual unit here is a group (Latane´ 1981). Given this logic, past research shows that a size of four persons is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for the impact of a group to be felt (Latane´ 1981). That is, four other persons are adequate for one to infer a norm and sense the normative impact. Increasing beyond four persons (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>ve, six) does not change further that the perception is of a group (Latane´ 1981). Thus, we included four treatment reviews.

## 4.3. Procedure

On arrival at the laboratory, participants were told that they would be testing two products and writing reviews for them. The cover story for this task was about evaluating popular products. To begin, participants tested the two products individually for 15 minutes: they read the product manuals, used the calculator to solve <sup>fi</sup>ve math problems, and connected the headphone to an iPad and listened to a song clip. All participants received the same math problems and song clip. We purposefully held the product-testing experience constant to minimize its possible impact on participants’ review writing. Next, participants were introduced to the experimental website and instructed to read the product information and the treatment reviews on the site. Then, each participant wrote one review for the product. After submission of the review, they were directed to the web page for the second product and repeated the process. The tasks were performed individually on computers in separate cubicles.

After submitting the reviews, participants completed a questionnaire that used the scale in Kumar and Benbasat (2006; see Online Appendix E) to measure perceived social presence.<sup>7</sup> As manipulation checks, they rated how relevant, thoughtful, informative, and helpful they believed the others had been in writing the reviews $( \alpha = 0 . 9 1 )$ and evaluated review valence. Finally, they reported age, gender, and past experience in writing online reviews and answered some dummy questions about the products before being debriefed and dismissed. A suspicion probe during debrie<sup>fi</sup>ng showed no participant was aware of the true nature of the study. A randomization check con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed that participants’ review-writing experience and demographics did not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the results reported in this study.

## 4.4. Measures for the Behavior of Writing Quality Reviews

The outcome variable of interest is the behavior of writing quality reviews. Note that a measure of the behavior of writing quality reviews is not equivalent to a measure of a high-quality review. The difference is important, and it also illustrates the conceptual basis of our measurement. For example, many metadata of a review, for example, timeliness, reviewer reputation, the ratings a review received, albeit good indicators of high-quality reviews, are not about ways of writing quality content. In contrast, length is a key dimension in our measure; writing a longer review represents a way to write higher quality reviews because longer reviews allow more information to be conveyed than shorter ones (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011). Seeing others write long reviews can exemplify an expectation to which subsequent writers can conform (do the same way). As such, we do not intend to measure review quality per se; that is, not all indicators previously identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed to relate to high-quality review should be included. Online Appendix G elaborates on the inclusion criteria of the dimensions of our measurement.

To leverage machine learning and ensure reliability and consistency, we adopted two measures of the focal behavior: a textual analysis composite measure (quality 1) and a human-coded composite measure (quality 2; see Table 2 for an overview). A machine learning–based measurement (quality 1) is merited because of its high scalability once the algorithms are trained and validated. Compared with the conventional measure (quality 2), quality 1 can be instantly calculated and applied to vast review content online. Quality 1 encompasses four dimensions (see Table 2). Three dimensions—length, objectivity, and readability of review content—were found to affect how relevant, informative, thoughtful, and helpful a review is (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2007, 2011; Lu et al. 2013), and they meet our inclusion criteria to measure the focal behavior. Thus, we integrated these dimensions into a composite measure.

In addition, we added the number of product (or service) relevant points as the fourth dimension. Discussing product (service) features in the review is helpful for consumer decision making (Archak et al. 2011). We trained a text-mining algorithm and extracted the relevant points in each review. Different from Archak et al. (2011), who mainly used unsupervised learning (i.e., clustering), we used supervised learning methods (naïve Bayes classi<sup>fi</sup>er) because the algorithms and their programming tools have now become more mature. Our trained algorithm had acceptable classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance as measured by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (Hand and Till 2001).

Quality 2 is a human-coded composite measure of the focal behavior and includes four items: relevance, thoughtfulness, informativeness, and helpfulness. These four items re<sup>fl</sup>ect the operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the focal behavior. It also provides a benchmark for quality 1. To evaluate the reliability of quality 2, two coders blind to the purpose of the study rated each review on the four items in a scale from one (very low) to seven (very high); their ratings had high interrater reliability (Krippendorff’s $\begin{array} { r l r } { \alpha _ { s } } & { { } \ge } & { 0 . 8 6 ) , } \end{array}$ ; coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

## 4.6. Post Hoc Analysis

Although the results highly support our prediction that participants would act in accordance with the space norm at a given site (Hypothesis 1), it can be argued that this outcome is simply because of imitation. Before proceeding further, it is warranted to explore this alternative explanation. Empirical evidence exists that people tend to use words, clauses, or writing styles similar to the persons with whom they interact (Niederhoffer and Pennebaker 2002). Assuming participants copy what others say about the products (i.e., the treatment reviews), it follows that certain attributes of participants’ reviews mimic those of the treatment reviews. We adopted two different approaches to measure similarity: the Zheng et al. (2006) natural language processing (NLP) framework for identifying the authorship of online content and the Blei et al. (2003) latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) model. We used both to boost robustness: the former is theory driven and examines how lexical (high-frequency words) and structural attributes (average sentence length) of participants’ reviews matched the treatment reviews (Zheng et al. 2006); the latter is datadriven and uses an unsupervised-learning algorithm to quantify overlap in writing style (topic dimensions) between each pair of reviews (Wang and Chaudhry 2018). In addition, we used formal hypothesis testing to compare similarity. Consistent results emerged from these analyses. Both approaches reveal that participants’ reviews did not imitate the treatment reviews (see Online Appendix O for the results and a detailed discussion).

## 4.7. Discussion

The results provide support for the proposed effect of space norms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the space norm inferred on a site on which others write high (versus low) quality reviews led participants to exhibit the behavior of writing high (versus low) quality reviews (Hypothesis 1). On average, compared with their low-norm counterparts, high-norm participants wrote reviews that were about 2.5 times longer and contained about 1.6 times more product-relevant points. They are believed to have expended considerably more cognitive effort in constructing the reviews and contributed notably more useful content.

We expected that perceived social presence would enhance the impact of space norms (Hypothesis 2). For both products, the data provide support for an enhancing effect in the low-norm condition but not in the high-norm condition. As expected, when the space norm was inferred on a site on which others posted poor reviews, participants who felt more social presence of the site were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly likelier to write poor reviews. However, the data failed to detect that PSP boosted the high-norm effect in eliciting high-quality reviews. The lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in the high-norm condition may have occurred because of a ceiling effect in the behavioral data because there is an upper limit to what one can say about a given product. High-norm participants seemed to have contributed very high-quality reviews (see Online Appendix L); thus, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to raise the dependent measures further to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant degree even when PSP increases. Another possible explanation is that writing quality reviews requires time and effort, but posting poor, thoughtless reviews is less costly to a reviewer; thus, one may be more tempted to enact the less costly behavior when one has a stronger feeling that one is together with others who also post poor reviews. We further our discussion on social presence in “Limitations and Future Directions.”

Together, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Study 1 offer support for the predictions derived from the theory of space norms. In Study 2, we use a different methodological approach to test the main proposition.

## 5. Study 2: Consumer Reviews on Real E-Commerce Sites

Study 2 tested the effect of space norms (Hypothesis 1) beyond the experimental setting. Using unobtrusive <sup>fi</sup>eld data of real consumer reviews on two e-commerce sites, it also assessed the external validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We collected review data from Philips.com (an electrical appliance retailer) and Aritzia.com (a fashion retailer); both offer diverse categories and sub-brands of products. We chose these two sites to maximize the overall variation in product types, price levels, and target demographics. This allowed us to collect reviews that vary considerably in focus, style, and content quality. Consumer reviews are clearly and saliently laid out on the product web page of each site (see Online Appendix P for screenshots). As on typical review sites, each consumer review contains the review text written by a reviewer and a star rating (on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-star scale) that the reviewer assigned to the product.

## 5.1. Data Collection

From Philips, we obtained the complete review data of 74 unique products across the three main product categories (i.e., household products, personal care, and mother and child care). In total, we obtained 12,035 reviews, including the star ratings. Some of the products were highly popular and attracted numerous reviews (see Figure 2). From Aritzia, we collected 1,162 reviews of 363 unique products that represented 20 product categories. Across the two sites, we obtained heterogeneous reviews on products with diverse characteristics (rating, price, and popularity) across 59 subcategories (e.g., baby sippy cups, men’s shavers, women’s premier designer clothing). Figure 2 shows the distributions of the number of reviews across the products on each site.

## 5.2. Sample

Hypothesis 1 predicted that the space norm inferred from a given site on which others write high (versus low) quality reviews would lead the focal review writer to write high (versus low) quality reviews. As noted, four other reviews would be adequate for a norm to be perceived. Thus, we tested the review writing behavior of the <sup>fi</sup>fth reviewer, that is, the <sup>fi</sup>fth earliest review on each product page. To strengthen the robustness of the hypothesis testing, for each product, we also tested the fourth and sixth reviews. Reviewers could only have seen the prior reviews, and it is likely that they saw the prior reviews for the same product (Shen et al. 2015). On the other hand, for the later reviewers beyond the sixth, there could be too many possibilities for which prior reviews they might have seen and, thus, might have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced their review writing behavior.

## 5.3. Variables

5.3.1. Dependent Variable. We only used quality 1 to measure the focal behavior because quality 2 would be infeasible for the <sup>fi</sup>eld study because of the vast amount of human coding required. Given the high consistency between qualities 1 and 2 (see Study 1 results), we expect that quality 1 is a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient measure of this variable.

5.3.2. Independent Variable. To measure the mean quality of prior review writing behaviors (i.e., mean quality) for each focal review (e.g., the <sup>fi</sup>fth review), we <sup>fi</sup>rst calculated the quality 1 score of each of the prior reviews (e.g., the <sup>fi</sup>rst through fourth reviews) and then obtained their average score. Mean quality re<sup>fl</sup>ects the central tendency of the behavior of writing quality reviews of the prior reviewers. Thus, it is a reasonable measure to capture the inferred space norm of writing quality reviews.

5.3.3. Control Variables. We included the variance of quality of prior review writing behaviors (i.e., quality variance) because it might be argued that mean quality may not be meaningful unless quality variance is controlled for.<sup>10</sup> Quality variance indicates the extent to which quality 1 scores spread and vary in the prior reviews of a given product. Besides, we included the star rating accompanying each review (i.e., rating) to account for any potential bias caused by reviewer sentiment.

## 5.4. Results and Discussion

5.4.1. Model. We used a <sup>fi</sup>xed effects (FE) model to test Hypothesis 1, that is, the local space norm inferred from the prior review writing behavior is positively associated with the subsequent review writing behavior. That is, we expect mean quality to be positively related to the quality of the focal review. The FE model is motivated by the assumption that unobserved product-level characteristics (<sup>fi</sup>xed attributes) could systematically in<sup>fl</sup>uence review writing. For instance, reviewers may be motivated to write long and helpful negative comments about an unsatisfactory product because of a defect; if uncontrolled, such unobserved product-level factors would exert a systematic in<sup>fl</sup>uence on reviewer behavior, resulting in an endogeneity bias caused by omitted variables (Wooldridge 2010). The crux is that these potential factors cannot be observed or fully controlled using variables readily available even though the star ratings may capture some of the variances. Meanwhile, such factors tend to be <sup>fi</sup>xed in time and unique to each product. Thus, an effective solution is to model their collective in<sup>fl</sup>uence by including the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects terms in a panel-data regression model (Wooldridge 2010).

5.4.4. Discussion. Our <sup>fi</sup>eld data provide strong and consistent evidence for the main claim of the space norm theory. ## 6. General Discussion and Conclusion

The results of the two studies offer converging support for the theory of space norms. The behavior of writing quality reviews largely aligns with the space norm inferred from how others write reviews on the local site. The results imply that perceived social presence of the website may augment the impact of the space norm in inducing the behavior of writing poor, low-quality reviews.

## 6.1. Implications for Researchers

Despite the recent consensus to view digital technologies as collective spaces in which norms emerge, the processes of norm emergence and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence are indeterminate. We strive to contribute to IS research by proposing a new concept of space norms, and with this concept, we put forward a theory that formulates the role of IT (i.e., digital spaces) in constructing norms and speci<sup>fi</sup>es the process by which the normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence is exerted to guide behavior. We empirically tested the predictions derived from the space norms theory in experimental and <sup>fi</sup>eld studies and obtained converging support. To that end, we potentially provide an initial, yet more precise, pointer to scholars as to how to specify the normative mechanism using the concept of space norms and operationalize this concept.

The theory of space norms departs from extant IS research in addressing what factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence reviewwriting behavior. Extant research generally assumes that review writing is intrinsically motivated (Khernam-nuai et al. 2018), and thus, intrapersonal factors (motives, abilities, and traits) are posited as the pri mary behavioral determinants. Working from this assumption, most studies theorize about reviewwriting behavior without considering the digital space (i.e., online review sites) as part of the theory. This issue is broadly re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the Grover and Lyytinen (2015) review article showing that a majority of IS research adopts theories that have no bearing on the IT construct. They stress that most studies “ … conceptualize IT as an exogenous force, with no speci<sup>fi</sup>c, clear, or consistent basis for building IT constructs, and frail logic tying them to the social context” (Grover and Lyytinen 2015, p. 283) and urge the <sup>fi</sup>eld to move toward richer theorizing about the relationship between IT and the social phenomena of collective actions and social norms. Faraj et al. (2016) further highlight that “central to new modes of theorizing is a stronger, perhaps constitutive, role of technology in the very phenomenon under study” (p. 680).

Our proposed theory of space norms offers a potential contribution in this regard. In conceptualizing digital technologies as the local social environment, this theory pinpoints how and why IT can give rise to space norms that shape the focal normative behavior. Thus, the theory of space norms directly addresses the calls of the IS scholars to advance theorizing about the IT construct. Further, there is much evidence that a critical determinant of behavior is the environment or situation in which an individual is (Bicchieri 2012). Nonetheless, situational factors receive relatively less attention in IS studies. We highlight the concept of space norms as an instantiation of social norms indigenous to the IT space; it is a situational (i.e., IT-contingent) behavior driver that can be useful to explain behavior situated in speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital environments. The value of such situation-contingent behavior drivers <sup>fi</sup>nds support from research showing that users’ acts and decisions in online spaces are highly malleable and context-dependent (Salganik et al. 2006, Hou 2017).

Our research extends the social norms literature. The concept of social norms is certainly not new. However, the extant utility of social norms is inadequate to account for the focal normative behavior. Re<sup>fi</sup>ning the conceptualization of norms, we propose the concept of space norms—a distinct instantiation of social norms stemming from the local IT space—and argue for its distinctiveness from other norms. We also reason that certain IT properties—the observability of social practices—can foster norm formation. We believe the rigorous speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of norm manifestations and effect conditions are crucial not only to properly theorize the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of norms, but also to extend the theoretical lens of social norms to account for different forces and forms of normative impact.

We also contribute to the scarce research in IS on the behavior of writing quality reviews (versus on whether to submit a review). Less research exists in this line potentially because of the challenges in conceptualizing and measuring quality reviewing behavior. Guided by the space norms theory, we approached it as a normative behavior; re<sup>fl</sup>ecting its conceptual logic, we developed and validated a comprehensive, text analysis–based measurement of the focal behavior. We leveraged a wide range of mature machine learning and text-mining algorithms that can be ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently executed using opensource programming tools (e.g., Python). We demonstrate that the behavior of writing quality reviews can be adequately captured by four dimensions of the written texts (length, objectivity, readability, and number of relevant points). This measure shows promising reliability and consistency against a human-coded composite measure. We hope this methodological contribution can assist future work related to writing quality reviews, especially given the growing scholarly interests in various social in<sup>fl</sup>uences within online review sites.

## 6.2. Implications for Managers and Information Systems

National polls show that 97% of American consumers consult online reviews in making purchase decisions, and 59% write their own reviews (BrightLocal 2018). A majority appreciate quality reviews and indicate they know what useful reviews are like (Smith and Anderson 2016). Meanwhile, both scholarly and anecdotal evidence testi<sup>fi</sup>es that the current marketplace is plagued by frivolous and trivial reviews, a challenging issue that is likely to proliferate (Shoham et al. 2017). Our own analysis of more than 15,000 Amazon reviews corroborates this evidence (see Online Appendix S) and shows that an overwhelming majority of reviews are deemed “unhelpful.” Why do people continue to write poor reviews when their content is so apparent and widely understood to be unhelpful? Our research may shed light on this puzzling observation: the prevalence of poor reviews conveys salient space norms and induces the perception that writing poor reviews is acceptable and, thus, likely a proper thing to do.

To the extent that consumers’ behavior of writing quality reviews depends on what they see is being done on the local site, current managerial efforts, which rely on guidelines and incentives, may have a limited effect on behavior change. We suggest that managers looking to promote quality reviews attend to the local website environments, especially to the pertaining space norms that convey what content is deemed proper there. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that effort can be made to proactively elicit an initial number of quality reviews. For instance, online merchants can actively seek consumers’ feedback via one-on-one interviews and assist consumers to compose quality reviews. Merchants can consider inviting expert consumers to try out their products (services) and provide thorough reviews. Managers may also leverage existing workshops (e.g., sales and promotions) and include a session to call forth quality reviews.

Digital platform designers can assist by making salient high-quality reviews on a site’s interface. Some interface design elements, such as highlighting and other visual prominence techniques, might be imple mented to direct attention to quality reviews. Because people tend to weight more on reviews that are more perceptually salient (i.e., salience bias effect; Kahneman et al. 1982), making the quality reviews more salient may lead to the perception that most others write high-quality reviews here. Some writing tools that utilize advanced algorithms, such as arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence with machine learning techniques, can also be incorporated. Such tools may help reinforce the space norm by showing exemplar high-quality reviews at the point of writing. Our novel contribution to practitioners is the offering of a comprehensive, reliable, and scalable measure for identifying high-quality review texts. This approach uses not only unsupervised but also supervised learning algorithms that demonstrate high predictive performances. Once trained, it takes seconds for the algorithms to process a vast number of review texts. Managers can instanta neously identify quality reviews and implement the aforementioned techniques cost-effectively.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also highlight the need for managers to more proactively intervene when a site starts to show that posting poor reviews is a frequent and, thus, acceptable act. In this regard, the algorithm can also be used to dynamically predict the quality level while a review is being constructed. They may identify poor reviewing behavior during the writing, direct the reviewer’s attention to the high-quality reviews, and intervene immediately with a nudge (e.g., “Could you elaborate on your point? FYI, here’s what other fellow reviewers say”). Moreover, if writing quality reviews takes more effort from the writers, hence discouraging the desirable behavior, it may help increase norm conformity if the behavior can be made less effortful. Some readily deployable writing tools, such as predictive typing or spelling that can save a good portion of the keystrokes, may be integrated to save writers time and effort.

Some other cost-effective practices can be considered that align with the space norm theory. For instance, some research shows that displaying images of eyes or eye-like illustrations can make people feel that they are being watched and, thus, make them more accountable for their behaviors, that is, the watching eye effect (Ernest-Jones et al. 2011), which can be translated into interventions. IT platform designers can utilize some watching-eye elements at the moment when reviewers are about to construct reviews. Of course, such interventions could make users uneasy and, thus, warrant careful validation before implementation.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Directions

As an initial attempt to develop a new theory, this study has limitations that offer opportunities for future work. Perceived social presence was not a manipulated factor in this study. Thus, the moderating effect of social presence is inconclusive. Yet our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may suggest interesting future directions. It seems that an elevated feeling of social presence augments undesirable behaviors but not desirable ones. Although increasing social presence has long been deemed a means to improve mediated interaction, it may back<sup>fi</sup>re to intensify undesirable normative impacts. This echoes the broken windows theory (Wilson and Kelling 1982) that <sup>fi</sup>nds people tend to deface an environment if it has already been defaced, especially when the misconduct is tolerated by the neighborhood. Considering the context of a review website, an important feature is that users enter or exit a site anytime and anywhere; thus, the social contact and connections unfold across time and space. It could be vague in terms of the size and boundaries of users; thus, one may not be clear to what extent one is with others on the site. In this sense, perceived social presence may become less dependent on how the medium produces the feeling of being together with others. This pushes us to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of social presence, which may need to be adapted as a new medium of social contact arises. For example, in the e-learning context, social presence is rede<sup>fi</sup>ned as learners’ ability to portray themselves as community members (Jahng and Littau 2016). Future work may develop social presence measures that are more applicable to the review context.

Additionally, whereas we posit that the theory of space norms applies to both product and service reviews, we did not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally test the latter. Thus, caution is required in generalizing the current <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the service review context. In particular, one measurement dimension of quality review writing that may need further validation is review objectivity. For service reviews, subjective descriptions, such as personal experiences about service staff and feelings of warm and caring, can be just as helpful if not more helpful than purely objective statements. Therefore, subjectivity of review content may also be included as a dimension to measure quality review writing.

Furthermore, although the space norms theory is developed to explain the phenomenon of writing quality reviews, future research may adapt it to examine other online user-generated content contexts. One promising domain is the e-learning context in which students con tribute to group or class discussion by writing posts about course topics and commenting on others’ posts in virtual forums. Whereas educators widely embrace the value of collaborative learning and online discussion, the quality of student postings has been a long-standing concern (Martono and Salam 2017). The space norms theory might be adapted to improve our understanding of the post-writing behavior and inform potential strategies to induce more deliberative and thoughtful content in discussion forums. Given the growth of online and hybrid learning worldwide, this phenomenon is worth future investigation.

We also call for future work to expand the space norms theory by including relevant social dynamics. For instance, the social identity theory (SIT; Tajfel and Turner 1986) holds that, as one’s social identity—that is, sense of belonging to a community or group— becomes stronger, one will be more likely to think and behave in a way that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the value of the community. For another, the status theory of collective action (STOCA; Willer 2009) suggests that the greater the status one achieves in a community, the more one will value the group’s interests and make community contributions. Thus, by integrating other theories (e.g., SIT, STOCA), future work may build meaningful moderators (e.g., social identity and status) into the current space norm proposition.

Most broadly, the conceptualization of IT as a social environment may lead to richer theorizing on how IT gives rise to effects in other social phenomena (e.g., community formation, collective actions). We believe that the social environment metaphor could energize IS scholars imaginations to reshape the kinds of questions they can investigate and recon<sup>fi</sup>gure the theories and methodologies they <sup>fi</sup>nd effective. For instance, theories of behavior as hinging on social and/or contextual factors (e.g., the nudge theory, environmental psychology theories) that so far have received scant attention in IS can inform scholars of how to frame problems or question assumptions in future research. It is our hope that this new conceptualization can enlighten new theoretical work and broaden empirical efforts in the IS community.
