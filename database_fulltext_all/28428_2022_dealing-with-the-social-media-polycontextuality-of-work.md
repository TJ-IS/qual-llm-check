# Dealing with the Social Media Polycontextuality of Work

Emmanuelle Vaast,<sup>a,</sup>\* Alain Pinsonneault<sup>a</sup>

Copyright: © 2022 INFORMS

Abstract. Existing scholarship on social media provides us with important but incomplete insights regarding how people rely upon social media as they work. Scholarship so far focuses on social media as technologies that enable and constrain people to do certain things. This study proposes a perspective on social media for work that views them not only as technologies that enable people to do certain things, but also as contexts with emerging norms and roles in which people participated. As they do so, people are confronted with opportunities and challenges that are inherent to social media polycontextuality. This study investigates the case of data scientists and how they dealt with the social media polycontextuality of their work. The study reveals that data scientists relied on strategies of enhancing, differentiating, or occasionally reducing their engagement with social media contexts. The study brings novel insights for scholarship on social media and work by unpacking the social media polycontextuality of work and the interconnections in the engagement with multiple social media contexts. It explains how these interconnections manifest in people’s deliberate participation in or temporary withdrawal from diverse and changing social media contexts. This study also contributes to scholarship by highlighting how people deal with an ensemble of social media applications rather than with a single, isolated information technology.

## Introduction

Social media, that is, web-based applications that make it possible for users to create, circulate, and share multimedia content (Boyd and Ellison 2008, Jarrahi and Sawyer 2015), pervade multiple aspects of contemporary social lives (Kane et al. 2014). Social media are particularly prevalent at work (Oostervink et al. 2016, Leonardi and Vaast 2017), where they deeply transform work and organizing on an ongoing basis (Wu 2013, Colbert et al. 2016). Extant information systems (IS) research on social media examines social media and work by viewing social media as sophisticated technologies that enable people to perform certain actions. For instance, research examines how social media enable and constrain work (Leonardi et al. 2013, Oostervink et al. 2016) and what they enable people to do (Treem and Leonardi 2013, Karahanna et al. 2018). IS scholars also study what people do when they use social media for work (Leonardi et al. 2013, Leidner et al. 2018) and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally how they use them to communicate with others (Huang et al.

2013), <sup>fi</sup>nd new sources of information and knowledge (Leonardi 2015, Kane 2017), or expand their professional networks (Bharati et al. 2015). Research also examines how people rely upon social media at work to learn, collaborate, or socialize (Leonardi 2014, Ali-Hassan et al. 2015).

While this perspective has provided signi<sup>fi</sup>cant insights so far, it leaves important gaps in our understanding. It does not fully account for a distinctive dimension of people’s engagement with social media. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, scholarship has not yet considered that social media not only allow people to perform workrelated actions, but that they also are contexts in which people participate (Vaast and Walsham 2013). By social media as contexts, we mean that social media constitute participation frameworks that offer participants particular characteristics as well as emerging norms and enable speci<sup>fi</sup>c patterns of social interactions (Chaiklin and Lave 1993, Beck et al. 2014). Therefore, what people may experience from social media may not only come from the use of the technologies but also from the fact that people participate and engage in social media as contexts (McFarland and Ployhart 2015).

Considering social media as both sophisticated technologies and contexts is key to gain a more complete understanding of the transformations of work associated with people’s reliance upon multiple social media.

The focus of most scholarship on social media to date is on what people do on one social media application independently from what they do on other ones. Yet people typically engage with diverse social media in their work (Forsgren and Bystrom¨ 2017). Social media, therefore, constitute additional contexts that add to, differ from, and are related to other, more traditional, contexts. As people engage with various social media, they are participating in multiple contexts. This is what we call the social media polycontextuality of work, that is, the fact that people participated in multiple workrelated social media contexts (Engestrom et al.¨ 1995, Crane<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. 2015). It is important to examine how people navigate multiple social media contexts because what they do as they participate in one social media context might be related to and shaped by what they do when participating in other social media as well as other contexts, such as the of<sup>fi</sup>ce, the organization, or the professional association.

While the social media polycontextuality of work is a prevalent and important phenomenon (McFarland and Ployhart 2015, Johns 2018), it is not yet much examined in existing scholarship. Because social media polycontextuality is associated with an increase in the number of contexts in which people participate, it can generate both opportunities (e.g., an expanded professional network) and challenges (e.g., risk of becoming overwhelmed by content from multiple sources) for people that warrant speci<sup>fi</sup>c responses. Such opportunities and challenges associated with social media polycontextuality need to be understood better along with the ways in which people deal with them.

We examine this speci<sup>fi</sup>c issue and address the following research question: how do people deal with the social media polycontextuality of work? To provide answers to this question, we <sup>fi</sup>rst expand upon the idea of social media as contexts. We explain that social media constitute contexts as participation frameworks that people shape through their use and also with whose emerging norms and roles they need to comply. We then detail the social media polycontextuality of work and highlight the opportunities and challenges that it holds for people. This leads us to consider that people need to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to deal the social media polycontextuality of their work. We turn to our qualitative study to theorize how people do so. The case study of data scientists enables us to elaborate, through the grounded theory method, upon how people deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work by enhancing, differentiating, or occasionally reducing their engagement with social media contexts.

This study adds to scholarship on social media and work and social media contexts and polycontextuality. First, this study contributes to scholarship on social media and work by presenting social media as contexts in addition to sophisticated technologies. This is important because existing IS scholarship has long relied on a con ceptual distinction between technology and the context in which it is used. This study adds to IS scholarship by explaining how some social media applications constitute key work-related contexts in which people participate. This study proposes an original conceptualization of the information technology (IT) artifact that is particularly relevant to understand dynamics associated with social media and work. This study contributes to scholarship on the affordances of social media (i.e., the action potentials associated with speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies; see Leonardi 2011, Volkoff and Strong 2013) by revealing affordances not simply as action potentials associated with a technology, but also as participation potentials. People, thus, need to take into consideration the norms and emerging roles associated with the social media contexts in which they participate to accomplish particular actions.

Second, this study brings original insights to scholarship on social media contexts and polycontextuality. Seminal scholarship in organizational behavior has started to conceptualize social media as contexts (McFarland and Ployhart 2015, Johns 2018). This scholarship, however, so far considers these contexts as <sup>fi</sup>xed and given, de<sup>fi</sup>ned by static features of social media. This study elaborates upon the theorizing of social media as contexts. It contributes to this scholarship by bringing in the importance of participants and their actions in de<sup>fi</sup>ning and transforming social media as contexts. This study also contributes to scholarship on polycontextuality. Seminal scholarship (see Crane-<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. 2015 in particular) calls for investigations of polycontextuality and of people’s behavior associated with it. This study contributes by developing an understanding of polycontextuality not only as a given phenomenon of today’s work, but also as an actively constructed one. People’s participation in social media contexts contributes to shaping their engagement with social media polycontextuality.

## Social Media as Contexts and the Social Media Polycontextuality of Work Social Media as Contexts for Work

Most of the IS scholarship on social media and work examines social media as technologies that enable people to perform certain actions (Treem and Leonardi 2013, Forsgren and Bystrom¨ 2017). For instance, scholarship examines changes in organizing and individual behaviors at work associated with the introduction of new technologies (Leonardi 2014, Ali-Hassan et al. 2015, Bharati et al. 2015). In this perspective, social media participate in changing the contexts in which they are used, but they are distinct from these contexts (Cardon and Marshall 2015, Cristea et al. 2019). Scholarship on social media at work also investigates how social media offer diverse affordances that enable people, among other things, to connect with others (Treem and Leonardi 2013, Kane et al. 2014), socialize (Leidner et al. 2018), and share knowledge (Bharati et al. 2015).

This scholarship yields important insights but does not account for some key, distinctive aspects of social media as contrasted with many other ITs. Social media are not simply technologies that people can use to accomplish certain things. Social media also constitute settings in which people participate and that have their own emerging sets of norms, ways of interacting, of sharing information, etc. (McFarland and Ployhart 2015, Johns 2018). For people to actualize the affordances of social media, they need to engage with social media as contexts and, thus, learn their norms and characteristics.

Evidence of social media as contexts can be seen in diverse instances. Certain social media applications, for example, Stack Over<sup>fl</sup>ow, are dedicated to the production and exchange of technical knowledge. These social media applications are not simple repositories of knowledge. They also constitute emerging settings with often formal rules and regulations as well as particular patterns of interactions (Ford et al. 2018). For people to access knowledge, they need to follow these rules and patterns of interactions (Ferro and Zachry 2014). Also, people go to large, popular social media applications, such as Facebook and Twitter, rather than simply use them. That is, within these applications, people develop particular relationships and ways of interacting with others that are distinct from other contexts in which they participated (e.g., workplace or home) (McLaughlin and Vitak 2012). These applications have become de facto “third places” for some of their participants (Aaen and Dalsgaard 2016).

We examine the de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics of a context to explain further what we mean by social media as contexts. A context corresponds to a participation framework (Engestrom et al.¨ 1995) in which people enact particular roles and respect speci<sup>fi</sup>c norms. A context encompasses “the set of circumstances in which phenomena (e.g., events, processes, or entities) are situated” (Grif<sup>fi</sup>n 2007, p. 860). Within contexts, particular practices take place (Lave 1988, Thompson and Walsham 2004). Examples of contexts of relevance to work include people’s work team, their broader employing organization, professional associations, or communities of practice (Vaast and Walsham 2009). A context holds particular characteristics that enable participants to do certain things rather than others (Johns

2006). For instance, the proximal context of an of<sup>fi</sup>ce space enables chance direct encounters, whereas an electronically mediated context may enable participants to keep written traces of all activities (McFarland and Ployhart 2015, Johns 2018). Contexts affect what people can do, but they are not <sup>fi</sup>xed once and for all (Grif<sup>fi</sup>n 2007). They change over time as participants, norms, and roles evolve (Lave and Wenger 1991).

We consider social media as contexts by highlighting the following three key aspects. First, social media constitute participation frameworks in which participants enact formal and/or informal, emerging roles; that is, they perform speci<sup>fi</sup>c functions and have different levels of activity (e.g., Vaast et al. 2017). Such roles involve, for instance, some participants being more active than others and being more likely than others to post content or rely upon certain features of social media (Bechmann and Lomborg 2013). Second, social media as contexts display speci<sup>fi</sup>c norms that illuminate particular patterns of interactions (McLaughlin and Vitak 2012). Norms constitute formal or informal sets of rules and acceptable ways of behaving that participants on a particular social media application are expected to follow (Fischer and Reuber 2011). Social norms of behaviors (e.g., associated with social support, communication, or reciprocity) characterize social media contexts (Maier et al. 2015a). Third, social media as contexts are not <sup>fi</sup>xed, but constructed over time. Their available features change (Kane 2017). Also, importantly, the emergent norms and roles of social media contexts <sup>fl</sup>uctuate and evolve over time.

## The Social Media Polycontextuality of Work

Considering social media as contexts illuminates the social media polycontextuality of work. Polycontextuality encompasses the idea that people are embedded in multiple, interconnected contexts in their daily practices (Engestrom et al.¨ 1995). The concept of poly contextuality emphasizes that people can participate actively in multiple contexts concurrently and that they are engaged in numerous tasks in diverse, interrelated contexts in an ongoing manner (Reder 1993). People take part in different contexts as they work with interconnections among these contexts affecting what they can and cannot do. As people participate in different contexts, they <sup>fi</sup>nd resources in one context that help them solve problems they encounter in another one (Crane<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. 2015). For instance, people can <sup>fi</sup>nd original solutions to issues they face in their immediate work team by conferring with others in a network of practice (Brown and Duguid 2001, Vaast and Walsham 2009) or an online community (Crane<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. 2015). Considering polycontextuality highlights that what people do in one context (e.g., in a social media application) is connected to what they do in another one (e.g., their employing organization or their work team).

Here, we focus on the social media polycontextuality of work, that is, on the polycontextuality generated as people participate in multiple social media as they work. Given our perspective of social media as contexts, the more people engage with social media, the more contexts in which they participated. This is the case because participating in various social media contexts does not substitute for participating in other contexts. It adds to them. So, as they participate in more social media applications, people participate in more contexts. The different contexts associated with social media polycontextuality involve distinct emerging roles, norms, and evolutions over time. The social media polycontextuality of work also implies that people’s behavior connects these contexts. One’s participation in a social media context is connected to one’s participation in other contexts.

The social media polycontextuality of work holds opportunities and also challenges for people. With regard to opportunities, as they participate in a multiplicity of contexts, including, in particular, social media contexts, people can access useful information from a variety of sources (Brown and Duguid 2001). They can engage in collaborations within and across multiple contexts and gain expertise from different sources (Crane<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. 2015). They can access resources beyond their local context and particular employment situations and nurture relationships with others in various settings, enabling learning and opportunities (El Ouirdi et al. 2015, Leonardi 2017). Moreover, people can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the social media polycontextuality of their work as their participation in diverse social media applications enables them to <sup>fi</sup>nd information from dispersed sources to solve complicated work issues; to connect with peers anytime, anywhere (Ferguson et al. 2013); and to gain professional opportunities beyond their local environment (Benson et al. 2014).

Social media polycontextuality, however, also poses challenges for people. For one, encountering differences associated with various contexts can create tensions. Engestrom et al. (¨ 1995, p. 319) consider that “multiple contexts demand and afford different, complementary but also con<sup>fl</sup>icting cognitive tools, rules, and patterns of social interaction.” People may also struggle to participated in multiple contexts because of, for example, cognitive, cultural, organizational, or professional differences (Levina and Vaast 2005). Moreover, as they participated actively in diverse contexts, people can face a fragmentation of viewpoints and inconsistent perspectives that can make it delicate for them to engage in their work practices (Engestrom et al.¨ 1995). Being involved in different contexts can, thus, overwhelm people. They can be confronted with con-<sup>fl</sup>icting viewpoints and perspectives that might be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to reconcile in their practices. The engagement in multiple contexts can also generate vulnerabilities for people, such as risks associated with information or knowledge leaks (Brown and Duguid 2001) or reputational damages (Oostervink et al. 2016). People may become stressed from an overabundance of connections and resources (Bucher et al. 2013, Maier et al. 2015b), lose control over their work, waste time (Leftheriotis and Giannakos 2014), or face complications in hiring (Berkelaar 2014) because hiring managers have access to information from applicants’ participation in social media contexts.

Because the social media polycontextuality of work presents both opportunities and challenges for people, it stands to reason that people devise ways to deal with it. IS scholarship notes that people need to be able to deal with IT to alleviate its negative consequences while seeking to strengthen its positive implications (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, Bala and Venkatesh 2016). However, IS scholarship focuses on how people react to single ITs. It is unclear how people might adjust to the discomfort associated with the engagement not with a single technology, but with a broad class of technologies, such as various social media.

Building upon these insights, we can expect a strategic aspect to the ways in which people deal with social media polycontextuality. How people speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work and which ways and strategies they enact, however, still needs to be discovered empirically and theorized. We turn to our methods to identify and analyze how people deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work as well as to build theory from the data.

## Methods

## Research Design and Setting

The research design for this study is a qualitative single case study following the grounded theory method (Glaser and Strauss 1967, Birks et al. 2013, Sarker et al. 2018) and, in particular, Charmaz’s (2006) guidelines for grounded theorizing. The research relies upon rich observations from a collection of diverse data used to develop an in-depth understanding of the examined phenomenon and to engage in theory development (Strauss and Corbin 1998, Urquhart 2012). Moreover, as detailed in Charmaz (2006), the study has a constructivist rather than a positivist epistemology: it focuses upon how the meanings of the social world are constructed rather than given. We aimed at developing an understanding of how people make sense of and react to the growing availability of social media as contexts in the domain of their work. Existing theoretical foundations sensitize but do not constrain our analyses (Charmaz 2006, Birks et al. 2013).

Data scientists and their engagement with social media were revelatory for this study for several reasons. Data scientists represent a new, highly reputed occupation (Davenport and Patil 2012, Vaast and Pinsonneault 2021). Many of the trends associated with social media as contexts and social media polycontextuality are present for data scientists (see Schutt and O’Neil 2013, Crowd<sup>fl</sup>ower 2017). Also, because their practices are still emerging and in <sup>fl</sup>ux, to accomplish their tasks, data scientists need to access data and technologies beyond their local workplace, which makes them prone to participated in multiple social media contexts. Moreover, data scientists constitute a new occupation that relies upon sophisticated computations, techniques, and complex knowledge domains (Van der Aalst 2014). They also heavily use social media to connect and collaborate with others or to gain access to new sources of information. The rise of the occupation of data scientists coincides in large part with the widespread availability of social media (Boyd and Ellison 2008, Kaplan and Haenlein 2010).

## Data Collection and Analysis

We collected the main data for the study through semistructured interviews with 89 data scientists. The interviewee selection was initially facilitated by a call for participants from the <sup>fi</sup>rst author publicized via social media. The call was publicized on Twitter and picked by a content aggregator popular among data scientists. It was also retweeted by several highpro<sup>fi</sup>le data scientists and was forwarded on other social media applications, such as Slack. The reach of the call for participants enabled us to engage in theoretical sampling by selecting data scientists to interview from diverse geographical and disciplinary backgrounds as well as at diverse stages in their careers. In addition, we were able to select data scientists that use a wide variety of social media applications, including GitHub, LinkedIn, StackOver<sup>fl</sup>ow, and Facebook (see Table 1). The selection of interviewees provided us with a broad range of data scientists experiences with the social media polycontextuality of their work We recorded and transcribed the interviews. Interviews took place in person, over the phone, or via teleconference. Interviews lasted 30 to 75 minutes with an average of 45 minutes. We added new interviews until the addition of a new respondent provided limited new insights. Online Appendix 1 presents information about the interview guide.

Respondents were located in different regions of the world and varied in their demographics and work statuses (see Table 2). Respondents came from various disciplinary backgrounds as characteristic of data science (e.g., physics, machine learning, statistics, software development, computer science, mathematics, economics, information systems). Interviewees were at different stages of their careers from <sup>fi</sup>nishing their training (usually, a Ph.D. and post doc) to being very established (see Table 2).

We also collected other data to gain an in-depth understanding of data scientists’ work, of the ways in which social media were becoming intricately related to their work, and of how data scientists dealt with what we came to understand as social media polycontextuality. To do so, the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher followed interview respondents’ and other data scientists’ use of publicly accessible social media applications and theoretically sampled archives of participation in social media from Twitter, LinkedIn, Stack Over<sup>fl</sup>ow, and GitHub. These data helped us understand better how data scientists engaged with diverse social media and how these engagements related to what respondents said in their interviews. This was helpful to achieve the constant comparison required for grounded theory studies (Charmaz 2006, Birks et al. 2013).

In a related manner, the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher joined a Slack group dedicated to the professional development of data scientists. Joining the Slack group helped access discussions and activities taking place in this social media application and understand how these compared with activities in more public social media applications, such as Twitter. The Slack group had about 50 members and 14 channels or threaded discussions. Discussions included, for example, how to learn a new technique or software, what to expect from meetup groups, or general socializing. Out of respect for the privacy of Slack group members, we did not take any direct quote, but made notes of activity observations. Furthermore, the study involved participant observation of four meet-up group meetings on topics typical of data scientists’ interests and practices (Action Analytics, Data for Good, Apache Spark, and TibCo). Each meeting lasted 90 to 120 minutes. We took extensive notes and collected digital documentation. We triangulated the different data sources and engaged in constant comparison to achieve a richer understanding of the opportunities and challenges of social media polycontextuality for data scientists and of the ways in which data scientists dealt with them.

As traditional in qualitative research (Denzin and Lincoln 2000) and, especially, in grounded methods (Glaser and Strauss 1967), data collection and analyses were deeply connected to each other in that new data enabled new analytical insights that were then re<sup>fi</sup>ned, critically evaluated, and deepened with additional data. The analytical process was iterative and included many iterations between the data and the theory development in progress. This ensured an intricate link between the data and the analyses (Birks et al. 2013). Atlas.ti, a qualitative data analysis software, helped keep record of all the data and assisted the analysis process.

The study started with a broader objective of understanding social media use for work among data scientists. The focus on examining social media as contexts and on how data scientists dealt with the opportunities and challenges of the social media polycontextuality of their work emerged gradually from the process of data collection and analysis as is typical of this type of research (Charmaz 2006). We became intrigued by the discovery that data scientists viewed different social media as contexts in which they could participate rather than simply as technologies they could use. This discovery contrasts with preconceptions that existing scholarship would suggest (Birks et al. 2013). It led us to develop early and intermediate memos and re<sup>fi</sup>ne the literature review to elaborate further upon social media as contexts and upon socia media polycontextuality. Then, digging through early interviews and further pushing on this issue with theoretical sampling in new interviews made us realize that data scientists both appreciated and resented social media polycontextuality and had to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to deal with it.

The analysis process turned to the elaboration of categories associated with how data scientists dealt with social media polycontextuality. We engaged in writing memos that gradually identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and characterized analytically the speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways in which data scientists dealt with social media polycontextuality. We went through the entire data set and engaged in constant comparison to qualify and differentiate what data scientists did in this regard. We discovered <sup>fi</sup>ve ways in which data scientists engaged with various social media in a deliberate manner to enhance the opportunities while limiting the challenges associated with polycontextuality. Because of the changing and <sup>fi</sup>ne-tuned ways of dealing with social media polycontextuality, we labeled these ways as tactics. We re<sup>fi</sup>ned our analytical elaboration of the tactics as we engaged in a theoretical sampling of observations from social media applications and from the meetup group meetings. Engaging in constant comparison of these data with the interview transcripts and the analyses in progress brought more depth to our elaboration of the tactics.

To further deepen the analyses, building upon the tactics, we turned toward understanding how various interviewees differed in terms of which combinations of tactics they used and what explained these differences. This new round of analyses led us to develop sorting memos and then integrating memos that enabled us to deepen our theory building by characterizing strategies through which data scientists dealt with social media polycontextuality (Glaser 2005, Urquhart 2019). We contrasted the interviewees’ patterns of reliance on speci<sup>fi</sup>c tactics and grouped them in a consistent manner. This led us to differentiate three ways that encapsulated distinct strategies through which data scientists dealt with social media polycontextuality: they enhanced, differentiated, or occasionally reduced their engagement with it. Online Appendix 2 presents the data-reduction table explaining how we went from the tactics to the strategies.

Once more, we went systematically over our entire data set to corroborate, refute, and re<sup>fi</sup>ne the analyses of tactics and their combination in strategies. To ensure reliability in the coding (Strauss and Corbin 1998), the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher worked with a research assistant and coded the interview data. The research assistant was versed in qualitative research and, especially, the grounded theory method and was experienced in data coding but was not informed of the topic or objectives of this study. The research assistant independently coded a <sup>fi</sup>rst random sample of 10% of the interviews. We compared the results with the <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher’s coding. We repeated the process and re<sup>fi</sup>ned the coding until we reached a mutual agreement 90% of the time. Online Appendix 3 provides extracts of the coding and coding process resulting in the categorical coding (Strauss and Corbin 1997, Charmaz 2006). Throughout the process, the second author and the research assistant played the important role of “devil’s advocates” as recommended in qualitative research (Feldman 1995). The <sup>fi</sup>eld researche maintained contact with key interview respondents and exchanged early interpretations and analyses to receive feedback from their perspectives. This allowed us to fine-tune the identification and characterization of the tactics and their combination. This also enabled us to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the understanding of how, as the contexts in which they participated shifted and as diverse social media applications and particular features available to them evolved, data scientists adopted different tactics and strategies. We reached theoretical saturation once we developed a deep understanding of the different strategies that data scientists relied upon and the ways in which they dealt with social media polycontextuality as well as what made them enact or change different strategies.

## Social Media as Contexts for Data Scientists

The social media applications with which data scientists engaged were not simply technologies that enabled them to do certain things. They were also contexts in which data scientists participated. Each major social media application constituted a speci<sup>fi</sup>c participation framework for them. To make the most of each social media application, data scientists learned its norms and understood the roles that other users enacted.

Social media constituted participation frameworks for data scientists. What participating in social media contexts could bring data scientists depended in part upon how others participated in social media contexts as well. For example, data scientists considered that participating in social media contexts, such as GitHub and Stack Over<sup>fl</sup>ow, was useful to learn about data science fundamentals and about newer developments. This learning potential associated with the participation in social media contexts, however, depended upon how other data scientists participated as well: it required contributions from others on social media contexts. Learning also depended upon data scientists having a particular knowledge base to make sense of the shared content. Nondata scientists could not easily gain much from these data science social media contexts. For instance, some respondents referred to “DS Twitter” for “[data science] Twitter” as the microblogging context populated by data scientists who shared a knowledge base and some professional interests and could understand and talk to one another.

Social media as contexts had norms and roles that data scientists enacted. With regards to norms, data scientists learned the acceptable ways of interacting with others. These norms included, for instance, the right ways of asking questions on different social media applications. As data scientists followed these norms, they showed others that they knew about data science and understood its technical foundations and social dynamics. Data scientists, at times, acknowledged that they could receive negative feedback from others if they did not respect the norms on social media contexts. This happened, for instance, when their publicly posted questions or contributions were considered too weak by others: “I think I need to ask a question on #StackOver<sup>fl</sup>ow. Pray that the #StackOverlords accept my wording & deem my question worthy” (Tweet, June 14, 2018).

This comment highlights an awareness of the norms that participants had to follow in social media contexts (here, Stack Over<sup>fl</sup>ow) as well as of the different roles that some played in them (see the reference to the “#StackOverlords”). Data scientists, thus, also expressed an awareness that people held different roles in social media contexts. Data scientists recognized that some among them were particularly active in social media contexts and acted as leaders. A data scientist, for instance, commented upon her discovery that data scientists were welcoming of one another as they interacted in social media contexts. She explained how some data scientists who were among the most productive and successful in the <sup>fi</sup>eld also took on active roles in social media contexts and welcomed newcomers: “I remember the <sup>fi</sup>rst couple of times that I was responded to by people who make all of these R packages, I was kind of starstruck. I was like: ‘I can’t believe these people could answer you on Twitter.’ I was really surprised by that I guess” (I34).

## Opportunities and Challenges of Social Media Polycontextuality for Data Scientists

Data scientists’ engagement with social media contexts generated social media polycontextuality. Data scientists often navigated multiple social media contexts and interacted with one another even when they had not met one another off-line or did not share organizational membership. A data scientist’s post illustrates this engagement with social media polycontextuality: “Spent my hack day building an #rstats package so my team has all their DB [database] connectors in one place. Thanks again to [@Someone], [@Someoneelse] & [@R-related organization] for the package [virtual] workshop—it made packaging our code straightforward and fun!” (Tweet, June 19, 2018).

This post expresses what this data scientist’s team within the local organizational context needed (i.e., having database connectors in a single place) and how the actions of this data scientist did (i.e., building an R-stat package) were made easier by what other data scientists did and made openly available on a social media context. Another data scientist engaged with the microblogging context Twitter to recount a workday that involved programming in Python and developing content for a blogging context: “Forgot to update yesterday, made some additional progress on freecodecamp and working on the <sup>fi</sup>nal version of my personal website. Also worked on the game engine with #Python and JSON with a small commit but at least I <sup>fi</sup>gured out a dictionary. #100DaysOfCode” (Tweet, June 16, 2018).

Data scientists, however, also expressed some awareness of challenges associated with the social media polycontextuality of their work. Other data scientists highlighted the prospect of online harassment as a potential outcome of participating in diverse social media contexts. A data scientist commented on how, in social media contexts, people could easily criticize others: “You are exposing yourself … You are kind of open to anyone to shout at you” (I13).

Data scientists, thus, at times expressed ambivalence toward social media polycontextuality as they feared that participating in different social media contexts could hold potential negative impacts for them. An interviewee pondered upon how actively they should participated in social media contexts to avoid future negative work and employment outcomes: “Then, there’s kind of the question if I’m too open about things, would that make someone not want to hire me or make someone not want to collaborate with me on something? And, on that question, that is a possibility” (I17).

## Strategies to Deal with the Social Media Polycontextuality of Data Scientists’ Work

Because the social media polycontextuality of their work held both opportunities and challenges for them, data scientists had to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to enhance these opportunities while limiting these challenges. We discovered that data scientists dealt with the social media polycontextuality of their work by relying upon speci<sup>fi</sup>c tactics that combined into distinct strategies.

With tactics, data scientists adjusted their engagement with social media polycontextuality. These tactics consisted of self-monitoring (self-censorship and careful editing of one’s participation in social media contexts), sheltering (using speci<sup>fi</sup>c social media applications to cultivate safe spaces in which to share freely online), unilaterally disconnecting (severing a previously existing relationship on a social media context after it has become detrimental), supporting off-line contexts (engaging with social media to support small, local contexts in which to share and develop relationships on the basis of shared professional interests and geographical proximity), and retreating from multiple social media contexts (fully, but temporarily, stepping away from multiple social media contexts). Table 5 details these tactics.

Having identi<sup>fi</sup>ed these tactics, we turned our attention toward examining what made data scientists follow them and combine them in distinct manners. For one, we discovered that data scientists, at times, adjusted their tactics as they started relying more prominently on some tactics than on others in part as new features of social media or new social media applications became available and popular. For instance, data scientists often noted that they became more adept at adjusting the content they posted and to whom since the social media application Facebook offered features to this effect and since another social media application, Slack, made it possible for them to control tiers of connections with whom to share different content more or less publicly.

Moreover, data scientists dealt differently with social media polycontextuality as their work changed, that is, as they faced new tasks and took on different positions that could be salaried, freelance, or even entrepreneurial. The opportunities and challenges associated with social media polycontextuality changed for data scientists, so they varied the tactics they relied upon and the ways in which they combined them. ## Discussion and Implications Discussion

Existing scholarship provides insights regarding social media as technologies that enable people to act in various ways (Treem and Leonardi 2013, Karahanna et al. 2018). Yet social media are not only sophisticated technologies. They also constitute new contexts in which people participate (McFarland and Ployhart 2015). Further, people often engage with multiple social media (Forsgren and Bystrom¨ 2017). Social media constitute a variety of contexts with which people typically engage as they work. The social media polycontextuality of work presents both opportunities and challenges for people, prompting them to deal with it.

This study set out to understand how people deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work. It develops an understanding of social media as contexts that add to the contexts with which people already engage in the course of their work. It explains how the social media polycontextuality of work leads to speci<sup>fi</sup>c opportunities and challenges to which people may respond strategically. This study relies upon the case of data scientists to identify speci<sup>fi</sup>c strategies that people employ. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal how data scientists participated in multiple social media contexts and dealt with the social media polycontextuality of their work through various tactics and their combination in distinct strategies. Here, we elaborate a grounded theory answering the research question summarized in Figure 2.

As depicted in Figure 2, people participate in diverse and changing social media contexts that generate ambivalent potentials for them. They need to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that, as people engage with more contexts, the opportunities and challenges increase. People face a web of interconnected contexts with which they deal through a repertoire of tactics combined into strategies. For people who participate in relatively few work-related contexts (left side of Figure 2), social media polycontextuality carries mostly opportunities (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nding a new job, learning a new competence) and few challenges. People then typically engage in enhancing their participation with social media polycontextuality, especially with the selfmonitoring tactic. Enhancing fuels further participation in more social media contexts because people work toward stimulating the opportunities associated with social media polycontextuality.

People who participate in more contexts in relation to their work (middle of Figure 2) encounter more opportunities (e.g., more connections, solutions to complicated problems) and also more challenges (e.g., organizational constraints, reputational damage reverberating across several contexts) with social media polycontextuality. This makes people engage in differentiating their engagement with diverse social media contexts by combining tactics, such as sheltering, supporting off-line contexts, and unilaterally disconnecting. Such tactics enable people to re<sup>fi</sup>ne their engagement with the social media polycontextuality of their work in a differentiated manner.

Ultimately, though, as people further deepen their engagement with social media polycontextuality (right side of Figure 2), the cost (in terms of, e.g., time and attention) of differentiating can become too high. Some people then start relying on occasionally reducing as they deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work by deliberately but provisionally removing themselves from many social media contexts. They disengage temporarily from many social media contexts in order to focus on pressing work demands in their most proximate context(s).

This study highlights an important processual dimension to the ways in which people deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work. People’s engagement with social media polycontextuality is not <sup>fi</sup>xed once and for all. It changes as people’s employing organization(s), career stage, and the diverse social media contexts in which they participate change as well. People adjust the overall ways in which they deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work and the different tactics they employ as their needs, situations, opportunities, and constraints evolve.

This study reveals how people deal with their participation in a changing ensemble of social media contexts. It clari<sup>fi</sup>es interconnections in the participation, nonparticipation, and changes in participation patterns associated with various social media contexts. This allows us to grasp, in a comprehensive manner, the ways in which people’s engagement with different social media contexts resonates with one another. This study illuminates how people experience and deal with opportunities and challenges associated with social media polycontextuality in ways that are closely connected to their engagement with or temporary disengagement from speci<sup>fi</sup>c social media applications. People engage and disengage with diverse social media contexts associated with their work in a changing and connected manner and in relation to their participation in other contexts. This study reveals how, for people dealing with social media polycontextuality, retreating from some social media contexts can be associated with greater participation in other contexts, social media-based or not. Disengagement from one social media context may also turn into new engagement and then back again into disengagement according to people’s changing needs regarding the social media polycontextuality of their work.

## Implications for Scholarship

This study holds implications for scholarship on social media and work, on social media contexts and polycontextuality, and on coping with IT.

Regarding scholarship on social media and work, this study explains that social media do not constitute simply sophisticated technologies that people can utilize. They also constitute de facto contexts. The perspective proposed in this study is consistent with and adds to existing scholarship on sociomateriality (Orlikowski 2007, Wagner et al. 2010). Sociomateriality scholarship proposes a relational perspective that connects the social and the technical (Orlikowski and Scott 2008) and exam ines the entanglement (Scott and Orlikowski 2014) or imbrication (Introna and Hayes 2011, Leonardi 2011) between them. This study <sup>fi</sup>ts with these tenets of sociomateriality and adds a speci<sup>fi</sup>c consideration of contexts. So far, much IS scholarship considers the context in which practices take place as important to shape practices and also as given. This study instead unpacks contexts as participation frameworks with speci<sup>fi</sup>c roles and norms and presents them as constructed and changing rather than as a given. It also explains how social media constitute new contexts in which people may participate. It illuminates that people participate in social media contexts and that, from their participation, they can undertake certain actions. This perspective helps to understand how people engage with social media in their work and unpack the dynamics of this engagement.

This perspective also apprehends better the opportunities and challenges associated with the engagement with social media as called for by, for example, Fox and Moreland (2015). This study adds to scholarship by elaborating upon how people deal with these opportunities and challenges as called for by Maier et al. (2015a). The perspective further helps make sense of what happens when people engage with mul tiple social media. It adds value by offering a way of considering how people’s behavior in one social media context is connected to their behavior in other social media contexts.

This study also holds implications for the understanding of social media and their affordances. When considering social media as contexts, this study reveals that it is not simply about action potentials, but also about participation potentials. What people can do with social media also depends upon the norms of the contexts and the roles they and others perform. This study also reveals that the norms and roles in social media contexts are emerging rather than <sup>fi</sup>xed. They can, thus, change over time and affect as well as be affected by people’s behavior.

This study further offers implications for scholarship on social media contexts and polycontextuality. Seminal scholarship in adjacent disciplines, such as organizational behavior, notes that social media constitutes contexts (McFarland and Ployhart 2015). This study adds to this scholarship by elaborating further the conceptualization of social media as contexts. Existing scholarship so far views social media as <sup>fi</sup>xed and given contexts, determined by their features (McFarland and Ployhart 2015). Our study adds to this scholarship by emphasizing the importance of social media contexts as participation frameworks and, thus, of participants and their actions that may contribute to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and transform social media contexts. Moreover, McFarland and Ployhart (2015) note the need for scholarship to investigate multiple contexts simultaneously. By developing the notion of social media polycontextuality, this study addresses this need. It not only considers social media contexts independently from one another, but also their interconnections with one another and with other contexts of relevance for work.

This study also adds to nascent scholarship on polycontextuality in IS. Crane<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. (2015) called for more scholarship that takes into consideration people’s engagement with polycontextual situations and the behaviors they adopt when they engage with polycontextuality. This study answers this call. By building further upon the conceptualization of social media as contexts that are constructed and change over time, this study adds a consideration of social media polycontextuality. This study helps understand how people participate in, respond to, and transform the social media polycontextuality of their work.

This study also holds implications for scholarship on coping with IT and on adaptation behaviors. Coping strategies in existing scholarship focus on how people dealt with a single IT (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, Stein et al. 2015, Pirkkalainen et al. 2019). Existing scholarship notes, for instance, how, when the use of a social media application turns problematic or even pathological, people may rely on distractions to cope (Tarafdar et al. 2020) or may think about and eventually quit the application (Maier et al. 2015b, Turel 2015). This study contributes to this scholarship by examining how people deal not with a single technology, but with a broad class of technologies. It highlights the relevance of considering people’s responses to their work-related engagement with multiple social media applications. This focus enables us to adjust our understanding of people’s responses to discomfort associated with one’s engagement with technologies. The strategies we discover take into consideration how people engage with multiple social media at a given moment.

Moreover, our study reframes the question of quitting one social media application by placing it within the larger situation of the engagement with multiple social media. Hence, people’s strategy deals with managing their engagement with diverse social media of relevance to their work. People privilege certain social media applications over others rather than simply quitting the use of a focal application. This study also reveals that strategies to deal with social media polycontextuality can change as people go through different experiences and participate in diverse contexts. Hence, a coping behavior is not a one-time-only decision. People’s ongoing engagement with multiple social media contexts for work prompts an ongoing re<sup>fl</sup>ection that may lead them to adjust differently how they deal with social media polycontextuality.

## Practical Implications

This study also holds practical implications. For one, this study offers guidance for people on how their participation in multiple social media contexts can affect their work positively and negatively as well as how they can manage the opportunities and challenges associated with social media polycontextuality. This study also holds practical implications by reveal ing how people’s engagement with social media poly contextuality may change as their employment status and work experiences change. It brings insights regarding how people may adjust <sup>fi</sup>nely their partici pation in multiple social media contexts. Moreover, much scholarship on managerial responses to social media highlights management concern with the blurring of the boundary between the professional and the personal on social media (see, e.g., Vaast and Kaganer 2013). This study holds implications for managers by bringing awareness to how employees’ participation in some social media contexts bypasses the organizational context and, thus, their typical managerial purview but is still associated with work rather than leisure. This study encourages managers to understand better their employees’ situations and to examine how diverse social media contexts affect them within and beyond organizational boundaries and shape what they can or cannot do in their work. A better understanding of social media polycontextuality brings managers new insights to communicate with employees. It also assists them in their work and in further developing produc tive participations in social media contexts.

## Limitations and Future Research

As any research project, there are limitations to this study that open avenues for future scholarship. For one, this study focuses on data scientists who are particular with regards to their work and expertise. We believe that the case enables us to engage in analytical generalizability (Lee and Baskerville 2003). However, the interviewees held key similarities in terms of their occupation and work practices. They also had different geographical, cultural, and institutional backgrounds. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that they dealt with social media polycontextuality in comparable manners. However, it would be useful for future research to dig more deeply into differences and similarities in how people deal with social media polycontextuality depending upon diverse backgrounds. Researchers could also examine how people located in environments with many or few peers may differ in the ways in which they deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work.

Moreover, future research could study people who hold different types of jobs and work in different ways. This could generate additional insights regarding how people deal with the social media polycontextuality of their work beyond enhancing, differentiating, and occasionally reducing the engagement with social media contexts. Also, while we consider that the collected data provide insights into how data scientists dealt with social media polycontextuality in changing manners, future research could adopt a longitudinal design. This could help grasp more precisely changes over time and transitions among tactics and their combinations in strategies and engage further in process theorizing.

It would also be interesting to examine how different work conditions might in<sup>fl</sup>uence the way people deal with social media polycontextuality of work. For example, workplaces have deeply changed with the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic has unleashed new work-related dynamics and made social media even more crucial for people to be connected to one another. The potential downsides of social media contexts are also exacerbated (Marabelli et al. 2021). Researchers could investigate how people enact and/or adjust their tactics and strategies as their work environment becomes disrupted and more virtual.

## Conclusion

This study develops a new perspective on social media as both sophisticated technologies and contexts that add to the more traditional contexts with which people engage in their work. This perspective enables us to examine how people address the opportunities and challenges of the social media polycontextuality of their work. It elaborates a theorizing of people’s tactics combined in strategies that respond to their situation and participation in diverse and changing social media contexts that bring in new challenges and opportunities. Given how omnipresent social media have become for work and how consequential the social media polycontextuality of work is, we hope that others <sup>fi</sup>nd value and inspiration in this study.
