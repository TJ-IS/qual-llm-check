# Generalizing the Information Systems Artifact

Manoj A. Thomas,<sup>a,</sup>\* Yan Li,<sup>b</sup> Allen S. Lee<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Sydney Business School, Darlington, New South Wales 2006, Australia; <sup>b</sup> Center for Information Systems & Technology, Claremont Graduate University, Claremont, California 91711; <sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, Virginia 23284 \*Corresponding author

Abstract. Established process steps for design science research have not addressed the usability of technology artifacts beyond the original research setting. Building on what is already known about generalizing a theory from one setting to another and the idea of an artifact as the interface between an inner environment and outer environment put forth by Nobel Prize–winning social scientist Herbert Simon, we propose a procedure for generalizing an information systems (IS) artifact from one setting to another. We illustrate the treatment of the procedure by examining how an IS artifact (which itself consists of a technology artifact, information artifact, and social artifact) is generalized from the setting of Haiti to the setting of Nepal. The generalization procedure is a contribution to the practically oriented design science literature, which emphasizes the importance of the production of problem-solving artifacts. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our conceptualization of generalizing IS artifacts emphasizes the practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of design science research and the successful creation of artifacts that work in new settings in addition to the one where a research study origi nally created it. Design science researchers and practitioners may <sup>fi</sup>nd the procedure and its real-world treatment and description a useful model to follow.

## Introduction

How may we generalize the information systems (IS) artifact? What does it mean to generalize the IS artifact? Given what we know about generalizing scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory, how might it be similar to or different from generalizing the IS artifact?

To answer these questions, <sup>fi</sup>rst, we offer a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of IS artifact and distinguish it from its betterknown sibling, information technology (IT) artifact. Lee et al. (2015) “conceptualize ‘IS artifact’ so that it refers to a system, itself consisting of subsystems that are (1) a technology artifact, (2) an information artifact, and (3) a social artifact” (p. 6). Although there are lengthy discussions on the IS/IT artifact in the design research community (e.g., Sein et al. 2011, Goldkuhl 2013, Iivari 2017, and Baskerville et al. 2018), this paper is based on the Lee et al. (2015) conceptualization of the IS artifact.

The capability to generalize an IS artifact is important so that an IS artifact that has been successfully created in one setting is not unique to that one setting but can be successfully recreated in other settings. In general, design science research can be said to have two purposes: the development of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge and the production of problem-solving artifacts.

The research in this paper falls under the production of problem-solving artifacts.

There is already a body of knowledge on generalizing that pertains to generalizing a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory (Lee and Baskerville 2003, 2012; Tsang and Williams 2012). However, generalizing an IS artifact and generalizing a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory are not the same. On the one hand, they do share the following quality: after demonstrating that an IS artifact or scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory works in one setting, generalizing involves demonstrating that it works in a new or different setting. On the other hand, for an IS artifact to work in a new setting and for a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory to work in a new setting require addressing very different sets of considerations. Generalizing a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory requires making assumptions about scienti<sup>fi</sup>c reasoning. Generalizing an IS artifact, however, requires making assumptions about (as we will later detail) the information artifact, the social arti fact, the technology artifact, and their environments. Our explanation for generalizing an IS artifact will build on, but diverge from as necessary, what is known about generalizing a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory.

There is, of course, already a large body of knowl edge on design science (E.G., Peffers et al. 2007, Baskerville et al. 2015, and Gregor et al. 2020). We conceptualize the design science literature as composed of two complementary parts: the “practically oriented design science literature” and the “theoretically oriented design science literature.” Where theory and artifact can each play the role of means or end, the two literatures conceive of means and end differently. In the practically oriented design science literature, a theory is the means to the development of a working artifact, which is the end. In the theoretically oriented design science literature, a working artifact is the means to the development of a theory, which is the end. Whereas the interplay between artifact design and theory development is required to articulate design science contributions (Baskerville et al. 2018), practically oriented design science is more about developing a working artifact than theoretical knowledge, and theoretically oriented design science is more about developing theoretical knowledge than a working artifact. The literature within which this paper situates itself is the practically oriented design science literature. Papers that we consider to be exemplars in this literature include Chatterjee et al. (2018), Ginige et al. (2020), Tulu et al. (2016), and Oppong-Tawiah et al. (2020). We regard the papers in the practically oriented design science literature that follow design science methodologies that consider the creation of an artifact by deliberate design or design by emergence (Iivari 2017) to be no less important than theoretical knowledge (Gregor and Jones 2007, Peffers et al. 2007, Vaishnavi et al. 2017). Our contribution to this literature will be a procedure by which to successfully recreate (i.e., “generalize”) an IS artifact so that it will also work in a new setting in addition to the one where a research study originally created it.

Also worth mentioning is that the design science literature has addressed generalizability. Rossi et al. (2012) sketched four levels of generalizing from design science research, yet they did not provide any actionable guidance on how to generalize the IS artifact. Some recent efforts have tried to provide such guidance. For example, Wieringa and Daneva (2015) investigated strategies to generalize software engineering theories, Lukyanenko et al. (2016) proposed an artifact sampling methodology to improve the instantiation validity for theorizing design knowledge, and Baskerville and Pries-Heje (2019) contemplated the concept of projectability to facilitate the generalization from design theories. However, these studies are contributions to the theoretically oriented design science literature, not the practically oriented design science literature. This paper, in developing and illustrating a procedure for generalizing an IS artifact, rather than design science knowledge, intends to be a contribution to the practically oriented design science literature.

Our paper makes three signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions to the body of literature on practically oriented design science. First, we devise a procedure to generalize the IS artifact based on a thorough examination of, and analogy to, generalizing scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory. For practi cally oriented design science researchers, the proce dure may facilitate the production of working design science artifacts in more than just the original research setting. Second, we demonstrate the treatment of this procedure based on the real-world implementation of an IS artifact. In doing so, we validate the concept and the procedure of generalizing an IS artifact from the original setting to the IS artifact in the new setting. Design science researchers and practitioners may <sup>fi</sup>nd the real-world treatment and its description a useful model to follow. Third, we show that generalizing the IS artifact should include all three supportive subsystems—namely, the technology artifact, the social artifact, and the information artifact.

The category of generalizing of interest to this paper is the generalizing of a theory or an IS artifact from one setting, where it is known to work, to a new or different setting.<sup>1</sup> What, exactly, is already known about generalizing a theory from one setting to a new setting? Then, given what is known about this, what may be derived for generalizing an IS artifact from one setting to a new setting? After this paper’s next section, in which we distinguish design science from natural and social science, we examine the former question in the following section and the latter question in the section following that. Then, in the subsequent section, a real-world example is provided, where an IS artifact that works in one setting is generalized to work in another setting.

## Distinguishing Design Science from Natural and Social Science

Because the ideas about generalizability on which we will draw pertain to generalizability in natural and social science, rather than design science, it is necessary for us to distinguish natural and social science from design science. In this paper, we assume the distinctions posed by Lee (2014), who bases them on the ideas of Herbert Simon (1996) as presented in his book, The Sciences of the Artificial: natural and social science research describes or explains what exists or has existed, whereas design science research describes or explains how to create what does not now exist or has not yet existed. Natural and social science research is rarely concerned, if at all, with the creation of new artifacts, as it is concerned with just describing or explaining what already exists or has existed. On the other hand, a central concern of design science research is the creation of new artifacts with practical utility.

As for what we mean by “design” and “artifact,” our thinking diverges somewhat from the general notion of the design science artifact in IS research. We argue that the perspective of design science needs to be broader than what IS design science researchers mean by design science. The meaning of design science that we adopt in our study is closer to the original meaning that Simon presented in his book. In particular, design science includes not only the design of the technical and the physical but also the design of the social (Lee et al. 2015). Just as the technical is designed, the world of people and their institutions also needs to be designed. Thus, for us, “design” and “artifact” refer to the design of the entire IS artifact, not just the IT artifact.

Returning to the question of how knowledge about generalizing a theory may inform knowledge about generalizing an IS artifact, it behooves us to examine the application of logic for generalizing theory in natural and social science such that parallels may be drawn for the application of logic in design science. In natural and social science, the logic for generalizing theory is motivated by questions such as the following: “Is the theory true?” “Is the theory successful in an empirical test?” “Will the theory be successful in other empirical settings?” “Is the theory generalizable?” In design science, the following corresponding questions apply: “Is the IS artifact practicable?” “Is the IS artifact successful in an empirical test?” “Will the IS artifact be successful in other empirical settings?” “Is the IS artifact generalizable?”

The scienti<sup>fi</sup>c method (Encyclopedia Britannica 2019) in natural and social science follows steps that include collecting information, making observations, and asking questions; formulating or building a theory; designing an experiment to test the theory; conducting the experiment; comparing expected results (what should happen if the theory is correct) with observed results (what actually happens); and drawing conclusions. If the comparison is not favorable, then the theory is re<sup>fi</sup>ned, and the steps addressing these questions are repeated. If the comparison is favorable, then the theory is considered corroborated but is still subject to additional testing in new and different experiments.<sup>2</sup>

By contrast, Vaishnavi et al. (2017) offer a set of process steps for design science. The process begins with the awareness of the problem or, more speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, a practical problem experienced by people in the everyday world, rather than a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c problem involving theory and evidence. The output is what Vaishnavi et al. (2017) call “a Proposal … for a new research effort” (p. 8). Next, the “suggestion” of a solution to the problem can involve “a Tentative Design and the performance of a prototype based on that design” (p. 9). As for development, “the Tentative Design is further developed and implemented in this phase” (p. 9), taking the form of the artifact. It is possible that development of the artifact could expose new facets of the problem, prompting a return to the “suggestion” of a new tentative design or new prototype. Vaishnavi et al. (2017) state, “Once constructed, the artifact is evaluated according to criteria [the performance measures] that are always implicit and frequently made explicit in the Proposal” (p. 9). Not living up to the performance measures could expose new facets of the problem, again prompting a return to the “suggestion” of a new tentative design or new prototype. The conclusion would be the “<sup>fi</sup>nale” (p. 10) of the research effort. With regard to satis<sup>fi</sup>cing, this happens when the results—the performance of the artifact—are judged to be “good enough” (p. 10). Other scholars have proposed similar design processes (e.g., March and Smith 1995, Hevner et al. 2004, and Peffers et al. 2007).

Our stance in this study is that we agree with the design science process presented by Vaishnavi et al. (2017), but we diverge from it in how it is applied. As mentioned, we argue that design science is more than just technical design science (I.E., the design of technical artifacts). We emphasize that the artifact to be designed is not just the technology (Kline 1985) or the IT artifact but the entire IS artifact, of which the technology artifact is just a part.

## Generalizing a Scientific Theory

An assumption upon which everyday commonsense generalizing rests is the uniformity of nature assumption. It is “the principle that the future will resemble the past, in that when suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar situations recur, similar effects follow” (Blackburn 2016). For instance, if the experimental conditions in two laboratory settings are alike, then, according to the uniformity of nature assumption, the experimental results predicted by the same theory in the two laboratory settings will be alike. A conclusion, it would seem, is that for two or more settings that are alike a theory that holds in one setting will hold across all the settings. Unfortunately, this commonsense generalizing does not stand up to scienti<sup>fi</sup>c scrutiny.

The philosopher David Hume (1711–1776) established that the validity of the uniformity of nature assumption cannot be demonstrated. Hume explained that any attempt at such a demonstration would only lead to an in<sup>fi</sup>nite regress in reasoning. This has become known as Hume’s problem of induction. A detailed explanation of Hume’s problem of induction is provided by Lee and Baskerville (2003). Scienti<sup>fi</sup>- cally speaking, what this means is that a theory that has been tested and demonstrated to work in one setting may not be generalized to work in another setting, until and unless it has actually been tested and demonstrated to work in the latter setting.

However, the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems has not only a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c side as a research discipline but also a pragmatic side as a profession, where approximations may be accepted as suf<sup>fi</sup>cient in the interests of solving practical problems. Regarding practicality, Kuhn (1957, p. 38) has pointed out that navigation instruments make the assumption that the earth is at the center of the universe where, despite the inaccuracy of this assumption, suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently accurate directions nonetheless result. If, in the same spirit, we accept the uniformity of nature assumption as true, then how may we proceed so that suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently accurate conclusions result—namely, that the results are generalizable?

On the basis of the uniformity of nature assumption’s being true, Lee and Baskerville (2012) provide detailed guidance on how to proceed to generalize a theory, empirically tested and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in one setting, to a new setting. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Lee and Baskerville (2012) address the following: short of actually empirically testing and con<sup>fi</sup>rming a theory in the new setting, how may one claim that the theory generalizes to the new setting? Appropriately, Lee and Baskerville (2012) frame their guidance in terms of what they call four “judgment calls.” They are the “uniformity of nature” judgment call, the “suf<sup>fi</sup>cient similarity in relevant conditions” judgment call, the “successful identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of relevant variables” judgment call, and the “theory is true” judgment call.

First, the uniformity of nature judgment call simply makes the assumption that the uniformity-of-nature assumption, as discussed in the preceding, itself is valid.

Second, the theory is true judgment call makes the assumption that the theory is true. “It makes sense for a person to generalize a theory to a new setting only if the theory is true” (Lee and Baskerville 2012, p. 752); however, owing to the logic of modus tollens and the fallacy of af<sup>fi</sup>rming the consequent, no theory may be proved to be irrefutably true despite how many settings in which it has been successfully tested in the past. At best, in choosing to use a theory in a new setting, one may only make a judgment call that it is true.

Third, the successful identification of relevant variables judgment call “is the judgment call that the theory, as already empirically tested and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in an empirical setting, indeed speci<sup>fi</sup>es all of the variables that have any in<sup>fl</sup>uence in shaping the phenomenon of interest” (Lee and Baskerville, 2012, p. 751).

Fourth and last, the sufficient similarity in relevant conditions judgment call returns us to what Blackburn’s (2016) uniformity of nature assumption states: “When suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar situations recur, similar effects follow.” Lee and Baskerville (2012, p. 751) explain, “The conditions in the two settings [the setting being generalized from, and the setting being generalized to] need to be judged ‘suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently close’ to each other for the uniformity-of-nature assumption to be applied.” In other words, this is a judgment call that the two settings are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar.

What forms do the four judgment calls take when we are no longer generalizing a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory but an IS artifact, from one setting where it works to a new or different setting where we are interested in knowing whether it will also work?

## Generalizing an IS Artifact

As mentioned, a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory and an IS artifact are not the same thing. How does generalizing an IS artifact diverge from generalizing a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory?

Also as mentioned, an IS artifact is a system, itself consisting of subsystems that are a technology artifact, an information artifact, and a social artifact. Here, we add the following details, taken from Lee et al. (2015): a technology artifact is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a human-created tool intended to solve a problem, achieve a goal or serve a purpose that is human de<sup>fi</sup>ned, human perceived, or human felt. The IS artifact also includes the social artifact, which refers to the people, the social structure, the culture, and the organization. An information artifact denotes the instantiation of information, “where the instantiation occurs through a human act either directly (as could happen through a person’s verbal or written statement) or indirectly (as could happen through a person’s running of a computer program to produce a quarterly report)” (Lee et al. 2015, p. 8).

Relevant to our conception of an IS artifact is Simon’s idea of an artifact as the interface between an inner environment and outer environment:

An artifact can be thought of as a meeting point—an “interface” in today’s terms—between an “inner” environment, the substance and organization of the artifact itself, and an “outer” environment, the surroundings in which it operates. If the inner environment is appropriate to the outer environment, or vice versa, the artifact will serve its intended purpose. (Simon 1996, p. 6)

Thus, the IS artifact’s constituent social artifact, information artifact, and technology artifact each has an inner environment and outer environment, where the appropriateness of the inner environment to the outer environment of the new setting—the setting to which the artifact is being generalized, as compared with the old setting where it has been demonstrated to work—is necessary for generalizing the artifact to the new setting. Such appropriateness needs to be considered at the technology artifact level, the information artifact level, and the social artifact level.

We now revisit Lee and Baskerville’s (2012) four judgment calls, which pertain to a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory, as they may pertain to an IS artifact. First, the uniformity of nature judgment call applies unchanged: the uniformity of nature is itself accepted as an assumption. Second, we apply the theory is true judgment call in the following way. The analogue to the “theory is true” in the case of generalizing a theory to a new setting is the “IS artifact is practicable” in the case of generalizing an IS artifact to a new setting, where “practicable” means “able to be … put into practice successfully.”<sup>3</sup> We will refer to this as the IS artifact is practicable judgment call. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, owing to the logic of modus tollens and the fallacy of af<sup>fi</sup>rming the consequent, just as a theory may not be proved to be irrefutably true despite how many settings in which it has been successfully tested in the past, an IS artifact may not be proved to be irrefutably practicable despite how many settings in which it has been put into practice successfully in the past; hence a judgment call needs to be made that the IS artifact is practicable in the new setting. Third, just as the successful identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of relevant variables judgment call refers to successfully identifying all the relevant variables, the corresponding judgment call in generalizing the IS artifact refers to successfully identifying all the relevant outer environment conditions. We will refer to this as the successful identification of relevant outer environment conditions judgment call. Fourth and <sup>fi</sup>nally, we apply the suf<sup>fi</sup>cient similarity in relevant conditions judgment call in the following way. In returning us (as previously mentioned) to what the uniformity of nature assumption states (I.E., “when suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar situations recur, similar effects follow”), it raises the question of what “suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar situations” and “similar effects” refer to in the case of generalizing an IS artifact. Because our interest is in whether the IS artifact will also be successful in a new or different setting, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne “suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar situations” as “suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar outer environment conditions” with regard to the technology artifact, the information artifact, and the social artifact; we simply de<sup>fi</sup>ne “similar effects” as the IS artifact’s functioning successfully in the new or different setting, just as it functioned successfully in the original setting. We will refer to this as the sufficient similarity in relevant outer environment conditions judgment call.

In summary, generalizing an IS artifact diverges from generalizing a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory in the second, third, and fourth judgment calls, as depicted in Table 1.

The four judgment calls suggest that the generalization of an IS artifact from a setting where it has been successful to a new setting where we wish it to be successful involves the following procedure for us to follow. First, we accept the uniformity of nature assumption and presume that the IS artifact is practicable in the new setting. Second, in the setting where the IS artifact has been successful, we identify all conditions in the outer environment, which we judge relevant to the IS artifact’s being successful. This also necessarily involves considering the inner environment, the appropriateness of which to the outer environment determines the success of the IS artifact. Third, we compare the relevant outer environment conditions in the original setting with those we <sup>fi</sup>nd in the new setting, where our judgment of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient similarity between the former and the latter would allow us to make the call that the IS artifact will be successful in the latter.

## Generalizing an Information Systems Artifact from the Setting of Haiti to the Setting of Nepal

In this section of the paper, we illustrate the general ization of an IS artifact from one setting to another. The IS artifact from which we generalize consists of a technology artifact (the computer on a stick, or COS) and its accompanying information artifact and social artifact in the setting of Haiti. The IS artifact to which we generalize consists of a technology artifact (the continuing medical education (CME) on a stick, or CMES) and its accompanying information artifact and social artifact in the setting of Nepal. We will refer to the former as the COS IS artifact and the latter as the CMES IS artifact. First, we describe the COS IS artifact in Haiti, including how it was successful. Second, inspired by the success of this IS artifact, we investigate how to generalize it to the setting of Nepal; this will involve applying the four judgment calls.

## The COS Information Systems Artifact in Haiti

Haiti is one of the poorest countries in the world, ranked 168th of 187 countries according to the United Nations Human Development Reports (United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) 2017). In total, 51.3% of the population is illiterate, and 50.7% are working poor, at \$3.10 a day purchase power parity. Of the rural population, 99.5% do not have access to electricity, and only 12.2% of Haiti’s population are internet users (UNDP 2017). The majority of the population has never used a computer.

Because of the limited availability of computing resources and the lack of computer literacy, young Haitians have diminished ability to participate in the digital economy or obtain related jobs that may bring them and their families out of poverty (Thomas et al. 2017). Information and communication technology for education (ICTE) facilitates the acquisition of basic technological skills such as computer literacy and numeracy (Romeo and Walker 2001) while simultaneously contributing to poverty reduction and human development (Bonnah Nkansah and Unwin 2010). The country recognizes that technology access and training have the potential to induce economic and social change and enable society to overcome the digital divide. The development and diffusion of ICTE in Haiti is thus a critical priority (Thomas et al. 2017).

The technology artifact developed for Haiti is, as mentioned, the COS, or computer on a stick, a thumb drive that is bootable from the USB port of a computer. It is based on the Edubuntu Free/Libre Open-Source Software (FLOSS) operating system. Users who own a COS gain access to a learning platform that includes Khan Academy instructional videos on topics such as science, math, art, history, and literature and additional material such as science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) classroom content produced by the MIT-Haiti Initiative, e-books from Project Gutenberg archives, a detailed world map, and a repository of FLOSS educational software. The COS includes a separate storage partition for customized content management; THAT IS, a user may store personal <sup>fi</sup>les, music, and videos on the COS alongside the built-in operating system or applications already on the thumb drive.

The overall COS IS artifact, consisting of the technology artifact (the COS) and both a social artifact and an information artifact supporting it, was implemented at six rural locations in the Centre province of Haiti. The intentions to use ICTE were empirically evaluated using data collected from 222 users (Thomas et al. 2017). The success of the COS IS artifact did not go unnoticed. A physician in Nepal, who was shown the COS, remarked,

The wisdom of the Haiti COS project is providing learning resources for students without the need for an Internet connection. In countries where Internet connection and electricity are unreliable … this [is a] leap over the limiting step to access CME [continuing medical education] content. This is a common problem in Nepal where load shedding occurs daily for 12 hours and for days in the rural areas. I started thinking about using the same model for medical education.

The question then was whether prescriptive knowledge of the IS artifact from Haiti could be generalized to the Nepal context. Inspired by the success of the COS IS artifact in Haiti, the project team embarked on generalizing the IS artifact from Haiti so that it would also work for CME in Nepal.

Fundamentally speaking, what is to be generalized from the Haiti setting to the Nepal setting is an IS artifact that includes a “solution on a stick”—namely, a portable thumb drive that can store and output educational content and be independent of the need for an uninterrupted internet connection, that a user can take it with him or her, and that can be customized to a certain extent. Can the COS IS artifact be generalized from the Haiti setting to the Nepal setting? This depends on the technology artifact, the information artifact, and the social artifact, each of which interfaces with its oute and inner environment. Regarding the “suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar outer environment conditions” mentioned earlier, it will be necessary to show that the outer environment conditions in the case of the Haiti setting and the outer environment conditions in the case of the Nepal setting are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar to one another to allow general ization from the former to the latter.

## The CMES Information Systems Artifact in Nepal

Nepal, a landlocked country in the Himalayas, ranks 149th according to the United Nations Human Development Reports (UNDP 2017). In total, 40.4% of the population is illiterate, and 32.6% are working poor, at \$3.10 a day purchase power parity. Although 85.2% of the rural population have electricity, only 19.7% of Nepal’s population are internet users (UNDP 2017).

CME in emergency medicine (EM) is an immediate need in Nepal owing to a large ethnic population living in mountainous regions and the country’s popularity as a destination for adventure travelers (Li et al. 2020). EM is one of the newest and fastest-developing medical specialties in Nepal. Yet national standards for CME and certi<sup>fi</sup>cation have not been developed or mandated. Although major urban hospitals have recently started to adopt EM practices and provide training, the concept of CME in EM is entirely lacking in rural areas (Pandey 2016). Disproportionate levels of mortality in the 2015 earthquake are a direct testament to this dire situation. CME challenges encountered in Nepal were described by one of the local medical practitioners as follows:

From remote villages to larger cities, my experience has been a lack of quality and affordable EM CME for all health providers including nurses, rural community health workers (CHW), midwives, and physicians. I realized my EM training and wilderness medicine background provided pertinent topics for Nepal's austere environments … the villages and even the cities. … Kathmandu is an austere environment considering strikes, earthquakes, the petro embargo, and rush hour traffic.

The CMES technology artifact, designed and modeled after the COS technology artifact, was developed for the delivery of free and high-quality CME content for medical practitioners in resource-constrained areas of the world. It is a Java-based application, residing on a thumb drive, with integrated capabilities for the storage and dissemination of CME content with minimal need for internet connectivity. CME content includes audio <sup>fi</sup>les and PDFs related to EM. The CMES technology artifact also includes a synchronization capability that enables the user to update the CME content on the thumb drive when the internet is available. A medical practitioner who has a CMES technology artifact, or thumb drive, may search for and access topics of interest without internet connectivity. He or she may customize the CMES by downloading new content to the thumb drive while at a location with internet connectivity or opting to store only topics of interest. The CMES IS artifact and its components (I.E., the technology artifact, the information artifact, and the social artifact) serve the information and social needs of rural medical practitioners in Nepal.

As for the end result, the new CMES IS artifact indeed functioned successfully. Over the past <sup>fi</sup>ve years, it has been used by 80 medical practitioners in 21 different health organizations in Nepal.

## Addressing the Four Judgment Calls in Generalizing the Information Systems Artifact from Haiti to Nepal

To proceed with the desired generalization requires consideration of the four judgment calls, summarized in Table 1. We address the uniformity of nature judgment call, the IS artifact is practicable judgment call, the successful identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of relevant outer environment conditions judgment call, and the suf<sup>fi</sup>cient similarity in relevant outer environment conditions judgment call. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two judgment calls are quickly and readily made. The third and fourth judgment calls require more detailed discussion.

The First Judgment Call. We make the uniformity of nature judgment call. We accept the idea that the more the two situations are similar to each other, the more their outcomes will be similar to each other. In other words, we accept that the more the Haiti and Nepal situations are alike, the more that success of the IS artifact in Haiti will generalize to Nepal.

The Second Judgment Call. We make the IS artifact is practicable judgment call. We acknowledge that an IS artifact’s having been practicable in one instance (I.E., in Haiti) is no proof that it will be practicable in gen eral (including in Nepal). In fact, as mentioned earlier, an IS artifact may not be proved to be irrefutably practicable despite how many settings in which it has been put into practice successfully in the past; hence a judgment call needs to be made that the IS artifact will be practicable in the new setting.

The Third Judgment Call. We make the successful identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of relevant outer environment conditions judgment call. In making this judgment call, we assume that we succeed in identifying all the relevant conditions in the outer environments of the technology artifact, information artifact, and social artifact. Table 2 presents three examples of these outer environment conditions; all relevant outer environment conditions appear in Appendix Tables A.1–A.3.

We note that the outer environment conditions of the IS artifact in both Haiti and Nepal share commonalities, but the inner environment that each artifact interfaces with (Simon, 1996, p. 6)—the technology artifact, the information artifact, and the social artifact—is different in Haiti and Nepal. In Table 2, the outer environment conditions of Haiti and Nepal appear in the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second columns, respectively, and commonalities in the set of outer environment conditions appear in the third column.

## Discussion

In this paper, we have proposed and illustrated a procedure for generalizing the IS artifact. Building on what is already known about generalizing a theory from one setting to another setting, we devised, by analogy, a procedure for generalizing an IS artifact from one setting to another setting. To recapitulate, this procedure consists of working with four judgment calls. The “uniformity of nature” and the “IS artifact is practicable” judgment calls can both be dealt with simply by accepting them; in the case of general izing the IS artifact from Haiti to Nepal, this involved, <sup>fi</sup>rst, accepting the idea that the more the Haiti and Nepal situations are alike, the more that success of the IS artifact in Haiti will generalize to Nepal and, second, accepting that the IS artifact, although demonstrated to be practicable in just the single setting of Haiti, will also be practicable in the setting of Nepal. The “successful identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of relevant outer environment conditions” judgment call requires a detailed account of the outer environment conditions of the technology artifact, information artifact, and social artifact for both the original IS artifact and the new IS artifact; in the case of generalizing the IS artifact from Haiti to Nepal, this involved assuming that we succeeded in identifying all the relevant outer environment conditions for the three artifacts, as recorded in Table 2 and Appendix Tables A.1–A.3. The “suf<sup>fi</sup>cient similarity in relevant outer environment conditions” judgment call requires a comparison of all the original IS artifact’s outer environment conditions with those of the new IS artifact, where the two sets of outer environment conditions need to be judged “suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar” for the generalization to be made; in the case of generalizing the IS artifact from Haiti to Nepal, the suf<sup>fi</sup>cient similarity of the two sets of outer environment conditions is indicated in the third column (“Commonalities in the outer environment conditions of Haiti and Nepal”) of Table 2 and Appendix Tables A.1–A.3.

Four points about our generalizing require emphasis: 0 1 1

1. Generalizing the IS artifact involves more than just generalizing the IT artifact, to which we have referred as the technology artifact. We do not advocate simply exporting the technology artifact from one setting to another setting; our premise is that unless the surrounding, supportive subsystems around the technology artifact—namely, the social artifact and information artifact—are also generalized to the new setting, the technology artifact is unlikely to be successful. Hence, we emphasize generalizing the overall IS artifact, not just the IT artifact. At the same time, we also emphasize that the attention we give to the overall IS artifact, not just the IT artifact, is fairly unique in design science research: What it means to extend the conception of the IT artifact to include the information artifact and the social artifact has been rarely considered by design science research. Indeed, a focus on just the IT artifact alone is behind the idea of IT artifact “reuse”— an idea that, we believe, is more likely to lead to failure as a result of its not accounting for the effects and the outer environment conditions of the social artifact and the information artifact. It is not the technology artifact alone but its interaction with the information artifact and social artifact that results in a successful IS artifact. It is evident that unless all three artifacts are generalized from Haiti, the resulting IS artifact in Nepal would be unsuccessful.

2. Generalizing the IS artifact to a new setting does not mean applying it unchanged in the new setting. What is important is that the inner environments of the technology artifact, the information artifact, and the social artifact are appropriate to their respective outer environments as instantiated in the new setting. For example, an attempt at generalizing the technology artifact from Haiti to Nepal transcends the idea that the design of the technology artifact could be exported either “as whole” or “in part” to the new setting where it is simply “reused.” Rather, the technology artifact itself is subject to redesign, no less than the information artifact and social artifact whose own redesigns are necessitated to support the successful functioning of the overall IS artifact in the new setting. The main point is that the overall IS artifact—as we discussed, involving a “solution on a stick”—is generalized, which may involve the redesign of the inner environment of its constituent technology artifact, information artifact, and social artifact. Thus, the IS artifact was changed when it was generalized from Haiti to Nepal, so that the inner environments of the technology artifact, information artifact, and social artifact are appropriate to their respective outer environments.

3. Our conceptualization of generalizing IS artifacts emphasizes the practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of design science research and successful creation of artifacts that work in a new setting in addition to the one where a research study originally created it. Generalizing the artifact rather than the design theory in this manner improves the practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of design science research, where practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance is one of the dominant goals of design science research.

4. We acknowledge that generalization from design science research may also be conceptualized from a theoretical perspective. For example, Rossi et al. (2012) suggested four levels of generalization around the theoretical knowledge from design, where the <sup>fi</sup>rst level involves generalizing the problem that the artifact aims to address to a class of problems, the second level entails generalizing the artifact from one solution instance to a class of solutions, the third level generalizes the artifact to design principles for a class of solutions, and the fourth level generalizes the design principles to design theories. Although we provided a procedure to successfully generalize an IS artifact from one setting to a new setting, we do not preclude the generalization of the theoretical knowledge of design. For example, research with Haiti’s COS IS artifact and Nepal’s CMES IS artifact show both level 1 generalization (I.E., the original problem in Haiti is cast as an instance of a class of problems) and level 2 generalization (I.E., the solution instance in Haiti is generalized to a class of solutions). Researchers may also conceptualize the lessons from both solution instances into design principles, which entails level 3 generalization. Future research may generalize the COS and CMES design knowledge as a design theory, for example, following the form and requirements suggested by Gregor and Jones (2007).

The study has limitations that call for future exploration. One limitation of the study is that the two cases (Nepal and Haiti) contained aspects that both implicitly incorporated design science methods and were arguably guided by a common design framework (the citizen-centric capacity development framework) (Thomas et al. 2017, Li et al. 2020). Future research may investigate how IS artifacts can be generalized from one setting to another when a common design science framework is not used. IS artifact generalization can be useful when design principles and theories are present (a kind of practical reasoning instead of theoretical reasoning), and it is perhaps even more useful when design principles or theories are not present. Future work could also consider the possibility of con<sup>fl</sup>icting outcomes between theory and IS artifact generalization, where one generalizes and the other does not.

Finally, an implicit point in our reasoning about generalizing the IS artifact has been that design rules or principles are not essential for transferring artifact design across contexts.

## Conclusion

This paper makes three contributions to the practically oriented design science literature. The <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution is a procedure to generalize an IS artifact, by which substantive knowledge from one setting can be applied to a new setting. This contribution extends the state-of-the-art knowledge in the design science literature—in particular, the practically oriented design science literature—so that it may facilitate the production of working artifacts in more than just the original research setting. This may be considered a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution where a focus of IS design science is the production of working artifacts, not just the development of new theory.

Another contribution is the paper’s treatment of the procedure in terms of the entire IS artifact and not just the IT artifact. In the procedure, the IS artifact, the parts of which are not only the technology artifact but also the information artifact and social artifact, is generalized from one setting to a new setting. As demonstrated using the case of generalizing the IS artifact from Haiti to Nepal, the process of generalizing the IS artifact transcends the mere process of hardware or software reuse, which pertains to just the technology artifact.

The third contribution of this paper’s research is the real-world implementation of a useful continuing medical education system, focusing on emergency medicine, in Nepal. It is the result of applying the concept of an IS artifact (in contrast to an IT artifact alone) and the concept of generalizing an IS artifact.

As already mentioned, we adopt the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of IS artifact from Lee et al. (2015), where “IS artifact” refers to a system, itself consisting of subsystems that are (1) a technology artifact, (2) an information artifact, and (3) a social artifact. In the original setting, which consists of primary and secondary schools in Haiti, the technology artifact is implemented on a USB drive; the information artifact involves educational content appropriate for primary and secondary schools; and the social artifact is the development, training, imple mentation, and maintenance that support the successful use of the technology artifact and information artifact. In the new setting, which is rural areas in Nepal where medical care is needed, the technology artifact is also implemented on a USB drive; the information artifact involves continuing medical education content; and the social artifact refers to procedures of development, training, implementation, and maintenance that support the successful use of the technology artifact and information artifact. Through the application of the four judgment calls, we provide a description of what it is to generalize the IS artifact from the original setting of Haiti to the IS artifact in the new setting of Nepal, as well as a description of the (successful) result of the generalization.

To conclude, we note that Niederman and March (2012) have stated that “where design science researchers do not propose reasonable generalizations from their work as they might apply to a broader range of phenomena, an opportunity to further extend the base of knowledge and facilitate adding to the cumulative tradition is missed” (p. 6). One of the reasons for such a missed opportunity is that current design science research methodology and practice have not provided a procedure for generalizing artifacts (vom Brocke et al. 2020). In this paper, we have provided such a procedure.
