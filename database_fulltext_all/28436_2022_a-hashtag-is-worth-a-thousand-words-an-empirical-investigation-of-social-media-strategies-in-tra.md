# A Hashtag Is Worth a Thousand Words: An Empirical Investigation of Social Media Strategies in Trademarking Hashtags

Naveen Kumar,<sup>a,</sup>\* Liangfei Qiu,<sup>b</sup> Subodha Kumar<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Firms of all sizes are “joining the conversation” on social media platforms and increasingly trademarking hashtags related to their products and brands. This added effort to protect intellectual property and its impact on social media engagement have not been investigated in the literature. In this study, we attempt to bridge this important gap in the literature by <sup>fi</sup>rst examining the impact of trademarking a hashtag on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media audience engagement. By adopting multiple causal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies to address the issues of self-selected trademarking, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that trademarking hashtags plays a pivotal role in increasing social media audience engagement and information dissemination. More importantly, this positive effect is stronger for <sup>fi</sup>rms with fewer Twitter followers. Digging deeper into the underlying mechanisms, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that trademarking hashtags makes composing tweets with certain linguistic styles more critical: It can amplify the positive effects of trademarking hashtags on social media audience engagement. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight important managerial implications of trademarking hashtags. First of all, we examine whether trademarking a hashtag helps or hurts a <sup>fi</sup>rm in terms of its social media audience engagement. Further, we show, to maximize the effectiveness of trademarking hashtags, how <sup>fi</sup>rms should develop the right social media engagement strategies by taking speci<sup>fi</sup>c communication and linguistic styles into account. Our results provide useful insights to <sup>fi</sup>rms in understanding the key bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of signaling through trademarking hashtags on social media engagement.

## 1. Introduction

In the past decade, social media platforms have become an important channel by which consumers and <sup>fi</sup>rms communicate and engage with each other (Bapna et al. 2017, Kumar et al. 2018b, Hu et al. 2019, Kumar et al. 2019, Chung et al. 2020, Kumar and Qiu 2022). Engaging with consumers through social media helps businesses reduce information asymmetry, gain insight into their consumer bases, turn highly dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers into satis<sup>fi</sup>ed ones, and elevate satis<sup>fi</sup>ed consumers into loyal buyers, leading to an increase in sales (Kumar et al. 2018a) and brand pro<sup>fi</sup>tability (Lee et al. 2018a).

To engage and motivate consumers to evaluate the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s products or services, <sup>fi</sup>rms usually employ different types of signals (Kwark et al. 2021, Hsu et al. 2022). With the help of these signals, consumers derive expectations about the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s characteristics that cannot be observed directly (Qiu et al. 2015, Lee et al. 2018b, Yang and Yuan 2019). An example of such signals to send credible information to consumers is a trademark (Chemmanur et al. 2020). Trademarks are typically used by <sup>fi</sup>rms that want to protect their intellectual properties and to differentiate their products or services from competitors (Morrin et al. 2006), encourage branding efforts (Keller 1993), and amplify shareholder value (Davidson 2004). The increase in the acceptance of trademarks has allowed them to be used as a standard for measuring a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovation (Flikkema et al. 2019) and its future product development and marketing strategy (Faurel et al. 2021).

A trademark re<sup>fl</sup>ects a set of complex attributes or characteristics that may not be readily observed (Economides 1988, Swann et al. 2001). Trademarks typically undergo a strict scrutiny process established by the trademark of<sup>fi</sup>ce.<sup>2</sup> The trademark act requires a veri-<sup>fi</sup>ed statement supporting the application signed by the owner or a person properly authorized to sign on behalf of the owner. The penalty imposed for an improper <sup>fi</sup>ling of intellectual property such as patent and trademark may far exceed that of merely losing it (MacKendrick et al. 2015, Philipp 2021, Siesel 2021). For example, it may formally exclude a party from bringing matters before the trademark of<sup>fi</sup>ce as an attorney, correspondent, domestic representative, and or signatory (USPTO 2021b).<sup>3</sup> Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms have inherent incentives to provide accurate information to the trademark of<sup>fi</sup>ce and follow the process to avoid facing penalties. Because of the strict <sup>fi</sup>ling and auditing process, for <sup>fi</sup>rms holding trademarks, consumers are certain of receiving messages from authentic sources, which further provides some assurances that the signals conveyed by <sup>fi</sup>rms are reliable. In addition, using a trademark provides credible signals of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s main performance measures, such as enhancing shareholder value, seeking higher initial public offering pricing, increasing revenue and future pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, and realizing higher stock returns (Davidson 2004, Hsu et al. 2022).

Although “using trademarks” has been widely studied, they mainly focus on the role of trademarking brands. Recently, <sup>fi</sup>rms have begun trademarking hashtags to protect this valuable form of intellectual property (Marshall 2016, Roberts 2017). Hashtags are typically used on social media as ubiquitous conversation starters, marketing campaign tools, and brand symbols. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using hashtags include boosting businesses’ brand recognition, generating buzz around new products, and attracting new customers while ensuring a high level of engagement (Lewis 2016, Buckhanon 2019). Original hashtags give <sup>fi</sup>rms control over a social media space in which they can show off their industry expertise, drive viral conversations, and promote a special event, contest, or campaign to interested consumers (Blaszka et al. 2016, McKelvey and Grady 2017, Morales 2018). In this study, we examine a particular form of <sup>fi</sup>rm-side social media signaling strategy—trademarking a hashtag—which has not been examined in prior literature.

## 1.1. Motivation

The U.S. Patent and Trademark Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (USPTO) con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed the registrability of hashtags as trademarks in 2013. The trademark is frequently rated as a primary intangible property owned by a <sup>fi</sup>rm (Yang and Yuan 2019). An increasing number of <sup>fi</sup>rms have been trademarking hashtags and taking advantage of this extra protection (Davey 2016). Per the Trademark Manual of Examining Procedure (TMEP 2021), hashtags can become registered trademarks only if [they] function as an identi<sup>fi</sup>er of the applicant’s goods and services.<sup>4</sup> This registration allows <sup>fi</sup>rms to gain a competitive advantage and build reputational value on social media with increased distance between <sup>fi</sup>rms and con sumers (Sherwin 2015).

To the best of our knowledge, little is known in the research community and practitioners’ guidelines about the signaling effect of a trademarked hashtag on audience engagement in social media platforms. What is the effect of a trademarked hashtag on audience engagement in social media platforms? Using online platforms, consumers have limited capacity to analyze a product and its quality, as they do not have direct means to feel, touch, or inspect a product (Lal and Sarvary 1999, Mehra et al. 2018). These limitations lead to higher information asymmetry in online channels, making it more important for <sup>fi</sup>rms to send credible signals, such as trademarking, when compared with of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels (Biswas and Biswas 2004). Once a <sup>fi</sup>rm registers a trademark, consumers may recognize speci<sup>fi</sup>c goods and services or perceive differences among brands. Therefore, a trademark may reduce consumers’ search costs by condensing complex meanings into concise, easy-to-remember, and unequivocal hashtags (Swann et al. 2001, Ty Inc. vs. Perryman 2002, Sipe 2021). In addition, because many consumers are aware of a trademark owned by a speci<sup>fi</sup>c brand, exclusive rights in a trademark eventually may induce pressure on the <sup>fi</sup>rm to adhere to a consistent quality of the product or service (Katz 2010). It allows consumers to depend on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s trademark as a mental shortcut, thereby further reducing the overall search costs (Tushnet 2008). Therefore, a trademark helps <sup>fi</sup>rms make stronger bonds with consumers and further reduces information asymmetry.

Firms use various types of signals to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers on social media (Mavlanova et al. 2012; Martin et al. 2015; Qiu and Whinston 2017; Qiu et al. 2018; Paruchuri et al. 2019; Na et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021, 2022; Pu et al. 2022). Users adjust their perceptions of the <sup>fi</sup>rm depending on the type of signals. Signals revealed directly by the <sup>fi</sup>rm or individuals, without any impartial third-party endorsement or through unreliable sources, may not be perceived as credible signals by consumers (Mavlanova et al. 2012, Williams et al. 2015). On the other hand, the signals from impar tial sources, such as the trademark of<sup>fi</sup>ce, are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, expensive, and time consuming to acquire (Connelly et al. 2011, Cheng et al. 2020). However, they are considered credible because of the strict <sup>fi</sup>ling and auditing process established by the trademark of<sup>fi</sup>ce and have a more positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumers in forming their perception toward the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the way consumers react on social media (Winter and Kramer¨ 2014). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>rms with trademarks issued by the agency, such as the USPTO, are perceived to be sending more credible and authentic signals to consumers. These <sup>fi</sup>rms invest considerable resources to establish their brand equity, which serves as a credible signal to separate them from those who are unwilling to invest in their brand equity.

In our case, it is unclear whether the speci<sup>fi</sup>c act of trademarking a hashtag plays the role of a credible signal on social media platforms where information asymmetry is usually high and perceiving direct product or service quality is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Drawing on the signaling theory, a <sup>fi</sup>rm can send signals to convey its commitment to build and protect its brand equity to consumers as well as to clarify any consumer confusion about their brand (Hsu et al. 2022). This signaling, in turn, leads to an increase in the intentions of the consumers to engage with the <sup>fi</sup>rm and purchase its product or service (Jeng 2016). From a big-picture perspective, we <sup>fi</sup>ll this important research gap by examining the signaling effects of trademarking a hashtag.

In terms of precise motivation, trademarking hashtags with the USPTO may send credible signals to consumers, which may reduce information asymmetry on social media (Hsu et al. 2022). On the one hand, the act of signaling provides key insights into which a <sup>fi</sup>rm in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer behavior (Yang and Yuan 2019). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, signaling through trademarking conveys vital information on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s new product, services, and marketing efforts (Faurel et al. 2021). The phenomenon of trademarking a hashtag may help in sending credible signals about <sup>fi</sup>rm commitment in building brand awareness, loyalty, and trust among consumers (Crass et al. 2019). Note that because the signaling effect depends on consumer awareness, in our context, <sup>fi</sup>rms publicize their acceptance of a trademarked hashtag on social media, such as Twitter, to ensure that the signal has been received by the consumers (see Figure D.1 in Online Appendix D). Therefore, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s act of trademarking may serve as a signal of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s credibility and increase audience engagement on social media. Moreover, the trademark protection of hashtags sends reliable signals to competitors and prevents them from using similar hashtags to mislead consumers (Chu 2017).

On the other hand, one could argue that a trademarked hashtag is not directly reported in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial statement or as a separate performance measure. Their economic value is also not straightforward to estimate (Seethamraju 2003). In addition, it is not easy to get virtually exclusive rights on a trademarked hashtag because of the trademark dilution phenomenon, which makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to sue competitors for trademark infringement (Heath and Mace 2020). Therefore, trademarked hashtags may not help reduce the information asymmetry on social media because the audience may not know about the newly acquired intangible assets of these <sup>fi</sup>rms. Overall, the act of trademarking hashtags by <sup>fi</sup>rms may not send credible signals and may not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on audience engagement on social media.

Given these two con<sup>fl</sup>icting arguments concerning how trademarking a hashtag affects information asymmetry, our overarching theme is to empirically investi gate whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s act of trademarking a hashtag can lead to an increase in social media engagement via a signaling perspective. A careful empirical analysis will give researchers and practitioners the tools and evidence to aid <sup>fi</sup>rms in their ongoing attempts to maximize the effectiveness of their social media engagement strategies.

The information systems literature provides a high level direction on content strategies associated with <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated contents (FGCs), information asymmetry, and consumer behavior and engagement. It includes broadcasting client-focused postings (Miller and Tucker 2013), delivering compelling content (Culnan et al. 2010), and providing quality content while fostering member embeddedness (Porter and Donthu 2008). This stream of literature has only recently begun to delve into what speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of FGCs is associated with consumer engagement in brand communities (Lee et al. 2018a, Bapna et al. 2019). Overall, our study adds to the literature around FGCs, signaling theory, and information asymmetry phenomenon on social media.

Several studies in the past have examined the sig naling role of third-party af<sup>fi</sup>liations (Gulati and Hig gins 2003). In addition, several other types of information technology-supported cues (such as technology or users-oriented features incorporated in online plat forms/websites) are used by the <sup>fi</sup>rms as signaling sources to enhance user perception and experience (Cenfetelli et al. 2008, Li et al. 2015, Chen et al. 2017, Qiu et al. 2021). For example, improving website quality, displaying simple features, such as signs or a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s logo, incorporating a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s address, or interactive features (such as live chat) on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s online platform are all signaling sources (Wells et al. 2011, Mavlanova et al. 2012). However, none of these stud ies has looked into the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag. Though the signals through these cues help consumers make positive perceptions about the <sup>fi</sup>rm and improve their experience, they may not be treated as authentic, reliable, or credible as trademarked hashtags as they do not require scrutiny from any credible and impartial sources. Our results show that <sup>fi</sup>rms’ intellectual assets on social media (i.e., trademarked hashtags) act as credible quality signals in social media engagement strategies.

Note that to understand the importance of trademarking hashtags, one should understand the difference between trademarks and copyrights, as they are commonly misunderstood and confused. Both forms of protection do not require registration. However, for the owner to have maximum legal weight, they must be registered. Copyrights protect the intellectual property created in a <sup>fi</sup>xed or tangible form, such as books, arts, or recorded music.<sup>5</sup> In most cases, names, titles, catchphrases, familiar symbols, methods, discoveries, and public domain cannot be copyrighted. Trademarks <sup>fi</sup>ll in the gaps where copyrights do not.

The common term trademarking also refers to another protection called a service mark. Trademarks are used in the protection of goods, whereas service marks protect services. Unlike copyrights, trademarks have the potential to never expire as long as the owner continues to pay annual fees. Note that the mark can only be considered if it “functions as an identi<sup>fi</sup>er of the source of the applicant’s goods or services.”<sup>6</sup> This de<sup>fi</sup>nition means if the hashtag contains words or a phrase that is too generic, it will likely be rejected for trademark protection (TMEP 2021). Based on the case Eksouzian vs. Albanese<sup>7</sup> where the court deemed hashtags as descriptive devices rather than trademarks (and thus cannot be protected), one may argue that the actual legal protections are somewhat dubious. In our private conversation with Professor Alexandra J. Roberts, a Professor of Intellectual Property at the University of New Hampshire School of Law, she kindly provided her endorsement on the legal protection of trademarking hashtags as follows: “I would, of course, disagree with the assertion that ‘actual legal protections are somewhat dubious.’.” Her detailed quote is provided in Online Appendix E.

## 1.2. Research Questions

Although “using trademarks” has been widely studied, the literature has mainly focused on the role of trademarking brands. Past literature on trademarks provides a positive association of trademarks with a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s degree of advancement regarding innovation (Schmoch and Gauch 2009), marketing (Mendonc¸a et al. 2004), new product or service development (Helmers and Rogers 2011), productivity growth, and stock market value (Greenhalgh and Rogers 2006). The ability to build a sustainable brand on social media through trademarks can be one of the most crucial success factors for a <sup>fi</sup>rm (Aaker 2004). Also, trademarks assist <sup>fi</sup>rms in attaining a competitive advantage by differentiating their products or services from their competitors (Besen and Raskind 1991).

We examine a particular form of <sup>fi</sup>rm-side social media signaling strategy—trademarking hashtags— which has not been addressed in past research. Signal ing theory provides a framework for understanding how extrinsic cues—signals—can be used by <sup>fi</sup>rms to convey service or product quality information to consumers, thereby reducing information asymmetry and driving purchase decisions (Wells et al. 2011). Our study draws on signaling theory and examines the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag on informa tion asymmetry measured through social media engagement. To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed the signaling effects of trademarking a hashtag. To systematically bridge this research gap, we begin by asking our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question: (1) What is the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag on a firm’s social media audience engagement (i.e., effectiveness)?

Drawing on the prior literature (Wells et al. 2011, Jeng 2016, Yang and Yuan 2019, Faurel et al. 2021, Hsu et al. 2022), we expect that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s act of trademarking a hashtag serves as a credible signal of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s seriousness and commitment on social media. The signaling effect of a trademarked hashtag helps reduce information asymmetry (increase audience engagement) by protecting consumers from confusion about social media promotions conducted by similar, competing companies. It also protects businesses by helping maintain the brand exclusivity they have worked hard to build. Therefore, we may observe a positive effect of trademarking a hashtag on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media audience engagement. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the act of trademarking a hashtag can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media audience engagement (retweets). A careful empirical analysis will aid <sup>fi</sup>rms in their ongoing attempts to maximize the effectiveness of their social media engagement strategies. In addition, an empirical challenge of examining our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question lies in establishing the causal effect of signaling through trademarking hashtags due to self-selection bias. Using several causal identi<sup>fi</sup>cations strategies, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to provide an empirically driven, comprehensive understanding of trademarking hashtags.

Second, after comprehensively examining the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media audience engagement, it is natural to take a step further and investigate how the magnitude of the effects may differ among different types of <sup>fi</sup>rms. We investigate the heterogeneity of the signaling effect of trademarking hashtags in a social media context through the lens of the signaling theory. In general, the signaling effect of credibility carried by trademarking is more critical for one type of <sup>fi</sup>rm versus another (Cao and Hsu 2011). The strength of sig nals depends on the level of information asymmetry:

Signals have more informational value (the effect of signaling becomes larger) when the information asymmetry is higher (Connelly et al. 2011, Hottenrott et al. 2016). In our context, consumers might not be familiar with <sup>fi</sup>rms with fewer Twitter followers, including their products and services, because of high information asymmetry. Therefore, the positive effect of signaling through trademarking a hashtag may be larger for <sup>fi</sup>rms with fewer Twitter followers. Note that the number of Twitter followers may indicate a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s efforts invested in social media. We use the number of Twitter followers to measure how consumers are familiar with the <sup>fi</sup>rm. If a <sup>fi</sup>rm has a large number of Twitter followers, it indicates that consumers are more familiar with the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The reason can be that the <sup>fi</sup>rm invests more in social media or has a larger size. Actually, <sup>fi</sup>rm size and social media efforts are strongly correlated. Prior studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that the larger <sup>fi</sup>rms with larger market capital expend more effort on social media and engage in communication and gain popularity (Xiong et al. 2018, Xiong et al. 2019).

On the other hand, one could also argue that the signal strength of the positive effect of trademarking a hashtag may be larger for <sup>fi</sup>rms with more Twitter followers. For these <sup>fi</sup>rms, the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag might be limited to reducing information asymmetry because these <sup>fi</sup>rms and their social media accounts are already well known (Yang and Yuan 2019). Both scenarios are theoretically plausible; the dominating effect may depend on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of <sup>fi</sup>rm, thereby requiring empirical investigation. Therefore, we ask our second research question: (2) Does the magnitude of the signaling effect of trademarking hashtags differ among firms with different levels of Twitter followers (i.e., heterogeneity)?

We empirically <sup>fi</sup>nd that the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial impact of trademarking a hashtag is not uniform across all types of <sup>fi</sup>rms, which suggests that the signaling effect of trademarking hashtags can be particularly important for certain types of <sup>fi</sup>rms. The positive impact of trademarking hashtags on social media engagement is only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for <sup>fi</sup>rms with severe information asymmetry problems (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>rms with a fewer number of followers). Therefore, the effect of signaling through a trademark might be more important for these <sup>fi</sup>rms because consumers have limited knowledge (because of high information asymmetry) about them (Yang and Yuan 2019).

Next, we empirically investigate how a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s use of social media to communicate with consumers impacts their social media effectiveness after an expected signaling event—trademarking a hashtag. It is important to understand not only what <sup>fi</sup>rms express, but the way in which <sup>fi</sup>rms express themselves on social media has some consequential (action-based) effects on the audience. What writing style, based on psychological constructs (linguistic characteristics), of the FGCs prompts the audience to retweet after a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s act of trademarking a hashtag? We examine this mechanism from the lens of speech act theory, which has been adopted in a wide variety of domains, such as information systems, consumer behavior, and marketing (Ludwig et al. 2016, Aleti et al. 2019, Villarroel et al. 2019, Fan and Ilk 2020).

The speech act theory deals with how words are used not only to present information but also to carry out actions (Austin 1975). The speech act, that is, the act of utterance of a word (FGC in our context), is typ ically divided into three categories: locutionary act, illocutionary act, and perlocutionary act. A locutionary act is an act of utterance that is used to simply express something. Illocution is a speech act where one presents intent through tone, attitude, feelings, or emotions to achieve something. Perlocution is a persuasion-based speech act that has some consequential effects on the audience. Through the lens of speech act theory, we assess the role of speci<sup>fi</sup>c linguistic styles of <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated tweets after trademarking hashtags. We develop linguistic styles based on psychological constructs (using speech act theory) that are correlated with the perlocutionary effect on the audience, which, in turn, persuade the audience to further share information by retweeting. We then ask our third research question: (3) What key linguistic characteristics (based on writing style) are associated with an increase in a firm’s social media audience engagement after trademarking a hashtag?

We draw on the prior literature and use the following key constructs: (i) Emotionally Charged FGCs, (ii) Narrative Style FGCs, and (iii) Inclusive and Con<sup>fi</sup>- dent Style FGCs. The usage of highly charged words in writing activates strong emotions, provokes consumers, and causes perlocution by sharing the contents frequently and widely (Rime´ 2009). Drawing on the literature on the news advertising and persuasive effect of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ interaction with consumers using a positive emotional tone (Berger and Milkman 2012, Araujo et al. 2015, Wang et al. 2021), we expect that emotionally charged words (positive) cause a perlocutionary effect (retweet) on social media. Narrative writing style involves the adoption of a storytelling style and using more descriptive words in writing. Consistent with the literature (Escalas 2007) on viral news content using a narrative style, we expect that the use of narrative writing style arouses excitement among consumers and subsequently persuades them to retweet. Also, with inclusiveness and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in writing style, the audience typically feels more empowered and knowledgeable and subsequently feels proud in sharing/diffusing the FGCs (Kim et al. 2016). Overall, we expect that the act of trademarking a hashtag makes the frequency and writing style (Clout/Inclusive, Narrative, and Positive Style) of FGC more critical and can amplify the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effects (perlocutionary effect) of writing more tweets with desirable linguistic styles.

Finally, linguistic styles of writing tweets may have both contemporaneous and lagged effects (Friedman 2011). It is important to examine the relationship between writing tweets with desirable linguistic features and the duration its effect lasts. The effect of writing more tweets with desirable linguistic styles may or may not be short-lived. Therefore, we ask our fourth research question: (4) What are the contemporaneous and lagged relationships between writing tweets with desirable linguistic styles and social media audience engagement after trademarking hashtags? The answer to this question provides insights into how long the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effect of speci<sup>fi</sup>c engagement strategies on social media may last after hashtags have been trademarked.

We expect that before trademarking hashtags, the effect of writing more tweets with desirable linguistic styles is short-lived; only the contemporaneous effect is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (Friedman 2011). One month later, the lagged effect may no longer be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. On the other hand, after trademarking hashtags, we see a correlation between writing more tweets with desirable linguistic styles and a longer duration of its effect. Therefore, corresponding to our research questions 3 and 4, we expect that after trademarking a hashtag, the ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed effects of writing tweets with desirable linguistic features may not only be more intense (answer to research question 3) but may also last longer (answer to research question 4).

## 1.3. Key Contributions

Our paper contributes to the literature on signaling theory, FGCs, and social strategy by showing the positive effect the act of trademarking a hashtag can have on social media audience engagement. We also highlight how the magnitude of the signaling (positive) strength varies by the number of followers associated with a <sup>fi</sup>rm because of information asymmetry. Our study also contributes to the speech act communication theory by testing how the adoption of speci<sup>fi</sup>c linguistic styles (based on psychological construct) can have a long-term perlocutionary effect on consumer behavior, hence in<sup>fl</sup>uencing them to further share information on social media through retweets.

With respect to contribution to practice, although it is costly to register, monitor, and renew a trademarked hashtag in the long run, the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effects of trademarking can still exist and help <sup>fi</sup>rms promote and differentiate products from the competition on social media. If business decision makers do not understand the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effects of trademarking hashtags and do not have a good estimate about the magnitude of those effects, they will not invest suf<sup>fi</sup> cient resources (underinvestment) in trademarking and managing hashtags.

Note that <sup>fi</sup>rms may apply for a trademark on a hashtag according to their business intuitions in practice. However, the cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis of trademarking a hashtag is still in its infancy. In business practice, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s credible signaling through trademarking hashtags is directly related to the causal effect on social media engagement and is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to estimate. Without estimating the magnitude of the causal effect of trademarking hashtags on social media engagement, the signaling effect of trademarking hashtags may not be optimal (overinvestment or underinvestment). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the efforts and subsequent bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from using a trademarked hashtag might not be the same among different <sup>fi</sup>rms, especially <sup>fi</sup>rms with a smaller number of Twitter followers. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for the social media strategies of certain types of <sup>fi</sup>rms. As part of managerial implications, our study provides guidelines, through the lens of signaling theory and speech act theory, on how <sup>fi</sup>rms generate FGCs and communicate on social media become even more relevant after trademarking hashtags. Also, we provide insights into not only how social media engagement strategies can amplify social media effectiveness after hashtags have been trademarked but also how long the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effects may last. In summary, our study helps advance understanding of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of trademarking hashtags and answers questions that are important to a wide range of stakeholders.

## 2. Literature Review

Our work is related to the following streams of literature: (i) Social media signaling and its impact on consumer behavior, (ii) content development and sharing strategies in social media, and (iii) hashtags and trademarking. In this section, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review each stream of literature and highlight our contributions. Our main contributions to literature are summarized in Figure D.2 in Online Appendix D.

## 2.1. Social Media Signaling and Its Impact on Consumer Behavior

There are mainly two different ways by which contents are generated and consumed by consumers and <sup>fi</sup>rms on social media: user-generated content (UGC) and <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated content (Kumar et al. 2016, Qiu and Kumar 2017, Kumar and Qiu 2022). UGC represents consumer-to-consumer communication or consumer-to-business communication. Consumer participation in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media effort increases the intensity of the relationship between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and its customers and leads to an increase in the frequency of customer visits (Rishika et al. 2013). As extensively documented in the literature, many studies have focused on the effect of UGC on key metrics, including sales (Rui et al. 2013, Dewan and Ramaprasad 2014) and user engagement, such as content diffusion on social media (Susarla et al. 2012, Qiu et al. 2015). By participating in the <sup>fi</sup>rm-initiated social media site, customers can voice their opinions and share experiences about the <sup>fi</sup>rm with other customers as well as the <sup>fi</sup>rm itself (Agarwal et al. 2009). Rishika et al. (2013) examine consumer transaction data with UGC on social media. Using a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s Facebook page, Goh et al. (2013) discover a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated data and usergenerated data on consumer purchase expenditures. Our study differs from this stream of research by focusing on the impact of FGC on the effectiveness of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media signaling strategy.

Firms are increasingly engaging with customers on social media by generating FGC, and its use on social media platforms is important because it in<sup>fl</sup>uences how consumers view the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Firms regularly interact with their customers through social media pro<sup>fi</sup>les, such as Facebook pages and Twitter accounts. Their use of Twitter to disseminate information has been widely reported in the literature. Chen et al. (2015) examine how <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated messages drive additional sales through a change in consumer behavior. He et al. (2017) explore <sup>fi</sup>rms’ social media strategies in product-harm crises. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they study how nonfocal <sup>fi</sup>rms adjust their joint social media strategies (offensive and defensive marketing) after a major unexpected incident. Ma et al. (2015) <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fi</sup>rm interaction with customer compliments and complaints on Twitter results in improved customer relationships but at the same time encourages more complaints later. Kumar et al. (2016) investigate the effect of FGC on social media on consumer behavior and spending. Chung et al. (2020) examine the impact of <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated social media responses to different types of consumer messages on the market performance of the <sup>fi</sup>rms. Lee et al. (2018b) study the relationship between different types of <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated advertising content and consumer behavior, such as the number of likes and comments.

Increasing engagement with customers via FGC can lead to enhanced customer loyalty and trust (Algesheimer et al. 2005) as well as better interactions and deeper connections between consumers and <sup>fi</sup>rms. A more active and vibrant community with regular messaging creates trust and allows customers to infer the level of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s commitment to bolstering the customer-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationship. Among different engagement measures, we follow Kumar et al. (2010), Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson (2013), Shi et al. (2014), Mallipeddi et al. (2021), and Mallipeddi et al. (2022) and use the number of retweets (content sharing) as a performance metric for social media audience engagement. Retweeting re<sup>fl</sup>ects the active participation critical for businesses seeking to engage consumers as the information disseminated in each retweet exposes a new or larger audience to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s content. Kumar et al. (2010) point out that sharing a brand’s social media content creates engagement and ampli<sup>fi</sup>es the impact of the original content. Shi et al. (2014) also argue that content sharing (retweeting) is a critical mechanism of information diffusion in social broadcasting networks and is vital to a network’s proper functioning and thriving. According to Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson’s (2013, p. 591) “ladder of participation” paradigm, content sharing is a very high level of online community participation and endorsement. Marketing practitioners also acknowledge that retweeting intensity is an appropriate measure of audience engagement on Twitter (Long 2015).

We extend the prior literature on FGC and consumer behavior to understand the impact of trademarking hashtags on consumer engagement in online brand communities. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we focus on one speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of <sup>fi</sup>rm-side social media signaling strategy, trademarking hashtags. This has not been addressed in past research and is very important because an increasing number of <sup>fi</sup>rms have been applying for trademarks on hashtags (Davey 2016). Prior literature has investigated the role of trademarks as a signal to protect intellectual properties, differentiate their products, promote brands, strengthen shareholder value, showcase future product releases, and realize higher stock returns in various ways (Keller 1993, Davidson 2004, Morrin et al. 2006, Flikkema et al. 2019, Faurel et al. 2021, Hsu et al. 2022). Our work aims to contribute to the information systems literature by extending the literature on signaling theory (in social media) and consumer behavior by examining the effect of trademarking a hashtag on social media engagement (retweets). Because signals have a stronger effect when the information asymmetry is higher (Connelly et al. 2011, Hottenrott et al. 2016, Khurana et al. 2019), we further examine the heterogeneity effect, that is, whether the signaling strength of trademarking a hashtag differs among <sup>fi</sup>rms with different levels of Twitter followers because of the information asymmetry phenomenon. A careful empirical analysis will give researchers and practitioners the tools and evidence to aid <sup>fi</sup>rms in their ongoing attempts to maximize the effectiveness of their social media engagement strategies.

## 2.2. Content Development and Sharing Strategies in Social Media

Prior information systems literature provides a highlevel direction on content strategies associated with engagement, including client-focused postings (Miller and Tucker 2013), a continuous supply of compelling content (Culnan et al. 2010), and providing quality content and fostering member embeddedness (Porter and Donthu 2008). This stream of literature has only recently begun to delve into what speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of content is associated with consumer behavior and engagement in brand communities. Lee et al. (2018a) <sup>fi</sup>nd that posts related to brand personality (e.g., humor, emotional appeals, casual banter, philanthropic efforts) have a positive effect on consumer behavior, whereas informative content (that can be used to optimize purchasing decisions, for example, price, availability, promotions, and product facts) by itself is associated with lower levels of engagement. An essential question in this research stream is what types of <sup>fi</sup>rm posts are associated with engagement in online brand communities.

Our paper contributes to the literature by investigating the role of trademarking hashtags: in particular, how <sup>fi</sup>rm posts with a trademarked hashtag are associated with engagement in online brand communities. Prior literature (e.g., Ransbotham and Gallaugher 2010) suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms use posts as a means of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the behavior of others. One way in which <sup>fi</sup>rms can introduce this in<sup>fl</sup>uence is through the use of speech act communications theory. We explain the key drivers or speech act style (psychology-based linguistic characteristics) that cause trademarking hashtags to increase a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s audience engagement through the lens of speech act theory (Austin 1975) and linguistic features (Lee et al. 2015) in social media.

Prior studies in information systems, consumer behavior, and marketing have relied on the emerging literature on the application of speech act theory and psychology-based linguistic features in social media (Ludwig et al. 2016, Aleti et al. 2019, Villarroel et al. 2019, Fan and Ilk 2020). Lee et al. (2015) develop linguistics-based, quality-level indicators to assess the content quality of user-generated textual content. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that the use of certain linguistic features, such as pronouns and other function words, is correlated with the content’s perceived quality. Several studies have used linguistics-based features to detect deception (fake reviews) on social media. Ludwig et al. (2016) use texts of archived email exchanges to derive linguistic cues and detect deception in computermediated communication. Ho et al. (2016) create spontaneous deception scenarios in an interactive online game environment and identify language-action cues (e.g., cognitive load, affective process, latency, and wordiness) that reveal patterns of information behavior manifested by deceivers in online communication. To the best of our knowledge, using psychologybased linguistic features and speech act theory to examine the impact of trademarking a hashtag is a new and important area of research and has not been explored in the prior literature.

## 2.3. Hashtags and Trademarking

The hashtag, also known as the “pound sign,” has revolutionized the way information is organized, discovered, and shared online. The idea of using “#” as an indicator for groups of posts was formally adopted by Twitter in 2009. Since then, the hashtag’s use began to grow, exploding in 2011, the year Instagram started hyperlinking hashtags. Fifty percent of all Super Bowl commercials contained a hashtag by 2013; in the same year, Facebook supported the use of hashtags via hyperlinks (Kolowich 2014). Hashtags are most prevalent on Twitter and Instagram, the platforms that revolutionized their use (Butwin 2016); but they are also frequently used on many other platforms, such as Facebook and Pinterest. Businesses have realized the power of hashtags as a way to steer other users toward posts about a particular topic or idea (<sup>fi</sup>rm generated content) on social media.

Hashtags facilitate searches by grouping topics of interest into keyword collections. Firms use hashtags as a social media version of trademarks to promote products and services, share news, and engage with consumers on social media platforms. Several studies have investigated the effect of trademarks on <sup>fi</sup>rms critical measures. For example, through trademarking in general, a <sup>fi</sup>rm conveys information regarding new products, services, marketing, and brand awareness efforts and intent to build trust and in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer behavior (Crass et al. 2019, Faurel et al. 2021, Yang and Yuan 2019). However, trademarking hashtags is a relatively new and important phenomenon. There are a few notable legal studies from a different stream of literature (Arthur 2014, Falconer 2016, Chu 2017, McKelvey and Grady 2017, p. 90). Chu (2017, p. 413) studies the trademark protection for hashtags and concludes, “Competitors, who use the hashtags to mislead consumers, pose a serious threat to the repu tation of companies who have the authority to use the hashtags.” They urge practitioners of trademark law not to lag behind the curve. Similarly, Curtin (2010) stresses the need for congressional legislation to prevent abuses like cybersquatting, the practice of capitalizing on public deception by owning domain names very similar to those of large companies (e.g., citibank.org versus citybank.org). McKelvey and Grady (2017) favor strict enforcement of trademarks; but they also call attention to “the risk in sti<sup>fl</sup>ing conversations stimulated by hashtags,” which “may call for more innovative tactics to achieve brand protection and consumer protection goals.” Another article, which uses sports as a case study, recognizes the speed with which damage can be done to a player or team brand, leading the authors to call for more consistency in the court rulings about trademarks in the social media realm (Arthur 2014). In fact, they deem social media to be inherently commercial, representing their vote that hashtags can be trademarked just like any other word or phrase. However, <sup>fi</sup>rms’ social media strategies will be different after an expected event—trademarking a hashtag. We <sup>fi</sup>ll this important gap by extending the prior literature on trademarks in a social media context: investigating the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media audience engagement.

## 3. Data Collection, Data Description, and Summary Statistics

We have collected data from multiple sources, processed it further, and derived key features to use in our empirical analysis. In this section, we explain the data collection process and data description in detail.

## 3.1. Data Collection

The USPTO maintains an extensive database of trademarks dating back to 1884. This data set stores detailed information on millions of trademark applications and registrations of individuals, groups, and businesses seeking intellectual property protection. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it maintains information on trademark contents, ownership, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, date of <sup>fi</sup>ling, registration, renewal or abandonment, the name of examining attorneys who examine the trademark applications, and so on.

We use the Trademark Electronic Search System supported by the USPTO. We search for the keywords “hashtag” or the pound sign symbol “#” in the entire trademark data sets for potential matches. We retrieve the following information for each trademark: serial number, registration number, year of registration, owner name, trademark description, and status (live or canceled). We process these data and include only trademarks that are marked as “registered” in the period from August 2013 to December 2017. Applications under review or rejected are excluded from our data construction as well. Also, we exclude trademarked hashtags that were assigned to individual applicants instead of to <sup>fi</sup>rms.

We complement our treated <sup>fi</sup>rms (that trademark their hashtags) with similar control <sup>fi</sup>rms (that do not trademark their hashtags). To <sup>fi</sup>nd these similar <sup>fi</sup>rms, we <sup>fi</sup>rst use Google’s “similar” feature, which appears in each search result. This feature recommends web pages or companies that are similar to the search result one has chosen (in our case, the website of one of our treated <sup>fi</sup>rms), which makes control and treated <sup>fi</sup>rms belong to the same industry. We then manually verify that the website of this similar <sup>fi</sup>rm recommended using this feature is indeed similar to our focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, meaning that it offers similar products or services. We perform this step as an extra level of veri-<sup>fi</sup>cation for our control group.

We manually identify the website of each <sup>fi</sup>rm that has registered a hashtag. From the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s website, we identify the Twitter account of each <sup>fi</sup>rm. We extract data from each <sup>fi</sup>rm’s of<sup>fi</sup>cial Twitter account before and after the approval of trademarking hashtags. In our study, we focus on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ social media campaigns: hashtags created by <sup>fi</sup>rms’ of<sup>fi</sup>cial Twitter accounts. We harvest each <sup>fi</sup>rm’s tweets, likes received per tweet, comments received per tweet, and retweets received per tweet. Note that we do not make a distinction in terms of who retweeted a speci<sup>fi</sup>c tweet and use a count of all the retweets posted against a particular tweet in this study. We further process the tweets by removing special characters, links, punctuation, stop words, and leading and lagging blank spaces. In total, we have 163,655 tweets in our data set. We then combine <sup>fi</sup>rm trademarked hashtag level information with the <sup>fi</sup>rm-level tweet data. Our data set consists of <sup>fi</sup>rms at the intersection of the trademark database and being active on Twitter.

## 3.2. Data Description

We have 27 treated <sup>fi</sup>rms and 75 control <sup>fi</sup>rms in our study. We have used data of the 12-month period before and after a <sup>fi</sup>rm trademarked a hashtag. The <sup>fi</sup>rms included in our study vary greatly by industry type. They include beauty/cosmetic products (L'Oreal´ USA), apparel (Hanna Andersson), fast food (Charleys), furniture (Crate and Barrel), pharmaceutical products (GenexaHealth), consulting (Chasse Consulting), food and beverage (West Pak Avocado), nonpro<sup>fi</sup>ts for social goods (Girls Inc. of the Paci<sup>fi</sup>c Northwest, JugLife, Marine Corps Marathon Organization, Champions for Kids), automotive parts, jewelry/ charms (RembrandtCharms and Monica Rich Kosann), and sports products (Taylor Made and Powersports). The inclusion of <sup>fi</sup>rms from multiple industries in our data set enhances the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Next, we describe the features we have developed and used in our study.

3.2.1. Features Development. Using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC), a computerized text analysis tool (Pennebaker et al. 2015), we operationalize the psychological construct we have de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Section 1.2 and develop the following features: Tone, Analytic, and Clout. In LIWC, these summary variables are derived from <sup>fi</sup>ndings and measures in the previous linguistic and psychology literature (Cohn et al. 2004, Kacewicz et al. 2014, Pennebaker et al. 2014). LIWC is a state-of-the-art psycholinguistic package that relies on an internal, default dictionary that de<sup>fi</sup>nes which words should be counted in the target text <sup>fi</sup>les. It can be used to analyze the linguistic features of texts by calculating percentages of words that belong to different word categories, each category re<sup>fl</sup>ecting different language and psycholinguistic dimensions of a written text. These categories include linguistic processes (such as function words’ usage and verb tenses), affective processes (such as positive words and negative words), cognitive processes (such as tentative, inclusive, and exclusive words), and many other words categories. LIWC has been widely used in various linguistic and psychological studies (Tausczik and Pennebaker 2010). LIWC is particularly helpful in our Twitter setting because it incorporates informal language that is common in Twitter and Facebook posts. For example, “:)” is coded as a positive emotion word. We describe the LIWC linguistic-based summary variables we use in this study.

Tone: We operationalize another psychological construct emotionally charged style with the help of a LIWC summary variable—Tone. The tone re<sup>fl</sup>ects a person’s optimism or pessimism using positive and negative emotion words (Cohn et al. 2004). The range of the variable Tone varies from 0–100. Using emotion-based dictionaries, LIWC assigns a low Tone score to the text with greater use of emotionally negative charged words. On the other hand, it assigns a high score to the text with greater use of emotionally positively charged words.

Analytic: We operationalize another psychological construct, narrative style, using another LIWC-based summary variable—Analytic. Analytic measures whether people write in an analytic, logical, or more intuitive and narrative style. Analytical thinking is indicated by the greater use of nouns, articles, and prepositions. Low analytic thinking (or narrative, informal thinking) is associated with fewer pronouns, auxiliary verbs, and common adverbs (Jurafsky et al. 2009, Pennebaker et al. 2014). LIWC Analytic scores range from 0–100 with higher scores indicating more analytic thinking and lower scores indicating more narrative thinking (Pennebaker et al. 2014).

Clout: Using LIWC, we quantitatively operationalize inclusive writing with con<sup>fi</sup>dence, leadership, and authoritative style with a summary variable called Clout (Pennebaker et al. 2015). The summary variable Clout is derived from several past research studies that relate the use of pronouns as a symbol of rank within the social hierarchy (Kacewicz et al. 2014). Its score (i.e., standardized scores that have been converted to percent) ranges from 0–100. A higher Clout score represents a highly con<sup>fi</sup>dent writing style with more usage of we-words (e.g., we, our) and social words (e.g., friend, ally, group). On the other hand, a low Clout score represents a more uncertain and doubtful writing style with more usage of I-words (e.g., my, mine) and swear words (Kacewicz et al. 2014).

It is worth noting that constructing the variables Analytic and Clout are mainly based on the count of function words, which is different from the conventional text analysis approach where only content words and keywords are used to analyze texts (Lee et al. 2015).

3.2.2. Text-Based Control Variables. To control for unobserved heterogeneity due to text-based key characteristics, following Aleti et al. (2019), we control for the following variables: log(WC), the log mean number of word count per tweet; log(WPS), the log mean number of words per sentence; and Sixltr, the mean percent of words in the text that are longer than six letters.

We address the issue of multicollinearity in regression equations by looking at the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIFs). The VIF quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the severity of multicollinearity in a regression. When the independent variables are correlated, the estimated standard errors of the <sup>fi</sup>tted coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are in<sup>fl</sup>ated, which can be used to check for the presence of multicollinearity (Chatterjee and Hadi 2012). In the absence of any linear relationship among independent variable j and other independent variables, VIF would be one. The deviation of the VIF value from one indicates departure from orthogonality and tendency toward collinearity. VIFs are widely used in the information systems literature to establish if multicollinearity is an issue (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013, Singh et al. 2014, Bapna et al. 2019, Khurana et al. 2019). Most prior studies rely on informal rules of thumb applied to the VIF. For instance, in Chatterjee and Hadi (2012, p. 250), “values of variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors greater than 10 are often taken as a signal that the data have collinearity problems.” We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the VIFs associated with each variable are less than two, which is well below the conventionally accepted threshold (VIF <10), indicating that multicollinearity is not an issue in our model and does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Note that we use <sup>fi</sup>rm-month as our unit of analysis instead of <sup>fi</sup>rm-hashtag to avoid a data sparsity issue. Data sparsity is an issue in which a relatively high per centage of a variable or a set of variables do not contain actual data. In our case, many hashtags posted by <sup>fi</sup>rms may have been tweets or liked in a particular month. In other words, if we construct the data using <sup>fi</sup>rmhashtag as our unit of analysis, the vast majority of observations might be zero, which is an inef<sup>fi</sup>cient rare event data set pointed out by King and Zeng (2001). From an estimation perspective, because of data spar sity issues, the estimated effects are usually biased in nature (King and Zeng 2001, Greenland et al. 2016).

Prior literature suggests several techniques, including data aggregation methods, to overcome or mitigate biased estimates issues due to data sparseness. For example, Hwangbo and Kim (2017) use several aggregation functions, such as average and sum, in recommendation systems settings where data sparsity is a major challenge. In another study, Bhowmik et al. (2016) show that the parameter estimation from aggregated data are more accurate than the parameter estimation obtained from nonaggregated or “individual” samples. Following the literature focusing on data sparseness, we aggregate our data set at the <sup>fi</sup>rmmonth level in this study.

4.1.2. Causal Identification Strategies in Addressing Endogeneity Concerns. To address different endogeneity mechanisms of self-selected hashtag trademarking, we use various identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies. If the selection process is unknown, we have two cases with different identi<sup>fi</sup>cation assumptions: (i) the selection process is driven by observable characteristics (observable to researchers) and (ii) the selection process is driven by unobserved characteristics. In case (i), we use a combination of the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model with the propensity score matching (PSM) approach to correct the possible endogeneity (see Section 4.1.3). In case (ii), we address the endogeneity concern by using a quasiexperimental design (see Section 4.1.4), which is essentially the Look-Ahead Propensity Score Matching (LA PSM; see Bapna et al. 2018). We validate the parallel trends assumption in Section 4.1.5.

4.1.3. Propensity Score Matching. To avoid model dependence, we also consider a <sup>fi</sup>xed effects regression model combined with matching techniques, such as propensity score matching. Following Goh et al. (2013), Li (2016), Chen et al. (2021), and Pan and Qiu (2022), we <sup>fi</sup>rst create a “proper” control group for treated <sup>fi</sup>rms by using PSM. We ensure that the control and treated groups are comparable in terms of observable characteristics. Then, we run the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects regression Equation (1). In this regression, for the treated <sup>fi</sup>rms, we use the observations before and after trademarking hashtags.

In the matching process, the treated <sup>fi</sup>rms are the ones that trademark hashtags. As stated, we have 27 treated <sup>fi</sup>rms and 75 control <sup>fi</sup>rms in our research study. Using PSM, we match each treated <sup>fi</sup>rm to the most “similar” control <sup>fi</sup>rm (closest propensity score, one-to-one matching) in terms of the following characteristics: (i) the number of days that <sup>fi</sup>rm i has joined Twitter, (ii) the number of followers/following/ tweets indicated on <sup>fi</sup>rm i’s Twitter account at the beginning of our sample period, and (iii) the industry the <sup>fi</sup>rm belongs to. Therefore, in the matched sample, we have 27 treated <sup>fi</sup>rms and 27 control <sup>fi</sup>rms.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst sort all <sup>fi</sup>rms in a random order to make sure that the ordering does not affect the subsequent matching. Then, we run a logit regression based on the pretreatment variables mentioned earlier and obtain the predicted propensity scores. We use the nearest neighbor-matching algorithm in which each treated unit is matched with the control unit with the closest propensity score. In order to assess the quality of matching, we perform t-tests of equality of means before and after the matching to check whether our PSM adequately balances characteristics between the treatment and the control group units. The results are presented in Table 4.

In Table 4, there is clear evidence of covariate imbalance between groups before matching. After matching, most of the differences of mean are no longer statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting that matching helps reduce the bias associated with the observable characteristics. We can see that the covariate imbalance has been greatly reduced after matching. After matching, only one characteristic, the number of following, is not completely balanced and the imbalanced degree of the number of following is reduced. Therefore, in general, we think the matching is successful. If we only use matching, the imbalanced characteristic will bias our estimation. However, our analysis does not depend only on the matching method. After we obtain the matched sample, we use a <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model to analyze the impact of trademarking hashtags. Because the number of following at the beginning of our sample period (the imbalanced characteristic) is a timeinvariant variable, it has been absorbed into the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects and been controlled in the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model.

Common support or overlap condition is a critical assumption in matching. The prior matching literature (Heinrich et al. 2010) suggests that checking the overlap or region of common support between treatment and control groups can be done through a visual inspection of the propensity score distributions for both the treatment and control groups. 4.1.4. A Quasi-Experimental Design (Fixed Effects Model Combined with LA-PSM). The gold standard of establishing convincing causality is to run randomized experiments. In our context, an ideal experiment is to randomly assign <sup>fi</sup>rms into a control or treatment group (whether they trademark hashtags or not). However, randomized trials are not always available because they are expensive. Following similar identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation ideas to those in Bapna et al. (2018) and Wang et al. (2018), we use a quasi-experimental design to account for the differences between control and treatment groups caused by unobserved characteristics.

The intuition of our quasi-experiment research design is to exploit the time sequence of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ decisions on trademarking hashtags and identify a more similar control group in terms of unobserved characteristics. For example, an econometric challenge in Garg et al. (2011) and Wang et al. (2018) is to separate causal peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence from homophily (the tendency of individuals to associate with similar others). Empirically, it is not dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd that a focal user’s behavior is more likely to be correlated with his or her online friends’ behaviors than with anonymous users. However, this empirical <sup>fi</sup>nding can be driven by either causal peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence or homophily. The dif<sup>fi</sup>- culty of establishing causal inference of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence is a result of lacking a proper control group. A focal user’s friends (treatment group) can be very different from anonymous users (control group) in many characteristics.

To ensure similarity between control and treatment groups, Wang et al. (2018) identify a proper control group by observing new social ties formed in a future time period: A proper control unit is a person who is not the focal user’s online friend now but will be his or her online friend at a future point. This quasiexperiment research design ensures that the treated units and the control units are similar in unobserved characteristics. Their identi<sup>fi</sup>cation intuition also applies to our context. Firms that trademark hashtags (treated units) can be very different from those that do not trademark hashtags (control units) in terms of observable and unobserved characteristics. We can use the same approach as in Bapna et al. (2018) and Wang et al. (2018) to identify a better control group. Basically, a better control unit could be a <sup>fi</sup>rm that has not trademarked hashtags but will trademark hashtags in the future. Similarly, this quasi-experiment design in our context ensures that the treated <sup>fi</sup>rms and the control <sup>fi</sup>rms are similar in unobserved characteristics.

To account for both observable and unobserved characteristics as best as we can, we adopt the LA-PSM method proposed by Bapna et al. (2018) to identify the proper control group and then we combine it with the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>rst split the whole sample period into two equally long time periods (period I and period II). The treatment group in our quasi-experimental design is the <sup>fi</sup>rms that trademark hashtags in time period I. For each treated unit, we match it to a control unit with the closest propensity score among <sup>fi</sup>rms that have not trademarked hashtags in time period I but will trademark hashtags in time period II. On the one hand, the closest propensity score ensures that the treated and the control units are similar in observable characteristics (the observable characteristics in PSM are the same as those in Section 4.1.3); on the other hand, choosing <sup>fi</sup>rms from those that have not trademarked hashtags in time period I but will trademark hashtags in time period II ensures that the treated and the control units are similar in unobserved characteristics.

After creating the proper control group, we construct a new data sample with treated and control units in time period I. We reestimate our <sup>fi</sup>xed effects regression Equation (1) using the new data sample. The results are presented in column (2) of Table 4 and are consistent with those in the PSM model. The differences in mean before and after LA-PSM is presented in Online Appendix B.

4.1.5. Additional Robustness Checks. If time-variant unobserved confounders cause a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant heterogeneity in the pretreatment trends, it suggests that the pretreatments may disproportionately affect treated units, as opposed to control units and the “parallel path” assumption is less likely to be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed (Angrist and Pischke 2008). In our context, the concern of pretreatment trends arises because <sup>fi</sup>rms’ unobserved efforts in marketing themselves could affect their decisions to trademark hashtags. In addition, there might be reverse causality concerns: A <sup>fi</sup>rm might be more likely to trademark hashtags because of the increased level of social media engagement. In Online Appendix A, we adopt the relative time model proposed by Autor (2003) to address the concerns of reverse causality and pretreatment trends.

We conduct a placebo test to examine if our results could be driven entirely by chance. The idea of the placebo test proposed here is akin to the framework in Bertrand et al. (2004): We estimate pseudocausal effects that are known to be equal to zero based on a priori knowledge. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, following Bertrand et al. (2004), we randomly generate placebo trademarked hashtags, where treated <sup>fi</sup>rms are chosen at random. Because these trademarked hashtags are <sup>fi</sup>ctitious, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of trademarking hashtags at the 5% level (5% signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level) should be found roughly 5% of the time. If in the placebo test, a signi<sup>fi</sup> cant effect at the 5% level is found at a value much larger than 5% of the time, then our interpretation is that our analysis could be driven by the placebo effect and does not provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant evidence of a positive effect of trademarking hashtags. On the other hand, if a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect at the 5% level is found at a value about 5%, then our interpretation is that our analysis provides evidence that the positive effect of trademarking hashtags is not driven by the placebo effect.

Following Bertrand et al. (2004), we reestimate regression Equation (1) using these placebo trademarked hashtags. The estimation generates an estimate of the effect of trademarking hashtags and a standard error for this estimate. We repeat this exercise 1,000 times. Because these trademarked hashtags are <sup>fi</sup>ctitious, we expect to reject the null hypothesis of no effect roughly 5% of the time (50 times). From our results for the 1,000 runs, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the fraction of simulations in which the null hypothesis is rejected is 3.2% (32 times), indicating that our results are unlikely to be driven by the placebo effect.

Additionally, we conduct another robustness check including <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c time trends by estimating a correlated random trend model (Angrist and Pischke 2008). The results are robust and are presented in Online Appendix A.

## 5. Discussion and Managerial Implications

In this study, we contribute to the literature on FGCs, signaling theory, and information asymmetry phenomenon by conducting research surrounded with the act of trademarking hashtags, which has not been fully explored. We believe that this lack thereof has produced an important gap in the literature and hence provides a unique opportunity for further empirical investigation. Our study differs from the broad literature dealing with UGC and FGC. Drawing on the signaling theory, we examine a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of <sup>fi</sup>rm-side (FGC) social media signaling strategy—trademarking a hashtag. We provide a theoretical framework (with four important research gaps) that highlights how a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s use of social media to communicate with consumers (through the lens of speech act theory) impacts their social media effectiveness after an expected signaling event—trademarking a hashtag. With the act of trademarking a hashtag, a <sup>fi</sup>rm sends credible signals to consumers about its seriousness to protect its brand and maintain brand exclusivity. Our study provides several important implications for businesses that are considering joining the hashtag-trademarking bandwagon as a social media signaling strategy.

First, we explore how a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s credible signaling act through trademarking a hashtag is associated with engagement in online brand communities. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that trademarking a hashtag is, in fact, effective in increasing social media engagement in online brand communities. Our results show that trademarking a hashtag can increase the number of retweets of a <sup>fi</sup>rm by about 27%, which is a considerable amount. Therefore, through the lens of signaling theory, we establish that the act of trademarking a hashtag helps a <sup>fi</sup>rm depict itself as credible by displaying its capability and commitment in building brand loyalty and thus increase audience engagement on social media. Our results are consistent with prior literature where a signaling event leads to an increase in audience intention to engage with a <sup>fi</sup>rm (Jeng 2016).

Using several causal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to provide an empirically driven, comprehensive understanding of trademarking hashtags. Not only is estimating the signaling impact of trademarking hashtags theoretically important but it also has practical implications. Trademarking a hash tag could be a resource-intensive and time-consuming endeavor. Without quantifying the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of trademarking hashtags, it is not clear whether <sup>fi</sup>rms should embrace trademarking hashtags actively. Our empirical analysis provides researchers and practitioners the tools and evidence to aid <sup>fi</sup>rms in their ongoing attempts to maximize the effectiveness of their social media engagement strategies.

Second, we investigate the heterogeneity of the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag on social media, that is, whether the signal strength or magnitude of the effect of trademarking a hashtag differs among <sup>fi</sup>rms with different levels of Twitter followers. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the strength of the signal indeed depends on the level of information asymmetry. In other words, the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effect of trademarking a hashtag is stronger for less-known <sup>fi</sup>rms with fewer Twitter followers. One possible reason is that consumers may not be familiar with small <sup>fi</sup>rms and their brands because of high information asymmetry. Therefore, the signals associated with trademarking a hashtag have more informational value for <sup>fi</sup>rms with fewe Twitter followers (Hottenrott et al. 2016). This <sup>fi</sup>nding has important managerial implications and highlights that the heterogeneous effect of trademarking hashtags does exist. Our results reveal that small and less popular <sup>fi</sup>rms should consider signaling through trademarking hashtags as it is found to be an effective and attractive social media strategy in terms of increasing <sup>fi</sup>rms’ social media audience engagement.

Third, using speech act theory, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that certain psychology-based constructs (linguistic characteristics) of <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated tweets are more important (perlocutionary effect) after trademarking a hashtag, which is crucial for managers’ social media marketing. Posting more tweets or writing tweets with certain desirable linguistic styles is not that important when hashtags have not been trademarked because the magnitude of those effects is relatively small. However, after the act of trademarking a hashtag, <sup>fi</sup>rms should pay close attention to their writing styles because trademarking a hashtag makes the effects of writing tweets with desirable linguistic styles much more salient. After the successful act of trademarking a hashtag, a social media strategy should focus not only on the quantity (writing more tweets) but also on the quality, that is, how <sup>fi</sup>rms express themselves by writing tweets with desirable linguistic styles. In particular, our results suggest that in order to increase social media audience engagement, <sup>fi</sup>rms should write tweets with a more positive tone and in a more con<sup>fi</sup>- dent style. Also, <sup>fi</sup>rms should pursue a more intuitive and narrative style (less analytic style of writing) in writing tweets. Our results reinforce the idea that a successful social media strategy is not only about writing more tweets and posting more messages. It has multiple dimensions including whether to trademark hashtags and how to write tweets. The underlying reason for the linguistic role of features on perlocution is based on speech act theory through <sup>fi</sup>rms’ use of utterance of words (FGC in our context). In our context, <sup>fi</sup>rms could use posts as a means of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the perceptions of others. Tone, Analytic, and Clout are <sup>fi</sup>rms’ use of actions to create a perlocutionary effect (retweets). This effect can be achieved through social media posts with a more positive tone, in a more con<sup>fi</sup>dent style, and a more intuitive and narrative writing style (less analytic style of writing).

Our empirical analysis shows that <sup>fi</sup>rms should pay close attention to how they express themselves on social media, especially after a credible signaling event—in this case, trademarking a hashtag. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, after trademarking a hashtag, <sup>fi</sup>rms adopting a speci<sup>fi</sup>c speech act style, that is, the utterance of words with more clout (inclusive and leadership style), narrative (storytelling), and positive emotional tone will have a stronger perlocutionary effect on the audience. Therefore, trademarking a hashtag makes the effect of writing more tweets with desirable linguistic styles (clout, narrative, and positive) larger. Our results are consistent with prior literature on communication based on a certain linguistic style and its impact on consumer behavior (Escalas 2007, Rime´ 2009, Araujo et al. 2015, Kim et al. 2016).

Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that trademarking hashtags makes writing tweets with desirable linguistic styles more important as the impact lasts longer. In summary, trademarking hashtags make both the contemporaneous and lagged effects of writing tweets with desirable linguistic styles larger and more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Therefore, summarizing the answers to our research questions 3 and 4, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that after trademarking a hashtag, the ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed effects of writing tweets with desirable linguistic features may not only be more intense (answer to research question 3) but may also last longer (answer to research question 4).

We believe our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to empirically investigate the signaling effect of trademarking hashtags on social media audience engagement. Therefore, we provide practical insights for researchers and businesses seeking to understand the real-world effects of trademarking a hashtag. The USPTO has allowed hashtags to be trademarked since 2013, and many <sup>fi</sup>rms are taking advantage. In general, the <sup>fi</sup>rms may not be interested in investing in trademarking hashtags if they do not see the potential returns in socia media effectiveness. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, despite the above concerns, it may be worth investing in trademarking hashtags. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the signaling effect of trademarking a hashtag plays a pivotal role in social media audience engagement. In other words, <sup>fi</sup>rms may observe a net positive impact in social media effectiveness after trademarking a hashtag. Moreover, <sup>fi</sup>rms may observe an indirect effect of trademarking hashtags: Consumer-side con<sup>fi</sup>dence and trust may increase because consumers may feel protected from exposure to false information disseminated through social media against a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. Therefore, in order to increase social media effectiveness—especially after trademarking a hashtag—<sup>fi</sup>rms should consider hiring public relations specialists and dedicating resources to the development of information diffusion strategies that take communication and design into account.

## 6. Conclusions and Future Research Directions

Our paper focuses on FGC using Twitter along with using signaling and speech act theories and investigates the impact of trademarking hashtags on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media audience engagement. This study <sup>fi</sup>lls an important gap in the information systems and consumer behavior literature by providing a deep understanding of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c social media signaling strategy:

trademarking hashtags. A trademark provides a certain level of trust for consumers so they can rely on the signals conveyed as brand equity (Katz 2010). This function instills con<sup>fi</sup>dence that the signals are authentic, reliable, and, therefore, trustworthy. Because of this trust function, <sup>fi</sup>rms seek to establish and protect their brand and send credible signals to consumers on social media. With credible signals received through trademarked hashtags, consumers adjust their perception toward the <sup>fi</sup>rm and engage with the <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated content more effectively (through retweets) and build a positive relationship with the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Boehmer and Tandoc 2015). Overall, it increases brand awareness and reputation, reduces consumer search costs, and fosters loyalty and trust among consumers.

We empirically examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence this strategy has on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media performance as well as the role of linguistic styles on writing tweets. Endogeneity concerns posed by self-selected trademarked hashtags are addressed through multiple causal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies. We establish a robust quantitative relationship between trademarking hashtags and the social media performance measure. It is important for any <sup>fi</sup>rm, regardless of its size, to send credible signals about building and protecting its brand reputation, through trademarking a hashtag, to consumers on social media. The signaling strength of trademarking a hashtag is higher for smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms because of a high level of information asymmetry. Because these small <sup>fi</sup>rms might be less known, a weak connection with consumers or any brand-tarnishing may push them out of business. In particular, we provide empirical evidence that the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effect of signaling through trademarking a hashtag is stronger for less-known <sup>fi</sup>rms with fewer Twitter followers.

Further, we provide insights into social media engagement strategies that can amplify social media effectiveness after hashtags have been trademarked. Firms can bene<sup>fi</sup>t more in terms of social media effectiveness by going beyond simply trademarking hashtags. It turns out that <sup>fi</sup>rms can more effectively connect with their customers by adopting speci<sup>fi</sup>c linguistic styles. For example, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that to increase social media audience engagement, <sup>fi</sup>rms should consider generating tweets in a more positive tone, with a more con<sup>fi</sup>dent style, and with a more narrative style (less analytic). These FGCs are more likely to be retweeted after trademarking a hashtag.

Next, as competition for attention among social media users rises, trending tags may become an increasingly prized commodity. However, <sup>fi</sup>rms may not seek trademarks if they cannot see the long-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (social media effectiveness) of spending their money, time, and other resources on trademarking hashtags. Firms may be tempted to think that trademarking hashtags and subsequently using well-crafted linguistic techniques will only bring short-term rewards. Our research suggests that this is not the case. FGC written in certain linguistic or conversational styles is very important in general. However, our results emphasize that, after trademarking a hashtag, the ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed effects of FGC with certain linguistic features will not only be more intense but also will last longer.

There are several possible extensions to our research. First, it would be interesting to measure the spillover effect that trademarking hashtags has on nonfocal <sup>fi</sup>rms. It may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for focal <sup>fi</sup>rms to understand relevant spillover effects on direct and indirect competitors because it is possible that trademarking hashtags may bene<sup>fi</sup>t or hurt their direct or indirect competitors. Understanding spillover effects may help focal <sup>fi</sup>rms devise appropriate strategies to further intensify information diffusion after a hashtag is trademarked. Sec ond, it would be intriguing to examine the contextual characteristics of the tweets, which may provide further insights about what hashtags to trademark and what not to trademark. Third, because the <sup>fi</sup>rms that we use in our study belong to different industry types, we believe the selection bias from the industries is not severe. At the same time, we believe that it would be important to study the heterogeneous effects of linguistic features for different industries. In addition, because many of the <sup>fi</sup>rms in our data set trademarked only one hashtag, we are not able to examine the diminishing returns of the number of trademarking hashtags in the current data set. Future research examining the diminishing returns of the number of trademarking hashtags can also guide the cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis of trademarking hashtags.
