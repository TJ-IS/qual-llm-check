# Analyzing the Impact of Public Buyer–Seller Engagement During Online Auctions

Arvind K. Tripathi,<sup>a,</sup>\* Young-Jin Lee,<sup>b</sup> Amit Basu<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Seller and product authentication problems are inherent in online markets, particularly in the time-constrained setting of online auctions. Although trust-building mechanisms such as seller feedback can mitigate these problems, they are retrospective, providing information about a transaction only after it is completed. Also, they have only indirect effects on buyers’ trust in sellers because they are not based on direct interactions between a trustor (buyer) and a trustee (seller). On the other hand, direct engagement between transacting parties during an online auction, particularly open and public engage ment, reduces information asymmetry between buyers and sellers and has a direct effect on buyers’ trust. In this paper, we analyze how public buyer–seller engagement via question and answer (Q&A) during online auctions impacts bidding behavior. Analyzing auction data from a large auction platform that supports public Q&A between buyers and sellers, we show that the seller’s engagement with buyers during an auction can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the behavior of both buyers who ask questions and lurkers who passively observe the engagement (spillover effect). Our analysis also shows that the impact of the seller’s engagement depends on features such as product type and seller reputation. A key insight from our study is that the impact of seller engagement on bidding behavior is moderated by seller feedback ratings, which indicates that trust-building mechanisms in online markets interact in their effects on buyers’ decision making. As sellers gain positive (negative) feedback, the impact of buyer–seller engagement on bidding behavior increases (decreases). Furthermore, the cost of an additional negative feedback rating outweighs the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of a positive one. Overall, this study provides valuable insights for online auction platforms and their stakeholders.

## 1. Introduction

Low barriers to entry for participation in online markets can result in buyers facing large numbers of offerings from different sellers that seem similar but can in fact be vastly different. Because potential buyers are largely dependent on online market mechanisms to estimate the quality of both the offered products and their sellers, online transactions can face signi<sup>fi</sup>cant authentication problems (Basu and Muylle 2003). These problems are exacerbated in online auctions, which have to be completed within a limited time window. This limits the information available to potential buyers and therefore increases buyers’ information asymmetry (Pavlou et al. 2007). Several studies have emphasized the role of trust in online markets (e.g., Ba and Pavlou 2002, Gefen et al. 2003). Because a majority of online transactions take place between strangers, a buyer’s trust in the seller is a key to mitigating information asymmetry (Dimoka et al. 2012) and increasing price premiums in online markets.

A variety of trust-building mechanisms have been developed in online markets, such as seller feedback systems and product reviews and ratings. These mechanisms are powered by various forms of usergenerated content (e.g., Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006) and have been re<sup>fi</sup>ned over the last two decades to increase buyers’ trust in sellers, reduce information asymmetry (Pavlou et al. 2007), and enhance market performance (Pavlou and Dimoka 2006). They have two important characteristics. First, they generate information about a transaction after it is completed, rather than during the transaction itself. For example, both product reviews and seller feedback ratings are provided based on the user experience of past transactions. Second, although these mechanisms do impact the behavior of potential buyers, their effect on buyers trust in a seller is indirect, because they are not based on any direct interactions between a trustor (buyer) and a trustee (seller). In other words, the information available to the buyers is based on other users’ experiences with the seller, possibly in different contexts.

In contrast, a direct engagement between the transacting parties during a transaction is relevant to that transaction itself and thus is likely to have a direct effect on buyers’ behavior. For example, direct and bidirectional communication between a buyer and seller can reduce the buyers’ information asymmetry by providing them information directly from the seller during the transaction. Sellers too (through their engagement with buyers) can directly signal their sincerity, diligence, and benevolence to those buyers. Furthermore, if the engagement is in an open and public forum, it can in<sup>fl</sup>uence other prospective buyers as well.

In setting up a listing for an online auction, the seller has to specify various attributes, such as the description of the offered item (including pictures and supporting documents), pricing parameters, auction duration, delivery commitments, and return options. Once an auction starts, the seller is not required to do anything, whereas prospective buyers usually stay engaged throughout the auction (though not continuously), because the buyer’s valuation process in an auction is dynamic, based on the observation of bidding activity. We argue that this participation by prospective buyers throughout the auction creates opportunities for direct buyer–seller engagement, which can change buyers’ perceived information asymmetry and eventually their bidding behavior.

One way for sellers to directly engage with buyers during auctions is through a question and answer (Q&A) process. These are initiated by buyers’ questions, and it is up to the seller to decide whether to participate and to what extent. The added bene<sup>fi</sup>t of open and public Q&A (where open implies that anyone can ask a question at any time during the auction, and public implies that both questions and answers are visible to all auction participants and observers) is that it may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the behavior of not only those who ask questions but also lurkers (Schlosser 2005), who observe this interaction without asking any question. Furthermore, we contend that the positive relationship between information disclosure via engagement and the outcome of the transactions (Lewis and Bridger 2001) may motivate sellers to engage with and respond to their customer base. Therefore, this engagement can be a substantial performance predictor for auctions because it is direct, focused, public, and transaction oriented.

Because engagement with buyers is not mandatory, a seller’s participation in Q&A signals their desire to help prospective buyers. However, its effect on buyers’ information asymmetry and eventually their bidding behavior has not been examined in the literature on online auctions. Two main sources of the information asymmetry faced by buyers have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the literature: seller uncertainty and product uncertainty (Dimoka et al. 2012). Seller uncertainty refers to uncertainty about seller characteristics such as quality, reliability, and trustworthiness, whereas product uncertainty refers to uncertainty about product condition, quality, and expected performance (Pavlou et al. 2007, Dimoka et al. 2012). Scholars have called for new information technology–enabled market mechanisms to mitigate seller and product uncertainties in online markets (Basu and Muylle 2003, Dimoka et al. 2012), and this research is an answer to that call with respect to online auctions.

We examine the effect of sellers’ active engagement in such processes on buyers’ information asymmetry and bidding behavior. We conjecture that the sellers responsiveness and active participation in these engage ments reveal relevant information about them and their offered products, and contribute to their perceived social presence (Short et al. 1976, Tu 2000), which may increase buyers’ trust in the sellers (Gefen and Straub 2004, Rafaeli and Noy 2005). In other words, direct and public buyer–seller engagement during online auctions could possibly reduce buyers’ information asymmetry in two ways: (1) by mitigating seller uncertainty by building buyers’ trust in the seller, and (2) by mitigating product uncertainty by providing timely and relevant information about the product during the transaction.

Our empirical study is based on auction data from a large online auction platform that supports buyer–seller engagement via public Q&A. Our analysis shows that signaling generated by the seller’s engagement has a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the bidding behavior of market participants. Furthermore, this effect is not uniform and, in particular, is impacted by the seller feed back ratings and the type of offered product. Finally, this engagement affects not only those buyers who actively participate but also lurkers (Schlosser 2005), who are just silent observers. These are useful managerial insights for online auction platform providers, many of whom currently do not support public Q&A features. Including such features on their platforms could lead to increased participation by both sellers and buyers and could motivate increased use of the plat forms, particularly for certain types of products.

This paper contributes to the literature on online auction mechanism design, on authentication in online markets, and on the role of buyer–seller interactions/engagement during an online auction. As discussed in the next section, although there is substantial literature on online auctions and feedback mechanisms, very little work has been done on intratransaction engagement between buyers and sellers (Li et al. 2017), and this paper serves to bring attention to this important aspect of online auctions.

This paper is organized in nine sections. In Section 2, we review the relevant literature, and we then discuss the research framework in Section 3. We describe our data collection procedures in Section 4. Section 5 discusses our models and results of the analysis of buyers’ behavior, and Section 6 describes our robustness checks. Section 7 discusses the extant seller behavior and its implications. Section 8 discusses the theoretical contributions and limitations of our study, and Section 9 concludes with the implications of these results for the design of auction platforms and some directions for future research.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Reputation Mechanisms

Feedback mechanisms such as reviews and ratings in online electronic markets have been examined as a form of online word of mouth (Dellarocas 2003, Jin 2005, Lewis 2011). These mechanisms allow trust building, product development, quality assurance, and many other activities in online markets, and have become crucial in fostering cooperation among strangers in online markets (Gefen et al. 2003). Indeed, sellers’ feedback ratings are key to the assurance of seller reputations/quality (Dimoka et al. 2012) and authentication of online transactions between strangers. Buyers rely on the market-enabled feedback mechanisms because sellers with higher feedback ratings are deemed more trustworthy and command a higher price premium (Ba and Pavlou 2002).

Moreno and Terwiesch (2014) examined the effect of the reputation in online service markets on outcomes. While reaf<sup>fi</sup>rming the enhancing effect of reputation on price (price premium on products from highly rated sellers), their study emphasizes the need to look beyond numerical reputation scores, such as textual feedback comments, to examine such relationships. Although the potential value of textual content in user feedback has been recognized (Ariely and Simonson 2003), researchers have mainly used numerically summarized feedback ratings of sellers, or star ratings of reviews, partly because assessing a large number of textual comments is challenging (Andrews and Benzing 2007, Li et al. 2016). For example, textual feedback reviews are short, cryptic, and unstructured, and are therefore dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to analyze and operationalize for user recommendations. Nevertheless, sellers’ feedback ratings have been shown to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on buyer behavior and price premiums, partly because buyers tend to rely on simple and concise numeric information.

Although feedback mechanisms such as ratings are widely used to authenticate products and sellers, they pose several challenges. First, a seller can only in<sup>fl</sup>u ence these signals (feedback ratings) indirectly. Second, for a prospective buyer, the seller’s feedback ratings are based on other customers’ experiences and therefore these ratings have only an indirect effect on a buyer’s trust in the seller, which has its own limitations. Third, reciprocity between transacting parties allows manipulations and in<sup>fl</sup>ation of ratings (Filippas et al. 2018), which makes feedback ratings less informative (Bolton et al. 2013). Fourth, the possibility of retaliation leads to reporting bias and distorts feedback rating distribution (Dellarocas and Wood 2008). Although scholars agree that these feedback mechanisms are critical for greasing the cogs of online markets, there is some concern about current trust-building mechanisms (Masclet and Penard´ 2012). Therefore, it is important to understand the role of feedback mechanisms in different market structures and how do they interact with alternate trust-building mechanisms. Indeed, a recent trend is to explore alternate mechanisms and solutions to mitigate these challenges (Carter et al. 2017), such as leveraging interpersonal relationships to develop swift guanxi (Ou et al. 2014) to facilitate buyer–seller engagement. Whereas engagements based on interpersonal relationships are private, our research adds to this body of literature by investigating an alternative mechanism, buyer–seller engagement via public Q&A, and its effect on buyers’ bidding behavior.

2.2. Buyer–Seller Engagement in Online Auctions Although the need to understand and explore buyer–seller engagement has been recognized in the literature (Godes and Mayzlin 2004), few studies have examined the effect of buyer–seller engagement on bidding behavior in online auctions. A recent study (Ducarroz et al. 2016) showed that sellers’ engagement with buyers, via promotion messages sent during online auctions, has a positive impact on buyers’ valu ations and bidding behavior. The authors showed that bidding behavior is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the volume of messages, irrespective of the content. However, promotional messages from sellers represent limited buyer–seller engagement, because they are one sided and may not address buyers’ queries or concerns. These blanket promotions during auctions from sellers to buyers are akin to mass advertising, focused on highlighting the positive aspects of the product, rather than mitigating buyers information asymmetry.

Despite technological advances including arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence and high-speed data networks to facilitate information exchange (Bergemann and Bonatti 2019), information asymmetry between transacting parties is still a major problem in online markets, where a majority of transactions take place between strangers.

Thus, mitigating information asymmetry is critical for the success of online markets.

In fact, information asymmetry (Akerlof 1970) is inherent in online auctions, because buyers’ inability to interact with sellers before they place their bids can increase uncertainty and decrease their desire to participate. In these markets, agents (buyers) look for every possible information or action/cue from other parties to mitigate their information asymmetry. Scholars argue that the physical separation of online auction participants from the sellers makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for buyers to use social cues such as physical interaction and body language to assess seller quality (Gefen et al. 2003), and therefore buyers rely on feedback ratings. Buyers in the online context can only assess product quality through the online interface rather than testing goods’ quality by “kicking the tires,” which also falls short in explicitly differentiating among complex/heterogeneous products (Kempf and Smith 1998, Forman et al. 2008).

In any transaction, because sellers have the most information about the product and transaction as a whole, buyers want to ask questions directly to the seller (Goh et al. 2013) in addition to gathering information from secondary and indirect sources such as online reviews and feedback ratings/comments. In particular, when reviews and ratings are not relevant to the product being sold in the current transaction, buyers seek direct communication with the seller. Indeed, market studies have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed a positive correlation between engagement with consumers and <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts (Tirunillai and Tellis 2012, Miller and Tucker 2013). Whereas much of this literature focuses on business-to-consumer or consumer-to-consumer engagement via social media brand community for brand awareness (Shahbaznezhad and Tripathi 2015), this study focuses on the buyer–seller engagement pertaining to speci<sup>fi</sup>c transactions in online markets to mitigate buyers’ information asymmetry.

A direct buyer–seller interaction allows buyers to initiate communications and seek information about the condition, quality, and expected performance of the product/service. Indeed, sellers’ public response to buyers’ questions can increase buyers’ satisfaction, even for casual observers of these exchanges (Gu and Ye 2014), because online exchange relationships are akin to interpersonal relationships (Kumar and Benbasat 2002). Furthermore, whereas sellers who interact with buyers get rewarded through higher sales and higher customer satisfaction, sellers who do not engage in such interactions incur a penalty (Kumar et al. 2018). Therefore, we argue that sellers have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient incentives to engage in these exchanges, which also have the potential to affect bidding behavior in online auctions.

The effect of trust on dyadic buyer–seller interactions has been studied in the marketing literature.

When buyers perceive a seller as trustworthy, their interactions are cooperative and mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial (Schurr and Ozanne 1985), and are termed as integrative interactions, but when buyers perceive a seller as untrustworthy, these interactions are not cooperative and are termed as distributive interactions (Pruitt 1981). Whereas these interactions were in the context of bargaining and negotiations between buyers and sellers, we examine the impact of buyer–seller interactions/ engagement via public Q&A in online auctions, where these engagements are not part of any negotiations, but are used to mitigate buyers’ information asymmetry and as a trust-building mechanism.

## 3. Research Framework

## 3.1. Research Context

Figure 1 illustrates a generic framework for different types of engagement between buyers and sellers in online auctions. It shows that engagement between buyers and sellers could take place before (Preauction), during (Intra-auction), or after (Postauction) the auction is completed. Most auction platforms facilitate buyer–seller engagement only after the auction is completed, in the form of feedback mechanisms, where both buyer and seller give feedback (ratings and comments) on each other. A few auction plat forms also allow private communication between buyers and sellers during an auction. Although this is helpful, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of such private communications are limited to the parties directly participating in the communications. Some online markets (e.g., Amazon.- com) have started including public Q&A information in product listings. However, this is still rare among online auction platforms.

Communicating with or messaging consumers during their purchase decision process has always been of interest to marketing managers because it can in<sup>fl</sup>uence purchase decisions. Although promotional messages from the seller that are directed to all potential bidders are helpful, they are somewhat similar to advertising, and do not allow any direct communication between a buyer and seller. Active seller engage ment during an auction can facilitate direct communication with the buyers and can in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decision making (Ducarroz et al. 2016). In this study, we analyze data from an auction platform that supports public Q&A between buyers and sellers, which also provides a window into the engagement activity for passive observers (Gu and Ye 2014) and has the potential to convert them into prospective buyers.

In terms of Figure 1, this research attempts to empirically examine the impact of seller engagement with buyers via public Q&A on bidding activity in an online auction. Next, we discuss the theoretical framework of this study.

## 3.2. Theoretical Framework

Communication between a buyer and seller is an antecedent of trust (Morgan and Hunt 1994), and more so in online auctions, where information asymmetry is inherent in the mechanism and most of the moral hazard risk lies with the seller (Bolton et al. 2013). Therefore, we contend that sellers can potentially bene<sup>fi</sup>t by engaging with prospective buyers (Liu and Arnett 2000) during online auctions.

The seller’s public and open responses to buyer questions signal their willingness, desire, and competence to mitigate buyers’ perceived product and seller uncertainty. By participating in such public Q&A, sellers are able to signal their transparency, benevolence, and integrity (Gupta et al. 2009), which can boost buyer con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the offer. Before participating in an auction, buyers are exposed to the seller’s feedback ratings, which are considered as a signal of the seller quality (Dimoka et al. 2012), and once buyers start participating in the auction (by placing a bid or just as an observer), they can experience additional signals of seller’s trustworthiness via direct seller engagement. Because buyers are exposed to these two signals that could affect their trust in the seller at different points in time, ordering effects, termed as primacy and recency effects (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), in<sup>fl</sup>uence interaction and the resulting net effect of these two signals on prospective buyers.

The literature on behavioral economics shows how buyers are susceptible to bias based on their experience (Wolf and Muhanna 2011). We conjecture that the strength of the initial signal from seller ratings may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the impact of the later signal from seller engagement. In practice, it is also intuitive that sellers with higher positive feedback ratings (a.k.a. highrated sellers), are more experienced, professional, responsive, and likely to be better in their engagement with buyers than low-rated sellers. Consequently, the perceived value of the signal from high-rated sellers through their engagement with buyers may be higher than that from similar engagement by low-rated sellers.

Buyer–seller engagements through Q&A processes facilitate sellers’ virtual presence and connection with the buyers and reduce perceptions of spatial and temporal distance between buyers and the seller. We conjecture that a direct and public buyer–seller engagement changes buyers’ perceived information asymmetry and eventually bidding behavior via two mechanisms: <sup>fi</sup>rst, by providing information about the product and thus mitigating product uncertainty, and second, by building buyers’ trust in the seller to mitigate seller uncertainty. Furthermore, because seller uncertainty also affects product uncertainty (Dimoka et al. 2012), this implies that buyers’ trust in the seller also affects product uncertainty, albeit indirectly. Overall, reduced information asymmetry leads to a higher price premium. Next, we further elaborate on the effect of buyer–seller engagement on product and seller uncertainty.

Buyers in online auctions face product uncertainty when they lack information about the product condition, quality, or expected performance. Buyer–seller engagement via public Q&A, during an auction, can reveal additional information about the product con dition, quality, and performance. Because this engagement is public and all buyers can see the Q&A exchange, they may act collectively for a common objective, that is, mitigating product uncertainty. Buyers can follow up on each other’s questions, to triangulate and con<sup>fi</sup>rm the validity of the information provided. This collective examination via public Q&A is akin to an open community assessment of the product in this virtual forum. This real-time engagement allows buyers to demand additional pictures, videos, thirdparty assessment documents/certi<sup>fi</sup>cates, and invoices from the seller. The public and archival nature of this Q&A is likely to dissuade sellers from providing false information, and thus this engagement increases buyers’ con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the quality of information. Overall, we contend that buyer–seller engagement facilitates timely and relevant communication between the transacting parties and therefore has the potential to mitigate buyers’ product uncertainty.

Buyers face seller uncertainty when they cannot assess the true characteristics of the seller, which is common in online markets. Feedback ratings build buyers’ trust in the seller, and this trust mitigates seller uncertainty (Gefen et al. 2003). However, feedback ratings have only an indirect effect on buyers trust in the seller because sellers can only in<sup>fl</sup>uence ratings indirectly, and buyers cannot directly observe or con<sup>fi</sup>rm sellers’ expected behavior. In contrast, seller engagement with buyers via Q&A is live and public during an auction, signals the seller’s social presence (Tu 2000, Short et al. 1976), and allows buyers to observe virtual social cues. In these open public forums, a seller cannot prevent a potential buyer from asking any question, however dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it could be, and their answers are visible to all interested platform participants. Therefore, buyers may perceive a public Q&A to be more credible. We argue that a public engagement and response to buyers’ queries and comments before an auction closes is timely and signals a seller’s willingness to listen and address buyers’ concerns. We argue that engagement of this kind, indicates sellers’ benevolence (Kumar et al. 2018), breeds familiarity, and therefore has a positive impact on buyers’ satisfaction (Thomas et al. 2012). Therefore, sellers’ responsiveness to buyers’ questions and comments on public Q&A is likely to have a positive effect on buyers’ trust and help sellers attract more customers (Kumar et al. 2018).

The Q&A process is initiated when a potential buyer asks a question on the public forum provided by the auction platform. The seller’s response con<sup>fi</sup>rms her virtual social presence (Shen and Khalifa 2008) in the Q&A forum and signals her availability and readiness to interact/engage with potential buyers. Furthermore, when these Q&A exchanges are public, the seller’s response signals her willingness to respond transparently to not only the buyer who asked the question but all potential buyers. Hence, we posit that buyers are virtually connected with the seller even if they do not ask a question. Also, the public Q&A capability facilitates multiple such connections between the seller and potential buyers.

We posit that a direct connection between the trustor (buyer) and trustee (seller), which is triggered by the seller’s engagement, affects the buyer’s perceived trust in the seller (Butler 1995). A large body of literature (Konovsky and Pugh 1994, Butler 1995, Malhotra and Murnighan 2002) has argued that direct engagements/interactions between a seller and buyer are key to a buyer’s perceived trust in the seller. Although a prospective buyer initiates the Q&A process by asking a question, the seller’s participation is voluntary. The seller’s effort in responding to these questions in real time, while the auction is in progress, is considered cooperative by the prospective buyers. Because voluntary and cooperative behaviors are considered trustworthy (Butler 1995), we posit that the seller’s engagement in online auctions has a direct effect on buyers’ trust in the seller, and eventually affects their bidding behavior. Furthermore, these public Q&A exchanges are not a part of the seller’s promotional campaigns, but instead are driven by the buyers. Because buyers are often skeptical of sellers promotions and marketing messages (Escalas 2007), this buyer-driven public Q&A is perceived as more trustworthy and is likely to have a direct impact on buyers’ trust in the seller.

And because this engagement is public, trustors (bidders) can also observe a trustee’s (seller’s) behavior. Therefore, observation and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the trustee’s expected behavior increase the trustor’s (bidder’s) trust in the trustee (seller).

Note that a trustor’s perception about a trustee’s trustworthiness is not only in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the direct connection between them but also the social context and third parties (Ferrin et al. 2006)—in this case, other potential buyers in the same auction. Indeed, prior research shows that in the absence of direct interactions with a seller, buyers use peripheral cues (Franco Valdez et al. 2018), such as the seller’s effort, responsiveness, and public communication with their peers (other buyers), to form their trust about the seller. Therefore, in online auctions, sellers’ engagement via public Q&A affects not only the ones who asked the question but also other observers (Gu and Ye 2014) or lurkers (Schlosser 2005). Furthermore, because this engagement is collectively driven by the prospective buyers seeking a meaningful exchange with the seller, bidders may perceive these exchanges as more trustworthy.

Prior research argues that the sequencing of information exposure impacts buyers’ decision making (Sage 1981). In this context, we argue that buyers are exposed to two trust-building mechanisms—seller feedback ratings and seller engagement, in sequence, both of which affect buyers’ trust in a seller. In this study, we also explore how these two trust-building mechanisms interact and examine their effect on buyers’ trust in the seller, and eventually buyers’ decision making in online auctions. We view buyers’ trust in the seller as a latent construct that we do not directly observe, and instead measure the effect of these trust-building mechanisms on bidding behavior.

## 4. Data

The data for this study were collected from a large online auction platform using an authenticated application programming interface (API). Like all other auction houses, bidders and sellers on this platform are required to register (at no charge) before they can participate in any auction. Auctions in our data set span a range of prices and diverse product offerings. In total, our data set contains 2,457 auction listings spanning over 88 different product subcategories.<sup>1</sup> Sample statistics are provided in Table 1.

The data include various details of the buyer–seller engagement process, such as the time stamps of questions and answers, as well as the seller’s feedback ratings accrued from previous transactions. Note that this auction platform does not support private communications between auction participants, so public Q&A is the only means of engagement between buyers and the seller. The buyer–seller engagement was measured by the extent of the seller’s participation during an online auction. We measure the seller’s engagement by the number of AnsweredQuestions during an auction. This captures the seller’s effort and indicates their virtual presence (Shen and Khalifa 2008), which can affect bidders’ direct trust in the seller (Gefen and Straub 2004). Along with the number of AnsweredQuestions, we also measure the number of questions that are not answered (Outstanding-Questions) by the seller. Before a question is answered, it is considered as an outstanding question. The number of outstanding questions (OutstandingQuestions) captures the lack of seller’s effort and may have a negative effect (Khern-am-nuai et al. 2022) on bidding behavior and thus has been included as a control in our model. These measures are similar to the measurements used in earlier studies to measure a vendor’s response to customer comments/queries (Kumar et al. 2018).

In contrast to retail channels where transactions are not time bound, the timeliness of information or response from the seller is critical for buyers’ decision making in online auctions. For a seller who is making an effort in engaging with buyers via public Q&A, one can argue that the seller’s responsiveness signals their desire to provide information in real time to help buyers’ decision making. Therefore, the seller’s prompt response (responsiveness) in these forums is likely to have a positive effect on buyers (Wongkitrungrueng and

Assarut 2020). We measure the seller’s responsiveness (AverageResponseTime) as an average time in responding to questions in an auction. Overall, the number of AnsweredQuestions shown in Table 1 measures the extent of seller engagement via Q&A in these auctions.

In particular, we examine the impact of seller engagement on bids as measured by BidAmount, which is the unit of analysis in this research. Our data set contains sequences of bids from these auctions. For the t<sup>th</sup> bid, we measure the cumulative impact of seller engagement prior to the t<sup>th</sup> bid. Engagement activity in any time period is indicated by a nonzero increment in the number of AnsweredQuestions. Because the amount of a bid in ascending price auctions can also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by situational factors (Ariely and Simonson 2003), we control for situational factors such as the listing attributes shown in Table 1.

Although buyers can observe the number of positive and negative seller feedback ratings, these ratings are often aggregated on auction platforms (Andrews and Benzing 2007) for ease of use for prospective buyers. Some platforms, such as eBay, also display sellers’ feedback ratings from recent transactions, such as from the last six months. Aggregation makes it easy for buyers to assess and compare sellers.

In the literature, seller reputation has been measured in two ways: (1) by aggregating their positive and negative feedback ratings and categorizing them in different groups such as established or honest sellers (Resnick et al. 2006), etc., and (2) by separately considering positive (PositiveVotes) and negative feedback ratings (NegativeVotes) from prior transactions (e.g., Ba and Pavlou 2002, Bajari and Hortacsu 2003). We use both the measures of seller reputation and explore differences in the impact of seller engagement across three seller categories, which we describe next.

For aggregate seller ratings, we partition sellers into three categories—EstablishedPositive, EstabishedNeutral, and EstablishedNegative—categorized to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the net sentiments of their reputations as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst specify the positiveness (or negativeness) of a seller, which is 1 (or 1) if the seller received positive (o negative) votes more than the median number of positive (or negative) votes, and otherwise is 0. We then create a binary indicator of EstablishedPositive 1 if the seller’s net reputation (positiveness plus negativeness) is 1 and a binary indicator of EstablishedNegative 1 if the net reputation is 1. Sellers, who are neither EstablishedPositive nor EstablishedNegative are considered as EstablishedNeutral. We now use both metrics— positive and negative votes and seller grouping—to assess how sellers’ reputations moderate the impact of seller engagement on bid amount. Using two different measures increases con<sup>fi</sup>dence in our results and the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 5. Analysis of Bidding Behavior 5.1. Model

We examine the impact of a seller’s engagement via public Q&A on bidding behavior, which in turn affects the outcome of online auctions. To address our research question, we investigate whether a higher level of seller engagement, measured in terms of seller responses to buyer questions during an online auction, has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the bid amount. Furthermore, because seller reputation based on feedback ratings re<sup>fl</sup>ects the seller’s performance in previous transactions and their trustworthiness (Ba and Pavlou 2002), these ratings may also affect their cooperation and effort for engagement in the current auction (Schurr and Ozanne 1985). Therefore, we also examine whether and how the impact of seller engagement via Q&A on bidding behavior differs by the seller’s reputation and by product type (Ba and Pavlou 2002). To address these questions, we develop an econometric model with a <sup>fi</sup>xed effects aspect, as shown below:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \log (B i d A m o u n t _ {i, t}) & = \boldsymbol {\alpha} S e l l e r E n g a g e m e n t _ {i, t - 1} \\ & \quad + \boldsymbol {\beta} (S e l l e r E n g a g e m e n t _ {i, t - 1} \\ & \quad \times S e l l e r R e p u t a t i o n _ {i}) \\ & \quad + \mathfrak {d} X _ {i, t - 1} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The variables in the above model are as follows. To analyze the impact of seller engagement on bidding behavior, we consider $\log ( B i d A m o u n t _ { i , t } )$ as the depend ent variable $( B i d A m o u n t _ { i , t }$ is the $t ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ bid submitted in an online ascending price auction i). For example, if an auction receives 10 bids, the order of the last submitted bid will be 10. To avoid having the results driven by extreme values given the large variance in distributions of the amount of bid, we log-transform the dependent variable BidAmount (Ducarroz et al. 2016). The varia ble $S e l l e r E n g a g e m e n t _ { i , t - 1 }$ is measured by the total num ber of AnsweredQuestions before $t ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ bid. As discussed earlier, the reputation of the seller in auction i (SellerReputation ) is measured in two ways: (1) the numbers of positive (PositiveVotes) and negative (NegativeVotes) and (2) the net sentiment indicators of seller’s reputation, based on positive and negative votes, that we created earlier—EstablishedPositive, EstablishedNeutral, and Estab lishedNegative. Note that $S e l l e r E n g a g e m e n t _ { i , t - 1 }$ varies with time during an auction, whereas SellerReputation does not change during an auction. In addition, $X _ { i , t - 1 }$ in Equation (1) includes time-varying control variables of a bid such as the prior bid amount $( \log ( B i d A m o u n t _ { i , t - 1 } ) ) .$ the average time taken to answer buyers’ questions within an auction i (AverageResponseTime ), and the number of bids $( B i d C o u n t _ { i , \mathrm { t - 1 } } )$ before the $t ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ bid. Also, $v _ { i }$ represents the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects to capture unobserved timeinvariant heterogeneity in sellers or auction items. Finally, $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is an idiosyncratic error term that captures unobserved effects that are not correlated with the variables, and we assume that this error term is normally distributed. Therefore, the vector <sup>a</sup> captures the main effects of Sell-$e r E n g a g e m e n t _ { i , t - 1 }$ on a bidder’s bid amount. The vector <sup>b</sup> captures the moderating effects of SellerReputation<sub>i</sub> on the effects of the $S e l l e r E n g a g e m e n t _ { i , t - 1 }$ on the bid amount.

We control for observed bid characteristics such as the number of prior bids, $B i d C o u n t _ { i , t - 1 } .$ , the order of the bid, and if the bid has been submitted by mobile or by proxy (Burtch and Hong 2014). The term $B i d C o u n t _ { i , t - 1 }$ also captures market interest or competition which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the bid amount. Because bidders in online auctions bid mostly in a reactive manner, reacting to bids from previous bidders (Zeithammer and Adams 2010), we control for the previous bid, $\log ( B i d A m o u n t _ { i , t - 1 } )$ . Feedback ratings (positive and negative votes), based on the seller’s prior transactions, have an indi rect effect on buyers’ trust in the seller. The primary effect is that of ratings on buyers’ trust, whereas the secondary effect is that on buyers’ trust via engagement. Therefore, one can argue that sellers have strong incentives to invest in reputation building (Cabral and Hortac¸su 2010) because positive reputation is not only a strategic advantage (Barney 1986), it also allows sellers to reap secondary bene<sup>fi</sup>ts via different trust-building mechanisms, as indicated by the results in Figure 2.

We know that both positive and negative ratings directly affect price premiums (Ba and Pavlou 2002). We extend that literature and provide new insights into the role of sellers’ reputations in online auctions. We show that ratings not only have a direct effect on price premium (Ba and Pavlou 2002) but also indirectly affect price premiums via other trust-building mechanisms, such as the buyer–seller engagement that we study in this research.

## 5.4. Effect of Product Type

Because buyers’ concerns about information asymmetry may vary with product type, we next examine whether the impact of seller engagement on bidding behavior also varies with product type. In particular, we investigate the impact of seller engagement in the case of used products, where the con<sup>fl</sup>uence of product condition, price, the complexity of features, and seller reputation becomes more salient in the success of the transaction. It has been shown in the literature that the information asymmetry between sellers and buyers can be more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for complex and multifeatured (Wolf and Muhanna 2005) or high-price (Muthitachareon et al. 2014) products. For example, in online auctions for used cars, buyers’ need for product and quality (condition) information is higher compared with that for other items (Dimoka et al. 2012), and thus, buyers’ dependence on the seller increases.

In this section, we explore how the impact of seller engagement via Q&A can vary by the level of product complexity. To address this question, we consider two product types sold via these auctions—used cars and other products—on this auction platform. In our data set, we have 2,073 listings for used cars and 384 listings for other products, which vary from art deco artwork to toys, etc.

Consumers buy used goods for the utility they gain from the purchase. However, a used car is not only a used good but also an expensive purchase, and therefore consumers are more concerned about the authenticity of the transaction. For example, although the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of a used car may be available online, the authenticity or reputation of the seller plays a big role in such a purchase, partly because of the cost, condition, and complexity of the product. Furthermore, buyers’ lack of expertise in assessing the quality of used goods makes them more reliant on the seller’s reputation. We argue that in the case of used-good auctions, product and seller uncertainty can be mitigated by sellers’ reputations and sellers’ engagement with the buyers.

In previous sections, our results showed bidders bias against EstablishedNegative sellers; therefore, in Table 3, we focus only on EstablishedPositive and EstablishedNeutral sellers. As discussed earlier, in this section, auction listings are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two types: used cars and other items. ## 5.5. Effect of Buyer Participation

Although the public Q&A process in this study facilitates a direct virtual connection between a prospective buyer and the seller, it is not clear whether the impact of engagement on bids will also depend on the level of buyer’s participation. Although some prior studies found that bidder participation has an effect on bidding behavior (Goes et al. 2010, 2012; Ghasemkhani et al. 2021), these studies measured a bidder’s partici pation via frequency and amount of bids from a bidder, whereas in this research, we aim to measure participation related to buyer–seller engagement through Q&A. Note that bidders gain information by participation in both of these activities—by placing bids and by asking questions. However, the type and scope of the information are different. For example, the information obtained via bidding in online auctions is related to demand and competition and is often private to participating bidders. In contrast, the information obtained by participating in a public buyer–seller engagement mitigates product and seller uncertainty and is public, not private.

Although it is expected that buyers who ask questions are outnumbered by those who do not, both of these groups may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from and are interested in

Q&A exchange (Preece et al. 2004) with the seller. At the same time, buyers who ask questions may feel a sense of membership in the community and may have higher stakes in the buyer–seller engagement. Buyers may have intrinsic or extrinsic motivations to ask questions. In this study, we do not focus on the motivations of contributors, but rather on the return to contributors, and we examine whether that is different from the return to non contributors.

To examine the effect of bidder participation on the impact of seller engagement on bid amount, we introduce a dummy variable, $B i d s A f t e r R e s p o n s e _ { i , t } ,$ to <sup>fl</sup>ag bids from bidders after the seller has responded to their questions. A positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant estimate for this dummy would con<sup>fi</sup>rm that seller engagement has an additional effect on the bid from the buyer who got a response to their question.

We report our results in Table 4, in which the models in columns (1) and (2) are similar to the models estimated in columns (2) and (3) in Table 2, with the addition of the new dummy variable $( B i d s A f t e r R e s p o n s e _ { i , t } )$ to capture the effect of the bidder participation. Also, the models in columns (3) and (4) in Table 4 are similar to the models estimated in columns (4) and (5) of Table 3. In summary, columns (1) and (2) in Table 4 indicate the effect of bidder participation in the presence of the moderating effect of seller reputation, whereas columns (3) and (4) in that table indicate whether this effect persists in the context of used-good auctions. The results in Table 4 strongly con<sup>fi</sup>rm the effect of bidder participation on the impact of seller engagement on bidding behavior. Note that the baseline impact of seller engagement on bidding behavior $( A n s w e r e d Q u e s t i o n s _ { i , t - 1 } )$ is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in all the columns in Table 4.

These results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that although seller engagement bene<sup>fi</sup>ts all the prospective buyers, it has an additional impact on those who ask questions. These results allow us to separate the impact of seller engagement on buyers who actively participate by asking questions and that on lurkers (Schlosser 2005), who do not ask questions in these Q&A forums (spillover effect). Although seller engagement via public Q&A facilitates a virtual direct connection between the seller and all the prospective buyers, we conjecture that the strength of the connection between the seller and those who ask questions is stronger. Earlier we argued that seller engagement via public Q&A impacts bidding behavior in two ways, (1) by increasing buyers’ trust in a seller, which mitigates seller uncertainty, and (2) by providing product information, which mitigates product uncertainty. Because the seller’s responses (product information) are open to everyone in a public Q&A forum, we conjecture that those who get their questions answered have a higher level of engagement and therefore higher trust in the seller, which is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in their higher bid amounts.

These are preliminary results. Although our results indicate a higher impact of engagement on those who get their questions answered, we still need to develop a deeper understanding of this engagement facilitated by Q&A and the resulting impact on both sides. For exam ple, Q&A dynamics can get complex, with the types and timing of the questions and participants and their motivations, interactions, and perceptions about seller ratings. Furthermore, adding seller strategies to the mix increases the complexity and, therefore, requires a thorough investigation of this exchange for different market settings and product types. For example, one may want to investigate whether the effect of bidder participation varies with seller reputation and with participation/ opinions of other buyers (Schlosser 2005).

## 6. Robustness Checks

In our <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model (1), two potential estimation issues could bias our results. First, unobserved timevarying confounders may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the level of seller engagement, even though we control for many observed time-varying variables in our <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model. To address this issue, we conduct our <sup>fi</sup>xed effects estimation with two matching methods: propensity score matching (PSM) and coarsened exact matching (CEM; Iacus et al. 2012). Second, the inclusion of the lagged dependent variable $( \log ( B i d A m o u n t _ { i , t - 1 } ) )$ in our model (1) can mechanically introduce a correlation between the error term $\left( \varepsilon _ { i , t } \right)$ and the regressor $( \log ( B i d A m o u n t _ { i , t - 1 } ) )$ , thus biasing our estimates. We mitigate this concern by conducting dynamic panel data (DPD) estimation (Arellano and Bond 1991).

## 6.1. Estimations with Two Matching Methods

To con<sup>fi</sup>rm the causal effect of seller engagement in the presence of potential confounding effects of unobserved time-varying variables, we utilize matching methods to control for some or all the confounding in<sup>fl</sup>uence of pretreatment control variables in our observational data (Blackwell et al. 2009). First, we partition the data into high and low buyer–seller engagement groups using a binary variable, $H i g h – E n g a g e m e n t _ { i , t - 1 } .$ If the seller’s Answered $Q u e s t i o n s _ { i , t - 1 }$ is greater than the median of Answered $Q u e s t i o n s _ { t - 1 } ,$ the seller is categorized in the highengagement group $( H i g h – E n g a g e m e n t _ { i , t - 1 } = 1 )$ , and 0 otherwise. Second, to make the empiri cal distributions of the covariates in the groups more similar, we use two matching methods (PSM and CEM; Iacus et al. 2012) to produce approximately balanced and matched data controlling for the covariates. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the median bias across covariates in the matched sample by PSM is 2.4, compared with 19.9 in the unmatched sample. ## 6.3. Evidence of Buyers’ Perception of Seller Engagement

In our theoretical framework (Section 3.2), we argued that the seller’s engagement builds buyers’ trust in the seller to mitigate seller uncertainty, and provides additional information to mitigate product uncertainty. Whereas buyers’ trust was not measured in this study, we explored the effect of engagement on buyers’ trust via a brief survey. Individuals with experience in engagement with sellers via public Q&A on the auction platform responded to the following statements on a seven-point Likert scale: (1) the seller’s response to buyers’ questions via public Q&A helps build my trust in the seller; (2) the seller’s response to buyers’ questions via public Q&A provides information about the product and therefore reduces product uncertainty. Overall, we received 34 usable responses, out of which 71% selected agree or strongly agree for statement (1) and 76% selected agree or strongly agree for statement (2). This supports our argument that seller engagement via public Q&A mitigates both product and seller uncertainty. Note that buyers’ trust in sellers directly affects seller uncertainty and indirectly affects product uncertainty. Therefore, any mechanism such as buyer–seller engagement that can affect buyers’ trust in the seller plays an important role in online markets. Future research could explore the nuances of this trust-building mechanism.

## 7. Analysis of Seller Behavior

In the previous section, we showed that in a setting that supports public Q&A, seller engagement does impact bidding in online auctions. We also showed that the impact of seller engagement on bidding behavior is moderated by the seller’s own reputation. In summary, we found that engagement via Q&A is more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for reputed sellers. Therefore, one would expect that rational sellers would appropriately leverage their reputation to maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of their engagement with the buyers. However, we do not know whether sellers are rational in their engagement with the buyers via public Q&A, and whether they realize and leverage the potential of such engagement. To understand sellers’ engagement behavior, we now ask, (1) Do sellers with different reputations engage differently via Q&A? And (2) if so, do they engage in a manner that is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to them? In other words, we will assess whether seller reputation explains the level of the seller’s participation in the buyer–seller engagement. It is intuitive that sellers with negative votes are more likely to engage buyers via Q&A to offset their negative reputation.

Overall, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that as sellers gain buyers’ trust (more positive votes), they are less likely to participate in live buyer–seller engagement, contrary to sellers with negative votes. It is possible that high-rated sell ers consider their feedback ratings as strong enough and do not see any need to engage with the buyers. Indeed, prior research has shown that sellers unfamiliar with the value and importance of the engagement often do not monitor the Q&A forum or respond to buyers’ questions/comments (Kumar et al. 2018). We suggest that auction platforms need to educate sellers about the value of direct engagement with the buyers.

## 8. Discussion and Limitations

This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical study to explore the effect of direct and public buyer–seller engagement on bidding behavior in online auctions. Drawing upon the literature in psychology, economics, and information systems, this study articulates how buyer–seller engagement has a direct effect on buyers’ trust in the seller and mitigates product and seller uncertainty. In addition, this study examines how different trust-building mechanisms interact in online markets. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it examines how feedback ratings interact and affect buyer–seller engagements in online auctions. And <sup>fi</sup>nally, it examines how product types and buyers’ participation can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the impact of seller engagement on bidding behavior. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for theoretical development in online auctions in particular and online markets in general, as we discuss next.

Although a few recent studies (Carter et al. 2017) have called for alternate mechanisms or modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of current trust-building mechanisms (Masclet and Penard´ 2012, Bolton et al. 2013), to the best of our knowledge, none of these studies explored how trust building via new market mechanisms is moderated by current mechanism, which is sellers’ feedback ratings.

When information is presented in a sequence, ordering effects explain information retention and decision making (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). We argue that seller reputation observed via feedback ratings gets anchored when buyers get exposed to it (Wolf and Muhanna 2011), and this anchor/bias moderates the impact of another trust-building mechanism on prospective buyers. Indeed, a large body of literature in organizational studies (e.g., Sumer and Knight 1996) has found that anchoring based on prior performance (feedback ratings) can affect future performance (trust building via new mechanisms). Future research needs to explore how different trust-building mechanisms need to collaborate and complement to improve trust building in online markets and allocative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Although this study is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>rst step in the study of buyer–seller engagement during online auctions, it has several limitations. First, we do not examine the content of buyer–seller engagement, merely its impact on bid amount. A <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained analysis of Q&A content may lead to a more nuanced understanding of buyer–seller engagement in online auctions. However, it is important to note that classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and analysis of short textual comments, such as the ones we observe in public Q&A, is anything but trivial (Li et al. 2016) because these comments are highly unstructured, con text oriented, and often full of shortened words or acronyms (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011). Therefore, given the nature of the content, textual analysis of the seriousness or importance of a question or comment is very challenging (Andrews and Benzing 2007). Furthermore, recent studies have shown that the level of a seller’s engagement (e.g., number of answers or number of messages) affects bidding behavior irrespective of the content of engagement (Ducarroz et al. 2016). Indeed, our study also shows that a higher level of seller engagement (number of answers) has a greater impact on bidding behavior.

Second, we do not consider the content and quality of auction listings beyond some basic indicators such as the number of photographs. A text mining approach can be applied to explore the linkage between the con tent of the listing and Q&A to examine seller strategy and bidding behavior. For instance, a seller may intentionally provide less information in a listing with the hope of jump-starting buyers’ questions for an eventual bene<sup>fi</sup>t from public Q&A. However, we have observed that the heterogeneity and subjectivity in auction listing descriptions make content analysis very challenging. Though auction platforms attempt to standardize the content or listing descriptions across different auctions (as we observe from auction platforms’ APIs) to make comparisons easier for the bidders, product heterogeneity and sellers’ desire to differentiate their listings to compete with other sellers often results in nonstandardized content and structure, complicating any text analysis/mining process.

Third, our data set is limited in terms of the level of sellers’ negative ratings. A larger spread in negative ratings may provide a better understanding of interactions of negative ratings and the seller engagement process. Because sellers with highly negative ratings are more likely to exit the market (Cabral and Hortac¸su 2010), it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain a real-world data set with highly negatively rated sellers. Furthermore, our categorization of sellers as EstablishedPositive, etc., is sensitive to median values in our data set. However, these issue can be tackled in experimental studies.

Fourth, the design of the auction platform from which we obtained our data posed some additional challenges. Because participants in online markets play the roles of both buyers and sellers (Gopal et al. 2006), seller ratings reported by the platform are accumulated ratings for both buying and selling transactions. Feedback ratings from selling transactions only may more accurately explain how seller reputation moderates the effect of Q&A on bidding behavior. Also, sellers’ ratings are accumulated since they joined the auction platform. For example, a negative rating earned 10 years ago still shows on a seller’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le and on the auction page. A more recent summary of seller ratings on pro<sup>fi</sup>le and auction pages may in<sup>fl</sup>uence buyers’ perceptions about seller reputation and bidding behavior. For instance, auction platforms such as eBay summarize ratings for one month, six months, and one year, probably in an effort to give more weight to recent behavior. eBay also attempts to unpack seller ratings by providing sellers performance in the item description, communication with buyers, shipping time, etc., based on input from buyers. Such detailed information about sellers’ performance in previous transactions likely helps buyers more than just a score from 1 (negative) to 1 (positive).

Finally, <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments to compare online auctions with and without public Q&A features may con-<sup>fi</sup>rm the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the mechanism in practice. However, platforms try to distinguish themselves from their competitors through innovative and often unique features, which makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to conduct a comparative study across different platforms.

## 9. Managerial Implications and Conclusion

The dynamics and outcomes of online auctions are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a number of factors relating to the market, its penetration levels, the number and types of participants, and the features of the products offered. Nevertheless, the results of this study demonstrate that buyer–seller engagement via public Q&A during online auctions has a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the bidding behavior . Given that a myriad of factors can affect bids, the effect of seller engagement that we observe is substantial enough to merit serious consideration. For instance, an increase in bids by a few percentage points may result in multimillion dollar incremental pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for large auction platforms. Thus, major auction platforms such as eBay and Yahoo that do not yet support public Q&A between buyers and sellers may consider enhancing their existing mechanisms with greater transparency and information sharing.

Our results indicate that the effects of the public Q&A process vary across auction dimensions such as product type and seller type/reputation. This suggests that the platform provider can take such factors into considera tion in determining the extent to which it promotes, eval uates, and supports public Q&A mechanisms. In addition, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings that (1) the impact of seller engagement is moderated by seller reputation (and more so for com plex products such as used cars), (2) the marginal effects of engagement have opposite trends for positive and neg ative seller reputation levels (votes), and (3) buyers’ par ticipation in buyer–seller engagement has a direct effect on their bids, with a spillover effect on passive observers, provide insights for sellers as well. Given that online auc tion platforms attract not only individual sellers but also retailers, dealers, and other businesses, the success of these businesses in their online auction offerings can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their participation in public Q&A processes during their auctions, and this is useful information for both the sellers and the platform providers.

Although the public Q&A process in online auctions is initiated by potential buyers, the participation of both buyers and sellers is critical for the process to be successful. Indeed, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the participation of both buyers and sellers in public Q&A results in higher bid amounts, which eventually bene<sup>fi</sup>ts sellers. Thus, an auction platform may be well served by offering Q&A mechanisms that are easy to use, accessible, and compatible with a variety of devices/services, thereby making it easy to ask questions and increasing buyer participation. Furthermore, they should alert sellers to buyers’ questions and encourage them to respond, and also notify interested buyers of Q&A updates in an auction, since such actions can increase buyers’ engagement and continued participation in the auction.

Finally, we believe that public Q&A-based engagement mechanisms could complement information exchanges such as online reviews for products/services (Banerjee et al. 2021) in other types of online markets as well, potentially transforming users from passive consumers of content to active participants, thereby increasing the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of market transactions.
