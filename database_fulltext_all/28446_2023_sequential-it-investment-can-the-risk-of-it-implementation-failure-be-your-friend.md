# Sequential IT Investment: Can the Risk of IT Implementation Failure Be Your Friend?

Vidyanand Choudhary,<sup>a</sup> Mingdi Xin,<sup>a,</sup>\* Zhe Zhang<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. An extensive literature studies the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to be the <sup>fi</sup>rst to invest in innovative technologies such as information technologies (ITs). However, IT investment often faces the risk of implementation failure. How would such risk affect an early adopter’s incentive to invest? Do late adopters bene<sup>fi</sup>t from information about the early adopter’s investment? In this paper, we investigate these questions in a context in which two <sup>fi</sup>rms, a leader and a follower, invest in a cost-reducing IT sequentially. This paper differs from prior literature in two aspects: First, IT adoption is nonexclusive and available to all client <sup>fi</sup>rms. Second, IT implementation can fail. In this case, the follower may have information about the leader’s IT investment level or implementation outcome before making an investment decision. We use a Hotelling model of duopoly competition to examine how IT implementation failure risks and the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment may affect the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to sequentially invest in IT. Our results show that the risk of IT implementation failure impacts the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment incentives and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts through three distinct effects: the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation; competition mitigation; and uncertaintydriven, cost-based differentiation effects, and these three effects may drive the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in opposite directions. The follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment level before making an IT investment decision gives the leader a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage and the follower a disadvantage. In contrast, the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT implementation outcome can bene<sup>fi</sup>t both the leader and the follower. Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that a spaced-out sequential IT investment schedule, in which the follower makes the investment decision after the leader’s IT investment level and implementation outcome are both known, leads to the highest industry-wide IT investment and social surplus.

## 1. Introduction

An extensive literature studies the advantages for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to be the <sup>fi</sup>rst to invest in innovative technologies such as information technologies (ITs). It <sup>fi</sup>nds that <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantages may arise from technological leadership because of the learning curve or patent protection, preemption of scarce resources, network externalities, and consumer preferences among competing products that are driven by the order of entry.

On the other hand, IT investment often faces the risk of implementation failure. In this case, a late adopter may have information about the early adopter’s IT investment level and implementation outcome before making an investment decision. How would the risk of implementation failure affect a pioneering <sup>fi</sup>rm’s incentive to invest in IT? Do late adopters bene<sup>fi</sup>t from information about the pioneer’s investment? These questions are of substantial importance as IT-driven innovation has become the engine of economic growth, and so understanding <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to invest in IT is critical to sustain this growth. In this paper, we investigate these questions in a context in which <sup>fi</sup>rms engage in sequential investment in an innovative IT.

This paper differs from prior literature in two main aspects: First, IT adoption is nonexclusive and available to all client <sup>fi</sup>rms. In contrast, prior literature mainly focuses on innovations that result in exclusive intellectual property rights, such as patent protection, and assumes that all returns go exclusively to the winner of the patent race (e.g., Green and Scotchmer 1995, Bessen and Maskin 2009). In addition, we focus on IT investment carried out with the objective of reducing marginal costs rather than creating new products. For instance, supply chain management systems have been adopted broadly by organizations to reduce production costs.

Second, IT implementation can fail. Studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT implementation faces persistently high failure rates (Nelson 2007, Dwivedi et al. 2015). Complex enterprise systems, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, face an implementation failure rate in the range of 50%–75% (Andriole 2016). In this case, a late adopter (the follower) may have information about the early adopter’s (the leader’s) IT investment (e.g., investment level or implementation outcome) before making an investment decision. We investigate how the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment affects the leader’s and the follower’s investment incentives and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and whether these effects differ depending on the type of information that the follower has about the leader’s IT investment. These research questions have not been considered in prior literature.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we consider a standard Hotelling model in which two competing <sup>fi</sup>rms are horizontally differentiated. There is an opportunity to invest in an IT to reduce the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ marginal production costs. Following prior literature, we assume that a higher IT investment leads to a lower new marginal cost if the technology is implemented successfully.<sup>1</sup> However, the outcome of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT implementation is uncertain. If a <sup>fi</sup>rm implements the IT successfully, then it achieves a lower marginal cost. If a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT implementation fails, then it retains its original marginal cost but bears the cost of the IT investment. In the main model, we assume that the leader and the follower have the same probability of achieving implementation success. We relax this assumption later in an extension.

The leader makes an IT investment decision before the follower. Sequential adoption of ITs by <sup>fi</sup>rms in the same industry is quite common. For instance, FedEx and American Airlines are known to have pioneered the adoption of several ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-enhancing ITs within their industries (Xin and Choudhary 2019). The sequential nature of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment may arise for many reasons, for example, if one <sup>fi</sup>rm becomes aware of the IT before the competitor. It is well-known that the successful deployment of American Airlines’ online reservation system, SABRE or Semiautomated Business Research Environment, was the result of a chance meeting between a high-ranking IBM salesman and American Airlines’ then president on a <sup>fl</sup>ight from Los Angeles to New York City. The sequential IT investment may also be the result of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ limited “attention budget” (Radner 2003). A client <sup>fi</sup>rm may not have the capacity to constantly look out for new ITs to adopt as soon as they become available. Instead, it is likely that a <sup>fi</sup>rm considers IT investment decisions only when triggered by certain organization-speci<sup>fi</sup>c events, such as periodic budget planning, changes in business needs, availability of organizational resources (e.g., budget or talent), or when the current system is due for a major upgrade. In these cases, one <sup>fi</sup>rm may consider investing in an IT before other <sup>fi</sup>rms.

The follower may have some knowledge about the leader’s IT investment before making an IT investment decision. In this paper, we consider the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s investment level and implementation outcome. To disentangle the impact of different knowledge types, we examine two cases: Case 1, the follower makes the IT investment decision after knowing the leader’s IT investment level but before knowing the leader’s implementation outcome. We label this case as “outcome unknown.” In Case 2, the follower makes the IT investment deci sion after knowing the leader’s IT investment level and implementation outcome. We label this case as “outcome known.” The follower may learn about the leader’s IT investment from public media, IT vendors, or third-party publications. For instance, CIO.com reported FedEx’s early adoption of a cloud-based technology management tool to reduce costs and described a successful implementation outcome.<sup>2</sup> FedEx’s adoption of a cloud-based ERP system is broadly covered by the vendor’s blogs and IT media (e.g., Oracle blogs and TechTarget.com).

We examine the impact of IT implementation failure risks and the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment on the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment incentives and expected returns. Our results show that the risk of IT implementation failure impacts the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment incentives and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts through three distinct effects: the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation; competition mitigation; and uncertainty-driven, cost-based differentiation effects, and these three effects may drive the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in opposite directions. Interestingly, in competitive markets, when IT is very effective in reducing costs, the leader’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rst decreases and then increases as IT implementation becomes more likely to succeed. The IT investment, however, increases continuously. The follower’s knowl edge about the leader’s IT investment level before making an IT investment decision gives the leader a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage and the follower a disadvantage. In contrast, the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT implementation outcome can bene<sup>fi</sup>t both the leader and the follower. Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that a spaced-out sequential IT investment schedule, in which the follower makes the investment decision after the leader’s invest ment level and implementation outcome are both known, leads to the highest industry-wide IT investment and social surplus. We consider several extensions of the main model and discuss the contributions of this paper at the end.

## 2. Literature Review

This paper is related to several streams of prior literature: (1) literature on the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage, (2) economics literature on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to invest in innovation, and (3) information systems (IS) literature on strategic IT investment.

We begin with the literature on the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage. Lieberman and Montgomery (1988) de<sup>fi</sup>ne a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage as the excess pro<sup>fi</sup>ts that accrue to pioneering <sup>fi</sup>rms. An extensive literature examines the sources of <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantages. It identi<sup>fi</sup>es several factors that give rise to <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantages: technological leadership as a result of the learning curve (Spence 1981) or patent protection (Reinganum 1983); preemption of scarce resources, including input factors and product characteristics space (Schmalensee 1978); network externalities; and consumer preferences among competing products that are driven by the order of entry (Carpenter and Nakamoto 1989). It also identi<sup>fi</sup>es factors that may bene<sup>fi</sup>t the <sup>fi</sup>rms that move later: free riding on the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover’s investment through spillovers and imitation, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from reduction in uncertainty over time—either technological or market uncertainty, leveraging new disruptive technologies, and reduction in the cost of investment over time. Lieberman and Montgomery (1988, 1998) and Kerin et al. (1992) provide great reviews of this literature.

Our paper differs from this literature in that we consider new factors that are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to IT investment and differ from those considered in prior literature. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, (1) adoption of IT is nonexclusive, and (2) IT implementation can fail. We investigate how the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment affects the leader’s and the follower’s investment incentives and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and whether these effects differ depending on the type of information that the follower has (e.g., investment level versus implementation outcome). (3) In our main model, the late adopter faces the same implementation risk (no spillover or imitation) and cost of IT investment as the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover.

The second stream of literature spans a range of topics related to <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to invest in innovation. This literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es a number of factors that may impact <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to invest in innovation, including the degree of knowledge spillover between early and late investors (Choi 1993, d’Aspremont and Jacquemin 1988), level of market competition (Arrow 1962, Schumpeter 2010), information asymmetry (Spulber 1995, Christen 2005), ownership structure of the <sup>fi</sup>rms (Matsumura and Matsushima 2004), and uncertainty regarding the investment outcome (Reinganum 1983). In general, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings depend on whether the innovation leads to exclusive intellectual property rights (e.g., patent protection) or not and whether it is a product- or process-based innovation. This paper is related to the substream of the literature that studies <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment in nonexclusive, process-based innovations with the objective to reduce production costs.

Only a few papers in this substream consider the possibility of investment failure on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to invest in a sequential investment game (Fudenberg and Tirole 1984, Hoppe 2000). Hoppe (2000) examines <sup>fi</sup>rms’ technology adoption decision when the value of the technology is uncertain but the leader’s adop tion of the technology reveals its value so that there is no uncertainty for the follower. Moreover, the cost of technology adoption decreases over time, and the technology requires a lumpy investment so that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can only choose whether to adopt but cannot change the level of investment. Fudenberg and Tirole (1984) develop a reduced form model to examine <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to invest in cost-reducing R&D in a sequential move game. In their model, the amount of cost reduction from successful investment is exogenously determined, whereas the probability of success depends on the amount of investment. Further, if only one <sup>fi</sup>rm succeeds, then it is assumed to have monopoly power. In contrast, in our model, the amount of cost reduction from a successful IT imple mentation depends on the amount of investment. The leader and the follower face the same probability of implementation failure and IT investment cost. Firms compete in a horizontally differentiated market, and IT implementation success does not lead to monopoly power: both <sup>fi</sup>rms may have positive market shares even if only one <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT investment is successful. A separate literature examines the timing of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ R&D investment (e.g., Reinganum 1983, 1989). The relative timing of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment is not relevant in our model because none of the key factors changes with time and the model remains the same as far as the sequence of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ decisions and the information structure remain the same.

Several papers in the IS literature examine <sup>fi</sup>rms incentive to invest in IT and their anticipated returns in a competitive market. Most do not consider the possibility of IT implementation failure, which is the focus of this paper. For instance, Thatcher and Pingry (2004) examine <sup>fi</sup>rms’ price and quality choices in a duopoly model given four different types of cost structures. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that marginal cost-saving IT investments increase pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, productivity, and consumer welfare, whereas their impact on <sup>fi</sup>xed and quality-related costs remains ambiguous. Zhu and Weyant (2003) study <sup>fi</sup>rms’ decision to adopt a technology in a Cournot duopoly game when one <sup>fi</sup>rm knows its competitor’s cost structure but the competitor does not know its cost structure (information asymmetry). The technology adoption requires a single lumpy investment. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that informational advantage can mitigate cost disadvantage depending on the relative degree of information asymmetry. Demirhan et al. (2005, 2007) analyze the role of declining IT costs on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to invest in IT to improve product quality in a duopoly game. Demirhan et al. (2005) <sup>fi</sup>nd that both <sup>fi</sup>rms increase quality with the reduction in IT costs in price-sensitive markets. Demirhan et al. (2007) analyze the impact of consumers’ switching cost in addition to declining IT costs on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investments. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that declining IT costs reduce the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t, but it retains market leadership when switching costs are endogenous. Barua et al. (1991) develop a reduced form model of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment to improve product quality in a duopoly setting. They compare the outcome of a simultaneous game to a sequential game and <sup>fi</sup>nd that the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of each competitor is greater in the sequential game. They also show that the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover invests less in the sequential move game than in the simultaneous game.

Only one paper considers the possibility of IT implementation failure. Xin and Choudhary (2019) analyze a simultaneous IT investment game to study the role of implementation uncertainty. Our paper differs from Xin and Choudhary (2019) in several aspects: First, we study a sequential IT investment game. As we show in Section 5, the possibility of implementation failure impacts the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second mover’s IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>t differently. Such a differential impact is absent in a simultaneous investment game. Second, when IT implementation can fail, the follower can make the investment decision after the leader’s investment strategy and implementation outcome are known and make contingent adjustment to maximize the expected payoff. Our results show that the follower’s such knowledge has a complex impact on investment incentives and may bene<sup>fi</sup>t or hurt the follower. In contrast, both <sup>fi</sup>rms have the same information on their competitors in a simultaneous move game. Finally, we show that the sequence of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment has important welfare implications, which is again absent in a simultaneous move game.

## 3. Model

We analyze a model of duopoly competition in which both <sup>fi</sup>rms may invest in an IT to reduce their marginal cost of production. We use the horizontal differentiation framework of Hotelling (1929) and d’Aspremont et al. (1979). The two <sup>fi</sup>rms are located at the ends of a unit line, and consumers are uniformly distributed along the line. Without loss of generality, we assume that the leader <sup>fi</sup>rm is located at one, and the follower <sup>fi</sup>rm is located at zero. Each consumer demands one unit of the product. The distance between the location of a consumer and that of a <sup>fi</sup>rm represents the difference between the consumer’s preferred product and the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s product offering. We model the disutility that a consumer incurs from buying a product that does not <sup>fi</sup>t the consumer’s preference perfectly (or consumer mis<sup>fi</sup>t costs) as a quadratic function of the distance between the consumer and the seller. That is, if a consumer located at x buys from the leader <sup>fi</sup>rm (or the follower <sup>fi</sup>rm), then the consumer incurs a mis<sup>fi</sup>t cost of $t ( 1 - x ) ^ { 2 }$ (or $t x ^ { 2 } )$ , where $t > 0$ . Parameter t represents the degree of differentiation between the two competing products and is often used as a measure for the intensity of market competition in prior literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Villas-Boas and Schmidt-Mohr 1999). When t is small, the products are less differentiated (or more substitutable) because con sumers do not have strong preferences for one product over the other (or $| t x ^ { 2 } - t ( 1 - x ) ^ { 2 } |$ is small). Thus, lower t implies more intensive competition. The payoff that a consumer gains from purchasing one unit of the product from the follower <sup>fi</sup>rm is $U - t x ^ { 2 } - p _ { F }$ , and that from the leader <sup>fi</sup>rm is $U - t ( 1 - x ) ^ { 2 } - p _ { L } ,$ where $U > 0$ and is the maximum utility that a consumer derives from the product, and $p _ { L }$ and $p _ { F }$ are the prices of the leader and the follower, respectively. We retain a common assumption for horizontal models that U is large enough so that the market is always fully covered. We relax this assumption later in an extension and allow the market to be partially covered in equilibrium.

Given the current technology, both <sup>fi</sup>rms have a constant marginal production cost $c _ { O }$ . There is an opportunity to invest in an IT to reduce marginal costs. The outcome of IT implementation is uncertain, however. With probability $\alpha ,$ where $0 < \alpha < 1$ , IT implementation succeeds, in which case <sup>fi</sup>rm i is able to reduce the marginal cost to $c _ { i }$ given an IT investment $f _ { i } = k ( c _ { O } - c _ { i } ) ^ { 2 } .$ , where $0 \leq c _ { i } \leq c _ { O } , k > 0 .$ , and i L or F (for leader or follower). This IT investment function implies that the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ marginal return to their IT investment decreases the more that they invest in IT. Parameter k is a scaling parameter, representing the inverse effectiveness of converting IT investment into marginal cost reduction. With probability 1 α , IT implementation fails, in which case the <sup>fi</sup>rm retains its original marginal cost $c _ { O }$ but bears the cost of the IT investment. As a <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we assume that both <sup>fi</sup>rms face the same probability of implementation success $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \alpha )$ . We relax this assumption later in an extension in which the <sup>fi</sup>rms face different probabilities of implementation success. The <sup>fi</sup>rms know each other’s success probabilities, possibly from observing each other’s IT implementation outcomes in the past.

The two <sup>fi</sup>rms make their IT investment decisions sequentially: the leader makes its IT investment decision before the follower. In this case, the follower may have some knowledge about the leader’s IT investment before making its own investment decision. We consider two alternatives:

Case 1. The follower makes its IT investment decision after knowing the leader’s investment level<sup>3</sup> but before knowing the leader’s implementation outcome. We label this case the outcome unknown case.

Case 2. The follower makes its IT investment decision after knowing the leader’s investment level and implementation outcome. We label this case the outcome known case.

The strategic interaction between the two <sup>fi</sup>rms happens in two stages (see Figure 1). In the investment stage, the leader makes its IT investment decision <sup>fi</sup>rst. In Case 1, the follower observes the leader’s IT investment level and makes its own IT investment decision before knowing the leader’s implementation outcome. In Case 2, the follower makes its IT investment decision after observing both the leader’s investment level and implementation outcome. After both <sup>fi</sup>rms have completed their IT implementation, they observe each other’s implementation outcomes and new marginal costs. Note that there are four possible implementation outcomes: (i) both <sup>fi</sup>rms have succeeded (ss); (ii) both <sup>fi</sup>rms have failed (ff); (iii) the leader has succeeded, whereas the follower failed (sf); and (iv) the leader has failed, whereas the follower succeeded (fs). Next, in the pricing stage, the two <sup>fi</sup>rms announce their prices simultaneously. Consumers observe the prices offered by both <sup>fi</sup>rms and make a purchase decision. Trade takes place.

This setup describes those scenarios in which the follower completes its IT implementation shortly after the leader (e.g., cloud-based IT implementations that require less time). Later, in an extension, we examine another scenario in which there is a nonnegligible time gap between the leader’s and the follower’s IT implementation, so the leader is able to take advantage of the IT investment before the follower completes the IT implementation. We show that the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings continue to hold in the extension (see Section 5.4).

We assume $6 t ( 9 k t - 1 ) / ( 1 + 5 4 k t ( 6 k t - 1 ) ) < c _ { O } < 3 t$ and $k \cdot t > 1 / 6$ . These assumptions ensure that both <sup>fi</sup>rms have positive market shares irrespective of the implementation outcome and the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ new marginal costs are nonnegative when their IT implementation succeeds. These assumptions allow us to focus on interior solutions.

We focus on the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium of the sequential investment game and solve the <sup>fi</sup>rms strategies through backward induction. We <sup>fi</sup>rst analyze the pricing stage and solve the equilibrium pricing strategies and the two <sup>fi</sup>rms’ pro<sup>fi</sup>ts given each implementation outcome. Next, we focus on the investment stage and solve the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ optimal investment strategies given the two alternative sequential investment schedules.

## 4.2. The Investment Stage

Now, we consider the investment stage of the game and solve the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ optimal investment decisions. Note that, in the event of a failed implementation, each <sup>fi</sup>rm retains its original marginal cost despite the IT investment, or $c _ { L , f s } = c _ { L , f f } = c _ { O }$ and $c _ { F , s f } = c _ { F , f f } = c _ { O } .$ On the other hand, in the event of a successful implementation, each <sup>fi</sup>rm obtains a new and lower marginal cost. Let $c _ { L }$ denote the leader’s new marginal cost and $c _ { F }$ the follower’s new marginal cost. Then, $c _ { L , s s } = c _ { L , s f } = c _ { L }$ and $c _ { F , s s } = c _ { F , f _ { \sf S } } = c _ { F }$ Because the IT investment level $f _ { i } = k ( c _ { O } - c _ { i } ) ^ { 2 }$ , there is a one-to-one mapping between a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT investment level, $, f _ { i } ,$ and its new marginal cost when IT implementation succeeds, $c _ { i } ,$ where $i = L , F$ . Effectively, the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s problem of <sup>fi</sup>nding the optimal investment level $( f _ { i } )$ is equivalent to <sup>fi</sup>nding a new marginal cost in the event of a successful implementation (c ) to maximize its expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Therefore, we formulate the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment decision as a decision to <sup>fi</sup>nd the optimal new marginal cost in the event of a successful implementation (c ) to maximize the expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

As we discussed previously, we examine the <sup>fi</sup>rms optimal IT investment strategies given two alternative sequences of IT investment: Case 1, sequential investment with the leader’s implementation outcome unknown to the follower (Section 4.2.1), and Case $^ { 2 , }$ sequential investment with the leader’s implementation outcome known to the follower (Section 4.2.2).

## 5. Uncovering the Role of IT Implementation Failure Risk and Information in Driving Sequential IT Investment

The objective of this research is to understand how the IT implementation failure rate and the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment may affect <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment incentives and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. We focus on the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment level in Section 5.1. We show that the leader enjoys a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage, whereas the follower suffers from a second mover disadvantage when the follower makes its investment decision after knowing the leader’s investment level. The risk of IT implementation failure mitigates the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage. However, both the leader and the follower may be better off when IT implementation can fail than when it cannot.

In Section 5.2, we focus on the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome. Interestingly, both the leader and the follower may bene<sup>fi</sup>t when the follower makes its investment decision after knowing the leader’s implementation outcome. We study the welfare implication of the <sup>fi</sup>rms sequential IT investment schedule in Section 5.3. Finally, we examine several extensions in Section 5.4 and show that our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings are robust to changes in model assumptions.

## 5.1. The Role of IT Implementation Failure Rate and Follower’s Knowledge About Leader’s IT Investment Level

In this section, we start with a benchmark case in which IT implementation does not fail, or α 1. We compare the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts when the follower makes its IT investment decision after knowing the leader’s investment level (Case 1) to those when the two <sup>fi</sup>rms make their investment decisions simultaneously. Xin and Choudhary (2019) solve the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ optimal IT investment strategies and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts when both <sup>fi</sup>rms make their IT investment decisions simultaneously. According to their paper, the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ equilibrium IT investment levels are $\begin{array} { r } { f _ { i } ^ { S } = k \Big ( \frac { 3 \alpha t } { 1 8 k t - ( 1 - \alpha ) \alpha } \Big ) ^ { 2 } } \end{array}$ and their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are $\begin{array} { r } { E ( \pi _ { i } ^ { S } ) = \frac { t ( 3 2 4 k ^ { 2 } t ^ { 2 } + \alpha ^ { 2 } ( 1 + 1 8 k t ) - \alpha ^ { 4 } - 3 6 k \alpha t ) } { 2 \left( 1 8 k t - ( 1 - \alpha ) \alpha \right) ^ { 2 } } , i = 1 , 2 } \end{array}$ (superscript S denotes the case in which both <sup>fi</sup>rms make their IT investment decisions simultaneously). Proposition 1. When IT implementation always succeeds (α 1),

b. The leader invests more in IT and expects a higher profit, whereas the follower invests less and expects a lower profit in the sequential investment Case 1 than in the simultaneous investment game.

c. The leader and the follower both have lower profits when they have the opportunity to invest in IT than when they do not in the sequential investment Case 1.

The leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantage in the sequential investment game arises from its ability to impact the follower’s return to IT investment and, ultimately, payoff through its own IT investment. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when the two <sup>fi</sup>rms make their investment decisions sequentially, the leader’s IT investment has a stronger impact on the follower’s investment decision and payoff because the follower makes its IT investment decision after the leader’s investment is already known. Figure 2 shows how the follower responds to the leader’s investment level in a sequential investment game. The follower’s best response function is downward sloping. That is, if the leader invests more, the follower responds with a lower IT investment. When IT implementation always succeeds, a higher IT investment allows the leader to achieve a lower new marginal cost, a larger market share and pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin than the follower. Therefore, the leader leverages the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage and invests heavily in IT (Proposition 1). The leader’s heavy IT investment lowers the follower’s marginal return to its IT investment, and so the follower responds with a lower IT investment. Ultimately, the leader achieves an advantage in marginal cost and a higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t compared with the follower.

Implementation Uncertainty-Driven, Cost-Based Differentiation Effect. The risk of implementation failure creates a new opportunity for marginal cost-based differentiation. This effect can bene<sup>fi</sup>t a <sup>fi</sup>rm if the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT implementation succeeds but can hurt it otherwise. The overall impact of the uncertaintydriven, cost-based differentiation effect depends on the probability of implementation success (α) and on whether the <sup>fi</sup>rm is the leader or the follower. The follower is always better off given a differentiated implementation outcome (i.e., sf or fs) even though its implementation may fail than an outcome in which both <sup>fi</sup>rms succeed (i.e., ss). On the other hand, the leader is better off given a differentiated implementation outcome (i.e., sf or fs) than an outcome in which both <sup>fi</sup>rms succeed only when α is small. When α is large, however, it is worse off.<sup>5</sup>

Evidently, these three effects may drive <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in opposite directions. In particular, the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation and the uncertainty-driven, cost-based differentiation effects predict that the follower’s IT investment may increase as IT implementation failure becomes more likely, whereas the competition mitigation effect predicts the opposite. The <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation effect predicts that the leader’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t suffers as IT implementation failure becomes more likely, whereas the competition mitigation effect predicts the opposite. The overall impact of IT implementation failure rate depends on which effect dominates.

When the market is competitive or IT investment is effective in reducing marginal costs (see Figures 3 and 4), the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation effect dominates as the probability of implementation success (α) declines initially from one. This is because the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage is most prominent when the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment always succeeds (α 1). As α decreases initially, the leader invests less, which improves the follower’s return to IT investment. In addition, the follower bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the uncertaintydriven, cost-based differentiation effect. This leads to an increase in the follower’s IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>t, whereas a decline in the leader’s IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>t, suggesting signi<sup>fi</sup>cant weakening of the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage. As α declines further, the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage is weaker, and so is the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation effect. The competition mitigation effect then dominates when the probability of implementation success (α) is relatively low. In this case, both <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investments decrease as IT implementation success becomes unlikely (α decreases), and their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts improve.

On the other hand, when the market is not competi tive (or is highly differentiated) or IT investment is ineffective in reducing marginal costs (or kt is large), the competition mitigation effect dominates for any α. This is because, <sup>fi</sup>rst, both factors diminish the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation effect. Market differentia tion weakens the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage: to win new customers in a highly differentiated market, the leader has to offer lower prices, which requires higher IT investment to reduce costs. This reduces the leader’s return to IT investment. With more market differentiation, the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage diminishes, and so does the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation effect. Similarly, when IT investment is ineffective in reducing marginal costs, the leader faces a weaker <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage because higher IT investment is required to reduce costs. The <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage mitigation effect also diminishes. Second, the uncertainty-driven, cost-based differentiation effect is also weaker when the market is highly differentiated or IT investment is ineffective in reducing marginal costs as higher IT investment costs are required for the <sup>fi</sup>rms to gain market share or to reduce marginal costs. Consequently, the competition mitigation effect becomes dominant. Both <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investments decrease as IT implementation success becomes unlikely (α decreases), and their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts improve. Both <sup>fi</sup>rms are better off when IT implementation can fail than when it cannot.

## 5.2. The Role of Follower’s Knowledge About Leader’s IT Implementation Outcome

When IT implementation may fail, can the follower bene<sup>fi</sup>t from knowing the leader’s implementation outcome in addition to the leader’s investment level before making an IT investment decision? How would the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome impact the leader’s IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>t? To answer these questions, we compare the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts when the follower makes its IT investment decision after knowing the leader’s investment level and implementation outcome (Case 2) with those when the follower makes its IT investment decision after knowing only the leader’s investment level (Case 1). Proposition 3 formalizes our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Proposition 3. The effect of the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome is

a. The leader invests more and expects a higher profit in Case 2 than in Case 1.

b. The follower’s profit may be higher or lower in Case 2 than in Case 1.

Therefore, the leader bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and the follower may bene<sup>fi</sup>t or be hurt by its knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome before making its IT investment decision. The leader bene<sup>fi</sup>ts because the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome creates an additional source of <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage and allows the leader to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the follower’s IT investment decision more directly. As we discussed previously, the leader’s IT investment in<sup>fl</sup>uences the follower’s return to IT investment. When the follower makes its investment decision without knowing the leader’s implementation outcome, the follower discounts the impact of the leader’s IT investment on its payoff because the leader’s IT investment may fail. Therefore, a higher IT investment by the leader discourages the follower from investing in IT but not by much. In contrast, when the follower makes its investment decision after knowing that the leader’s implementation has succeeded, the follower takes full account of the impact of the leader’s investment on its payoff. (If the leader’s implementation fails, its investment level is irrelevant to the follower in both cases.) Thus, an increase in the leader’s IT investment leads to a larger decrease in the follower’s return to IT investment in this case. This effect adds to the leader’s advantage as a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover and improves the leader’s IT investment incentive and expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

The leader’s additional advantage does not necessarily lead to the follower’s disadvantage, however. The follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome can either bene<sup>fi</sup>t or hurt the follower.

Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Resulting from the Knowledge. Having such information allows the follower to vary its IT investment contingent on the outcome of the leader’s IT implementation so that its investment decision maximizes its expected payoff given the speci<sup>fi</sup>c scenario. If the leader’s implementation fails, then the follower’s IT investment decision is independent of the leader’s investment amount. If the leader’s implementation succeeds, then the follower makes its investment decision taking full account of the impact of the leader’s investment on its payoff. In contrast, without such information, the follower makes its investment decision based on an expected outcome, and the investment amount does not maximize the follower’s expected payoff given either of the leader’s implementation outcomes (i.e., success or failure). Indeed, the follower’s expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t is always higher when it makes the investment decision with instead of without knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome if we hold the leader’s IT investment constant.

Loss Resulting from the Knowledge. As we discussed previously, this information creates an additional source of <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage for the leader and leads to the leader’s increased IT investment, which hurts the follower’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t when the leader’s IT implementation succeeds.

Therefore, having information about the leader’s implementation outcome can either bene<sup>fi</sup>t or hurt the follower, depending on the extent of the bene<sup>fi</sup>t compared with the extent of the loss. As we discuss subsequently, the trade-off relies on the probability of implementation success (α), the extent of market competition (t), and the effectiveness of the IT in reducing marginal costs (k). Corollary 1 formalizes our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Corollary 1. The follower’s gain from knowing the leader’s implementation outcome in addition to the leader’s invest ment level outweighs its loss from this knowledge when k t < 0:17 and the probability of IT implementation success α is close to one.

5.2.1. Impact of Implementation Failure Rate on the Follower’s Payoff. Figure 5 shows how the follower’s expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t changes with the probability of implementation success (α) when the follower makes its investment decision with or without knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome. The left and right plots show the follower’s expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t given different ranges of α. Evidently, the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome hurts the follower’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t when α is relatively low but can bene<sup>fi</sup>t the follower’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t when α is close to one.<sup>6</sup>

The intuition is as follows. Recall that, when the follower makes its IT investment decision after knowing the leader’s implementation outcome, the leader enjoys a stronger advantage as a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover but only when the leader’s implementation succeeds. As α increases initially, the leader’s implementation is more likely to succeed, and the enhanced <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage effect becomes stronger. Therefore, the leader invests more in IT, which hurts the follower’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Even though the follower gains from its ability to make contingent investment decisions relative to the leader’s implementation outcome, such gain is less than the follower’s loss from a worsened second mover disadvantage. Thus, the follower is worse off from knowing the leader’s implementation outcome when α is not large.

In contrast, as α approaches one, the follower may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from knowing the leader’s implementation outcome before making its own investment decision. As α approaches one, the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage arising from the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome diminishes: even if the follower does not know the leader’s implementation outcome when making its investment decision, it does not discount the impact of the leader’s investment on its payoff by much because the leader’s investment is likely to succeed. Therefore, the leader’s IT investment has a similar effect on the follower’s payoff whether the follower knows the leader’s implementation outcome or not. Indeed, as shown in Figure 6, the leader’s IT investment strategies in these two cases converge as α approaches one. This limits the follower’s loss from knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome. On the other hand, the follower continues to gain from the ability to make contingent IT investment decisions based on the leader’s implementation outcome, especially when the leader’s implementation fails, as the follower would need to drastically change its investment strategy to take advantage of the unlikely outcome (see Figure 6). The intuition is as follows. When the market is more competitive (or t is small), it is easier to sway customers and gain market share because it requires smaller price differences. Consequently, the leader is more motivated to invest in IT to lower production costs and improve market share. When the leader’s implementation outcome is known to the follower before the follower’s investment decision, the follower responds with a lower IT investment if the leader’s implementation succeeds because the leader’s higher IT investment lowers the follower’s return to IT investment; if the leader’s IT implementation fails, the follower responds with a higher IT investment when t is small to gain market share. This leads to the follower’s more divergent investment levels contingent on the leader’s implementation outcome given a highly competitive market (or t is small). In this case, not knowing the leader’s implementation outcome becomes more costly to the follower because the follower would have to compromise between these two diverging forces. Therefore, the follower’s gain outweighs the loss from knowing the leader’s implementation outcome when the market is highly competitive (t is small). ## Proposition 4. As the market becomes more competitive (lower t),

a. The leader invests more in both sequential investment cases: when the follower knows only the leader’s IT investment level (Case 1) and when the follower knows the leader’s IT investment level and implementation outcome (Case 2) before making its own IT investment decision.

b. The follower may invest more or less in Case 1.

c. The follower invests more when the leader’s implementation fails and invests less when the leader’s implementation succeeds in Case 2.

The impact of IT effectiveness (k) is similar to that of market competition. When IT is very effective in reducing marginal costs (lower k), the leader is more motivated to invest in IT to improve the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin and market share. This leads to the follower’s more divergent optimal investment levels given the leader’s different implementation outcomes. In this case, not knowing the leader’s implementation outcome becomes more costly to the follower as it is constrained to choose one investment level that compromises between the two diverging forces. Thus, the follower’s gain outweighs the loss from knowing the leader’s implementation outcome when IT is very effective in reducing marginal costs (or k is small). Otherwise, the fol lower’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t can be hurt by such knowledge.

Whereas in our model, the timing of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment decisions is exogenous, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, if the follower has a choice, it would prefer knowing the leader’s investment level and implementation outcome (Case 2) to knowing only the leader’s investment level (Case 1) before making an investment decision if the probability of implementation success is relatively high and either the market is highly competitive or IT is very effective in reducing marginal costs.

## 5.3. Social Welfare

We thus far focus on the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment incentives and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts when they make their IT investment decisions sequentially. In this section, we examine how IT investment and the sequence of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment decisions may affect social welfare. The following proposition shows that the opportunity to invest in the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-enhancing IT always improves social welfare.

Proposition 5. In both Cases 1 and 2, social welfare is higher when the firms have the opportunity to invest in the IT sequentially than when they do not have the opportunity.

Next, we compare social welfare, consumer surplus, and industry pro<sup>fi</sup>t under three different sequences of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment decisions: (1) the sequential investment Case 1 with the leader’s implementation outcome unknown to the follower before the follower’s investment decision, (2) the sequential investment Case 2 with the leader’s implementation outcome known to the follower before the follower’s investment decision, and (3) simultaneous investment by both <sup>fi</sup>rms. Proposition 6 formalizes our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Proposition 6. Among the three sequences of firms’ IT investment: the sequential investment Case 1 outcom unknown, sequential investment Case 2 outcome known, and the simultaneous investment game,

a. Social welfare is the highest in the sequential investment Case 2, outcome known.

b. Industry-wide profit is higher in the simultaneous investment game than in the sequential investment games (i.e., Case 1 or 2); industry-wide total IT investment is the highest in the sequential investment Case 2, outcome known.

c. Consumer surplus is the highest in the sequentia investment Case 2, outcome known.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst result (a) regarding social welfare can be counterintuitive. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Case 2 can lead to the most asymmetric investment incentives: the leader has the strongest <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantage and invests the most in IT. Such asymmetric incentives may hurt social surplus because, <sup>fi</sup>rst, the leader’s extensive investment can fail. Because the leader and the follower face the same probability of implementation success, an IT investment schedule that induces a more even distribution of investment incentives may offer an intuitively appealing diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Second, the leader achieves a much lower marginal cost than the follower if its extensive IT investment is implemented successfully. This allows the leader to offer lower prices and gain market share against the follower, which may increase the total mis<sup>fi</sup>t cost because more consumers end up buying a product that is cheaper but not a good <sup>fi</sup>t for their needs. This can hurt social surplus.

To understand why social surplus is the highest under the most spaced-out sequential IT investment schedule in which the follower makes its IT investment decision after the leader’s investment level and implementation outcome are both known (Case 2) despite these potential costs, we separate social surplus into two components: total industry pro<sup>fi</sup>t and consumer surplus. Proposition 6(b) shows that total industry pro<sup>fi</sup>t is lower when the two <sup>fi</sup>rms make sequential investment decisions. In contrast, Proposition 6(c) shows that consumer surplus is the highest in the outcome known Case 2. Therefore, the increase in social surplus in Case 2 is largely driven by the increase in consumer surplus.

Why would consumers bene<sup>fi</sup>t the most when the <sup>fi</sup>rms space out their IT investment decisions? When IT implementation can fail, the leader’s and the follower’s IT investments have four possible outcomes: both succeed, both fail, or one succeeds and one fails. Consumer surplus does not change if both <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investments fail. Given the other three outcomes, however, the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gain by the cost leader, the <sup>fi</sup>rm with the lower marginal cost post–IT investment, is the highest when the follower makes its IT investment after knowing the leader’s investment level and outcome (i.e., in Case 2). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, if the leader’s investment succeeds, we know that the leader invest the most in IT and achieves the lowest new marginal cost in Case 2. If the leader’s investment fails and the follower’s succeeds, the follower invests the most in IT and achieves the lowest new marginal cost in Case 2. Indeed, the industry-wide IT investment is the highest in the outcome known Case 2, creating an opportunity for substantial ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains. Competition then forces the <sup>fi</sup>rms to pass much of their ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency gains on to consumers through lower prices. Even though the total consumer mis<sup>fi</sup>t cost may increase in Case 2, consumers’ gain from lower prices in this case more than compensate for this loss.

## 5.4. Discussion

We examine several extensions to ensure that the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the main model are not sensitive to changes in model assumptions. These extensions also reveal interesting new insights, which we discuss in this section.

In one extension, we allow the follower to have a higher probability of implementation success than the leader. It is conceivable that the probability of successfully implementing an IT may increase over time in some cases as more clients adopt the technology, which would bene<sup>fi</sup>t the follower. In a second extension, we allow the leader to take advantage of its IT investment during the follower’s IT implementation. This setup describes those scenarios in which IT implementation takes a relatively long time to complete. In a third extension, we relax the assumption that the market is always fully covered in equilibrium so that both <sup>fi</sup>rms can gain new customers and expand their market coverage through IT investment. Please refer to the online appendix for the technical details of each extension.

Our results show that the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the main model continue to hold in all three extensions: the risk of implementation failure affects <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts through the three distinct effects identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Section 5.1, and these three effects can drive <sup>fi</sup>rms IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in opposite directions. In addition, the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment level bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the leader but hurts the follower. In contrast, the follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT implementation outcome may bene<sup>fi</sup>t both the leader and the follower.

These extensions also reveal interesting new insights.

The Follower Having a Higher Probability of Implementation Success Than the Leader. Interestingly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, despite having an advantage in achieving implementation success relative to the leader, the follower may see a declining pro<sup>fi</sup>t as the success probability increases. The intuition is as follows. The leader continues to have a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage, whereas the follower enjoys an advantage of a higher probability of implementation success. The overall impact on the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts depends on the trade-off between the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage and the follower’s advantage from a higher success probability. In some cases, the leader leverages its <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage and increases its IT investment to offset the follower’s advantage, which can hurt the follower’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t (e.g., when the leader’s probability of implementation success is relatively high and the market is highly competitive). Whereas, in other cases, the leader decreases its IT investment, which bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the follower.

<sub>•</sub> Nonnegligible IT Implementation Duration. In the main model, we assume that IT implementation completes in a relatively short period of time so that the leader does not have much to gain during the follower’s IT implementation. In this extension, we relax this assumption and allow the leader to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from its IT investment, if implemented successfully, before the follower completes its IT implementation. This setup is suitable for those scenarios in which IT implementation takes a nonnegligible period of time.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, given this additional opportunity to take advantage of its IT investment, the leader can improve its pro<sup>fi</sup>t through IT investment. Nonetheless, the leader invests less in IT. Recall that the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment is partially driven by competitive pressure as their investments also change their competitors return to IT investment. Indeed, <sup>fi</sup>rms often face a quandary of prisoner’s dilemma and invest even though it does not lead to higher expected pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. The leader’s ability to take advantage of the IT investment before the follower completes its IT implementation creates a window of opportunity for the leader to monopolize the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of IT investment without concerning the follower’s action, which alleviates the competitive pressure on the leader to invest more in IT.

Possibility of New Market Expansion. In the main model, we assume that the market is always fully covered, in which case one <sup>fi</sup>rm’s gain would mean the other <sup>fi</sup>rm’s loss in market share. In this extension, we relax this assumption and assume that the market is not fully covered without IT investment and IT investment may allow the <sup>fi</sup>rms to serve new customers. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, in this case, both <sup>fi</sup>rms can be better off given the opportunity to invest in IT than without such an opportunity (i.e., no prisoner’s dilemma). IT investment gives both <sup>fi</sup>rms the opportunity to lower costs and serve new customers, which improves their expected pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 6. Conclusions

## 6.1. Contributions

This paper studies <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentive to sequentially invest in an ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-enhancing IT and their returns in a game theoretic model. This paper makes three main contributions.

First, when IT implementation can fail, the follower may have information about the leader’s IT investment level and implementation outcome before making its own investment decision. Would such knowledge bene<sup>fi</sup>t the follower? How would it affect the leader’s IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>t? Our analysis on these two questions generates economic insights not considered in prior literature.

We show that the impact depends on the type of knowledge.

a. The follower’s knowledge about the leader’s IT investment level creates a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage for the leader and a second mover disadvantage for the follower.

b. The follower’s knowledge about the leader’s implementation outcome creates an additional source of <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage for the leader. However, the leader’s gain does not necessarily mean the follower’s loss in this case. The follower may also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from having such knowledge under some conditions.

We analyze how different information structures affect the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment incentives and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and how the results may depend on the nature of the IT being adopted and the level of market competition.

Second, we show that, when competing <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt an IT sequentially, the risk of IT implementation failure in<sup>fl</sup>uences the leader’s and follower’s investment strategies and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts differently. Prior literature that studies strategic IT investment in a sequential setting has not considered the possibility of implementation failure, and so our analysis adds new insights to the literature.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the risk of IT implementation failure bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the follower and can bene<sup>fi</sup>t or hurt the leader. It impacts the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts through three effects:

First mover advantage mitigation effect: it mitigates the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage.

<sub>•</sub> Competition mitigation effect: It mitigates the competitive pressure for both <sup>fi</sup>rms to invest in IT.

Uncertainty-driven, cost-based differentiation effect: It creates a new opportunity for uncertainty-driven, costbased differentiation. This happens when one <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT implementation fails but the other’s implementation succeeds and does not depend on the <sup>fi</sup>rms making different levels of IT investment.

These three effects may drive the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT investment and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in opposite directions. The overall impact of IT implementation failure rates depends on which effect dominates. We discuss the conditions under which a certain effect may dominate and the intuition behind the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Third, we show that a spaced-out IT investment schedule, in which the follower makes its investment decision after the leader’s investment level and implementation outcome are both known, leads to the highest industry-wide IT investment and social surplus. The gain in social surplus is mainly driven by the increase in consumer surplus arising from higher levels of industry-wide IT investment. This result highlights the importance of considering sequential IT investment.

## 6.2. Managerial Implications

Our results have several managerial implications. First, there is an ongoing debate about the strategic value of IT investment provoked by Nicholas Carr in his controversial $^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { I T }$ Doesn’t Matter” (2003) article. Carr argues that IT is the “costs of doing business that must be paid by all but provide distinction to none.” (Carr 2003, p. 6) He advises <sup>fi</sup>rms to delay IT investment and manage IT costs and risks. In contrast, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that IT investment can lead to differentiation in production costs when <sup>fi</sup>rms make their investment decisions sequentially or when IT implementation can fail. Whereas we do not consider <sup>fi</sup>rms decision on the timing of their IT investment, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, given a choice, a <sup>fi</sup>rm does not bene<sup>fi</sup>t from delaying IT investment relative to its competitors. Indeed, even if such delay leads to lower implementation risks, <sup>fi</sup>rms may still prefer to invest early. The early adopter’s IT investment can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the follower’s expected payoff and optimal IT investment. This <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage leads to higher expected pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. This result is consistent with the active adoption of cutting-edge ITs, such as arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence and 3-D printing by many businesses (e.g., Boeing).

Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that part of the leader’s <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage arises from its ability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the follower’s investment decision via awareness of the leader’s investment actions. Therefore, pioneering IT adopters should publicly announce their investment decisions early in order to in<sup>fl</sup>uence potential followers. This insight is consistent with the frequent public announcements that <sup>fi</sup>rms make about their IT investment. For example, Amazon announced its miniature unmanned air vehicle (UAV) service back in December 2013 (source: Wikipedia) even though the project still had a long way to go before full implementation. This service aims to increase ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by using UAVs to deliver purchases to customers. The company continues to invest in this advanced technology and showcases progress on its website under the brand “Prime Air.”<sup>7</sup> Google X announced its competing investment in “Project Wing” in August 2014.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Work

In our model, the follower either knows or does not know about the leader’s IT investment. Future research may consider a more general setup in which the follower does not know the leader’s IT investment with certainty. The binary cases of knowing or not knowing become the limit cases of this more general setup. Moreover, in this paper, we focus on the role of IT in reducing <sup>fi</sup>rms’ marginal production costs. This focus is consistent with the empirical evidence that a major bene<sup>fi</sup>t and motivation for adopting ITs is to improve production ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996). Nonetheless, IT may impact other aspects of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s performance that are not captured in this model. Future research can study <sup>fi</sup>rms incentive to invest in IT for these other bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.
