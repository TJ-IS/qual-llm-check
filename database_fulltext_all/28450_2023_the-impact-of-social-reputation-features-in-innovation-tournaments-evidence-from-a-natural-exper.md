# The Impact of Social Reputation Features in Innovation Tournaments: Evidence from a Natural Experiment

Swanand J. Deodhar,<sup>a,</sup>\* Samrat Gupta<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. This study examines how a change in an online reputation system, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the addition of a social reputation feature, affects contestant performance in innovation tournaments. Drawing from the literature on peer recognition and social evaluation anxiety, we project competing effects whereby the feature could either enhance or diminish contestant performance. Furthermore, we hypothesize a series of contingent effects involving the soft reserve, a competitive dynamic that unfolds in tournaments, and a determinant of performance in its own right. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we hypothesize that the direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the social reputation feature on contestant performance would be predicated on the level of two types of soft reserves in an innovation tournament: that created by the focal contestant and that created by competitors. We test these hypotheses leveraging a natural experiment, where an innovation tournament platform (Kaggle.com) introduced a social reputation feature, allowing contestants to follow other contestants unilaterally. Estimates obtained using a panel data set bracketed within a narrow time window (15 days) around the feature launch reveal that the feature signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improves the performance. We further report that the two types of soft reserves signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly moderate the positive effect of the social reputation feature on contestant performance, whereby the higher the soft reserve, the weaker the effect of the social reputation feature on contestant performance. These <sup>fi</sup>nding have several theoretical and practical implications for managing innovation tournaments.

## 1. Introduction

Enabled by specialized digital platforms, innovation tournaments bring together members of the public (hereon, contestants) from around the world to address issues of varying complexity.<sup>1</sup> These contestants compete to solve a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned problem statement within a stipulated time (Camacho et al. 2019) and often for a reward (Riedl and Woolley 2017). In such tournaments, the contestants' performance is the critical outcome because it determines the payoff for the tournament organizer (hereon, seeker). Consequently, understanding the antecedents of contestant performance has been a central concern in the innovation tournament literature (Lee et al. 2018, Jin et al. 2021). Our study builds on this body of work by focusing on the performance implications of the platform's reputation system.

We begin by observing that innovation tournament platforms build and maintain elaborate reputation systems. We categorize these features into performative and social. Reputation system features are termed as performative if they directly afford reputation as a function of a focal contestant's activities (e.g., based on past performance). Instances of such features include standardized numeric scores (e.g., ratings), which are updated after every tournament in which a contestant competes (Boudreau et al. 2011, 2016), and a dynamic leaderboard, which indicate the latest relative standing of the contestants (Lemus and Marshall 2021). In con trast, social reputation features award reputation in the form of peer endorsements of the contestant's activities. An analogy in other online contexts is that digital badges are based solely on the number of reviews authored would be a performative reputation feature while a feature that allows users to follow a speci<sup>fi</sup>c reviewer would be a social reputation feature (Goes et al. 2014).

A social reputation feature is “a good <sup>fi</sup>t” for innovation tournament platforms for several reasons. First, such features are common in reputation systems of other knowledge-based platforms (Bosu et al. 2013, Moqri et al. 2018). The rationale for their presence is that knowledge-intensive voluntary activity can be socially recognized and that platform users value such recognition. Second, innovation tournaments are inherently a social activity because they allow and encourage interactions among contestants (Tausczik and Wang 2017) that are instrumental for contestants performance (Jin et al. 2021). However, related prior work (see Table B.1 in Online Appendix B) has not examined the implications of such a feature, resulting in an important gap in the extant understanding of how reputation system changes could in<sup>fl</sup>uence critical outcomes in innovation tournaments. Our study addresses this gap as we examine the following research question:

Research Question: How does a social reputation feature affect contestant performance in innovation tournaments?

Our study also relates to another stream of information systems (IS) research, namely, online reputation. In the prior work, the effect of online reputation has been examined on users' behavior. For instance, Goes et al. (2014) found that as online review writers attract more reputation (i.e., incoming ties), they change the textual features of the review (e.g., use of emotive words and phrases). Similarly, Moqri et al. (2018) studied the effects of new followers on a developer’s subsequent contributions (i.e., the number of commits). In these studies, it is shown that as the user gains reputation, their behavior takes a desirable turn. Our work builds and extends on this body of work in terms of antecedent and consequent. First, we depart from examining the user's prevalent reputation as an antecedent. Instead, our focus is on a platform feature, which, over time, could afford reputation. That is, we do not examine the effect of followers but of a feature that enables follower acquisition. This shift is rather important because it allows us to draw out important implications for the platform, given that feature is a platform-level artifact. Second, we examine performance as a consequence instead of behavior. This distinction is also important given that the former has “many potential impediments that are outside the control of the individual or group of individuals” (Beal et al. 2003, p. 990). As a result, behavior and performance are qualitatively distinct outcomes. This point is further emphasized, given that prior work is ambiguous about the effect of online reputation on performance. For instance, Goes et al. (2014) report no meaningful change in the readability of reviews as a function of the reviewer's reputation while showing a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant change in the linguistic features.

To address our research question, we develop a series of related hypotheses, drawing from studies involving social evaluation anxiety (Mills et al. 2016) and peer recognition (Jabr et al. 2014). We draw on the central mechanism that the social reputation feature operates as an evaluative tool wherein a focal contestant is assessed by others before the contestant can acquire a reputation in the form of peer recognition. Consequently, we argue that performance implications of a social reputation feature are likely to advance in competing ways. On the one hand, the inherent feature of peer evaluation may lead to higher social evaluation anxiety (i.e., a negative motive). Thus, social reputation features could lead to poorer performance by contestants (Mills et al. 2016). On the other hand, the possibility of peer evaluation leading to peer recognition (i.e., a positive motive) could improve contestant performance (Jabr et al. 2014, Dong et al. 2020). Our <sup>fi</sup>rst set of hypotheses (Hypotheses 1a and 1b) captures these divergent rami<sup>fi</sup>cations of a social reputation feature.

Our subsequent hypotheses articulate additional nuances about the effects of a social reputation feature. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, borrowing from the observation that the quality of prior submissions (hereon, soft reserve) directly in<sup>fl</sup>uences a contestant's performance in tour naments (Liu et al. 2014), we postulate the contingent effects of soft reserve on the relationship between social reputation features and contestant performance. These hypotheses relate to different soft reserves created by the focal contestant (Hypotheses 2a and 2b) and competitors (Hypotheses 3a and 3b). Through these hypotheses, we examine how an exogenous change in the reputation system (i.e., the addition of a social reputation feature) and the contestants' reaction in terms of performance changes could be predicated on competitive dynamics unfolding in live innovation tournaments. Our rationale for choosing soft reserve as a contingency stems from not only the contrasting predictions about how soft reserve affects contestant performance (Liu et al. 2014, Wooten 2021) but also the emergent and ever-present nature of soft reserve in a tournament, which, we argue, has the potential of combined and nuanced effects.

We test these hypotheses using a highly granular data set of Kaggle.com, a leading platform for hosting data science innovation tournaments (Dissanayake et al. 2018, Lee et al. 2018). Our econometric identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy is based on (1) a natural experiment enabled by a platform-wide rollout of a social reputation feature (allowing contestants to follow each other) and (2) the inclusion of multiway <sup>fi</sup>xed effects. Our data set follows the panel structure comprising contestants' submissions in different tournaments over time. We use the feature launch as an exogenous event to test the hypothesized effects on contestant performance.

An important empirical consideration of a platformwide launch of the feature is that it precludes constructing a “control group” of users because all contestants are simultaneously exposed to the same feature (Cavusoglu et al. 2016). However, we overcome this limitation by computing within-contestant estimates that capture the change in a contestant's performance in a short time window (i.e., 15 days) before and after the launch of the feature. Thus, each contestant's preshock performance acts as a baseline for their postshock performance. Studies examining the implications of platform-wide policy and design changes have termed this approach as a “single-group interrupted time series” design (Cavusoglu et al. 2016).

Our results show that, on average, a social reputation feature leads to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvement in contestant performance. Thus, a contestant is positively motivated as the feature offers a chance to gain peer recognition. Furthermore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd strong support for the contingent effects of the soft reserve. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the positive effect of the social reputation feature on a given contestant's performance weakens (1) when the contestant has a higher self-created soft reserve and (2) when the contestant faces higher competitor-created soft reserve. Collectively, these effects reveal the direct implications of a social reputation feature for contestant performance in innovation tournaments and demonstrate that soft reserve acts as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contingency in driving the said effect.

Our work primarily contributes to the literature on innovation tournaments. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we provide evidence that a social reputation feature in<sup>fl</sup>uences contestant performance. Thus, our work extends the current understanding of drivers of contestant performance in innovation tournaments. Furthermore, although studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a range of contestant-centric (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010) and tournament-centric antecedents of contestant performance (de Beer et al. 2017, Riedl and Woolley 2017), our study focuses on the dynamic reputation systems maintained by the platform. Thus, our work advances more recent attempts of examining how platform-level feature modi<sup>fi</sup>cations could affect contestant-level outcomes (Mo et al. 2018, Jin et al. 2021). In general, our study is a step toward bringing the technology center-stage when considering innovation tournament literature, which, to date, has relegated the features of tournament platforms as static and ambient (Jain and Deodhar 2021).

We also contribute to the innovation tournament literature by demonstrating the joint in<sup>fl</sup>uence of different soft reserves and the social reputation feature. As a competitive dynamic that unfolds in ongoing tournaments, the soft reserve affects contestant performance (Liu et al. 2014). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, however, show that merely emphasizing direct effects underrates the importance of soft reserve. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study demonstrates that the soft reserve could drive contestant performance in more subtle, indirect ways, acting as a contingency for platform-wide design changes. To that extent, our study emphasizes that the effect of a design change is unlikely to be detached from the more organically emerging dynamics in innovation tournaments.

Finally, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study have important practical implications. For example, the positive association between a social reputation feature and contestant performance indicates that platforms should consider such features as they do not seem to hinder performance. Furthermore, the longevity of the observed positive effect indicates some degree of sustainability in the performance uptick from a social reputation feature (see Online Appendix C). Moreover, for the seeker, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings recommend that holding a tournament close to the timing of the launch of a social reputation feature could be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial as such features appear to boost contestant performance. Thus, the seeker could attract greater quality submissions.

## 2. Background Literature

## 2.1. Contestant Performance in Innovation Tournaments

Innovation tournaments are used to accomplish various tasks, ranging from creative artwork (Wooten and Ulrich 2017) to scienti<sup>fi</sup>c problems (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010). Regardless of the task domain, how ever, the tournament organizer's main objective is to obtain high-quality submissions from contestants (Terwiesch and Xu 2008). Therefore, in innovation tournament research, contestant performance has emerged as a prominent outcome of interest (Boudreau et al. 2011).

To that end, studies have offered several insights into the determinants of contestant performance. This literature can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two broad categories. One stream has focused on contestant-centric antecedents of performance, including contestant's country of origin (Bockstedt et al. 2015, Chua et al. 2015), expertise (Boudreau et al. 2016), personality traits (Hutter et al. 2015), motivations (Leimeister et al. 2009, Zheng et al. 2011), and gender (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010). Another set of studies has considered tournament-centric antecedents of performance such as reward structures (Liu et al. 2014, Acar 2018), feedback mechanisms (Wooten and Ulrich 2017, Camacho et al. 2019, Mihm and Schlapp 2019), and appropriation and intellectual property rules (de Beer et al. 2017, Mazzola et al. 2018). Finally, some studies have examined the attributes of a seeker, such as their status (Pollok et al. 2019).

Our study builds on this body of work. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we focus on the platform intermediary as an active entity, arguing that salient platform-wide changes could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact contestant performance. Thus, we depart from the prior work, which has revealed several performance antecedents, while considering the tournament platform as an ambient entity that plays almost no role in driving contestants actions. Instead, our work builds on the view that features and tools, which constitute the tournament platform, have performance implications. To substantiate our view, we report on recent limited work that has considered the effect of the platform's features and tool on contestant actions. Mo et al. (2018) presented a comprehensive recommender system, which, if implemented as a feature, can improve contestants' performance and their probability of winning a tournament (Mo et al. 2018, p. 936). In addition, Deodhar et al. (2019) showed that the platform's decision to change the existing rank-ordering could drive contestants to increase their efforts. Finally, Jin et al. (2021) showed the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of a knowledge-sharing feature on contestant performance (Jin et al. 2021, p. 13). These studies clearly indicate that alteration in the features and tools of a tournament platform could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence contestant actions.

Building on this literature, the present study focuses on the impact of a change in reputation features, which forms an important aspect of innovation tournament platforms, on contestant performance. The importance of the reputation features can be analyzed based on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect that online reputation has on contestant behavior (see Table B.1 in Online Appendix B). For instance, Archak (2010) showed that highly reputed contestants tend to use this feature strategically to deter other contestants from participating in the tournament. Similarly, Boudreau et al. (2016) showed that moderately reputed contestants are more averse to increased competition. Finally, in a more recent study, Zhang et al. (2019) showed that “superstars,” referring to highly reputed contestants, can foster a learning environment through participation such that others may learn and improve their skills by competing against superstars. Overall, online reputation appears to have diverse effects on contestant behavior. Consequently, a system that affords reputation would be a pivotal digital artifact for a tournament platform. However, as discussed in the next section, platforms can and do choose to alter the reputation features, which raises the question of how such features in<sup>fl</sup>uence contestant outcomes.

In examining the impact of a change in the reputation system on contestant performance, our study marks another important departure from earlier studies (see Table B.1 in Online Appendix B). Although the studies have provided evidence that contestants’ accumulated reputation (e.g., higher performance rating; Archak 2010) or positions on the leaderboard (Lemus and Marshall 2021) affects their actions, this study examines whether and how a platform-driven change in the underlying reputation system in<sup>fl</sup>uences contestant performance. This distinction is important given that it connects a platform-wide decision (i.e., the addition of a reputation feature) to a change in contestants' actions.

## 2.2. Online Reputation Systems

Classically, reputation can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the “distribution of opinions, estimations, or evaluations about an entity, such as people, item and organization, in an interest group” (Cai and Zhu 2016, p. 103). Reputation is known to be a desirable social attribute in various online plat forms such as online auction portals (Ye et al. 2014), two-sided marketplaces (Luca and Zervas 2016), online knowledge repositories (Jabr et al. 2014), open-source software communities (Cai and Zhu 2016), and online labor markets (Kokkodis and Ipeirotis 2016). Such prevalence stems from the fact that, generally, users value their online reputation even to the extent of engaging in unethical behavior to improve and maintain the same (Luca and Zervas 2016).

Online platforms implement a wide array of features, which constitute the platform's reputation system (Liu and Munro 2012). These artifacts include discrete signals such as Upvotes/Downvotes and star ratings (Hollenbeck 2018), elaborate textual reviews, direct performance feedback (Ye et al. 2014), and records of earlier contributions and achievements (Cai and Zhu 2016). This study focuses on the dynamic nature of the reputation system, whereby platforms can add/modify features. For example, eBay frequently tweaks its reputation system (Klein et al. 2016). Google is also known to modify its business rating system to create a relevant and transparent reputation system (Nelson 2017). In addition, TripAdvisor recently changed a key feature of its reputation system, namely, the Popularity Index (Came 2018). These anecdotes show that features of online reputation systems could undergo salient changes.

Such changes tend to elicit strong behavioral responses from users. For example, eBay's decision to disable any revocation of negative feedback signi<sup>fi</sup> cantly increased the service quality among “strategic sellers” on the platform (Ye et al. 2014). In related work, Klein et al. (2016) found that when eBay launched a one-sided rating system, where buyers could rate sellers but not vice versa, the sellers' performance signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved. Furthermore, Cai et al. (2014) showed that incorporating “centralized feedback” as a feature in the reputation system of an e-auction platform reduced a part of the asymmetric bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (repeat purchases) that accrued to sellers with preexisting high reputation.

Although limited, these studies suggest that (1) online reputational systems could change and (2) such changes could be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant from a behavioral standpoint. We extend this argument to innovation tournament platforms to examine how a platform-wide change to reputation system could in<sup>fl</sup>uence contestant performance. In doing so, we hope to extend earlier studies in several ways. First, studies have examined reputation system change as a predictor of behavior (i.e., number of bids by a bidder; Ye et al. 2014). Instead, in our study, we focus on the reputation system change as a predictor of quality (i.e., the performance of a contestant in a tournament). To that extent, we examine how a reputation system change could shape an actor's market evaluations, which is a qualitatively different outcome from the actor's behavior (Beal et al. 2003). Second, the tournament setting is qualitatively different from the prosocial settings such as open source (Moqri et al. 2018) and online reviews (Goes et al. 2014) that have been the focus of the extant literature because, in tournaments, a user is motivated by private bene<sup>fi</sup>t rather than collective welfare. Third, we examine a novel form of reputation system change that allows a contestant to acquire reputation through social means. As described in the subsequent section, this form of reputation is distinct from the more “performative” reputation signals that directly re<sup>fl</sup>ect a contestant's past actions (Archak 2010).

## 2.3. Social Reputational Feature

In tournament platforms, contestants could earn a reputation in several ways. For instance, as discussed earlier (Section 2.1), contestants' reputation stems from past activities and is represented through signals such as dynamic numeric rating (Boudreau et al. 2016). Another prominent feature includes continuously updated leaderboard positions (Lemus and Marshall 2021) and accumulated digital badges (Liu et al. 2017). We consider these reputation signals as performative, whereby they directly re<sup>fl</sup>ect the contestant's expertise. However, contestants can earn reputation through social signals, where the reputation is based on others' evaluations and perceptions about the focal contestant.<sup>2</sup>

Similar features can be observed in other online settings involving knowledge work. For example, on Stackover<sup>fl</sup>ow, a user mainly acquires reputation through “upvotes” and “acceptance” received from peers for the user's answers (Bosu et al. 2013). Likewise, in open-source software communities, prominent reputation signals for the focal user include the number of followers and “stars” received from peers (Moqri et al. 2018). Similarly, reputation systems on innovation tournament platforms incorporate such social reputation features because tournament platforms offer contestants features akin to a social media platform (Mcfarland and Ployhart 2015), including a personalized pro<sup>fi</sup>le page and online conversation channels (e.g., forums).

Our baseline argument is that the central process underlying social reputation features relates to peer evaluation. Peers subject a focal user to some form of evaluation and examination before they afford the contestant reputation through favorable endorsement. To connect this logic with the examples cited earlier, Stackover<sup>fl</sup>ow users would evaluate the content that the focal contestant has posted and then decide whether to “upvote” the same. By the same logic, in innovation tournaments, the presence of a social reputation feature would imply that the focal contestant would be evaluated by others. We contend that this “evaluative core” of a social reputation feature has competing implications for contestant performance.

Although in the subsequent sections (Sections 3.1 and 3.2), we explore this tension in greater detail, to foreshadow our arguments, a social reputation feature offers the opportunity to acquire peer recognition (a positive predictor of performance), whereas the preceding peer evaluation could trigger evaluation anxiety (a negative predictor of performance). Thus, the implications of the social reputation feature for contestant performance remain equivocal. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst set of hypotheses elaborates this con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

## 3. Hypothesis Development

## 3.1. Peer Recognition Motivation

The <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis suggests that a social reputation feature would have a positive effect on contestant performance. Our argument draws from the earlier work on peer recognition as a motive, which strongly affects user behavior in online settings (Chen et al. 2017). Peer recognition acts as a “community-based inducement mechanism that celebrates the participation of community members” (Jabr et al. 2014, p. 191). As a motive, peer recognition “ful<sup>fi</sup>lls an individual's innate psychological needs of gaining attention and boosting self-worth” (Dong et al. 2020, p. 3). Consequently, given an opportunity, users tweak their behavior to garner peer recognition (Rhue and Sundararajan 2019). Peer recognition motivation is normative in nature, leading to shifts in users' actions in a socially accepted and desired direction. For instance, in online platforms that rely on voluntary contributions, peer recognition motivation is known to increase the number of user contributions (Jin et al. 2015), the relevance and quality of contributions (Jabr et al. 2014), and the length of user participation in the community (Fang and Neufeld 2009). In other settings, such as user-generated content, peer recognition motivation leads to higher product reviews (Goes et al. 2014). In conclusion, peer recognition motivation drives user behavior in a direction desired by an online platform.

Extending these arguments to social reputation features in innovation tournaments, a contestant would see a social reputation feature as an opportunity to garner reputation through peer recognition (given that social reputation feature results in endorsements from one's peers). Thus, a social reputation feature is likely to trigger a contestant's peer recognition motivation (Moqri et al. 2018). Once triggered, the peer recognition motivation would result in the contestant spending greater resources for accomplishing the tournament task. Consequently, we anticipate that the contestant's performance will improve (Bockstedt et al. 2016). In sum, a social reputation feature will improve the performance by offering a contestant the opportunity to gain peer recognition. Therefore, our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis is as follows.

Hypothesis H1a. A social reputation feature positively influences contestant performance in innovation tournaments.

## 3.2. Social Evaluation Anxiety

The second hypothesis postulates that a social reputation feature could hinder contestant performance. As mentioned in Section 2.3, a social reputation feature is based on peer evaluation. Before a contestant acquires favorable recognition and earns a reputation, they would be assessed by their peers. We contend that this evaluation could trigger social evaluation anxiety, which is a powerful negative motive that elicits an adverse response, such as poor performance (Mills et al. 2016). These implications of social evaluation anxiety arise from an individual's social self, which refers to others' perceptions and the resulting identity of the focal individual in a given social context (Baumeister and Twenge 2003). The concept has long been the basis for explaining individual behavior in several social settings, including online platforms (Pan et al. 2017).

Social self, often valued at par with one's physical self (Dickerson et al. 2004), is predicated on others evaluations of the focal individual. Therefore, when others evaluate an individual's actions (Rohleder et al. 2007), the social self of the individual can be altered signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the process of reshaping the social self starts with an audience, who assesses the focal individual through some markers of skills and form a judgment. Such judgments constitute the basis for the changes in one's social self such that a negative (positive) judgment can lead to a poorer (better) social self (Rohleder et al. 2007). As a result, an evaluation could lead to changes in the focal individual’s social self. The uncertainty of whether such changes in social self will be favorable (i.e., would the evaluation enhance or demote one's social self?) fosters social evaluation anxiety in the focal individual.

Extending this mechanism to the current study, we suggest that a social reputation feature, and the contestant's evaluation it spawns, increases the possibility that the contestant experiences a change in their social self, thus triggering a negative motivation of social evaluation anxiety. Because such anxiety is known to in<sup>fl</sup>uence performance negatively (Mills et al. 2016), we assume that the social evaluation feature may have an adverse effect on contestant performance. Thus, our second hypothesis is as follows.

Hypothesis H1b. A social reputation feature negatively influ ences contestant performance in innovation tournaments.

## 3.3. Soft Reserve in Innovation Tournaments

Having presented the competing effects of a social reputation feature on contestant performance, we now hypothesize a series of contingent effects. In particular, we focus on the soft reserve, an evolving competitive dynamic that emerges in ongoing innovation tournaments, which refers to the previously entered high-quality submissions (Liu et al. 2014, p. 2010). There are two underlying reasons for our choice of the soft reserve as a contingency for the effects of social reputation effects. First, studies on innovation tournaments have focused less on the notion of the soft reserve, except for those by Liu et al. (2014) and Wooten (2021). However, even with the limited exposure, soft reserve reveals an interesting tension, with stud ies reporting contrasting effects of soft reserve on the focal contestant's performance (see Section 3.5). Given this uncertainty and the fact that soft reserve remains a salient competitive dynamic, additional empirical research is warranted in this direction. Second, and more importantly, we note that the exogenous change in the reputation system (i.e., the inclusion of a social reputation feature) would be contiguous with soft reserve, which is a more organically emerging feature of a tournament. Therefore, a contestant’s performance may be driven by multiple forces, thus raising the prospect of contingent effects. With this logic, our subsequent hypotheses project how the soft reserve and a social reputation feature could jointly in<sup>fl</sup>uence contestant performance. To this end, we <sup>fi</sup>rst categorize soft reserve into two groups. First, the focal contestant could create soft reserve by entering high-quality sub missions (hereon, self-created soft reserve), given that tournaments allow a contestant to enter multiple sub missions. Second, along the same lines, competitors could also generate soft reserve (hereon, competitorcreated soft reserve).

## 3.4. Moderating Influence of Self-Created Soft Reserve

In an ongoing innovation tournament, self-created soft reserve represents the focal contestant's capability of generating high-quality submissions for a given problem. Thus, we project that if self-created soft reserve increases, the contestant could “go easy” as the pressure to perform in a given tournament is fairly mitigated.

Similar arguments have been presented in a prior work, which examines the relationship between past success and future performance. In a study of Dell's idea generation community, Bayus (2013) showed that earlier success (i.e., a high-quality idea) leads to poorer subsequent performance as the contributor becomes “<sup>fi</sup>xated” on their past success and produces only incremental improvements (Bayus 2013). A similar effect is observed in innovation tournament settings, where contestants with past success adopt the “local maxima” strategy for their solution search-space, providing only incrementally better solutions in innovation tournaments (Wooten 2021). Another mechanism reported in prior work refers to complacency and overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence. Wood and Lynch (2002) found that those with prior knowledge exhibit inferior subsequent learning for reasons such as the “feeling-of-knowing” (p. 417). Similarly, Berger et al. (2013) found evidence of complacency among successful contestants in tournament settings where past success leads to poorer subsequent performance. To summarize, regardless of the variety of underlying mechanisms, studies have suggested that past success could inhibit later performance, especially in knowledge-intensive settings such as innovation tournaments.

By this logic, we propose that higher self-created soft reserve could inhibit subsequent performance in the tournament as contestants may “bank on” their soft reserve to increase their chances of winning and be reluctant to spend further resources.<sup>3</sup> Therefore, provided the arguments presented in Hypothesis 1a are supported, we suggest that high self-created soft reserve would suppress performance gains resulting from peer recognition motivation, which are triggered, in turn, by the social reputation feature (as argued in Hypothesis 1a). Consequently, the hypothesized positive effect of the social reputation feature on contestant performance would weaken as the contestant's self-created soft reserve increases. These arguments lead to the following hypothesis (Hypothesis 2a).

Hypothesis H2a. Self-created soft reserve weakens the positive effect of social reputation feature on contestant performance.

If the arguments presented in Hypothesis 1b are supported, we again anticipate a mitigating effect of the self-created soft reserve. The argument for the same is as follows: higher self-created soft reserve would act as a salient and favorable indicator of the contestant's capabilities. Thus, the contestant may anticipate a positive peer evaluation after the launch of a social reputation feature. Consequently, a contestant with high self-created soft reserve would be shielded from, at least partially, social evaluation anxiety and the resulting threat to the social self. As a result, such a contestant would be less sensitive and concerned about the socia evaluation anxiety (as argued in Hypothesis 1b) resulting from the launch of the social reputation feature. This argument implies that high self-created soft reserve would weaken the hypothesized negative effect (Hypothesis 1b) of a social reputation feature on contestant performance. Therefore, we predict the following contingent effect (Hypothesis 2b) if the baseline effect of social reputation feature follows the argument in Hypothesis 1b.

Hypothesis H2b. Self-created soft reserve weakens the neg ative effect of a social reputation feature on contestant performance.

## 3.5. Moderating Influence of Competitor-Created Soft Reserve

We now postulate the contingent effects of the competitor-created soft reserve, which in itself has competing implications for contestant performance. One stream of work shows that a higher competitorcreated soft reserve has a demotivating effect where the focal user underinvests their resources in that tournament. This effect occurs because of several interrelated dynamics. First, innovation tournaments generally attract more contestants than the prizes offered; hence, a majority of the contestants spend resources and receive a nearly zero payoff (i.e., the “all-pay” regime discussed in Liu et al. 2014). Second, the eventual winner selection is based on “one-shot high-quality” submissions such that a contestant with a single high quality submission has a higher chance of winning than one with several moderate-quality submissions (Boudreau et al. 2016). Thus, the accumulation of competitor-created soft reserve could dramatically reduce a focal contestant's chances of winning, dissuading the contestant from engaging in further irrevocable resource spending (Liu et al. 2014). In conclusion, higher competitor-created soft reserve could create a strong competitive deterrent, leading to lower resource spending and poorer subsequent performance from the focal contestant. We argue that this baseline nega tive effect of the competitor-created soft reserve may counteract any performance gains arising from a social reputation feature (as predicted in Hypothesis 1a). Therefore, our subsequent hypothesis (Hypothesis 3a) is as follows.

Hypothesis H3a. Competitor-created soft reserve weakens the positive effect of a social reputation feature on contestant performance.

In contrast, the other stream of work involving competitor-created soft reserve suggests that it could have a motivating effect on the focal contestant, where “others’ performance might help the contestant know what is feasible” (Wooten 2021, p. 7). This logic suggests that high competitor-created soft reserve may expand the solution search space for the focal contestant who, otherwise, would not have perceived such high scores as likely in a given tournament. Therefore, competitor-created soft reserve, which reveals to the contestant that “better things are possible,” would act as a motivational trigger and drive the performance. In such a scenario where the competitor-created soft reserve has a positive effect on the subsequent performance in the tournament, we postulate that the analogous motivational effect of social reputational feature (as argued in Hypothesis 1a) would be compounded as contestants are already striving for better performance. That is, the competitor-created soft reserve would amplify the peer recognition dynamics underlying the social reputation feature. Therefore, our subsequent hypothesis (Hypothesis 3b) is as follows.

Hypothesis H3b. Competitor-created soft reserve strengthens the positive effect of a social reputation feature on contestant performance.

If one considers the possibility that the arguments presented in Hypothesis 1b are con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, we project an analogous set of contingencies involving competitorcreated soft reserve. First, if competitor-created soft reserve acts as a competitive deterrent (Liu et al. 2014) and leads to poorer performance, we anticipate a strengthening contingency effect. That is, the negative effect of social reputation on contestant performance (Hypothesis 1b) would be compounded by the higher competitor-created soft reserve. In an ongoing innovation tournament, the contestant would simultaneously face (1) a lower probability of winning a tournament in the presence of high competitor-created soft reserve (Liu et al. 2014) and (2) heightened social evaluation anxiety owing to the launch of the social reputation feature (as claimed in Hypothesis 1b). Collectively, we project that these contiguous negative antecedents would lead to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly poorer performance than the performance resulting from their marginal baseline effects. Hence, our penultimate hypothesis (Hypothesis 3c) is as follows.

Hypothesis H3c. Competitor-created soft reserve strengthens the negative effect of a social reputation feature on contestant performance.

Finally, if competitor-created soft reserve acts as a motivator by showing the possible performance levels that one can attain in an innovation tournament, the contestant is likely to have a stronger drive to perform better (Wooten 2021). This positive effect would clearly contradict the negative effect of social reputation features arising from the social evaluation anxiety and the threat to a contestant's social self (as per Hypothesis 1b). With the concurrent presence of antecedents that drive performance in opposite directions, we project that competitor-created soft reserve would mitigate the negative effect of the social reputation feature. Our <sup>fi</sup>nal hypothesis states this prediction (Hypothesis 3d). Table C.1 in Online Appendix C summarize the entire set of hypotheses. Given their competing nature, only a subset of these is likely to be empirically supported.

Hypothesis H3d. Competitor-created soft reserve weakens the negative effect of a social reputation feature on contestant performance.

## 4. Research Methodology 4.1. Empirical Context

This study's empirical context is an innovation tournament platform called Kaggle.com (Dissanayake et al. 2018, Lee et al. 2018, Jin et al. 2021). Founded in 2010, Kaggle specializes in organizing and managing data science-related tournaments. Some prominent organizations such as General Electric, Allstate, Merck, Ford, and Facebook use Kaggle (Dissanayake et al. 2015, p. 19). Some estimates show that Kaggle has more than half a million registered contestants representing nearly 200 countries (Khasraghi et al. 2017).

An innovation tournament on Kaggle follows a rather straightforward procedure. The tournament begins when the seeker broadcasts a call for submissions. This call is coupled with tournament governance rules (e.g., duration, monetary reward, and evaluation techniques). Once the call is published on Kaggle, contestants enter the tournament by entering a submission. Each contestant can submit multiple entries. Given the platform's focus on data science tournaments, most submissions comprise prediction and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation models. Therefore, the platform conforms to the well-established approaches of testing and training datasets to evaluate such submissions (Lee et al. 2018). First, the data set used for evaluation is divided into two parts. Then, the in-tournament evaluation is conducted using one of these portions of the data set. This evaluation results in the submission's public score (e.g., root mean squared error). Thus, to a contestant, the public score acts as initial feedback of their performance.

Once the submissions are closed, the platform allows the contestant to nominate a subset of thei submissions for <sup>fi</sup>nal evaluation. This evaluation is conducted with the so-far unused portion of the data set. This evaluation results in the submission's private score. The evaluation algorithm remains consistent across the two evaluation stages. The <sup>fi</sup>nal selection of the winners among the contestants is solely based on the private score. Consequently, the private score of a submission can be viewed as a better indicator of submission quality; hence, we use it as a measure of contestant performance. However, we observe a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive correlation between public and private scores $( \rho = 0 . 9 6 2 8 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ), indicating that the two scores are largely connected.

## 4.2. Econometric Identification

Our econometric identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is based on a natural experiment in which Kaggle launched a social reputational feature on May 25, 2017. The feature allowed contestants to follow each other, even unilaterally. The feature launch was broadcast through a dedicated blog entry (Hammer 2017). Furthermore, the feature was not launched as an “opt-in,” ensuring that Kaggle contestants had little control over its operationalization. Thus, consistent with our research question, our study examines the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of this new feature on contestant performance.

To arrive at the causal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the new feature on contestant performance, we apply several <sup>fi</sup>lters to the Kaggle baseline data set (https://www.kaggle.com/ kaggle/meta-kaggle). Although we describe the process of data construction (see Section 4.3), the two important <sup>fi</sup>lters related to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy are as follows: we retain submissions entered in a 15-day time window on either side of the feature launch date. Therefore, our sample for estimation includes only submissions made between May 10, 2017 (15 days before the shock) and June 9, 2017 (15 days after the shock), given that the feature was launched on May 25, 2017 (Figure A.1 in Online Appendix A). This strategy, termed as “single-group interrupted time-series” (Cavusoglu et al. 2016, p. 857), attempts to mimic an experimental design in observational data and is frequently used in online contexts when a researcher-controlled exogenous manipulation is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve (Cavusoglu et al. 2016). In the next <sup>fi</sup>lter, we exclude all submissions made on the date of the feature launch to attribute a submission as either pre- or postfeature launch cleanly.

Despite its popularity, the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy of using a platform-wide design or policy change as a treatment presents several challenges. First, it is likely that the change went unnoticed by contestants, which would reduce its implications for performance or any other behavioral change. However, we observe that since the feature's launch, an average daily count of follower ties was nearly 250, with approximately 1,500 ties occurring in the <sup>fi</sup>rst week of the launch (Figure A.2 in Online Appendix A shows the temporal distribution of the follower count). Therefore, it is unlikely that the feature remained unused. Second, contestants may have been primed for the new feature as any prior announcement regarding Kaggle's launch of a social reputation feature may weaken the reliability of submissions made before the shock as the baseline because contestants may have changed their behavior in anticipation of the upcoming feature. Similarly, other concurrently occurring platform-wide changes could confound the implications of the new feature. If there were simultaneous changes, any estimated effect of the social reputation feature might be spurious.

We address these concerns by thoroughly reviewing the Kaggle platform, particularly the blog posts related to the feature announcement and different discussion forums on the platform, to assess whether the launch of the social reputation feature was previewed/preannounced. We could not locate any relevant prior announcements regarding the launch of the feature. Furthermore, we note that the announcement of the feature launch (Hammer 2017) attracted several comments from Kaggle contestants. These comments indicate a general lack of prior awareness about the feature launch. Additionally, we could not locate any announcement regarding a platform-wide design change on Kaggle occurring concurrently with the experiment window. Given these observations, we conclude that the launch of the social reputation feature is a reasonably exogenous event that is unlikely to be confounded with (1) any ongoing platform-wide feature or policy change or (2) priming of Kaggle contestants with prelaunch announcements.

## 4.3. Data Set Description

We draw from Kaggle's publicly available data set (https://www.kaggle.com/kaggle/meta-kaggle), which comprises the submission activity in innovation tournaments between 2010 and 2018. Each observation in the data set represents submission s made by contestant i in tournament t. To strengthen the causal inference, we apply several <sup>fi</sup>lters, eventually arriving at the <sup>fi</sup>nal set of observations. First, as mentioned in Section 4.2, we restrict the sample to submissions that occurred within a 15-day window on either side of the feature launch. However, the estimates do not depend on the choice of the time window (see Tables C.5 and C.6 in Online Appendix C). Next, we retain only the submissions made by single-contestant teams. This <sup>fi</sup>lter ensures the comparability of the treatment among contestants because multicontestant teams may involve internal dynamics such as coordination (Dissanayake et al. 2015), which may confound the effect of the shock on performance.

Subsequently, we exclude all submissions in contests that were either “In-Class” or did not have a monetary reward. The rationale for implementing the former <sup>fi</sup>lter is that submission activities in “In-Class” tournaments occur in an educational setting (e.g., a tournament may be hosted on Kaggle as a class assign ment). Thus, contestants competing in such tournaments may not be concerned with changes in the Kaggle plat form because their primary motivation for performing is likely to secure higher grades. Similarly, we exclude tournaments with nonmonetary rewards because motivations for competing in such contests can widely differ (Lee et al. 2018), possibly interfering with the motivations to which Kaggle's social reputation feature may relate. Our estimates, however, remain unchanged if these tournament-level <sup>fi</sup>lters are not applied (Models D5 and D6; Table D.1 in Online Appendix D).

Finally, to ensure that our observation set does not suffer from attrition bias (Cavusoglu et al. 2016), we consider only those <tournament, contestant> pairs that occur on both sides of the shock. That is, we only consider those contestants who have made at least one submission in the given tournament before and after the feature launch. This <sup>fi</sup>lter ensures that we observe the same set of contestants competing in the same tournaments before and after the feature launch. After applying these <sup>fi</sup>lters, we obtain the <sup>fi</sup>nal data set consisting of 38,562 observations, representing 1,677 distinct contestants who competed in seven innovation tournaments.

## 4.4. Variable Description

4.4.1. Dependent Variable. Contestant performance, which is the study's primary outcome, was measured using the private score of each submission. As explained in Section 4.1, Kaggle provides two measures of performance: public and private scores. We use the private score as the primary measure of contestant performance, given that it is used for determining the winner. However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not change if the public score is used instead (Models D3 and D4; Table D.1 in Online Appendix D).

There is, however, an important empirical caveat when one directly uses the private score. There is no consistent way to claim that a higher (or lower) private score indicates a better performance. Some evaluation metrics (e.g., R<sup>2</sup>) assign a higher private score to submissions with higher quality, whereas others (e.g., root mean squared error) assign a lower score. To overcome this inconsistency, we multiplied the platform-provided private scores by <sub>−</sub>1 if the submission was made to a tournament in which a higher private score amounted to poorer performance. As a result of this transformation, across our sample, higher private scores consistently represented better performance. We also account for scale effects, given the wide numerical range of private scores in our data set. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we normalize the scores within every tournament such that the resulting values range between [0,1]. The formula used for this transformation is as follows (s is the submission entered in tournament t) (PrivateScoreRescaled):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Private Score Rescaled} _ {s t} \\ = \frac {\left(\text {Private Score} _ {s t} - \text {Min Private Score} _ {t}\right)}{\text {Max Private Score} _ {t} - \text {Min Private Score} _ {t}}. \end{array}
$$

4.4.2. Independent Variables and Moderators. Our primary independent variable relates to the launch of the social reputation feature. We measure this variable as a dummy, which equals one, provided the date of a given submission is after the feature's launch (Feature-Enabled). Otherwise, the variable is coded as zero. Next, we develop two measures for soft reserve (Liu et al. 2014) to test the contingent hypotheses. First, for contestant i competing in tournament t, we calculate the SelfSoftReserve (Hypotheses 2a and 2b) variable as the count of submissions that satis<sup>fi</sup>ed two conditions: (1) it was entered by i in t before the focal submission and (2) achieved a private score of higher than 0.75. As explained earlier, the private scores were rescaled between [0,1], and thus, across tournaments, a private score above 0.75 indicates that the submission belonged to the upper quarter of the score range. However, the robustness analysis (Table C.10 in Online Appendix C) con<sup>fi</sup>rms that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are robust to the choice of the threshold. Similarly, we calculate the CompetitorSoftReserve (Hypotheses 3a–3d) variable as the count of submissions that met the following two conditions<sup>4</sup>: (1) it was entered by i's rival contestant before i entered their focal submission and (2) achieved a rescaled private score higher than 0.75. These measures are consistent with the conceptualization of soft reserve, which refers to the quality of the submissions (Liu et al. 2014).

4.4.3. Control Variables. As explained earlier, the study's identi<sup>fi</sup>cation relies on an exogenous launch of the feature. In addition, to account for sources of unobserved heterogeneity from time-invariant attributes, we include a series of <sup>fi</sup>xed effects, namely, contestant, tournament, and week of the submission date. Besides, the study incorporates several time-varying control variables.<sup>5</sup> First, it is plausible that contestant performance may be driven by their submission activity not captured by the two previously discussed soft reserve variables. Accordingly, we construct their counterparts, namely, PriorLowSubmitsSelf and Prior-LowSubmitsCompetitor. These measures are computed similarly as SelfSoftReserve and CompetitorSoftReserve, respectively. The only distinction is that the quality threshold now represents low-performing entries (i.e., rescaled private score less than 0.25). Yet again, our estimates are unlikely to be contingent on this threshold (see Table C.10 in Online Appendix C). Next, we consider the possibility that a contestant may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from their prior exposure to competing in different tournaments on Kaggle. We account for this effect by including the number of tournaments in which the contestant has competed before entering the focal tournament (PriorCompete). Finally, we control for the possibility that a contestant may be competing in multiple tournaments simultaneously. Therefore, we construct an indicator variable coded as one if the contestant competes in at least one parallel tournament before entering the focal tournament (Parallel). In

Online Appendix C, Table C.2 summarizes the key variables, Table C.3 presents the pairwise correlations, and Table C.4 provides the descriptive statistics of the variables in our raw data. We note that the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor value is less than 3 and that condition number is below 9, which are well below acceptable thresholds (Rai et al. 2015).

## 6. Conclusion

## 6.1. Summary of Key Findings

This study examines how a social reputation feature in<sup>fl</sup>uences contestant performance in innovation tournaments. Leveraging a natural experiment involving the platform-wide inclusion of such a feature, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that after the feature launch, contestant performance improved signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Furthermore, we show that the observed improvement in contestant performance is contingent on the soft reserve. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the positive effect of the feature weakens as the self-created soft reserve increases and competitor-created selfreserve increases (Table C.12 in Online Appendix C). Finally, a series of robustness checks indicate the stability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 6.2. Implications

This study contributes to the literature on innovation tournaments. Building on the prior understanding that contestant-centric (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010) and tournament-centric attributes drive contestant performance (de Beer et al. 2017, Riedl and Woolley 2017), we show that a platform-wide system change, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the addition of a social reputation feature, could result in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvement in contestant performance. With our emphasis on a platform feature, we signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly depart from the earlier work on innovation tournaments in that the literature has treated platforms as static and ambient (Jain and Deodhar 2021, p. 24). Our study thus builds on the recent studies that have examined platform feature changes as predictors of contestant performance (Jin et al. 2021). Generally, our study addresses the limitation where the technology is viewed only as a context in IS research on knowledge work (Havakhor and Sabherwal 2018, p. 267).

On a related point, we also contribute to the literature on reputational effects. Although the prior work has examined the effect of users' accumulated reputation on their subsequent behavior (Goes et al. 2014, Moqri et al. 2018), we show that reputational feature change could have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for performance. To that extent, our work not only examines a qualitatively different antecedent (i.e., social reputation feature as against accumulated reputation), we also examine a meaningfully different consequent (i.e., performance as against behavior; Beal et al. 2003). To further highlight the importance of studying behavior and performance distinctly, we point out that prior work has been ambiguous about the reputational effect on performance and quality in online settings (Goes et al. 2014). To that extent, we call for additional research on performance effects of reputation, especially in instrumental settings such as innovation tournaments.

Although we focus on platform features as antecedents of contestant performance, we do not claim that such effects would be completely divorced from the more organically emerging circumstances. Instead, support for our contingent effect hypotheses (see Table C.12) advocates that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of exogenous technological modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to tournament platforms is likely to operate jointly with unfolding competitive dynamics (such as soft reserve) in ongoing tournaments. Hence, future work should examine such possible interactions between different antecedents of contestant behavior. Incidentally, the contingent effects further indicate that (1) emphasizing merely on soft reserve's direct effects or (2) considering soft reserve as a unidimensional attribute (Liu et al. 2014) could severely underrate the importance of soft reserve as a determinant of contestant performance.

Similarly, our study also relates to the uncertainty about how competitor-created soft reserve affects performance. Although one school of thought has indicated a negative effect (Liu et al. 2014), others have suggested a positive, motivating effect (Wooten 2021). Although addressing this tension was not the primary objective of the study, we found evidence of the former effect where the contingent effect supports the claim that higher competitor-created soft reserve is a strong competitive deterrent. We call for further empirical research in this direction.

Next, the observed effects extend the limited yet insightful work on reputation in innovation tournaments (see Table A.1 in Online Appendix A). Studies have shown that the accumulated reputation of a contestant (e.g., a performance rating) affects their own (Archak 2010) and others' behavior (Boudreau et al. 2016). Building on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we show that salient changes in the underlying reputation system could affect contestant performance, even after accounting for the preexisting reputational differences between contestants (Models 10A and 10B; Table C.7 in Online Appendix C). Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that changes in the reputation system could in<sup>fl</sup>uence contestant performance, independent of contestants' prevalent reputation. Accordingly, we call for additional research examining the impact of changing other features of a reputation system, such as the contestant leaderboard (Lemus and Marshall 2021).

Finally, our study has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant practical implications. We recommend that tournament platforms could seriously consider the inclusion of a social reputation feature as we do not observe adverse effects of such a feature on contestant performance. Furthermore, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect over an extended period (nearly three months after the feature launch; Models 3A and 3B; Table C.5 in Online Appendix C) indicates that the potential improvement in performance may extend well beyond the immediate period after the feature launch. Next, for a tournament seeker, we recommend that, before launching a tournament, they should consider whether a tournament platform has recently launched a social reputation feature. Such changes could result in a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant uptick in contestant performance and, hence, be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for the seeker.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be viewed in light of several limitations. First, our study examines a social reputation feature as a predictor of contestant performance. However, additional work is needed to ascertain the analogous effects of other changes to the reputation system. Thus, future work should conduct empirical studies with other reputation features such as rating scores (Boudreau et al. 2016) and badges. Second, we do not examine the possible contingencies related to sociopsychological attributes, such as contestants’ personality (Zhang et al. 2019) and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Mihm and Schlapp 2019), in shaping the effect of social reputation features. Given our empirical strategy of limiting observations to a short time window around the shock, such attributes are unlikely to bias our estimates. Yet, incorporating such attributes could provide an even richer understanding of the effects of a social reputation feature. Third, our study does not adopt the follower’s perspective who endorses the focal contestant. Given the plurality in the follower’s underlying motivation (e.g., learning from the focal contestant), future work should examine behavioral outcomes from the peer's perspective (e.g., a binary decision to follow a focal contestant). Along the same lines, while our work has examined the feature that affords followers, we have not considered the aspects of what happens when the contestant gains followers. This direction certainly merits future research consistent with recent similar efforts in other knowledge work in online contexts (Moqri et al. 2018). Finally, our study is limited to a certain type of innovation tournament (i.e., data science). However, given the variety of tasks for which tournaments are used (e.g., creative work, see Bockstedt et al. 2016), future research could examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social reputation features in other types of innovation tournaments.
