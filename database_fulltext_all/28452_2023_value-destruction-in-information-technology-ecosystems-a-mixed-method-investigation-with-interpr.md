# Value Destruction in Information Technology Ecosystems: A Mixed-Method Investigation with Interpretive Case Study and Analytical Modeling

Arvin Sahaym,<sup>a</sup> Joseph Vithayathil,<sup>b</sup> Suprateek Sarker,<sup>c,</sup>\* Saonee Sarker,<sup>d</sup> Niels Bjørn-Andersen<sup>e</sup>

Abstract. Many of today’s software systems are created by leveraging ecosystems consisting of heterogeneous “complementors” and “hub” <sup>fi</sup>rms. In fact, the reliance on ecosystems is prevalent in the enterprise resource planning (ERP) domain, where larger ERP vendors form collaborative relationships with smaller industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c vendors to co-create value for themselves and their customers. However, value creation and destruction processes are often intertwined. A key motivation for this study is to shed light on the behavioral contingencies and underlying mechanisms that might lead to value destruction over time instead of the initially intended value co-creation. Furthermore, although value co-creation in col laborative relationships associated with ecosystems is often highlighted, research has been scarce on offering an in-depth analysis of the challenges in these relationships that can destroy value. This study attempts to address this issue by uncovering the underlying mechanisms that lead a hub <sup>fi</sup>rm and its complementors toward value destruction. Our mixed-methods approach involves the use of a combination of interpretive case study and analytical modeling to highlight nuances and develop conceptual propositions about the conditions that can potentially lead to value destruction. Our context is a globally reputed information technology (IT) <sup>fi</sup>rm known for providing business solutions (SOFTCo, a pseudonym) and numerous relatively small, less powerful customer-facing service <sup>fi</sup>rms (PartnerCos, a pseudonym). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that opportunism, unjust appropriation of rents, shirking, exploitation of asymmetric power, and undue dependence can initiate a value destruction process. Furthermore, our study revealed an unexpected emergence of a “pack of wolves,” where resentful PartnerCos formed a collective to tackle the opportunistic behaviors of SOFTCo by starting to align with its competitor, further destroying value for SOFTCo’s ecosystem. Overall, this study contributes to the literature on value co-creation/destruction in IT ecosystems. It also offers an illustration of a mixed-methods study where seemingly incommensurable approaches are harnessed to develop a theoretical understanding.

## 1. Introduction

The world of software development, distribution, and implementation of custom software has undergone many changes in the past few decades. Baskerville et al. (2020, p. 519) suggest that “individuals, organizations, and <sup>fi</sup>rms no longer develop and implement their information systems in isolation” and that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of today’s software systems is created by leveraging ecosystems consisting of heterogeneous “complementors” and users. Jansen and Cusumano (2013, p. 41), for instance, note that the “relationships between software development <sup>fi</sup>rms and service companies [have] shaped the product software landscape into software ecosystems,” where the primary technology developer, component builders, and service providers collaboratively create value for all. Such a view is also expressed by other researchers such as Selander et al. (2013, p. 184), who state that much of present-day application development and deployment unfolds through “participation in digital ecosystems.”

The reliance on ecosystems has been prevalent in the enterprise resource planning (ERP) domain for some time, where larger ERP vendors have formed collaborative relationships with smaller industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c vendors with deep industry knowledge to sell and implement ERP packages in a wide range of industries (Antero and Riis 2011). Ceccagnolli et al. (2012, p. 264) argue that ERP companies often “have partnership programs for members of their ecosystems. Members of these partnership programs co-create value with [them] by developing applications and solutions to be used on the platform.” Such a symbiotic relationship between the large ERP vendor (the “hub”) and the smaller industry vendors (the “complementors,” also referred to as partners in our context) starts off as a means for creating synergistic value for both the supply side (e.g., coproduction) and demand-side (e.g., customer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c market value creation via customized implementation and support). This particular concept has come to be known as the “cocreation of value” associated with mechanisms such as exchange, addition, and integration (Sarker et al. 2012), and it has received much attention within the information systems (IS) discipline. Since Grover and Kohli (2012) highlighted the importance of understanding how “ITbased value is co-created among multiple partners in multi-organizational relationships (p. 226),”<sup>1</sup> there has been a growing body of work on this topic within the IS discipline.

However, value co-creation processes entail numerous challenges, tensions, and behavioral contingencies that can initiate a vicious cycle (Sarker et al. 2012, Majchrzak et al. 2016), with the originally intended value co-creation and potential rent generation degenerating into substantial value destruction and multidimensional losses. For example, Bruce et al. (2019, p. 176) argue that sometimes, during the “pursuit of value creation,” certain actions by one of the parties “allow for value destruction.” They propose that although resource integration starts off with the intent of value creation, misuse of the same resources or opportunistic behaviors can end up destroying the potential value, thereby suggesting that the processes of value co-creation and value destruction are often intertwined. In the context of the ERP ecosystem, we often witness instances of such value destruction. For example, a number of partners of a leading ERP vendor (name withheld to protect the identity) mentioned to Search-ITChannel.com<sup>2</sup> that con<sup>fl</sup>ict with the vendor’s direct sales force has increased over time. “Every day is a struggle, and it’s getting worse,” said one signi<sup>fi</sup>cant technology partner. “If [the vendor’s] reps would simply focus on doing more deals rather than scavenging our deals, everyone would be better off,” this partner said. This example suggests that, although the initial intention of the vendor and its partners had been to co-create value for both parties within the ecosystem, this goal may have been compromised as the vendor sought to retain most of the value at the expense of its partners, leading to the destruction of value for the ecosystem partners and eventually for itself. Indeed, Ple´ and Caceres ( ´ 2010) caution that an over-optimistic view on relationships in ecosystems has led to the notion of value destruction being relatively overlooked, and they suggest that more attention needs to be paid to the possible downsides of value creation as indicated in the example above. In a similar spirit, drawing on the ecological symbiosis perspective, Yu et al. (2008) suggest that, although the objective of the ecosystem symbiosis is mutualism wherein, all members bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the relationships, often parasitism sets in, wherein one-party bene<sup>fi</sup>ts while others are harmed. Furthermore, as we will see in this study, all parties can eventually lose value. Given the intricately connected nature of value co-creation and value destruction, the primary motivation for this study is to examine how an intended value co-creation process degenerates to value destruction over time and to shed light on the underlying mechanisms by which value destruction and occasionally value co-destruction<sup>3</sup> may occur (Lintula et al. 2017).

Extant literature in this emerging area has primarily focused on the bright side of the value co-creation process despite acknowledging the possibility of value destruction (Ple and C´ aceres´ 2010, Bruce et al. 2019, Pathak et al. 2020). Our review of the literature on value co-creation and related topics, especially in the context of ecosystems and business-to-business alliances, con<sup>fi</sup>rms that scant attention has been paid to the phenomenon of value destruction. Prior research has instead focused mainly on the enablers of value co-creation such as communication and sharing of complementary resources (Rai et al. 2012), the role of participation in increasing the business performance of all <sup>fi</sup>rms involved (Ceccagnoli et al. 2012), the dynamics of co-creation through knowledge sharing and wealth spillover (Han et a. 2012), and the different types of value co-creation that result from leveraging complementary resources, sharing of knowledge, and integration of the partners (Sarker et al. 2012). In short, most, if not all the studies, although illuminating, appear to highlight the positive or the bright side of co-creation. However, there is not a cohesive body of work in IS that focuses on the theme of value destruction—this is despite the acknowledgment that value destruction is a reality in ecosystem relationships (Ple and C´ aceres´ 2010, Pathak et al. 2020). Prevalence of positive stories appear to suggest that relationships amongst ecosystem partners typically end in “win-win” situations (Spohrer and Maglio 2010, p. 173); however, as both practical anecdotes and sparse academic literature suggest, such a focus might blind us to the realities of a “darker” side (e.g., win-lose, lose-win, or lose-lose) that cannot be ignored, especially if we aspire to provide a balanced theoretical picture and propose practices that can help manage such ecosystems more effectively.

Scholars have called for the examination of challenges inherent in the value co-creation process and emergent contingencies that could take value co-creation toward value destruction (Grover and Kohli 2012, Sarker et al. 2012, Bruce et al. 2019). For example, Majchrzak et al. (2016) note that in studying ecosystem dynamics, researchers should focus on how tensions among the parties arise and how to manage and adapt to them Sarker et al. (2012, p. 333) allude to the abuse of power and the existence of politics as inhibitors to value co-creation, recognizing the presence of opposing interests, con<sup>fl</sup>ict, and a dark desire to bene<sup>fi</sup>t solely at the cost of partners’ interests as contributing to such value destruction (see also Agarwal et al. 2010 and Lacity and Willcocks 1998). When the partners engage in con<sup>fl</sup>ictual interactions driven by opportunistic self-interest, agency issues, and power play, a vicious cycle of mistrust and misappropriation starts, resulting in value destruction for individual parties and the ecosystem in the long run (Bakker 2016, Oliveira and Lumineau 2019, Pathak et al. 2020). Such value destruction, visible in the form of misappropriation of collective rent generated in the ecosystem, shirking of contractual obligations in letter and spirit, and intraecosystem rivalry at the cost of potential value co-creation and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, often results in the loss of tangible and intangible resources and capabilities for the ecosystem. This study responds to numerous calls by scholars, both within and beyond the IS discipline, to examine this challenging issue and is guided by the following speci<sup>fi</sup>c research question: How and why might value destruction occur in IT ecosystems, specifically between the hub firm and its complementors? Our secondary research question is as follows: What are some of the broader consequences of value destruction in such ecosystems? We seek to address these questions in the context of SOFTCo (a pseudonym), a globally reputed IT <sup>fi</sup>rm with worldwide operations, and PartnerCos (again, a pseudonym) that are relatively small customer-facing service <sup>fi</sup>rms or complementors, some of which also have multinational operations and multiple lines of business. While acknowledging that value co-creation is often the intention and, in many cases, value creation and value destruction can occur simultaneously for the same parties, our focus in this paper is on the inadequately understood value destruction phenomenon that occurs over time.

Methodologically, we broadly use a hermeneutic approach, which represents a “primordial” way of how we come to understand (Gadamer 1975, Boland 1991). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we adapt the conceptualization of the hermeneutic circle of Agar (1986) to integrate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of an interpretive case study and analytica modeling. We believe that our methodological approach of using multiple methods, qualitative, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally interpretive case study on the one hand, and analytical modeling on the other, offers a novel and potentially valuable illustration of the use of mixed-methods research (Tashakkori et al. 1998, Venkatesh et al. 2013). We use interpretive qualitative case study because it enables researchers to conduct an in-depth examination of exemplary organization(s) in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, often longitudinally, delivering holistic and unique insights on the topic (Walsham 1995, 2006). Additionally, we use the gametheoretic approach within analytical modeling because it provides a theoretical scaffolding and a methodological approach that enables us to analytically formalize the assumptions and <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the case study (Gibbons 1992, Osborne and Rubinstein 1994, Myerson 2013). The mixed-methods approach overcomes the limitations of single-method studies by bringing the rigor of multiplicity of perspectives (Sarker et al. 2018a). Harnessing the strengths of interpretive qualitative case study and game-theoretic approach led us to the understanding that in the long run, value destruction is a multilevel (e.g., value is destroyed for the entire ecosystem, although in the short term, it may seem that the opportunistic party is gaining value via misappropriation) and multidimensional (e.g., value destruction hurts not only tangible aspects such as economic value but also intangible aspects such as reputation in the market) phenomenon. Value destruction can even take the form of opening up parts of the market, previously “owned” by the SOFTCo’s ecosystem, to competitors.

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows: First, we describe our mixed-method approach in more detail. We then provide an overview of the case context, followed by an elaboration on how we developed the theoretical understanding over multiple iterations or passes. During our iterative passes, we also discuss our emergent theoretical understanding (which we refer to as “schemas”) that include lemmas and propositions on value destruction. Finally, we conclude with the theoretical and practical implications of this work and a few directions for future research.

## 2. Iterative Mixed-Method Study: Hermeneutic Circle Integrating Analytic Modeling and Qualitative Case Data Interpretation

In this study, we integrate two different methodological approaches (interpretive case study and analytical/game-theoretic modeling), drawing on the notion of the hermeneutical circle (Lee 1991, Klein and Myers

1999, Trauth and Jessup 2000, Sarker and Lee 2006) to develop a theoretical understanding of value destruction. According to the hermeneutic tradition, “human understanding is achieved by iterating between considering the interdependent meaning of parts and the whole that they form” (Klein and Myers 1999, p. 72). Such understanding is derived from reading “texts” and “text analogues” such as actions (Ricoeur 1991). The meaning of the text is contextual and can change over different readings of the same text (Sarker and Lee 2006). Indeed, the frequency of occurrence of words or the sequence in which they were initially encountered or recorded does not necessarily determine the understanding of the text. Smith (1993, pp. 191–192) elaborates on the process of hermeneutic interpretation:

An inquirer begins with a hypothesis (or hypotheses) … and then searches for evidence that will call the hypothesis into doubt. If such falsification evidence is uncovered, the inquirer must revise the interpretation … The process of interpretation cannot be reduced to a rule-bounded or mechanical process. However, this absence of rules does not mean that “anything goes” because the attempt to interpret an author’s meaning is constrained by constant testing, criticism, and so on …

This quote suggests that understanding emerges through the iterative process of considering provisional understanding (“hypothesis”), attempting to falsify or discover its limits, and then revising the understanding if needed. This process can be seen to represent the hermeneutic circle. Given that the notions of hermeneutics and hermeneutic circle are abstract and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to operationalize, we adapt a tangible representation of the hermeneutic process offered by Agar (1986) in his discussion of ethnographic interpretation. We next describe this iterative process (Figure 1) based on Agar (1986). Also, please see Online Appendix 2 for a brief description of the terms used in our methodology, such as hermeneutics and preunderstanding, among others.

In step 1, we start with a preunderstanding, which may be based on the researcher’s awareness of the phenomenon, the literature, personal experience, and so on. This preunderstanding can be seen as an initial theory or perspective on the phenomenon of interest and is represented as Schema 1 in Figure 1. Our preunderstanding is based on the literature on value co-creation, especially from within the IS literature. A critical idea underlying the hermeneutic circle is that our understanding at any point need not be the most “valid” understanding. In fact, it is often the case that our initial understanding is not valid because of our biases and “prejudices” being embedded in this initial understanding (Gadamer 1975). Next, in step 2, the researcher deliberately identi<sup>fi</sup>es “strips” of data (Agar 1986) that would invalidate schema 1, that is, causing a “breakdown.” These strips of data (Strip Set 1) on the left side in the middle box of Figure 1 represent bounded evidence that could be an act, utterance, or an event related to the phenomenon (Agar 1986, Ricoeur 1991) and are helpful in bringing the focus onto certain chunks of related data in a particular iteration. This is valuable since handling of data in its entirety in any given iteration is largely infeasible. In our case, the strips of data were drawn from the case study we conducted. The effort toward falsi<sup>fi</sup>cation or “mandating break down” is Step 3; we note that the focus on falsi<sup>fi</sup>cation rather than on af<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the existing understanding enhances the validity of the interpretation process. Once the breakdown (see, for example, B1 in Figure 1) occurs, the researcher now uses the data or strip in focus and develops Schema 2. This is Step 4. Schema 2 can be based on induction, an imaginative leap based on data (sometimes referred to as abduction), or, in our case, on the use of analytical modeling that incorporates assumptions uncovered through induction/abduction from the relevant strip(s) of data considered in the pass to invalid ate Schema 1. This completes one iteration or pass.

In the following iteration, Schema 2 is again subjected to the same kind of examination as Schema 1 in the earlier pass, using a deliberately selected strip(s) of data (Strip Set 2) with the goal of mandating a breakdown. If a breakdown of Schema 2 occurs, a new understanding in the form of Schema 3 needs to be formulated. This iterative process continues until Schema “n” is consistent with the data; that is, additional strips of data fail to contradict or expose the limits of the latest schema under consideration. In other words, there is coherence between the parts (i.e., individual strips of data) and the whole (i.e., the schema), and there are no “apparent absurdities” remaining (Sarker and Lee 2006). This represents a “valid” understanding.

We capture the behavioral complexities from the perspectives of both the primary ecosystem members (i.e., the hub <sup>fi</sup>rm and the complementors) using the interpretive case approach. Our prolonged period of engagement with SOFTCo and the PartnerCos, along with intensive data collection primarily focusing on value creation through semistructured interviews and other documentary evidence, contributed to our broader understanding of the ecosystem and business environment, including competitors. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 25 interviews (semistructured and detailed) focusing on value co-creation and destruction were conducted between August 2009 and January 2011 (see Online Appendix 3 for details about interviewees). During this period, we conducted interviews with representatives from SOFTCo; its partners, that is, PartnerCos (P1, P2, P3, P4, and P5); and SOFTCo’s competitors (COMP1 and COMP2). We remained engaged informally with the company and its partners until 2015. These conversations, along with company documents and published sources, were the source of the “strips of data” that we retrospectively sought to make sense of and theorize from, using the hermeneutic circle, as discussed previously.

We represent our schemas using analytical/game-theoretic models and related propositions. We believe our methodological approach allows us to unpack the black boxes of the parties’ behaviors that lead to value destruction. As mentioned earlier, for many scholars, interpretive approaches fall short in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently representing formal and robust propositions as a new schema that resolves a breakdown, whereas analytica modeling approaches are not particularly well positioned for incorporating insights and assumptions from the <sup>fi</sup>eld; thus, the interpretive case and analytical modeling (i.e., the use of game theory as a theoretical scaffolding) complement each other in a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant way. The iterative approach ensures a balance between “dogmatism” and “skepticism” (Ricoeur 1991) in that we do not accept the <sup>fi</sup>rst set of propositions as truth, and the repeated deductive examination of emerging interpretations offers increasing reassurance of the validity of the understanding (Hirsch 1967), even if temporarily (Gadamer 1975).

## 3. Elaboration of the Case and Interpretation Process

As we mentioned earlier, we examine the issues related to value destruction within the context of SOFTCo, the hub, and its many partners or complementors, who together form a major part of the ecosystem. Our context is salient because the very essence of SOFTCo’s business model is dependent on an ecosystem of partners, as outlined: First, SOFTCo and PartnerCos possess different strengths and diverse competencies that brought them together in the partnership with the intent of cocreating value (Sarker et al. 2012). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the participants bring the following to the partnership: (i) SOFTCo, the global software developer, has unique resources and capabilities for providing business solutions (in the form of its generic ERP package, a distinct intellectual property) to small- and medium-sized customers, a global reputation, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial capital; and (ii) PartnerCos, the nonequity partners in this ecosystem, have inimitable customer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c complementary assets and competencies (e.g., tacit knowledge and human capital), and locational advantages in different markets (e.g., presence in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c location and location-speci<sup>fi</sup>c networks) for providing customized services (e.g., implementation and maintenance) to particular sectors and/or verticals (e.g., iron and steel, wood and pulp, food and beverage). In such a context of signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant differences between the parties, there was not only the potential for value co-creation from complementary capabilities (Grover and Kohli 2012) but also, as the case data will illustrate, a high potential for value destruction when the differences in guiding logics led to a divergence of interests and actions over the lifespan of the ecosystem.

## 3.1. Abbreviated Case Background

As mentioned earlier, SOFTCo develops and owns one of the major ERP solutions for the small- and medium-sized enterprise (SME) sector and is one of the top business solutions providers in its country of origin.<sup>4</sup> It has a noticeable presence in the global markets and offers its business solutions in more than 40 countries, primarily in Asia-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c, Europe, and North America. SOFTCo has alliances with more than 1,000 PartnerCo complementors to implement its solutions in small- and medium-sized business customers. Within SOFTCo, the development of new generations of ERP products and the overall strategy formulation is undertaken centrally<sup>5</sup> with a view toward the global market, whereas the sales and channel-management functions are organized nationally to manage the relationship with its partners in a given country. SOFTCo’s success in the SME market, in large part, can be attributed to its partnership strategy.

Many of the PartnerCos are categorized as independent software vendors (ISVs), and they play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in SOFTCo’s ecosystem. They serve as SOFTCo’s link to customer SMEs, often implementing their ERP systems, and providing sales support, consulting, customization, and enhancement services. These partners serve a market segment replete with SME customers that SOFTCo cannot itself afford to serve directly because of limitations of its industry vertical-related expertise and the low <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts expected from having direct relationships with the individual SMEs compared with the costs associated with managerial time, attention, and allocation of resources that would likely be incurred. However, the market segment in total is a source of substantial revenues from the generic ERP software developed by SOFTCo. At the time of our data collection, the license fee for SOFTCo’s popular ERP system per SME was generally in the range of 50,000 to 100,000 USD compared with several million USD associated with one ERP system sale in the large enterprise market. However, it is important to stress that an SME customer typically pays two to four times the value of the ERP system over time for customizations, implementation, maintenance, related services, and so on. The license fee is typically split 50/50 between SOFTCo and the relevant partner (i.e., a PartnerCo). However, SOFTCo gets a higher share in some circumstances because it owns the proprietary technology. Thus, SOFTCo’s primary interest is to rapidly sell as many proprietary ERP packages as possible to the customers.

The ecosystem partners are the centerpiece for SOFTCo’s business model in the SME segment because SOFTCo wants to stay lean and nimble enabled by the partners rather than become a large hierarchy that serves every industry vertical. Given the nature of the business involving implementation, sales support, consulting, customization, and enhancements, it would be almost impossible for a business solutions developer to remain ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and lean and deliver the value provided by partners to industry verticals. In addition, many PartnerCos seemingly serve as an extension of SOFTCo’s software development workforce, creating add-on products for specialized industry verticals, and incorporating local speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Considering the structure and arrangement, PartnerCos primary interest is in selling their services (e.g., consulting, customization, and enhancements) to the customers for a sold package for as long as possible. This is because the services tend to generate signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher revenues over time compared with the revenues from the license fee associated with selling new generations of the packages. Furthermore, services offered on the sold packages use accumulated knowledge, human capital already attuned to the package, and prior contracts, which save the PartnerCos time, economic costs, and transaction costs associated with new learning and developing new contracts. This puts the interests of the partners at odds with those of SOFTCo.

In terms of responsibilities, SOFTCo’s corporate strategy unit primarily handles/manages the ecosystem and industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c sales. It works directly with the ISV partners and rarely, if ever, gets involved in direct interaction with SME customers—this is to avoid potential channel con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Relationship managers are assigned to manage and support partners, particularly those that are very pro<sup>fi</sup>table. Generally, SME customers interact closely with one partner, although multiple partners may collaborate or even compete for a customer’s business on rare occasions. SOFTCo’s solutions development and global marketing divisions do not generally communicate directly with customers.

## 3.2. Starting the Interpretation Process: The Pre-Understanding or Schema 1 Acknowledging Value Co-Creation

Although not the focus of this study, we start our interpretation with a baseline preunderstanding that acknowledges the value co-creation motive underlying the alliance formation or becoming a part of the ecosystem. Indeed, SOFTCo entered into multiple, independent bilateral arrangements with a set of complementors, that is, PartnerCos (and, likewise, the complementors entered into this arrangement with SOFTCo) based on the expectation of higher value creation for both SOFTCo and PartnerCos.

Our pre-understanding partly builds on the synthesized insights of Grover and Kohli (2012), Han et al. (2012), Kale and Singh (2009), and Sarker et al. (2012). This body of work highlights the fact that each of the partners who participates in symbiotic relationships brings its unique and complementary resources and capabilities to contribute toward the success of customers’ ERP projects, thereby synergistically creating higher value. In the normal course, synergies are expected to develop and evolve during the execution of the project, and payoffs are anticipated to be much superior compared with when each partner would individually explore the opportunities in the ERP market space.

We formalize this understanding as follows: SOFTCo develops ERP software for its customers. However, SOFTCo cannot satisfy all SME customer requirements and adds additional resources through cooperation agreements with ecosystem alliance partners (Partner-Cos), or $A P _ { i } , i = 1 , 2 , . . . . . , n$ who meet certain thresholds for expertise, quality, and complementarity. Each party has potential gains from trade when a partnership between the speci<sup>fi</sup>c partner and SOFTCo is established to service customers (i.e., SME customers). SOFTCo makes multiple bilateral two-party agreements with a number n of their ecosystem partners. The two parties go through a bargaining process and reach an agreement on the allocation of $P _ { T i }$ (total payoffs) to each party. The well-known models of Nash bargaining and its derivatives (Muthoo 2002) may be appropriate in this setting because of the use of outside options. Each party will evaluate its outside options, and it is intuitively evident that SOFTCo (i.e., the larger and more dominant partner) will have larger outside options and will negotiate a larger portion of the surplus.

The ex ante assumption is that both parties wil carry out their respective obligations under the agreement (Elitzur and Wensley 1997, Meng and Pakath 2001). The allocation of the surplus or gains from executing the joint project is the result of the bargaining outcome. We model this allocation of surplus as the equilibrium outcome of a Nash bargaining process (Nash 1953). Nash bargaining is used in economic modeling and general bargaining applications (Binmore et al. 1986, Muthoo 2002). The allocation of ex ante surplus or payoffs to SOFTCo are designated as $P _ { i } ^ { C C } , i = \bar { 1 } , 2 , . . . . . , \bar { n }$ and the payoffs to the PartnerCos are designated as $P _ { C i } ^ { C C } , i = \bar { 1 } , 2 , . . . . . , n$ where such payoffs include prospects for both tangible and intangible gains and losses. The letters in the payoff superscript $^ { \prime \prime } { \cal C } { \cal C } ^ { \prime \prime }$ refer to cooperation by each party in the agreement. The <sup>fi</sup>rst letter in the superscript of the payoff refers to the PartnerCo and the second letter refers to SOFTCo, and this notational protocol is maintained in the subsequent analysis. Later, we introduce the concept of cheating or defection where the superscript $\mathrm { ^ { \prime \prime } D ^ { \prime \prime } }$ is used. 3.3. Pass 1: Mandating a Breakdown of Schema 1 Next, we sought strips of data from the case evidence (see Figure 1) with the goal of mandating a breakdown for Schema 1 (represented as Lemma 1 and Proposition 1). Three patterns emerged from the case study data, all of which contributed to the breakdown of schema 1.<sup>6</sup> These patterns revealed the following: (1) SOFTCo appeared to be appropriating value from the relationship at the expense of select partners; (2) some PartnerCos appeared to be appropriating value from the relationship at the expense of SOFTCo; and (3) in some cases, both SOFTCo and select PartnerCos appeared to be attempting to appropriate value from each other, which often, led to a vicious cycle, resulting in the destruction of value for both sides $\mathrm { ( i . e . , ~ } ^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { c o - }$ destruction”).

3.3.1. Empirical Evidence of Opportunistic Actions by SOFTCo While PartnerCos Are Cooperating. With respect to pattern 1, some PartnerCos alleged that SOFTCo was behaving opportunistically and violating the moral integrity of the symbiotic arrangement. For example, SOFTCo was seen as appropriating certain ideas behind algorithms and software code modules from PartnerCos and incorporating those in new versions/upgrades of the generic package. Such behavior left the relevant PartnerCo by the wayside as it lost the investment made in the development of these add-ons and suffered a destruction of value. SOFT-Co\_MANAGER#1 admitted:

If you’re a SOFTCo ISV partner [developing add-on software] with the solutions you had yesterday … [you should understand that] we will sometimes chip away at what you built as part of improving our [core] product … we alway try to put more and more [features and] capabilities int our [core package’s] offerings over time [and some of those could be from add-ons developed by partners in different verticals and locations] …

SOFTCo\_MANAGER#7 indicated that they were aware that this behavior could result in a loss of trust that was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to rebuild in the ecosystem:

It is a big fear in the channel that SOFTCo will develop the [modules for the] vertical on its own … what the partner has done already … SOFTCo can take away the functionality … [and] eat the partner’s business.

Interestingly, SOFTCo did not necessarily see this behavior as misappropriation but as mere happenstances and a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effect of knowledgespillovers. From the perspective of some SOFTCo managers, they typically laid out development plans for the new generation of ERP package for several years, and some of the development plans included improvements that “happened” to be similar to improvements introduced by add-ons from the complementors. SOFTCo managers also pointed to knowledge spillovers from customers that had led to the development of “add-on solutions” by the complementors and how SOFTCo elevated speci<sup>fi</sup>c add-ons to the level of ERP software’s upgrades. The point that often appeared to be ignored is that such elevations were done at the expense of certain partners value—without offering tangible compensation, intangible rewards, or even due credit. Even worse, sometimes SOFTCo’s sales executives opportunistically started discussing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of future releases of the ERP package that incorporated these add-on functionalities directly with customers, bypassing PartnerCos. This behavior not only breached the trust and implicit contractual obligations, but it also made PartnerCos suspicious that SOFTCo wanted to take credit for the contract and “eat up” their portion of licensing revenue, thereby appropriating their payoffs. This behavior created a trust de<sup>fi</sup>cit and fear in the minds of some of the partners.

SOFTCo further diminished trust and goodwill by launching rushed, error-prone new releases of the ERP systems without suf<sup>fi</sup>cient testing. Using this approach, SOFTCo sought to meet its own quarterly pro<sup>fi</sup>tability targets or respond to the market or get early feedback; however, it exposed partners to dissatisfaction, and sometimes, to the anger of customers. Partners being at the frontline were often required to “get the system running” for the customers, and the untested modules posed major challenges. P5\_MANAGER#1 recounted:

What we have seen is that some products [new versions] were not tested thoroughly … and we have many unsatisfied customers [with] destroyed and corrupted data … [retrieving and correcting all] this required a lot of resources from us and the customers … it has been expensive for us and the customers, and SOFTCo has no way to compensate for those losses.

Although partners were interested in versions being as bug free as possible because bugs meant wasted energy, time, and resources for them, SOFTCo was interested in developing (and releasing to the market) newer versions of ERP software to sell licenses. This was because SOFTCo was less affected by the presence of bugs in the initial stages because it was not actually involved in implementing the package at the customer’s site. The interests of partners and SOFTCo were thus at odds because partners wanted to maintain the prior versions of software they had assiduously cleaned of bugs and add services of customization whereas SOFTCo was focused on the release of new versions as fast as possible to earn revenues via new licenses.

To summarize, the evidence presented previously highlights that SOFTCo was explicitly engaging in misappropriation of the collective rent generated in the ecosystem while apparently collaborating with PartnerCos. This led to the loss of investments that PartnerCos had made in developing their own algorithms and software modules, thereby diminishing their pecuniary bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. By shirking its contractual obligations of cocreating and sharing value in letter and spirit and by creating intraecosystem rivalry, SOFTCo initiated a process where trust-based relational value started to erode for the partners and even for the customers—this had implications for the ecosystem because the generic ERP package that requires services (e.g., customizations, and upgrades) essentially thrives on relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets. Clearly, the evidence in the chosen strips is not consistent with Schema 1 that suggests cooperation, and this highlights a need for additional theorizing. Lemma 2a and Proposition 2a in Schema 2 presented in Section 3.4 are based on the patterns here, arising from the opportun istic behaviors of SOFTCo.

3.3.2. Empirical Evidence of Opportunistic Actions by PartnerCos While SOFTCo Is Cooperating. Value destruction did not just occur because of opportunistic actions of SOFTCo. Many PartnerCos also pursued strategies that were inconsistent with the spirit of the cooperative ecosystem. We recall that whenever partners sell SOFTCo’s new generation ERP system, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant enhancements, or global add-on products, SOFTCo gets a major proportion of the licensing fees. However, consulting-based and other services-related revenues resulting from the partners’ engagement with the customers stay with the partner alone, prompting many partners, especially those not looking for growth, to simply not put necessary effort to seek out new customers for SOFTCo’s products. SOFTCo\_MANAGER#1 explained:

We do have a number of partners who’ve been in the game [of selling services] for decades … [they] have built a fairly large customer base and are basically just minding that customer … but if they’re not out there selling [our new product] actively … that to us is a problem.

P5\_MANAGER#2 acknowledged that this was indeed the case with P5 to a large extent:

We are not focusing on selling [the new product] … but focusing on delivering knowledge and upgrades to our customers. We have a customer base that we keep updated at a lower cost.

The same point was highlighted by P1\_MAN-AGER#1 as well. Furthermore, P2\_MANAGER#1 observed that an important partner in the region mostly focused on making money through consulting and other services (e.g., maintenance) on prior versions of the software for which SOFTCo would hardly receive any income:

That company [ecosystem complementor] … they’re not selling any software lately … Nothing. They are the number one SOFTCo partner [in the region] but not getting any new money for SOFTCo … they’re counting on the revenue the company produces in terms of consultancy hours sold … That’s what they’re making money on, consulting services, not on the software.

Another way in which partners sought to create value for themselves at the expense of SOFTCo was by convincing the customers that they need more customization, as SOFTCo\_MANAGER#1 explained:

Partners definitely have an incentive to go out and customize applications … quite significantly … partners know that if they customize, they will probably make more money in the implementation project, which from our point of view jus makes the implementation project more expensive and therefore makes us less competitive in the marketplace. For the customer, it will cost them more money eventually … over the large life span of the application because upgrades etcetera become harder … . [emphasis added]

Simply stated, many of these partners leverage on SOFTCo’s brand and technological platform to generate a revenue stream for themselves alone. In other words, they tend to engage in intentional misuse of resources to lower the overall value that could potentially be generated from the partnership (Ple and C´ aceres´ 2010). This is not consistent with Schema 1 (which suggests cooperation), and this highlights a need for additional theorizing. Please see Lemma 2b and Proposition 2b in Schema 2 presented in Section 3.4.

3.3.3. Empirical Evidence of Opportunistic Actions by Both SOFTCo and PartnerCos. Yet another related but troublesome variety of opportunism and agency issues appeared to occur when entities on both sides of the relationship contributed to value destruction (for both entities), often in a recursive manner. For example, SOFTCo\_MANAGER#2 noted that partners, who were not upgrading their vertical-speci<sup>fi</sup>c solutions promptly to match SOFTCo’s new generation ERP package or were reluctant in developing service capability for it, had the potential to start a downward spiral resulting in losses for both parties:

If partners don’t upgrade their solutions, if the partners don’t take up [the new generation], it will be a vicious circle, because we gonna continue on development in that direction on the new platform to make it better and better … but our partners are going in that [opposite] direction because they are staying on the old platform, on the old architecture … . The longer they wait, the bigger their jump will be, and we will reach a point where we cannot help them to do the jump [say] two versions from now … . We are driving the train … we are driving it fast [with upgrades to match the market] … . If they wait too long, the train will go too far for them to get on … over time we will lose customers needing new systems and solutions, and the partners too … [and] our ecosystem will go down …

In other words, the partners who did not upgrade customers with new vertical-speci<sup>fi</sup>c solutions would gradually become irrelevant and be squeezed out of the marketplace because eventually the customers would require contemporary applications and streamlined business processes for within-<sup>fi</sup>rm ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and market responsiveness (e.g., the then novel cloud based solutions). Partners’ reluctance in selling new generation products (see Section 3.3.2) and their incapability to develop appropriate customized solutions/ service for earlier versions were perceived negatively by the customers—a few customers showed their disappointment with the limitations of their existing older version for the latest needs and choices available at their location through the relevant PartnerCo, as they learned about competitors’ products that offered the (then) state-of-the-art solutions (e.g., cloud-based solutions, and security features). Such customers would check directly with SOFTCo about the availability of cutting-edge applications, and this provided an opportunistic opening for SOFTCo to bypass the partner and directly deal with the customers, especially if they were assessed to be lucrative. In fact, SOFTCo did engage in such behavior a few times, although it was against the spirit of their contract with PartnerCos.

Our data indicate that opportunistic behaviors by both SOFTCo and PartnerCos contributed to value co-destruction as follows. On the one hand, the reluctance of partners in adopting new generation ERP systems and self-interest based recommending customized add-ons to the customers that could at best serve as “temporary patches/workarounds” hurt both the parties and the ecosystem in the long run. Because these workarounds often fell short in meeting customers comprehensive objectives effectively, customers got motivated to consider competitors’ products for longterm ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and market responsiveness, even though SOFTCo’s new generation system had the capabilities and applications needed by the customers—indeed, SOFTCo had already invested heavily in developing state-of-the-art technologies and applications and it expected partners’ support for return on investment and market position. On the other hand, opportunistic bypassing of the partners by SOFTCo for lucrative customers on a few occasions contributed to the erosion of trust and relationship capital between the parties and within the ecosystem in general.

Overall, the relevant strips of data presented demonstrate that the value co-creation objective of the ecosystem (as per Schema 1, including A1) is not realized primarily because of the opportunistic behavior of SOFTCo, PartnerCos, and/or both. Indeed, opportunistic behavior becomes evident as efforts to “mislead, distort, disguise, obfuscate, or otherwise confuse” (Williamson 1985, p. 47), given that opportunism is “self-interest seeking with guile” (Williamson 1975, p. 255). This suggests a breakdown of Schema 1 and the need to develop Schema 2 (a revised theoretical understanding) based on the data strips examined along with insights of game theory that explicitly recognizes the role of opportunism in the partnership. Lemma 2c and Proposition 2c in Schema 2 presented in Section 3.4 show the previously described patterns.

## 3.4. Schema 2: Opportunistic Behavior and Its Consequences Under Three Game-Theoretic Conditions

The important conceptual framing of the analytical model is that when the joint projects are underway, project uncertainties are erased, and contingencies are realized. Hence, it is important to note that payoffs in Figure 2 have changed from the ex ante stochastic payoffs evaluated by PartnerCos and SOFTCo, which led to the partnership agreements. Ex post payoffs for each party can be viewed as having four independent components with no uncertainty where the value is determined by strategic choice of both parties: PartnerCo and SOFTCo. The <sup>fi</sup>rst component is the payoff from both parties staying with the agreement as originally planned and is represented by the superscript CC. Therefore, if both parties continue in line with the original strategy of partnership which we denote as “Cooperate,” the payoffs are $P _ { i } ^ { C C }$ and $P _ { C i } ^ { C C } , i = 1 , 2 , . . . . . , n$ . The three remaining components are comprised of one party or both parties defecting or cheating on the agreement denoted by the D superscript. Clearly, the cheat or defect strategy denoted by the D superscript generates payoffs from other opportunities that have been revealed ex post. The partner may see better opportunities elsewhere or be presented with opportunities to cheat for any number of reasons. These payoffs are the components shown in Figure 2. Game theory informs us that the resulting equilibrium choice of strategy depends on (i) the relative value of payoffs for the strategic choices for the party making the decision, and (ii) the strategic choice of the other party. Consequently, each of the parties may deviate from the original partnership based on the realized ex post payoffs.

A nuanced point that does not change the theoretical framing is that ex post revelations may show that payoffs can be negative and that simply abandoning the agreement is a better course of action than being subject to negative payoffs. Hence, only the relative value (and not the absolute value) of payoffs is relevant. Lemmas 2a, 2b, and 2c are generated from the three possible scenarios of cheating or defecting that can emanate from each of the two-party agreements. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c choices depend on the relative values of the ex post payoffs. Because there are multiple two-party agreements, there are differences in the ex post payoffs for each of the parties. Such asymmetries are associated with resource and knowledge dependence (Pfeffer and Salancik 2003, Casciaro and Piskorski 2005, Hillman et al. 2009, Howard et al. 2017), agency issues (Eisenhardt 1989), and size and status (Emerson 1962, Casciaro and Piskorski 2005). Such asymmetries have several consequences that we observed in our case, which are not fully explained by Schema 2.

SOFTCo changes strategies all the time … they change directions about add-on products after we have invested in those … we would like to know in advance, but they don’t care much … they are so $b i g \ldots$ they use our add-ons in updates … hurt our revenues … impact our business, but we can’t do much … we hang in there … we are tied to them … it’s not like we have options outside [SOFTCo] …

## 3.8. Behavioral Realizations During and After Value Destruction

During the course of our study, we found that each party’s behavior sometimes went into uncharted territory, not necessarily consistent with the actions predicted by theory. Engaging with these anomalies helped us present a more complete picture of the situation, beyond what classical game theory and extant literature can offer. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, drawing on our empirical data, we had the following realizations. First, a consideration of the temporal dimension is a sine qua non for research on ecosystem relationships. Activities engaged in by the relevant parties—here, the hub and complementors—inherently involve repeated interactions that the parties use toward achieving their own strategic objectives in the ecosystem; thus, empirical studies must consider repeat interactions over a time horizon. We realized that the parties, when they develop or join an ecosystem, begin with the strategic intent of value co-creation and initially work toward agreed on outcomes. However, in repeat interactions over the long run, they can engage in or encounter opportunistic, agency-based, and asymmetry-based behaviors that start a vicious cycle overcoming the collaborative intent for value creation and resulting in value destruction for the ecosystem and individual parties unless ecosystem management mechanisms are deliberately developed (Kale and Singh 2009, Pathak et al. 2020). Our study aligns well with prior literature on opportunism. Although opportunism has often been seen as a “monolithic phenomenon” (Das 2004, p. 744), contemporary literature seems to suggest that it can be of many forms depending on the opportunism horizon (short term versus long term) and level of relational risk (high versus low). Our study highlighted both type I opportunism (short-term effects with low relational risks) and type IV behaviors (high relational risks with the formation of alternate coalition thereby causing long-term impacts) (Das 2004).

Furthermore, the literature on opportunism suggests that when there is a shared vision between the partners, less opportunism is likely to be present, especially if this shared vision leads to cooperative goals (Wong et al. 2005). Although in our study, the ecosystem did develop with a shared vision and aligned goals, we still observed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant opportunism. To seek answers to this relatively anomalous result, we turned to the IS literature focusing on the partnerships formed in the context of software development. Our results appear to be consistent with existing work on such relationships, where it has been suggested that when the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm in the ecosystem has high “peripheral knowledge” in the domain of the core activity (Tiwana and Keil 2007, p. 625), it tends to impose high levels of controls on the lesser partners, restricting them, reducing their autonomy, and thereby leading them toward opportunism and other dysfunctional behaviors—something that we observed in the case of PartnerCos. The alliance literature alludes to the potential of opportunism engaged in by the dominant partner in an asymmetric alliance, for example, in the context of MNCs versus local <sup>fi</sup>rms (Luo 2006, Khuntia et al. 2019). A similar conclusion was made by Prior and Marcos-Cuevas (2016) in their study of ecosystems. They found that when there was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in power between the ecosystem’s partners, value destruction, owing to opportunistic behaviors by the powerful partner, often ensued.

Although we did observe this pattern, our study also highlighted signi<sup>fi</sup>cant opportunism and agency behavior engaged in by the weaker partners in the ecosystem as well (i.e., the PartnerCos). This aligns well with the “logic of mutual hostages,” where asymmetric power leads to a perception that decisions are being aligned toward the dominant party, “thus propelling interest divergence and increasing opportunism” by the less dominant partner (Luo 2006, p. 65). Ple and C´ aceres´ (2010) refer to this as sabotaging behaviors, intentionally designed to cause their own well-being while decreasing that of the other partner. Our study’s results also bring to bear the time horizon component of opportunism and highlight that opportunistic behaviors can both develop and change over time (Park and Ungson 2001, Das 2004). A key realization was that contextual technological dynamism (e.g., continual development of add-ons or introduction of new generation products) affects temporality as values and behaviors change faster. These changes then in<sup>fl</sup>uence the levels of opportunism, power, and agency issues, which in turn contribute to value destruction.

The case study data also highlighted the fact that if the vicious cycle is not nipped in the bud, it destroys the value co-creation motive, trust, goodwill, social cap ital, and cooperative spirit rapidly (Sarker et al. 2012), leading to value destruction. For example, prior to taking collective action against SOFTCo, the partners recognized that the relationship is a long-term arrangement. Hence, they met with SOFTCo’s managers and discussed their concerns and requested them to help alleviate those issues and reset the ties (see Section 3.5.3). Although SOFTCo had an opportunity to control the potential vicious cycle and reset the ties, it decided to ignore those issues and continued with its opportunistic and unilateral practices of misappropriation. Such persistent behavior over time persuaded partners to band together, seek acquisition, or diversify into other businesses. This is consistent with the temporal perspective on alliances that focuses on the time of the event (e.g., when), the role of the event under emergent conditions, and the consequences (Shi et al. 2012).

## 4. Implications of the Study, Limitations, and Conclusion

## 4.1. Theoretical Implications

We started our research with the goal of examining value destruction in the context of real-world IT eco system, leveraging on an iterative mixed-method approach for a multifaceted and in-depth understanding. We believe that our study makes a few distinct contributions on the theoretical front. First, despite suggestions by practitioners and academic researchers, both within and outside of the IS discipline, that value creation processes (such as those within a software development ecosystem) can potentially spiral into value destruction, we have had very little research on how value destruction unfolds and what its consequences can be. Our study addresses this important area by empirically examining the possibility of, and the potential consequences of, the intended value co-creation process taking a wrong exit toward the dark side, that is, toward value destruction. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the possibility of relational risks and uncertainties whenever parties engage with each other to co-create value (Lintula et al. 2018). The actualization of the risks and uncertainties leads the parties to end up with value destruction because these are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict despite legal contracts. They surface in the face of diverse contingencies experienced by focal players and the ecosystem over time, testing them on mutual trust, coordination capabilities, and resolution mechanisms.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also validate the view that value destruction is a multidimensional and multilevel construct and phenomenon and that when the spiral of value destruction starts, multiple types of value can get eroded and lost. In this regard, we extend the work of Grover and Kohli (2012, p. 232) and other researchers in this domain, particularly on the types of value that can be destroyed. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study reveals that during a value destruction process, the following dimensions of value could potentially be lost because of agency issues, asymmetry, misappropriation, and opportunism: (1) relationship-specific assets (i.e., hurtful partnership and resulting resentment destroy parties’ relationships and the ecosystem created around the ERP package that needs customization and continual upgrades), (2) knowledge-sharing routines (i.e., loss of collaborative learning and development of package’s functional capabilities), (3) complementary resources and capabilities (i.e., giving up on the codevelopment of the application by leveraging technology and industry knowledge), and (4) effective governance (i.e., disregarding ecosystem synergies and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies). This observation also complements prior work of Sarker et al. (2012, p. 319 and 335), who highlight the multidimensionality of the value construct, although their study focused on co-creation. Our study uniquely reveals value destruction in the form of reduced tangible (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nancial) and intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., trust and reputation in the market). Such losses are eventually experienced by the individual parties as well as the ecosystem. Multiple levels of the ecosystem are affected in the long run as the vicious cycle of value destruction wipes out value of the entire ecosystem and individual parties, even though in the short term, it may seem that the opportunistic party is gaining value via misappropriation of rents. Indeed, such multidimensional and multilevel value destruction and subsequent reaction by the distressed and resentful parties may open up opportunities for the competitors in the market, leading to further loss of value.

Third, our study provides more depth to the existing understanding of underlying mechanisms of opportunism in collaborative relationships, especially emphasizing that opportunism itself can unfold in multiple forms, with one or more parties involved in the ecosystem relationship reneging on their commitments. Our study also sheds light on the role of agency behavior and opportunism between partners that have asymmetric power, and that, in such cases, these behaviors are not necessarily exhibited by the one that is powerful but also by the ones with less power. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst schema of value destruction we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, the powerful hub <sup>fi</sup>rm opportunistically engages in cheating or unjust appropriation of rents. The second schema shows value destruction through service partner’s (i.e., a given complementor’s) self-interest seeking via shirking of contractual obligations, serving its own interests at the cost of time and attention to its partner’s (i.e., the hub <sup>fi</sup>rm’s) products, and exploiting speci<sup>fi</sup>city of location and resource to take advantage of the partner’s dependence. The third schema re<sup>fl</sup>ects value destruction via opportunistic and agency behaviors by both the partners (i.e., the hub <sup>fi</sup>rm and a given complementor), resulting in substantial losses in terms of intangible and tangible resources and capabilities. In each of these schemas, our study reveals not only the nature of behaviors exhibited but also the speci<sup>fi</sup>c impacts it has on the partners.

Our study also reveals controllable aspects of the process of value destruction itself, drawing attention to the fact that the vicious cycle of value destruction can be halted and reversed by the managers within a certain timeframe, and ties can be assessed and reset as part of the ecosystem management process. If not done strategically within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c interval, this cycle can rapidly destroy the value co-creation initiative and can erode not only the value that was to be generated through the ecosystem arrangement but also some of the original value of the IT <sup>fi</sup>rms involved. Through this important implication, we shed light on the management of ecosystems associated with software development and computing in general, and thereby respond to calls to build more contextualized studies surrounding this important phenomenon (Hong et al. 2013).

Fifth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have implications for strategic decision making and governance of exchange relationships such as alliances in general and IT ecosystems in particular, which are inherently complex (Ple and C´ a ceres 2010, Sarker et al. 2012, Selander et al. 2013,

Majchrzak et al. 2016, Pathak et al. 2020). Most of the extant literature in social sciences points toward relatively static factors (e.g., legal contracts or alliance/ ecosystem structure) for the failures (Park and Ungson 2001, Das 2004, Kumar et al. 2019, Pathak et al. 2020). Kumar et al. (2019, p. 158) notes that “curiously underrepresented in the literature is an emphasis on how alliances are managed in practice.” Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight dynamic and temporal nature of exchange relationships suggesting that behavioral appropriateness focused on long-term value is important. Impressions of incremental tactical and strategic decisions (e.g., misappropriating rents, violating the spirit of relationship) accumulate over time and impact the nature of the relationship as it evolves. Formal contracts are not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient; governance mechanisms focusing on the relationship itself such as a dedicated committee, or a function/unit to manage coordination, trust, and emergent issues need to be implemented to prevent such arrangements from degenerating (Kale and Singh 2009). Identifying the destructive behavioral signals such as those revealed by our study and intervening well in time to reset the ties toward value co-creation can bene<sup>fi</sup>t all the stakeholders while keeping competitors at bay.

Finally, our study offers a relatively novel methodological illustration within the IS discipline. During the last three decades, there have been many calls within the IS and our reference disciplines for conducting mixed-methods studies. This is because scholars agree that mixed-method studies can draw on the strengths of multiple methodologies to provide a more rigorous, deeper, and broader understanding of a phenomenon of interest (Tashakkori et al. 1998, Venkatesh et al. 2013). Our study responds to such calls. Furthermore, the typical mixed-methods studies within the discipline have used qualitative inquiry followed by traditional quantitative methods (e.g., surveys) in a sequential “dominant-less-dominant” design (Sarker et al. 2018b, Califf et al. 2020). We contribute to this body of work by providing a potentially novel illustration of mixedmethods, involving approaches that are often considered very different from each other and thus not used together. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we use a combination of analytical modeling and an interpretive case study as part of a concurrent triangulation approach (Venkatesh et al. 2013) enabled by the hermeneutic circle. We believe that the use of a case study and game-theoretic modeling was instrumental in achieving the often-missing balance between “dogmatism” and “skepticism” of researchers (Ricoeur 1991, p. 160), revealing certain unexpected behaviors with some degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence and formulating relevant propositions. We hope that through this methodological approach, we have also been able to demonstrate the value of combining economics-based perspectives and qualitative inquiry (Sarker et al. 2018b)

as opposed to seeing them as incommensurable and divergent perspectives to scholarly inquiry. This study can also provide an impetus for investigating a variety of phenomena in IS and related disciplines that involve exchanges. These may include digital platforms and sharing economies that bring together multiple stakeholders with the intention of cocreating value for all involved.

## 4.2. Managerial Implications

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study also have important implications for managers, especially of software <sup>fi</sup>rms who increasingly <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves participating in ecosystems. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings caution against taking the value co-creation assumption as granted and alert managers to instances where the original objectives of co-creation of value can be severely compromised. Our revelations draw attention to the dangers of opportunism and agency behavior in combination with power tripping, and the vicious cycle of destruction such a combination can potentially generate for the parties and the ecosystem. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings underscore the view that such self-interested behaviors do not necessarily pay off in repeat partnerships, that such behaviors can be exhibited irrespective of the power one holds in a relationship within an ecosystem, and that with persistent opportunism and power tripping, returns can keep going down and potentially become negative through collateral and unintended losses for the parties. Managers also need to be aware that opportunism and power tripping in an ecosystem relationship have local and global impacts. In other words, value destruction can have short-term impact in terms of the loss of resources and capabilities for the partners; it could also change the competitive landscape of the industry because of formation of newer partnerships outside of the original ecosystem.

Finally, we believe that our study not only makes an important contribution to our understanding of value destruction in ecosystems but that the study’s results have important implications for other related areas such as participatory user design, open-source software development, and sourcing of IT work, all of which involve collaboration and a close coupling between stakeholders with asymmetric power (Hahn et al. 2009). We hope that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings will enable the managers to set up relatively transparent and trustworthy relationships with other organizations, control moral hazards, and help <sup>fi</sup>rms realize synergistic objectives.

## 4.3. Limitations

As with most research, the results of this study should be interpreted keeping the limitations in mind. One limitation of our study is that we were unable to interview several of the secondary stakeholders on both sides (that is, PartnerCos and SOFTCo). Similarly, we could not collect data from other external observers who saw this process unfold during the period. Nevertheless, we feel that we had suf<sup>fi</sup>cient immersion in the context for a long period to have an authentic understanding of the value destruction process.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are based on the unfolding of relational dynamics in business-to-business setting over time, repeat negotiations in a continuously changing technological and business environment, evolving customer needs, and multiple interactions between parties with shifting power asymmetries. As such, the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not be fully applicable in the context of one-time business-to-consumer exchanges (e.g., purchase of a simple of<sup>fi</sup>ce application suite by a regular customer or purchase of a tax <sup>fi</sup>ling software by an individual). Furthermore, we believe that distressing environmental conditions such as economic recession could affect the levels of opportunism, asymmetric powerplay, and formation of collectives. Future research could explore the impact of such conditions on diverse aspects of arrangements in ecosystems. We also acknowledge that given the nature of our study, we could not offer de<sup>fi</sup>nitive levels of contingency factors that lead to speci<sup>fi</sup>c outcomes. For example, what levels (including threshold) of opportunism-mitigating and powerbalancing factors might keep an ecosystem running, thereby saving it from value destruction? Or what is the threshold of behavioral contingencies and external conditions (e.g., quanti<sup>fi</sup>able levels of exploitation, and competitors’ presence in the market) for motivating the partners to form a collective to push back the dominant player for fair negotiations and for creating equitable environment to save value co-creation from degenerating into value destruction? Finally, under what combinations of governance structures and contingencies would the structurally asymmetric parties not take the wrong exit toward value destruction, and even if they take it, they could be brought back on the path of value co-creation in time? Although we have discussed some tendencies seen in the data (Walsham 1995), with more empirical work, the conditions and their levels can be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed with greater precision.

## 4.4. Conclusion

Ecosystems related to IT products and services are of immense interest to IS researchers. Although there is a widespread assumption that such ecosystems generate positive value, our study draws attention to the idea of value destruction, which is not an uncommon occurrence in reality but is seldom a topic of scholarly work. Drawing on a mixed-method study, we develop an understanding of how value co-creation can degenerate into value destruction. We hope that our study will prompt future researchers to further engage in the exploration of the dark side of IT-based ecosystem and help reduce the extent of the ecosystems’ failures by being aware of how, when, and why such intended symbiotic relationships can turn dysfunctional.
