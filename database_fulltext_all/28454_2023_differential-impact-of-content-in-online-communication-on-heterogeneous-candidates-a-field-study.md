Abstract. Recruitment is a critical activity for companies, and the research community has also shown signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest in examining technology recruitment. Companies often communicate how they value their employees along with job requirements to potential candidates in a bid to attract them. However, there is an overall lack of understanding of how candidates react to such information and how their motivation toward the job changes with such online communication. Although there is substantial work that exam ines the decision-making process of managers who do technical hiring, to the best of our knowledge, there is a paucity of work that investigates the decision-making process of technical candidates. The broad research question studied is how including certain content in online communication about a technical job opportunity may (de)motivate heterogeneous candidates differently in applying for the job. We theorize the underlying process of how including certain content in online job communication may in<sup>fl</sup>uence candidates propensity to apply and their minimum acceptable salary increase if they were to join the company. Additionally, we capture mediating variables that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the effect of different online content on candidates’ propensity to apply and on candidates’ minimum acceptable salary increase. By testing actual job application behavior in a <sup>fi</sup>eld study, we con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the content that can attract high performers while discouraging low performers from applying.

## Introduction

“Hiring top-notch technical talent is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and competitive these days,” says Peter Johnson, a human resources executive at a SaaS company in the western United States. “We’ve tried everything that we can think of to attract high-performing employees, but the few good resumes we get are often lost in the sea of other candidates that we have to wade through” (Johnson 2018). Mr. Johnson’s experience is not unique, as companies struggle to attract high-performing technical talent to apply for their positions. These struggles are expected to increase further, especially for technical talent, as the demand for junior-level technical job candidates such as computer programmers is expected to grow by 28% over the next two years, whereas positions for high-experience jobs, such as information technology (IT) management roles, are expected to increase by 18% according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2018). An additional challenge is that even if the <sup>fi</sup>rms can recruit someone, the new recruits often leave the company too soon. As much as 60% new hire turnover has been reported in technology services departments (Leidner et al. 2018, U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2020a), re<sup>fl</sup>ecting candidates’ expectation mismatch with what the company values. Several works (Weitz 1956, Dugoni and Ilgen 1981, O’Reilly and Caldwell 1981, Breaugh and Starke 2000, Billsberry 2007) have documented that companies need to communicate carefully to avoid expectation mismatches, highlighting the strong need for researchers to help companies <sup>fi</sup>gure out how they can better communicate with job candidates to reduce hiring mismatch.

Recruitment is a critical activity for companies, and the research community has also shown signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest in examining technology recruitment, particularly studying the needs and focus of the hiring managers (Todd et al. 1995, Ang et al. 2002, Zwieg et al. 2006, Bangerter et al. 2012, Sharaburyak et al. 2020).

Researchers initially focused on increasing technology acceptance and making the case for hiring more technology workers in companies (McLaughlin 1979, Stibbens 1981, Hartog and Herbert 1986). Later, when acceptance was less of a challenge, several studies highlighted the larger problem of misalignment of recruitment processes with organization objectives (Todd et al. 1995, Roepke et al. 2000, Zwieg et al. 2006, Gallagher et al. 2010). Using a survey on recruitment practices in technology, Ferratt et al. (1999) investigated human resources (HR) practices used by hiring managers and suggested that a company can differentiate itself from competitors by how it communicates about itself to job candidates. Companies often communicate how they value their employees along with job requirements to potential candidates in a bid to attract the candidates (Cable et al. 2000, Watkins and Smith 2018). However, there is an overall lack of understanding of how technical candidates react to such information and how their motivation toward the job changes with such online communication.

In this paper, the broad research question that we explore is how including certain content in a company’s online communication about a technical job opportunity (de)motivates heterogeneous candidates differently in applying for the job. Because it is easier for companies to communicate their values and culture through online recruitment content (Puncheva-Michelotti et al. 2018), it is important to understand how this content affects heterogenous candidates. Our work investigates heterogeneity among candidates for technical positions across two dimensions—performance and seniority. We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examine how companies’ engagement in technical recruiting by promoting online communications content on technology employees’ work efforts and outcomes can affect candidates’ perceptions of remuneration, which in turn, in<sup>fl</sup>uences candidates’ decisions to apply. We also study how including online communications content on employees’ personal interests and beliefs can affect technical candidates’ perceptions of self-expression and their decisions to apply.

We contribute to the growing stream of research on technical recruitment. Although there is existing work that examines the decision making of managers who do technical hiring (Todd et al. 1995, Ang et al. 2002, Zwieg et al. 2006, Bangerter et al. 2012, Sharaburyak et al. 2020), to the best of our knowledge, there is far less work that investigates the decision making of candidates, especially as it relates to candidates for technical positions. The lack of understanding of technical candidates’ motivations makes it hard for employers to attract candidates and motivate them to apply. A related concern is also understanding how to demotivate candidates without the desired characteristics, such as low-performing senior candidates. Unlike general recruiting, technical recruiting is complicated by the fact that, although there is an overall shortage of technical labor in the marketplace (Decker and McGill 2017, Armstrong et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2019), industry data suggest that most experienced technical employees tend to be passive job seekers (Monster. com 2020). In technical recruiting, the challenge is to motivate high-performing candidates to apply and demotivate candidates who are not high-performing candidates. Currently, recruiters end up wasting a lot of their resources going through a large pool of candidates only to reject them and are left with little time to motivate high-performing candidates to join their companies. To the best of our knowledge, no extant research addresses these speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues, and this study <sup>fi</sup>lls an important gap in the information systems (IS) research. We can show that different types of candidates are both motivated and demotivated differently. Without this understanding, companies may assume that the effects of these factors on subgroup motivations to apply are homogenous. The differential effects would then be averaged out and the subgroup effects would be masked. By teasing out the different motivations of these subgroups of candidates, this study can provide better insights for companies about how to better attract technical candidates with the characteris tics that they prefer. Additionally, the extant work on technical recruiting has been focused on intentions to apply or other proxies for motivation to apply for positions. We <sup>fi</sup>ll these gaps in the literature and theorize how including certain content in online communication about a job opportunity can change the motivation of various candidate subgroups to submit an actual application for the job. We also con<sup>fi</sup>rm the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by using an alternate operationalization of the motivation to apply for a job, which is the minimum acceptable salary increase.

We conducted a <sup>fi</sup>eld study in partnership with a technical recruitment <sup>fi</sup>rm and one of its clients, a technology company, who was particularly interested in understanding what content to include in online communication about a job to attract be<sup>fi</sup>tting prospective candidates. For the study, candidates were sent a job opportunity and certain additional content via email. The additional content was decided based on random assignment of candidates to one of the three groups: treatment group with employees’ posts about their work efforts and outcomes (WE), treatment group with employees’ posts about their personal interests and beliefs (PI), and a control group with no WE or PI posts. We observed candidates’ actual decision-making processes including the perceptions they formed about the job opportunity in the email. Particularly, we captured how heterogeneous candidates weigh these perceptions differently and how these weights in<sup>fl</sup>uence candidates motivation or demotivation to apply for positions and their minimum acceptable salary increase. By accounting for the heterogeneity among candidates, we can tease out contrasting behaviors of heterogeneous candidates and show how, when ignored, it can lead to averaging out of the effects and leading to incorrect conclusions. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold despite controlling for an exhaustive list of plausible controls including perceptions of job skills <sup>fi</sup>t, future growth prospects, work–life balance, social welfare opportunities, and culture <sup>fi</sup>t.

In the remainder of this paper, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review the relevant literature on technical recruiting, and motivation theory. Using motivation theory, we then illustrate how including certain content in online communication about a technology job can potentially motivate candidates. We then theorize the hypotheses, discuss the <sup>fi</sup>eld study design, and provide details of the data collection process. Finally, we conclude with the research and managerial implications of this study.

## Literature and Background

Recruitment for technical positions is a critical issue for companies and the research community has also shown signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest in the topic. Researchers are increasingly studying recruitment in the technology sector. However, most of the focus has been on the preferences of hiring managers. Using a survey on recruitment practices in technology, Ferratt et al. (1999) investigated HR practices used by hiring managers and found that hiring managers preferred to use employee referrals for sourcing, focused on interpersonal skill requirements, and overall technical aptitude for IT recruitment. Although they did not study the recruitment from candidates’ perspectives, they suggested that a company can differentiate itself from competitors by how it communicates about itself to the candidates. Ang et al. (2002) investigated the role of compensation strategies used by hiring managers in attracting and retaining technology professionals. By analyzing salary data for 1,576 technology professionals, they found that candidates with higher experience and better education receive higher compensation. They also found that technology professionals with high (low) education receive a higher salary in bigger (smaller) companies.

In recent years, researchers have looked at another dimension that may affect hiring managers’ decisions in recruiting IT professionals, and that dimension is social media. Anecdotal and academic evidence suggests that hiring managers use social media to access personal information about job candidates while making their hiring decisions (Sharaburyak et al. 2020). For example, in recent work, Wade et al. (2020) looked into how accessing social media content could negatively impact a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s process of hiring new employees. By analyzing experimental data of 191 graduate student participants, they showed that viewing job-irrelevant social media content posted by candidates impacts hiring managers through evoking feelings of similarity and liking, which in turn affects their perceived hireability of the can didate. That is, when the hiring managers <sup>fi</sup>nd the viewpoints expressed in candidates’ job-irrelevant posts to resonate with their own viewpoints, they are more likely to hire the candidate. These effects were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant even in the presence of information relating to a job candidate’s knowledge, skills, and abilities.

Although this is extant literature in IS addresses the use of online social media in the management decisionmaking process in recruiting, there is an overall lack of understanding of candidates’ motivation and what they look for in technology jobs. This is especially important because companies often struggle to hire technology professionals (Raadabadi et al. 2018, Zoll et al. 2018). Another challenge is that, even if they can recruit someone, candidates often leave the company too soon (Wingreen and Blanton 2018, Lin 2020). As much as 60% of new hire turnover has been reported in technology departments, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting expectation mismatch between companies and candidates (Igbaria et al. 1994, Leidner et al. 2018, Putri and Aldrin 2019). Hence, there is a need to better understand how to communicate with candidates about jobs, set appropriate expectations, and either motivate them to apply if they are a good <sup>fi</sup>t for the company or demotivate them from applying if they do not have the characteristics that are desired by the company.

Unlike general recruiting, technical recruiting is complicated by the fact that, although there is an overall shortage of technical labor in the marketplace (Decker and McGill 2017, Armstrong et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2019), industry data suggest that most experienced technical employees tend to be passive job seekers (Monster.com 2020). These passive job seekers, who are currently employed and not actively searching for a job, can be particularly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to motivate to apply (Steel 2002, Trusty et al. 2019). In addition, the cost of technical recruitment has skyrocketed because of the highly variable quality of potential candidates in the technology labor pool (Agrawal 2019). Although online recruitment efforts are more likely to reach these passive seekers (Cober et al. 2000, Parry and Tyson 2008, Thompson et al. 2008, Amadoru and Gamage 2016, Acikgoz 2019), if high-performing candidates cannot be accurately targeted, the company may spend their resources trying to motivate candidates that may not meet their needs. In addition to requiring additional expenditures in recruitment, technical workers tend to have higher than average salaries for nonmanagement roles (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2020b). Additional costs in onboarding and training of technical workers can also be very high (Thatcher et al. 2002, Dinger et al. 2011). Therefore, attracting and motivating the most appropriate candi dates allows the <sup>fi</sup>rm to avoid these costs, along with wasted time and effort.

Motivation theory has been well established in the literature to study such decision-making processes (Vroom 1964, Vroom 1966, Reinharth and Wahba 1975, Van Eerde and Thierry 1996). This body of work states that an important contributor toward motivation to perform an action is the expectation that the action will yield an anticipated bene<sup>fi</sup>t, which is called instrumentality. The greater the expectation the individual has that the instrumentality will be realized, the more in<sup>fl</sup>uential the instrumentality will be in the decision-making process. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of instrumentalities also depends on the importance or weight that the candidates place on them in their decision-making process. This weight is called valence. The total motivational effect depends on the product of the expectation of instrumentality and the valence that the individual places on that instrumentality. As a result, one way to understand candidates’ motivation is to identify the instrumentalities which they might value.

There are several instrumentalities that might motivate candidates to apply for technical positions. The literature suggests that pay is an important motivating instrumentality for job candidates (Schuster et al. 1971, Rynes et al. 1983, Bakker and Demerouti 2007, Ruizpalomino et al. 2013, Wang and Kaarst-Brown 2014); however, research in performance-based salary suggests that pay motivates people differently based on their priorities and characteristics (Rynes et al. 1983, Park and Sturman 2016). Additionally, prior research suggests that opportunities for self-expression in the workplace can also be a motivator for candidates to apply for positions (Alam and Campbell 2017, Green et al. 2017); however, the intensity of this motivation can depend on the intrinsic need to self-express (Green 2007). There are other potential factors that can motivate candidates to apply for positions. Work–life balance is frequently cited by job candidates as important to their job decision process (Proost and Verhaest 2018). Research has found support for a relationship between lack of work– life balance in organizations and high levels of personal stress (Westman et al. 2009) and increased turnover (Chimote and Srivastava 2013). Research also suggests that a company’s social welfare programs (Jones et al. 2014, Slack et al. 2015), and future growth opportunities for the candidate (Tepeci and Wildes 2002, Trank et al. 2002) may also be important factors in motivating candidates. Similarly, the <sup>fi</sup>t between the position and the candidate (Carless 2005) and the <sup>fi</sup>t between the workplace culture and the candidate (O’Reilly et al. 1991, Cable and Judge 1996) are also considered important motivating factors for candidates.

Despite this extant research in potential motivational instrumentalities, there is still gap in the information systems research related to how technical candidates perceive these instrumentalities from companies’ electronic communications and how the candidates’ characteristics and heterogeneities may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the value that they may place on these instrumentalities. Our work differs from extant and cited literature in that we not only identify factors that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence candidates’ decisions to apply, but we also use the instrumentalities of performance-based remuneration and opportunities for self-expression to demonstrate the mechanics by which company communications might either motivate or demotivate technical candidate applications. We use these two instrumentalities because prior research suggests that performance-based remuneration and opportunity for self-expression have the ability to demonstrate different behaviors from subgroups of potential employees. Next, we discuss remuneration and self-expression in more depth and form our hypotheses based on this theoretical discussion.

## Theory Development and Hypotheses

It is generally accepted that pay is an important motivator for a candidate looking to apply for a position (Schuster et al. 1971, Rynes et al. 1983, Bakker and Demerouti 2007, Ruiz-palomino et al. 2013, Wang and Kaarst-Brown 2014); however, research in performancebased salary suggests that pay motivates people differently based on their priorities and characteristics (Rynes et al. 1983, Park and Sturman 2016). High performers tend to be more motivated by pay structures where rewards are proportionate to their contribution, whereas lower performers prefer pay structures where compensation is not tied to individual performance (Rynes 1987, Zenger 1992, Trank et al. 2002). Because of these differences in preferences toward performance-based pay, especially between high and low performers, we would expect that this factor would motivate job applicants differently (Rynes 1987, Zenger 1992, Kuhn and Yockey 2003).

Singh et al. (2014) suggest that employees that engage with online posts on work efforts tend to be higher performing compared with employees that engage with posts on personal interests. Delivering high performance takes a lot of effort and discipline. High performers tend to be more focused on their jobs and may think about it even during their free time (Martel 2002). Because of their extra effort and the higher contribution that they make to the company, high performers often expect to be compensated more than others (Schaubroeck et al. 2008). High performers may feel cheated when they observe recognition being conferred to those who contribute less toward the com pany (Cherrington et al. 1971). They are generally more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with companies that administer recognition to those who contribute more toward the company (Podsakoff et al. 1982). Hence, it is important for compa nies to set rewards and recognition that correspond to work performance. However, companies often may not have good processes in place to identify employees performance levels accurately, and this could be frustrating for high performers (Martel 2003). When candidates see employees celebrating their work efforts and observe their companies’ appreciation for their work efforts in online communication, the candidates may perceive that the company has better processes in place to recognize employees’ efforts and that the company values their employees’ dedication toward their work. They may interpret such online communication as an indication that companies value these employee contributions and will reward employees for their effort. High performers will likely feel that they will be fairly treated at such a company and thus be more motivated to apply. By contrast, low performers, relatively speaking, may not think about how they can improve their productivity at work routinely (Hausknecht et al. 2009). They are more likely to pick activities outside work that are not focused on their work efforts. Therefore, they are likely to feel marginalized in a company that is perceived to observe employees’ productivity closely and recognize employees’ work.

If content on employees’ work efforts and outcomes in online communication increases the expectation of performance-based remuneration, then including such content should increase the motivation of the highperforming candidates to apply more than when they receive no such content. This may be especially true for technical workers and therefore important for technical recruiting. Although the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of performancebased pay is somewhat unclear in general recruiting (Hyde 2005, Lee and Jimenez 2011), research suggests that IT workers prefer company practices that promote performance-based pay (Agarwal and Ferratt 2001, Ferratt et al. 2005, Bloom et al. 2012). Conversely, including such content should decrease the motivation of the lowperforming technical candidates, because they are likely to be demotivated to apply for these positions because they would not be rewarded for their low performance (Judge and Piccolo 2004, Han et al. 2015). Because the perception of performance-based remuneration is suggested by employees’ posts on work efforts and outcomes, we would also expect that the perception of performance-based remuneration would mediate the effect of WE content on the propensity of technical candidates to apply for positions. These relationships are represented by Hypotheses H1a and H1b.

Hypothesis 1a. Including content on employees’ work efforts and outcomes (WE) in an online communication about a job should (decrease) increase (low-performing) high-performing technical candidates’ propensity to apply for the job.

Hypothesis 1b. Effect of WE content on technical candidates’ propensity to apply is mediated by the perception of performance-based remuneration.

Another instrumentality that might motivate tech nical candidates differently to apply for positions is the opportunity for self-expression in the workplace (Twenge 2006). Prior research associates self-expression with an individual’s ability to present their personal interests and beliefs to others (Trammell et al. 2006, Lee 2010). Because technology has been shown to be an enabler of self-expression (Kervegant´ 1996, Lee et al. 2011), technical workers that are comfortable and familia with technology would be more likely to engage in self expressive activities online than their nontechnical counterparts. Workers in technical <sup>fi</sup>eld are generally curious and creative people, who want to make a differ ence, whether in their <sup>fi</sup>eld or in the world at large (Thatcher et al. 2012, Holden 2015). This suggests that technical workers can be particularly motivated b opportunities for self-expression (Pittinsky and Shih 2004), which makes this an important factor in technica recruitment. Technical employees might actually be alienated if they feel that their companies do not allow enough opportunity for self-expression in the work place (Nair and Vohra 2010, Petriglieri et al. 2019). Online communication about employees’ personal interests can also indicate that employees have more freedom for expressing themselves within the organization, which can often contribute to increased employee engagement with these activities (Grant 2012). The can didates should therefore anticipate opportunities for self-expression in the company when they see such con tent about existing employees’ personal interests. This effect might be augmented when companies provide recognition to their employees for their personal activ ities because it can provide the employee with a platform for their self-expression to be observed by others (Fisher and Ackerman 1998). When job candidates see content describing employees’ personal interests, they are likely to expect that they could have similar opportunities to express themselves if they were to join the company.

Furthermore, prior work suggests that younger professionals should feel more comfortable when others see them as they see themselves because people tend to seek validation for their most strongly held beliefs (Swann et al. 2004, Twenge 2006). Although prior research suggests that opportunities for self-expression in the workplace can also be a motivator for candidates to apply for positions (Nair and Vohra 2010, Alam and Campbell 2017, Green et al. 2017), the intensity of this motivation can be dependent on the intrinsic need to self-express (Green 2007, Lee and Whitford 2008). In particular, younger workers in comparison with senior workers are more likely to value the opportunities for self-expression in the workplace (Howe and Strauss 2009, Balda and Mora 2011). The literature also suggests that this need for younger workers to self-express is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than for senior employees and extends both inside and outside of the workplace (Cherrier 2007, Kim and Jang 2014). These younger workers also tend to desire recognition for their activities because this acts to promote their self-expression to others (Balda and Mora 2011). The implication is that younger employees should have a stronger preference for a workplace that promotes selfexpression because of the higher value that they place on this bene<sup>fi</sup>t. Within the perspective of IT recruitment, we would therefore posit the following.

Hypothesis 2a. Including content on employees’ personal interests and beliefs (PI) in an online communication about a job should increase junior technical candidates’ propensity to apply more than that of senior candidates.

Hypothesis 2b. Effect of PI content on technical candidates’ propensity to apply is mediated by the perception of self-expression.

## Field Experiment Design and Data

We conducted a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in collaboration with a large technical recruiting <sup>fi</sup>rm that specializes in identifying and approaching suitable candidates to <sup>fi</sup>ll technology positions for its clients. The recruiter maintains and uses a large database of technology professionals. Two real-world job positions with multiple openings for a client, a technology company, were used for the study. An entry-level software developer position, Software Developer, represented the junior (lower-experience) role requiring candidates with zero to two years of experience. A project manager position, Senior Project Manager, represented the senior (higher-experience) role requiring candidates with at least seven years of experience. We worked with the client to create two different online social media pages that represent employee-originated online communication curated by the company. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one had employees’ posts on their work efforts and outcomes. These are the posts that employees publish about their work responsibilities and recognition for those efforts. These posts may include how an employee’s clever redesign of a data model reduced the volume of data stored, reduced the time for data retrieval and improved the manageability of the system, or how some client has appreciated an employee’s ability to ask pertinent questions and their systematic methodological approach to improving processes, not just technical processes, but also people and operational processes. The second social media page had employees’ posts on personal interests and beliefs. These are the posts that employees publish related to their actions on their beliefs and values, volunteering efforts, and pursuing their passions. These posts may cover how a few employees competed in a sports tournament or helped raise money for a pet shelter.

We worked with the recruiter to identify a pool of potential candidates from the recruiter’s candidate database for the previously mentioned two job positions. For each job position, the recruiter shortlisted two separate pools of 450 potential job candidates. Because the same manipulation process was used for both jobs, we will next discuss the control group and manipulations for a single job only. From the shortlisted 450 candidates for a job, 150 candidates were randomly picked as a control group. The recruiter sent emails to these control group candidates with information about the job. For the remaining 300 candidates, in addition to the job information, the recruiter sent links to and the content of posts from this client’s online social media pages. Of these 300, 150 randomly selected candidates received content from the client’s social media page covering employees’ work efforts and outcomes. The remaining 150 of 300 candidates received content from the client’s social media page covering employees’ personal interests and beliefs.

The structure of the email was as follows. The subject of the email mentioned position availability for the job title. The email started with a pleasantry and then discussed the job details. Three hundred emails of 450 total emails for the job also had content from the client’s online social media page. Of these 300 emails, 150 emails also included content in the email body from the client’s social media page with employee posts about their work efforts and the rest had content from the client’s social media page with employee posts about their personal interests. Before this online content, emails noted that the buttons to apply for the job and to explore other jobs were at the end of the email. If a candidate clicked to explore other jobs without applying for the job, then they were prompted to answer whether they want to apply for the suggested job. Candidates were requested to take a survey, irrespective of whether they applied for the job, clicked to explore other jobs, and then applied or did not apply for the job. The survey included questions on instrumentalities, which are the participants’ perceptions of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that they might receive if they were to work for the company, across various control dimensions, and self-expression and performance-based remuneration. The survey also gathered data on other controls such as current savings, car loans, home loans, volunteer hours, and time spent reading social media. The candidates were informed that answering the questions of the survey was optional and had no bearing on their application submission or status. The content of the survey can be found in Online Appendix A.

We also tracked and controlled for whether candidates clicked on the links in the email and opened the client’s source social media page. We compared candidates’ pro<sup>fi</sup>les who opened the client’s social media page with those who did not and found no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant difference between the two. As an attention check, we also asked candidates whether they remembered the content that they saw. Only a small number of candidates answered that they did not, and we dropped the data points for those candidates from our analyses. We also compared the pro<sup>fi</sup>les of the candidates who did not remember the included content in online communication about a job with those who did, and we found no signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant difference. Comparison details are reported in the next section. We also tracked and controlled for whether candidates applied to a job after clicking the button to see other jobs.

## Measures

We recorded whether candidates applied for the position as a binary variable “Apply,” with the value of one if the candidates applied for the job and zero if they did not. The experimental treatments of online communication including content about work efforts and outcomes (WE) and about personal interest and beliefs (PI) activities were recorded with two binary variables. These dummy variables are one when the content was about work efforts and personal interest, respectively, and zero otherwise. The value of zero for both binary variables represented the control group without any links to online content. The measures for the candidates’ perceptions of the instrumentality variable, performance-based remuneration, were designed based on a survey instrument proposed by Schuster et al. (1971) and included three items, such as “In this company, how likely would you get a salary increase based on your performance.” The responses to these three items were recorded on a seven-point Likert scale of Extremely Unlikely to Extremely Likely. Candidates’ perceptions of the instrumentality, freedom of self-expression, were suggested by Brown and Leigh (1996) and were recorded using four items, such as “In this company, how likely would you need to misrepresent your views that you maintain about things outside the work.” The responses to these four items were recorded on a seven-point Likert scale as well. Additional control variables were also included in the survey instrument. Social welfare items were developed based on the work of Homburg et al. (2013). We also consulted literature on work–life balance (Jang 2009), personjob <sup>fi</sup>t (Caldwell and O’Reilly 1990), growth opportunities (Allen et al. 2009), and work culture (Judge and Cable 1997) to create corresponding items in our survey and were measured on a seven-point Likert scale. These are reported in Online Appendix A. The face validity of all the items was tested by consulting with an expert panel of three faculty members and two individuals from the recruiting company. Feedback from these experts on the appropriateness of the items for measuring the concepts, wording, and overall readability was accommodated in the items (Hardesty and Bearden 2004). Factor loadings for the above measures are provided later in this section.

For each of the candidates, we also had additional information from the recruiter’s database on demographic variables such as gender (gender 1 for male, 0 for female), age, current job tenure (number of months), current annual salary, past work experiences, education, and the number of employees at their current employer (grouped into 1–10, 11–20, 21–50, 51–100, 100 ). A control for their social media use was recorded as the amount of time spent on social media (grouped into 0, 1–15, 16–30, 31–60, 61–90, 91–120, 120 minutes). Candidate perform ance data gathered from candidates’ managers at previous jobs by the recruiter was also obtained from the recruiter database. The performance data were aggregated in four groups, 0%–25%, 26%–50%, 51%–75%, and 76%–100%, indicating the candidates’ work performance compared with their peers. The data were converted into a binary variable with the value of one for the top group indicating high performers and zero for others. The <sup>fi</sup>nal sample consisted of 288 usable responses, of which 220 completed applications that were submitted for the job.

We added a dummy variable, opened social media page, to the model to control for any potential bias because of candidates opening the link to the online content. This dummy takes the value of one when a candidate clicked on a link to the online content and zero otherwise. We added a dummy variable, direct apply, to the model to control for any potential bias because of candidates applying after they clicked to see other jobs. This dummy takes the value of one when a candidate did not click the button to see other jobs and zero otherwise.

In addition, we also asked the candidates for thei minimum acceptable salary increase as another depend ent variable for testing the robustness of our model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the candidates answered the question: What is the least percentage increase in your current salary that you would expect to accept this job? The possible values ranged from 0% to 100% in intervals of 2.5%. The candidates were also provided an option to type in any value outside the provided range. Using the candidates’ current salary data combined with the mini mum acceptable increase also allowed us to test the robustness of our model using the minimum acceptable salary increase to join the company. Additional control variables such as candidates’ average monthly savings (in percentage), if they have a car or a home loan (1 yes, zero otherwise), the number of hours they spent on social responsibility activities (average hours pe month), and the type of social responsibility activities in which they participate were collected through the questionnaire and used in the robustness test. Two-hundred sixteen candidates provided this information.

## Test of Constructs

The appropriateness of using factor analysis was determined by using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. For the collected data, the KMO is 0.65, and the chi-square statistic is 2,502.28 $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , thus indicating that factor analysis is appropriate (Hair et al. 1998).

The next step in factor analysis is to perform principal components analysis (PCA). PCA examines the factor loading of each indicator, extracting items that do not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly load on the construct of interest. An item is extracted if it does not load above 0.60 on its own construct or above 0.40 on another construct. After determining the appropriate number of factors, the <sup>fi</sup>nal factor matrix was rotated using Varimax rotation. As seen in Table 4, all the factor loadings are greater than 0.6, which is the accepted standard.

Reliability analysis used Cronbach’s α and composite reliability to assess internal consistency (Chin 1998). Composite reliability (similar to Cronbach’s α) of 0.70 or higher is considered adequate (Fornell and Larcker 1981) and is considered more robust than Cronbach’s α because it weighs items differently depending on factor loading considerations (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000). Looking at Table 4, composite reliability and Cronbach’s α are greater than or equal to 0.699, indicating high reliability and stability for the measurement items of each construct.

Convergent validity used the three standards recommended by Bagozzi and Yi (1988) to assess the measurement model: (1) all indicator factor loadings should exceed 0.5 (Hair et al. 1998); (2) composite reliability should be above 0.7; and (3) the average variance extracted (AVE) of every construct should exceed 0.5 (Fornell and Larcker 1981). As Table 4 shows, the indicator factor loading of every item in the measuring model of this study exceeded 0.7. Composite reliability of constructs ranged from 0.84 to 0.98. AVE ranged from 0.603 to 0.721, therefore meeting all conditions for convergent validity.

## Mediation Analysis for Hypothesis H1b

Hypothesis H1b examines the mediating effect of performance-based remuneration on candidates’ motivation to apply for jobs. Table 8 shows the results for the effect of work efforts–related content on the perception of performance-based remuneration in the second column. The table also shows the effect on the propensity to apply for a job when the mediating variable, performance-based remuneration, is included in the third column. Finally, to conduct the mediation test, we used the mediator analysis advocated by Preacher and Hayes (2008). This approach has several advantages that are well suited for testing our model. First, this approach can estimate indirect effects for multiple mediators while accounting for covariance between the mediators. Second, this approach relies on bootstrapping and therefore does not require large sample sizes (Shrout and Bolger 2002, Preacher and Hayes 2008). Third, because indirect effect estimation involves the product of terms, they tend to have a skewed sampling distribution. The con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals (CIs) computed by the bootstrapping approach are robust to such nonnormal distributions (Wood et al. 2008).

## Robustness Checks

For the attention check, the candidates in our study were asked whether they remember seeing the included posts on employees’ work efforts or employees’ personal interests in the online communication about a job opportunity. About 91% of the candidates indicated that they remember seeing the content. Our analysis with responses from only those candidates who remembered the content yielded qualitatively similar results to those who don’t remember them. In addition to identifying whether the candidates actually applied for a position, we also used the questionnaire to ask for the minimum acceptable salary increase to join a company. The minimum acceptable salary increase is another operationalization for the motivation to work for a company. It is well documented in the literature that if candidates are interested in a company, then they may be willing to take a smaller salary increase to join the company compared with what they would expect for salary increase to join a company in which they were less interested (Coleman 2013, Ertug and Castellucci 2013, Burbano 2016). Someone who is more motivated to work for a company than others should be willing to take a smaller increase in the compensation than others. Because of this inverse relationship, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient signs that we see for the likelihood to apply should <sup>fl</sup>ip in the case of minimum acceptable salary increase, which we see in our analysis. Because this questionnaire also included additional controls such as the candidates’ volunteer hours, current savings, and loans, we have included these vari ables in our model. Because all participants did not complete this questionnaire, the sample size is smaller for this robustness check, consisting of 216 participants rather than 288 in our main models. The results are reported in Table 13 and are consistently opposite to the signs we see for the propensity to apply.

From Table 13, we see that, for high-performing candidates, the effect of content of work efforts on minimum acceptable salary increase is negative and signi-<sup>fi</sup>cant at α < 0.01, that is, 5.824 (4.459 10.283), for high-performing candidates. This shows that the high performing candidates are willing to accept a lower increase in salary if they see employees’ online content about their work efforts and outcomes, which suggest opportunities for performance-based remuneration. When high performers see content on employees’ work efforts and outcomes, they are more motivated to join the com pany in comparison with when they do not come across such posts in the company’s online communication. They seem to believe that they will earn higher future growth in pay after joining, and hence, they are willing to take a lesser salary increase at the time of joining. Likewise, low performers that see employees’ content about work efforts and outcomes would desire a greater increase in salary because they would expect that they would not receive any additional performance-based remuneration. Although the effects of PI content on minimum acceptable salary increase is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in this model, we note that there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interaction with performance, and therefore we include this for completeness.

These results are consistent with our main analysis and show that the effects hold even when other operationalizations are used for studying the candidates motivation. We also included additional controls from this questionnaire about their current debt obligations that could have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on their job applications. As an additional robustness check, we also operationalized the minimum acceptable salary increase in absolute value in addition to a percentage increase. The results were qualitatively similar.

For candidates as well, it is important to join a company where they feel they are valued, and they do not have to switch jobs too soon. Furthermore, low unemployment and high demand for IT professionals often make it very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for companies to get the attention of highperforming candidates. Using content on employees work efforts in online communication, companies can help reinforce the perception that they reward performance, motivate more high-performing technology candidates to apply and decrease their minimum acceptable salary increase for joining. Another important implication of our work is that content in online communication needs to be different for attracting junior and senior technology workers. Companies in many business domains, such as technical support, often face high attrition and must hire a large number of employees for junior technology roles on an ongoing basis. In such scenarios, by using content on employees’ personal interests and beliefs in online communication about a technology job, a company can project the perception that it provides employees with opportunities for selfexpression, motivating more technology candidates for junior roles to apply. This is consistent with past works that show the perception of self-expression allows technology workers to experience autonomy and independence in a workplace where they use their creative abilities and experience a feeling of competence and self-esteem and has been reported to be an important factor of consideration by junior candidates (Bonjean et al. 1994, Howe and Strauss 2009, Balda and Mora 2011, Cable et al. 2013). Overall, this work provides practitioners with actionable guidelines on how to get better matching of technology candidates for a technical job by manipulating content in online communication.

There are also numerous research implications and potential extensions of this work. Recruitment is an area of great interest to researchers, but there is little literature on examining the decision-making process of technology candidates. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt in this direction. In this study, our treatment group saw only one type of post. A natural research extension of our work would be to have more than one type of post in the treatment group and examine how it may motivate technology candidates differently. Particularly, posts on personal interests and beliefs may encourage junior candidates to apply for technology jobs irrespective of their performance level, thus increasing the workload for hiring managers. Although hiring managers would want to get more applications from high-performing junior candidates, they would not want to increase the applications from low-performing junior candidates. A solution to this problem might be to present both types of content simultaneously so that high-performing junior candidates will be motivated by both self-expression and remuneration content. It seems that low-performing junior candidates should be indifferent if they were to see both types of content because one of the posts will motivate them while the other one will demotivate them. Anothe interesting tradeoff to study in technical recruitment could be improved hiring potentially at the risk of losing current employees. Our results suggest that companies would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from their employees sharing posts related to their performance. If an employee is consistently talking about how the employer rewards their great work and their con tributions, then it should elevate the employee’s image in their professional network. They should receive more interest from recruiters and hiring managers with the intent to recruit them away from their current employer. Hence, employee posts may be a double-edged sword for an employer. Such posts should help the employer recruit more talent, but it may potentially increase the poaching of their employees. It could be an interesting tradeoff to study as a future research extension.

We investigated the effect of content in online communication in the context of technology recruitment. However, as noted by Aral et al. (2013), there is a dearth of literature on how content in online communication can affect decision making in differen business contexts. Therefore, another line of research extension is to study how heterogeneous decision makers react to the same content in online communication in contexts different from technical recruitment. Without accounting for heterogeneity, effects may get averaged out and the difference in the behavior of stakeholder subgroups may go unnoticed. Another such business context could be where the effort involved in taking an action by the stakeholder is high, that is, unlike a click to apply for a job. Such a business context may see a higher impact of the content of online communication among readers who interact with the content than the rest. One could also investigate the reaction of one stakeholder group based on companies’ posts about another stakeholder group. For example, could online content, describing the way a company treats its current employees, impact the motivations of customers to spend money with a company? Might employees be (de)motivated at work based on the online content they read about their company’s interactions with its customers? The underlying framework of motivation theory could also be used to address these kinds of cases and provide interesting contributions to online communication literature. Finally, given the investigation was focused on recruiting for technical positions, it would be worthwhile to explore these same questions in the context of recruiting for nontechnical positions as well. Differences in results might suggest candidate motivations speci<sup>fi</sup>c to technical roles, which could provide insights either to technical recruiting speci<sup>fi</sup>cally or recruiting in general.

There are a few limitations of this work. In this study, the treatment groups saw content on performance-based remuneration and self-expression, while other factors such as social welfare and work–life balance were kept as controls. We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any discussion in academic and trade press about the potential of these other factors to incite a different reaction from heterogeneous candidates. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that these other factors may also have the potential to motivate heterogeneous candidates differently, and it may be interesting for future studies to manipulate content around these other factors. One of the data limitations is that we do not have the data on how frequently candidates switch jobs. Some candidates may change jobs more frequently than others and controlling for this job switching behavior should make the results more reliable. This could be an interesting dimension to control for in future research studies.
