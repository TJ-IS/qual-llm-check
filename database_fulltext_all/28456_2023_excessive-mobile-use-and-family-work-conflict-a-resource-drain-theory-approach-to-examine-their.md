# Excessive Mobile Use and Family-Work Conflict: A Resource Drain Theory Approach to Examine Their Effects on Productivity and Well-Being

Abstract. While acknowledging the many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of anytime-anywhere connectivity, recent research has called for further investigation into the maladaptive side of mobile tech nology use in the work-family interface realm. We rely on resource drain theory to investigate how family-work con<sup>fl</sup>ict (FWC) is linked to excessive use of mobile devices for work purposes during nonwork hours, which, in turn, affects individual productivity and physiological, psychological, and relational well-being. Furthermore, we examine the role of competitive climate as a boundary condition. We conducted a <sup>fi</sup>eld study across two measurement periods involving 324 individuals and their live-in partners. Our results suggest that FWC affects productivity and well-being through excessive mobile use and that competitive climate ampli<sup>fi</sup>es these effects. The study contributes to a better understanding of the excessive mobile use phenomenon focusing on its determinants and its consequences. We discuss the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings both for theory and practice, and we outline directions for future research.

## Introduction

The use of smartphones for work-related purposes has nearly doubled over the last few years, with 76% of employees in the United States handling workrelated e-mails during nonwork time (Adobe 2019). This trend has been further ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the COVID-19 pandemic, emphasizing the pivotal role of constant connectivity (Mazmanian et al. 2013) and distributed work arrangements (Wildeler et al. 2015, Qureshi et al. 2018, Davis and Green 2020). A key enabler in these work practices can be found in the mobile technologies allowing employees to accomplish work-related tasks during nonwork time and to maintain connection with their workplace (Maruping and Agarwal 2004, Sarker et al. 2012, Windeler et al. 2017a, Chen and Karahanna 2018).

Although recognizing these advantages, extant evidence indicates that 57% of those employees who use mobile devices to stay connected with their workplace during nonwork time reported that they do so at least two hours per day, with spikes in use late at night (Davis and Green 2020). Spending considerable amounts of time on technology, to the point that users lose track and control of time, has been described as excessive use (Caplan 2002, Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths 2012, Kwon et al. 2016), and previous studies have urged for a better understanding of the causes and consequences of being plugged into the workplace during nonwork hours (Sarker et al. 2012, Derks et al. 2015). In the attempt to answer this call, our research focuses on the drivers as well as outcomes of excessive mobile use for working purposes during nonwork hours.<sup>1</sup>

Most of the studies that examined the use of mobile technology for working purposes during nonwork hours emphasized the deleterious effects of individuals’ excessive use by citing how the work domain invades the private sphere (Duxbury et al. 2006, Bos well and Olson-Buchanan 2007, Diaz et al. 2012). For instance, Boswell and Olson-Buchanan (2007) found that the constant connection between the individual and the work environment through mobile technologies is likely to increase the level of con<sup>fl</sup>ict between work and private life. In a similar vein, Turel et al. (2011) demonstrated that compulsive behaviors in mobile use exacerbate the individual feeling that work responsibilities interfere with family activities. While recognizing the contributions of this research, we note that several important gaps still remain untapped.

First, previous research does not provide adequate explanations of why individuals engage in excessive use of mobile devices for work purposes. Because a substantial portion of mobile use to accomplish jobrelated tasks occurs outside the boundaries of the organization (Boswell and Olson-Buchanan 2007), it is paramount to consider how the family environment affects individuals’ excessive mobile use for two main reasons. On one hand, a number of recent studies looking at the family-work interface have indicated that employees are increasingly experiencing an interference of family demands with their work duties (i.e., family-work con<sup>fl</sup>ict (FWC)), and they have urged research to take into consideration the role of FWC in studying the work and family boundaries (Sarker et al. 2018; for a meta-analysis, see Amstad et al. 2011). This is even more critical in light of the COVID-19 pandemic because only 36% of employees stated that they set boundaries between work and personal time (Schrage et al. 2021) and have experienced an increase in technology-based interactions during nonwork time (DeFilippis et al. 2020). On the other hand, the phenomena pertaining to the interface between family and work have acquired even more salience in the light of the inherently ubiquitous nature of mobile devices (Sarker et al. 2011, 2018). Indeed, mobile technologies transformed the way we conceive work in terms of not being limited to speci<sup>fi</sup>c hours or location, and enabled employees to be productive outside conventional working hours. As such, employees can maintain a constant connection with their workplace and deal with the unaddressed work demands while at home (Sarker et al. 2012), thus making it possible to compensate the intrusions of the family demands during work time. With these considerations in mind, we theorize that individuals try to compensate for FWC by increasing the use of mobile devices during nonwork time to keep up with work-related tasks that they are not able to complete because of the family demands. Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>rst goal is to examine how FWC may trigger excessive mobile use for work related purposes during nonwork time.

Second, although extant studies have helped to precipitate greater consideration into the potential effects of excessive use, they take a perspective that contemplates the consequences of excessive use on individual outcomes by focusing on a general conceptualization of work-life con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Turel et al. 2011). However, phenomena rooted in the interface between the work and the family domains are inherently multifaceted, and previous research falls short in providing a better understanding of the potential paradox between the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (i.e., productivity) and the costs (i.e., well-being) associated with excessive mobile use.

Reconciling this tension between the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and the costs of individuals’ excessive use of mobiles for working purposes during nonwork time is considered an imperative for those organizations and individuals that are concerned with a more conscious mobile technology use (Day et al. 2010). Thus, we argue that, on one hand, excessive mobile use leads to individual increases in productivity because it supports employees in completing their task independently of time and location (Chen and Karahanna 2018). On the other hand, we claim that the reliance on mobile technologies for work-related purposes during nonwork time comes with a heavy cost in terms of physiological, psychological, and relational well-being. In the light of these arguments, the second goal of this study is to investigate the consequences of excessive use by offering a more nuanced perspective that looks at both individual productivity and well-being.

To achieve these goals, we draw from resource drain theory (Edwards and Rothbard 2000), which represents one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential frameworks informing research on the work-family interface phenomena. Resource drain theory builds on the assumption that individuals possess a <sup>fi</sup>nite reservoir of resources (such as time and energy) to allocate across family and work domains. The inherently ubiquitous nature of mobile technologies provides <sup>fl</sup>exibility that enables individuals to maintain a constant connection with the workplace. Furthermore, it allows individuals to compensate for the incursion of family demands during work time by dedicating resources to the work demands during personal time. Moreover, the pervasiveness and the portability of mobile technologies make the permeability between work and personal domains extremely porous (Sarker et al. 2021). This facilitates individuals to make seemingly effortless transitions between the work and family domain that, while allowing them to deal with work-related demands, severely hamper the process of resource restoration. In the light of these arguments, resource drain theory provides a better understanding of how the interference from family demands during work hours can shape excessive mobile use, which, in turn, can affect individual productivity and well-being.

Finally, because individuals are increasingly subject to work pressure (Moore 2000b, Windeler et al. 2017a), recent studies have urged researchers to develop a deeper understanding of the boundary conditions that play important roles in work environments where work and family borders are blurred (Butts et al. 2015). To respond to this call, we examine the role of a competitive climate as a boundary condition<sup>2</sup> because it shapes the expectation of the extent employees should attend to work demands even during nonwork time (Schneider 2000). Consequently, our third goal is to examine the effect of competitive climate in amplifying the effect of FWC on productivity and well-being through excessive mobile use.

This research offers several important theoretical and empirical contributions to the literature. First, by invoking resource drain theory, our study contributes to previous research by considering mobile excessive use as a phenomenon that can be triggered by the need to balance family and work in the allocation of <sup>fi</sup>nite resources. By embracing a perspective that considers the interplay between the family demands and the work climate, our study goes beyond the prior research looking at mobile use during nonwork time solely as an organizational phenomenon. Second, building on resource drain theory allows us to advance previous literature by providing a better understanding of the potential paradox of excessive mobile use for working purposes during nonwork hours. Although investing energy in using mobiles for work purposes during nonwork hours offers bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of convenience, our study also indicates that this kind of behavior depletes individuals’ resources and carries costs to employees well-being. Third, with respect to methodology, most of the studies that examined the family-work interface have relied on cross-sectional research designs and a single data source (Turel et al. 2011), thus falling short in providing an accurate representation of the phenomenon under investigation (Podsakoff et al. 2012, Burton-Jones and Lee 2017). Our study offers an important contribution from this standpoint by relying on data collected at two points in time and by involving both the subjects and their life partners, as they represent privileged observers outside the work domain.

## Theoretical Background

## Excessive Mobile Use

In the technology domain, excessive use refers to the use of technology for a considerable amount of time during which users lose track and control of time (Caplan 2002, Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths 2012). Research on excessive use of technology can be traced back to the studies related to problematic Internet use that revealed how the excessive use of Internet may lead to negative effects in the personal and relational spheres (Davis 2001, Caplan 2002). Building on the seminal research on Internet use, excessive use has received considerable attention within the domain of social networking sites because of their widespread diffusion (Cao et al. 2018). For example, Zheng and Lee (2016) underscored that excessive use of social networking sites can have detrimental effects both on individuals work and personal domains, whereas Cao et al. (2018) found that excessive use of social networking apps may favor individual strain.

Although similar patterns exist in relation to the notion of excessive use of technology, previous research warns against considering technology excessive use in general terms and urges researchers to focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c domains within which use occurs, such as Internet (Caplan 2002), social networking apps (Cao et al. 2018), or mobile technologies (Kwon et al. 2016). By considering the importance of focusing on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c domain for studying excessive use and by relying on previous de<sup>fi</sup>- nitions (Caplan 2002, Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths 2012), in the current study, we focus on excessive mobile use that takes place for working purposes outside of working boundaries.

Individuals who are subject to excessive mobile use attempt to maximize utility from their mobile consumption choices through embracing self-interested behaviors aimed at ful<sup>fi</sup>lling their demands and avoiding discomfort (Kwon et al. 2016). Such a view is particularly consistent with our research framework involving the family-work interface from the perspective of FWC in a competitive climate. Indeed, FWC represents a situation of discomfort where individuals feel that their family demands interfere with their work demands, and individuals may try to reduce this discomfort by heavily relying on their mobile devices to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll work responsibilities (e.g., replying to e-mails) during nonwork time. Consistent with this conceptualization, a competitive environment is likely to amplify the effect of family interference on excessive mobile use because of the individuals attempt to alleviate the sense of discomfort and cope with the pressure exerted by the work environment.

## Resource Drain Theory

Resource drain theory suggests that demands in one domain inhibit individuals to meet the expectations of another domain because individuals have to make tradeoffs in allocating time and energy because of the incompatibility of the two domains (Greenhaus and Beutell 1985, Edwards and Rothbard 2000, Rothbard 2001, Rothbard and Edwards 2003). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, resource drain theory suggests that family and work roles are often in con<sup>fl</sup>ict because individuals have a limited pool of resources (such as energy and time) that they are able to allocate between family and work demands. Based on the assumption that work and family are linked rather than distinct, the investment of resources in one role increases the likelihood of not being able to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the demands of the other role (Greenhaus and Beutell 1985, Hochschild 1997, Windeler et al. 2017a).

When confronted with demands that challenge their reservoir of resources, individuals experience a sense of discomfort (Moore 2000a), and they attempt to minimize this feeling by engaging in behaviors aimed at managing speci<sup>fi</sup>c demands that are appraised as taxing or exceeding the resources of that individual person (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Through such behaviors, individuals invest resources that enable them to meet the demands and to relieve discomfort (Vinokur and Schul 2002, Ito and Brotheridge 2003). Previous research has suggested that such behaviors, by inherently investing resources, may allow individuals to meet some demands in one domain, while producing fatigue and deplete resources in other domains. For example, Ng and Feldman (2012) examined the way in which an employee’s voice may have both positive and negative outcomes in a simultaneous fashion. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, by speaking up to supervisors, employees might be able to convince the supervisors to intervene in alleviating the pressure, thus relieving the discomfort. However, voice behavior might also lead to negative outcomes in terms of resources depletion because exercising voice requires effort in terms of time and energy that individuals have to invest.

From such a perspective, resource drain theory is particularly informative in providing a better understanding of how the interference of family demands in the work domain may lead to excessive mobile use, as well as in providing a theoretical explanation of its effects. First, according to resource drain theory, individuals must make tradeoffs when allocating time and energy across family and work domains, and FWC creates discomfort because the family demands drain time and energy that are supposed to be targeted toward the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of work activities. However, the ubiquitous nature of mobile technologies enables individuals to compensate such interference during work time by investing time and energy in the use of mobile technologies while at home in the attempt to keep up with the work demands. Thus, the reliance on mobile technologies during nonwork time represents an active behavior through which individuals invest energy to overcome the family interference they experienced during work hours (Bakker and Demerouti 2007).

Second, consistent with the tenets of resource drain theory, the investment of time and energy in behaviors aimed at coping with a discomforting situation, such as constant mobile reliance during nonwork time, can lead to both positive and negative outcomes. On one hand, excessive use of mobile technologies may lead individuals to perceive that they are effective in dealing with those work demands they were not able to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll because of family interference in the work domain. On the other hand, resource drain theory emphasizes that negative family-work dynamics may affect individual outcomes by leveraging on the <sup>fi</sup>nite reservoir of resources that individuals invest in ful<sup>fi</sup>lling work demands during nonwork time. Consistent with this tenet, the excessive reliance on mobile technologies during nonwork time is cognitively burdensome because it requires individuals to invest further time and energy in dealing with work activities during their time off. As such, individuals are subject to a further depletion of resources with harmful consequences for their well-being in terms of physiological symptoms, psychological states, and health of one’s relationships. ## Research Hypotheses

## FWC and Excessive Mobile Use

FWC is a form of inter-role con<sup>fl</sup>ict that originates from family demands and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the incompatibility between family and work domains such that “participation in the work role is made more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult by virtue of participation in the family role” (Greenhaus and Beutell 1985, p. 77). Thus, the crucial aspect of FWC is that family and work are characterized by different demands in terms of responsibilities, expectations, and commitments, which are not always compatible with each other (Eby et al. 2005). Such conceptualization of FWC can be understood in terms of the resource drain theory perspective, which implies that the demands from family roles require time and energy, making it more challenging to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the demands from work roles (Edwards and Rothbard 2000).

According to resource drain theory, investing time and energy in family demands during work time reduces individual’s ability to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll job demands. Essentially, this means that resources meant for work are reallocated to the family domain, leaving fewer resources for work. However, the same characteristics of the mobile technologies, ubiquity and <sup>fl</sup>exibility, that allow them to reallocate resources during work hours also make it possible to compensate by reallocating resources meant for the family domain back to the work domain. Thus, individuals who experience FWC rely on mobile technologies to get work tasks done during family time to rebalance the resource allocation between work and family obligations. In other words, as family demands (resulting in FWC) encroach into work time, individuals will be more likely to take advantage of the work access provided by mobile devices during nonwork time to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the demands of their work environment that are still untapped. This argument is consistent with previous research indicating that mobile technologies liberate individuals from the constraint of traditional of<sup>fi</sup>ce settings (Mazmanian et al. 2013, Sarker et al. 2018). Along these lines, previous research underscores that individuals may experience an “absent presence” situation characterized by the fact that they are physically present at home, but they are interacting with their mobile technologies to maintain a connection with the workplace to complete work-related tasks (Gergen 2002).

Thus, the <sup>fl</sup>exibility that comes from mobiles can help employees to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their work responsibilities during nonwork time and extends their opportunity to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their work activities beyond the work day (Towers et al. 2006). Individuals who perceive that the demands originating in the family domain interfere with ful<sup>fi</sup>lling work-related responsibilities are more likely to experience excessive use during nonwork time in the attempt to alleviate the potential negative effects that such demands may have on their job.

## Moderating Role of Competitive Climate

The concept of climate re<sup>fl</sup>ects the descriptive beliefs individuals hold regarding properties of their organization. It captures the meaning employees ascribe to the overall pattern of organizational policies, practices, and procedures that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the attitudes and behaviors of organizational members (Reichers and Schneider 1990, Ostroff et al. 2003). Brown et al. (1998) introduced the concept of competitive psychological climate, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the degree to which employees perceive organizational rewards to be contingent on comparisons of their performance against that of their peers” (p. 89). A competitive climate has important implications in terms of how the individuals perceive their relationship with the workplace and the way through which individuals allocate their resources between the family and work domains (James et al. 1990).

From such a perspective, mobile technologies engender the demands of availability and responsiveness (Mazmanian et al. 2013), and the perception of a competitive climate can further enhance the effects of FWC. Therefore, we expect that individuals engage in excessive mobile use when they are subject to high FWC and a competitive climate. Individuals experiencing FWC must invest a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of time and energy in dealing with family-related issues, which can take them away from work tasks. Under such circumstances, they are likely to attempt to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their work obligations while away from work through the use of mobile technologies. Furthermore, under the conditions of a highly competitive climate, support of colleagues is less likely to be available, which can otherwise allay work demands and provide understanding while dealing with family tasks. At the same time, individuals are likely to not be willing to admit to have dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in coping with their workload. As a result, they will be more likely to increase their mobile use to signal that they are on top of thei work activities while trying to deal with family issues, in an attempt to ward off any potential negative relative performance appraisal.

By contrast, a climate that is less competitive may prevent the effects outlined previously because individuals would not feel the extra pressure that makes them want to demonstrate their contribution at any cost by being constantly connected. A climate that is not characterized by competition among employees entails the availability of relational resources that can be drawn on social support when individuals need to deal with family demands. Moreover, the pressure to outperform peers is mitigated in less competitive climates. Indeed, individuals are less likely to invest resources in continuously relying on their mobile technologies to signal that they are on top of workrelated demands when they feel supported by their colleagues in dealing with the pressure to manage the con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands between family and work domains.

Based on this, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. Competitive climate moderates the relationship between FWC and excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time, such that the relationship is stronger when competitive climate is high.

## Effects of Excessive Mobile Use on Productivity and Well-Being

According to resource drain theory, individuals deal with demands by activating coping behaviors that may lead to both positive and negative outcomes in a simultaneous fashion. Following this logic, individuals who attempt to deal with FWC and competitive climate through excessive mobile use may present the same pattern of consequences. On one hand, the heavy reliance on mobile technologies may have positive effects on individual productivity. Indeed, using mobile technologies during nonworking hours allows individuals to deal with work-related issues even outside organizational boundaries, thus enhancing the perception of being productive. On the other hand, excessive mobile use for working purposes during nonwork time contributes to further depletion of individuals, and does not allow for restoring resources, which are considered a pivotal element of individual well-being (Tangney et al. 2004, DeWall et al. 2007). By relying on such theoretical underpinning, we expect that excessive mobile use has a positive consequence on individual productivity and a negative impact on well-being from its physiological (i.e., somatic symptoms), psychological (i.e., need for recovery), and relational components (i.e., family undermining).

Productivity. Individuals who experience excessive use of mobile are characterized by an extended use of their device for working purposes outside organizational boundaries. In embracing such behavior, individuals invest resources to achieve their work goals, developing the feeling that they can be productive even during nonwork hours because they can constantly rely on their mobile. This reasoning is consistent with previous studies outlining that after-work reliance on mobile technology is likely to increase individuals’ perception of being able to complete un<sup>fi</sup>nished tasks or communications (Chen and Karahanna 2018). A typical example is a parent that continuously checks e-mails and makes phone calls while attending a child’s baseball training session, or a physician that checks medical records while waiting to pick up his/her partner at the train station. In these situations, the heavy reliance on mobile devices while ful<sup>fi</sup>lling family responsibilities may lead individuals to feel particularly productive because they can keep up with work-related tasks during nonwork time while responding to the family demands. Consistent with this perspective, we expect excessive mobile use to enhance employees’ productivity by enabling them to leverage mobile technologies in their nonwork domain and bring un<sup>fi</sup>nished work-related tasks to closure.

Hypothesis 3. Excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time is positively associated with individual productivity

Physiological Well-Being. Physiological well-being is experienced and expressed in terms of somatic symptoms. Somatic symptoms are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as self-reported experiences of physical distress and they re<sup>fl</sup>ect indi viduals’ physiological reaction to their inability to self-regulate their efforts (Meier et al. 2013), leading to physical indicators such as headache, shortness of breath, or stomach problems. For example, Ilies et al. (2010) have suggested that individuals who perceive that they are not able to effectively cope with pressure would experience negative physical symptoms. Similarly, other studies have found that in situations characterized by discomfort, individuals are more likely to experience somatic symptoms (Meier et al. 2013) as they attempt to cope with demands that are a source of distress. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be traced back to psychophysiological studies elucidating that the activation of coping mechanisms requires individuals to exert extra effort that depletes resources (Cohen et al. 1986). Such a perspective is consistent with resource drain theory because the body activates defensive mechanisms in the form of physiological responses (Nixon et al. 2011) to regulate the resource taxation arising from excessive use of mobile technologies. Because mobile technologies enable individuals to extend their work beyond the workplace, mobile technologies lead to constant connectivity and contribute to the erosion of individual resources. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, resource depletion derived from the reliance on mobile technologies during nonwork time is particularly subtle because mobile technologies make possible to “be” at work while sitting on the couch at home. Answering e-mails and handling phone calls from the living room seem trivial, and individuals may not immediately notice the resource depletion associated with these behaviors (Derks et al. 2015). Even if individuals do not immediately perceive the consequences of their behavior related to excessive use of mobile technologies, they are draining resources during nonwork time, thus activating a physiological reactions to cope with such resource demand. The drain of resources is accompanied by exaggerated sympathetic nervous system activity, and alterations in sympathoadrenal activation, which triggers a physiological response in terms of somatic symptoms, signaling resource depletion. Conversely, individuals who are not required to cope with work demands during nonwork time are less subject to resource depletion, thus being less exposed to a physiological response (Shimazu et al. 2012). Therefore, individuals who are less engaged in the excessive use of mobile technologies during nonwork time to accomplish work related tasks conserve their resources and are less likely to experience somatic symptoms. Based on these arguments, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4. Excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time is positively associated with somatic symptoms.

Psychological Well-Being. Need for recovery represents a common indicator of psychological well-being (Van Veldhoven and Broersen 2003) and it is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which individuals perceive a high requirement for a rest period. The concept of need for recovery refers to the effort-recuperation process that allows individuals to recover from the costs of energy invested during the day. Previous research has shown that the pervasiveness of mobile technologies made the need for recovery even more critical, because these technologies shaped the traditional working pattern, making the cycle between ful<sup>fi</sup>lling demands and rest more permeable (Konradt et al. 2000). Typically, the resting process takes place outside the organizational boundaries; however, the continuous reliance on mobile technologies for working purposes outside the work domain may hamper individuals’ recovery. The introduction of mobile technologies into our private domain increases the salience of work into our nonwork life, thus hampering the important process of disengaging from work (Derks et al. 2015). The pervasiveness of mobile technologies transforms anytime, anywhere access into all the time, everywhere availability (Sarker et al. 2021), with consequences to an individual’s well-being and ability to properly recover. For example, making job-related phone calls or scrolling e-mails while at home does not allow individuals to stop thinking about their job-related affairs, thus hindering the possibility to “switch off” during off-job time and preventing the recovery process to take place (Sonnentag and Bayer 2005). For example, individuals can engage in exchanging messages with colleagues to coordinate their work activities at home. Although this behavior is apparently harmless because it seems effortless, the heavy reliance on mobile technologies prevents individuals from unwinding from work-related matters and impacts their recovery process.

This perspective is consistent with resource drain theory because the excessive use of mobile technologies requires individuals to invest energy and time in dealing with work-related activities while at home. When users do not reduce mobile use, it takes longer to unwind the mental resources occupied in the effort involved using mobiles to accomplish work-related tasks during nonwork time. Conversely, individuals are more likely to recover and restore their energy if they dial back from mobile devices entangling them to their work activities after hours. Therefore, based on the aforementioned logic, we argue that excessive use of mobile technologies is likely to consume resources and hampers the resting process, thus increasing the individuals’ need for recovery.

Hypothesis 5. Excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time is positively associated with the need for recovery.

Relational Well-Being. Undermining represents nonviolent, although aggressive, behaviors (e.g., criticizing, ignoring, and demeaning) that displays negative affect and evaluation of the persons to whom such behaviors are directed (Restubog et al. 2011). We focus on family undermining because the family environment represents the place in which aggressive behaviors tied to con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands between family and work are more likely to occur (Aquino et al. 2001). Indeed, previous research has indicated that individuals tend to “blow-off steam” within the family domain (and not at work) to avoid retaliation in the workplace (Aquino et al. 2001, Restubog et al. 2011). Resource drain theory suggests that resource demands affect individual self-regulation and impair individuals ability to control aggressive behaviors (Rothbard 2001). Mobile use during nonwork time hampers individuals’ detachment from ongoing work tasks and prevents them from investing resources in their relationship with their partners. It acts as a barrier to quality interaction, and thus keeps individuals from being present with their partners. When individuals heavily rely on mobile technologies while at home, they are likely to experience moment-to-moment microtransitions between the work demands and interactions with their partners. Recent studies suggest that even the mere presence of mobile technologies has been found to undermine perceived closeness, connection, and conversation quality because of the expectation to hear from the work domain (Lapierre and Lewis 2018). This causes individuals’ attention to be split between the work and the personal domains (Lapierre and Lewis 2018). When individuals are distracted by their mobile devices, or simply feel tempted to check their mobile device for work e-mails or messages because of the fear of missing out, their attention is diverted at the expenses of their partners.

Because of the individuals’ limited pool of attentional resources, the excessive device use prevents individuals from having suf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources to be fully present with their partners, increasing the likelihood of engaging in undermining behaviors. In other words, the frustration of not having enough resources to deal with the work input coming through the mobile technologies, whereas maintaining the focus on the relationship with their partners is likely to be displaced by undermining behaviors (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Furthermore, individuals who are engaged in excessive use of mobile technologies during their nonwork time for work-related activities contribute to a further depletion of their resources, thus increasing the likelihood of undermining behaviors occurrence. Therefore, excessive use of mobile devices hampers individuals’ control mechanisms that restrain undermining behaviors. As such, individuals are less likely to keep aggressive impulses in check because they do not have enough resources. Based on this, we suggest the following.

Hypothesis 6. Excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time is positively associated with family undermining behavior.

## Indirect Conditional Effect of FWC and Competitive Climate Through Excessive Mobile Use

Extending the rationale of previous hypotheses, we argue that the effect of FWC on individual productivity and well-being through excessive mobile use is exacerbated by a competitive climate. First, when individuals feel the existence of a competitive climate, they are less likely to rely on their coworkers or reach out and ask for help. When individuals perceive they can rely on mobile technology outside the organizational boundaries to overcome the interferences of the family domain, they are likely to perceive that they are more productive because mobile technology can help them out to satisfy their work demands while being at home, thus allowing them to deal with work-related demands outside the work boundaries. This is consistent with previous research indicating that individuals’ perception of a competitive climate leads them to invest more resources in their jobrelated activities to demonstrate their effectiveness in comparison with their colleagues (Fletcher et al. 2008). For example, Mazmanian et al. (2013) provided evidence that in a high-demand climate, employees attune their work practices toward maximum avail ability and accordingly use mobile devices to ensure that they can be reached when needed. Thus, because a competitive climate increases the individual need to signal to be on top of things even outside the work hours, the use of mobile technologies to attend to work tasks may increase the individual perception of being productive. This argument is consistent with previous research underscoring that individuals tend to engage in behaviors that pertain to the work role while in the family domain when such behaviors are supported and encouraged by the organization (Ashforth 2000).

Second, when individuals perceive their environment as competitive, they are less poised to modulate their energy because they cannot rely on the support of others and they deplete resources for dealing with both family and work demands (Moore 2000b, Windeler et al. 2017b). Therefore, individuals who have to deal with family demands, but cannot rely on the support of colleagues in dealing with work-related issues, would be more likely to engage in excessive use of technology outside the working boundaries, thus increasing their exposure to resources demand. Individuals who are embedded in an organization characterized by high internal competition and experience interference of the family demands would be more likely to experience an excessive use of mobile technologies to demonstrate both their <sup>fi</sup>t with the organizational environment and their ability to always be on top of things, even during nonwork time. Such excessive reliance on mobile technology to compensate for the family interference increases resources depletion and leads to lower levels of well-being. Moreover, the presence of a competitive climate prevents the occurrence of a buffering mechanism rooted in others’ support, thus favoring the emergence of negative psychological, physiological, and social consequences associated with the energy taxation of an excessive use of mobile technologies outside the work boundaries. This perspective is consistent with previous studies underscoring that social support lessens the effects of demands on individual distress behaviors and strain by providing supporting resources (Van Yperen and Hagedoorn 2003).

Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 7a. Competitive climate moderates the indi rect effect of FWC on productivity through excessive mobil use for work purposes during nonwork time, such that this indirect effect is strengthened at higher levels of competitive climate.

Hypothesis 7b. Competitive climate moderates the indirect effect of FWC on somatic symptoms through excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time, such that this indirect effect is strengthened at higher levels of competitive climate.

Hypothesis 7c. Competitive climate moderates the indirect effect of FWC on the need for recovery through excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time, such that this indirect effect is strengthened at higher levels of competitive climate.

Hypothesis 7d. Competitive climate moderates the indirect effect of FWC on family undermining through excessive mobile use for work purposes during nonwork time, such that this indirect effect is strengthened at higher levels of competitive climate.

## Method

## Participants and Procedure

To test our research model, we conducted a <sup>fi</sup>eld study in the United States by recruiting an online panel of respondents through a data collection company, which facilitated the interactions with participants (e.g., recruitment, recalls, payment) and the data collection across multiple points in time. Following previous research that adopted a similar research design, participants were recruited from a variety of organizations from different industries (e.g., technology, banking and insurance, healthcare, retail) to obtain a diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed sample and increase the generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Montes and Zweig 2009, Ng and Feldman 2012, Kier and McMullen 2018, James et al. 2019). Because one of the key criteria examined in this research involved partners’ ratings, only participants with live-in partners were eligible to take part in the study. To this end, the study invitation speci<sup>fi</sup>ed that the study was longitudinal and that it required the partner’s involvement. Participants received payment for their involvement in the study. A total of 421 focal individuals participated in the Time 1 survey related to demographic questions, competitive climate, and FWC. A total of 397 self-report surveys at Time 1 were complete and usable. Approximately 2 weeks later, participants who responded to the <sup>fi</sup>rst survey were invited, along with their partners, to <sup>fi</sup>l out the Time 2 survey. In the cover letter, partners were instructed to answer their questions without the input of the focal subject. Out of the 346 questionnaires returned, 22 had to be discarded because either the focal subject or the partner did not complete the survey. This resulted in a total of 324 completed surveys. Thirty-four percent of focal subjects were less than 35 years old, 37% were between 35 and 44 years old, 21% were between 45 and 54 years old, and 8% were 55 years or above. Fifty-<sup>fi</sup>ve percent were female, 70% had at least a bachelor’s degree, and 68% have worked in their organization for more than three years.

## Measures

Responses were collected with a time lag of two weeks between Time 1 and Time 2 surveys. Except for excessive mobile use (completed by the partner at Time 2), all measures were completed by the focal subject at either Time 1 (FWC, competitive climate, and control variables) or at Time 2 (productivity, somatic symptoms, need for recovery, and family undermining). Unless otherwise stated, all variables were measured on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert-type scale with values ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” (see Online Appendix A for the scales).

We assessed FWC using <sup>fi</sup>ve items from Netemeye et al. (1996). Focal employees evaluated these items at Time 1. We assessed competitive climate using three items from Fletcher et al. (2008). The scale taps into the individual perception that the work environment is characterized by competition among peers. Focal employees responded to those items at Time 1. Excessive mobile use was assessed through a four-item scale from Caplan and High (2006), adapted to the mobile technology setting. The scale taps into both the excessive time spent using the mobile technology and the sense of control loss in terms of time spent using the technology for working purposes outside the organi zational boundaries. Because the partner represents a privileged observer of the focal individual behaviors outside the working boundaries, we asked the partner to evaluate these items at Time 2. This approach alle viates the potential occurrence of in<sup>fl</sup>ated results because of the single source bias (i.e., relying only on the focal subject responses), and it is consistent with previous research that looked into behaviors that pertain to the interface between work and family (Bolino and Turnley 2005, Boswell and Olson-Buchanan 2007). We assessed individual productivity using four items from Torkzadeh and Doll (1999), tapping into the degree to which mobiles support the focal individ ual in being more productive during nonwork hours. Focal subiects evaluated these items at Time 2. We measured individual somatic symptoms using <sup>fi</sup>ve items from Manning et al. (1989), asking the focal individua the frequency of somatic complaints: headaches, stom ach/intestinal problems, sweaty/trembling hands, shortness of breath, feeling dizzy/light-headed. Focal subjects evaluated these items at Time 2. We assessed need for recovery using three items from the scale of Van Veldhoven and Broersen (2003), which assesses the degree to which the focal individual feels the need to rest for recovering for work related activities. Focal subjects evaluated these items at Time 2. We meas ured family undermining with a <sup>fi</sup>ve-item scale from Restubog et al. (2011). Focal individuals were asked to evaluate whether they engaged in undermining behaviors directed toward their family members. Focal individuals evaluated these items at Time 2.

<sup>Control Variables.</sup> We included a number of theoretically relevant variables to control for other potential mechanisms outlined in the work-life balance literature. First, we controlled for gender and age because previous studies underscored that such demographic characteristics could represent factors affecting individual behaviors by in<sup>fl</sup>uencing how individuals make resource allocation decisions (Allen and Finkelstein 2014, Courtright et al. 2016). Given that situational factors may alter the way through which individuals allocate resources and thus affect their behaviors either at home or at work (Jackson et al. 1983), we also controlled for number of children, and whether the focal subjects were caregivers to others (e.g., elderly person living in the same place). For example, previous research underscored that parental demands are tied to time-based con<sup>fl</sup>ict and tardiness (Carlson and Perrewe´ 1999, Matsui et al. 1995). Moreover, we controlled for negative life events relying on Holmes and Rahe (1967). For each event, the respondents indicated zero (no) or one (yes) to whether they had experienced the event during the previous six months. Following Westman et al. (2004), the negative life events score consisted of the sum of events that were checked “yes.” Indeed, life events affect the way through which individuals allocate their resources and requires them to readjust their behavioral patterns. Finally, we also controlled for work characteristics by looking at the type of contract and the organizational tenure, along with the work <sup>fl</sup>exibility of the focal individual and the partner, because these factors have been found to be associated with employees’ outcomes and behaviors (Olsson 2009). ## Discussion of Results

The results of our analyses support the hypothesized relationships (Table 5). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our results indicate that FWC favors excessive use of mobile technologies for work purposes during nonwork time, thus corroborating our argument that individuals compensate for the intrusion of the family demands into the work domain by using mobile technologies for work during nonwork hours (Hypothesis 1). Moreover, the results of our analyses show that the effect of FWC on excessive mobile use is more salient when employees experience their work environment to be competitive, thus corroborating the fact that this kind of environment makes individuals feel pressured to show that they are available after hours, and they are on top of things even during personal time (Hypothesis 2).

Our research also examined the consequences of excessive mobile use in terms of well-being and productivity. Results show that the heavy reliance on mobile technologies for work during nonwork time allows individuals to deal with work-related demands and thus enables higher levels of productivity (Hypothesis 3). However, our results underscore that excessive use of mobile technologies comes with a cost in terms of well-being because mobile technology use during nonwork time is likely to deplete individual resources related to physiological, psychological, and relational well-being, thus inhibiting the recovery process (Hypotheses 4–6). By linking the effects of FWC and competitive climate on excessive mobile use and downsteam consequences, our study shows that the effects of excessive mobile use triggered by FWC on productivity and well-being is more salient when individuals work in a competitive climate (Hypotheses 7a–7d).

## Robustness Checks

Taken together, the results of the data analysis provide support for the hypotheses. To corroborate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and ensure the robustness of our results, we conducted several additional analyses.

Although our study relies on the partners’ perspective to alleviate the potential occurrence of single source bias, it is possible that some partners are not fully attentive to the use of the focal subject’s mobile or are themselves too busy to fully grasp the excessive mobile use outside of organizational boundaries. Our approach in ruling out such a potential risk was two-fold. First, we ran a correlation between subject ratings and partner ratings of excessive mobile use. The correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient was 0.73 (p < 0.01), which is consistent with previous studies investigating individuals work-related behaviors outside the work environment using different informants (Bolino and Turnley 2005), and it outlines that partners are a suitable source for observing usage behaviors. Second, we ran further regression analyses to test our model using focal subjects’ assessment at Time 2 of excessive mobile use. The results of these analyses show the same pattern of results (see Online Appendix C), suggesting that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are not sensitive to different source speci<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Results show that excessive mobile use positively affects WFC $( \nu = 0 . 2 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus corroborating evidence obtained by previous studies suggesting that WFC can be a negative consequence of maladaptive use of technology outside the work hours. Moreover, going above and beyond a general conceptualization of the consequences of excessive mobile use allowed us to disentangle its costs in terms of well-being from the advantages in terms of productivity.

## Discussion

In the contemporary work environments, heavy reliance on mobile devices and management of employees’ work-life balance has become a strategic imperative for organizations because of their effects on workers’ health, psychological well-being, and productivity. In light of this, the goal of this research was to develop a better understanding of the drivers and outcomes of excessive mobile use for working purposes during nonwork hours.

## Theoretical Implications and Future Research Directions

Our study provides several contributions to information systems research and resource drain theory. The <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution is to the research area of maladaptive technology use. We add to this stream of research by taking a novel theoretical perspective of the resource drain theory, which suggests that individuals have limited resource supply and that resources used in one domain become unavailable in another domain. We found that excessive mobile use can arise out of the con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands of the family environment and work context. Depletion of resources can be traced back to excessive use of mobile devices for work-related purposes during nonwork time and result in counter-productive outcomes in the attempt to compensate the interferences of the family environment during the workday. Our study extends the important results outlined by previous research by incorporating the context in which excessive mobile use occurs and by focusing on the demands that different contexts require of individuals. More speci<sup>fi</sup> cally, we found that individuals who experience an interrole con<sup>fl</sup>ict in which demands from the family make it more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for individuals to perform work duties (FWC) are more likely to experience excessive mobile use in their attempt to manage their work demands after-hours.

These results become even more important in light of the COVID-19 pandemic, which has resulted in high levels of permeability between the work and family environments. As outlined by McKendrick (2021), technology-mediated and distributed work is here to stay, and in the near future, we are quite likely to see an increase in work from home con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, and with that, an increase in the need to meet family demands during work time. All of this further increases the reliance on mobile technologies during nonwork time, thus triggering a perilous spiral for individuals’ well-being. As such, we urge scholars focused on work from home environments to investigate how these negative outcomes can be mitigated, how individuals and organizations can effectively deal with the inevitable blurring of the boundaries in hybrid form of works, and how we might maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of <sup>fl</sup>exible work arrangements and mobile technologies. Indeed, as outlined by Sarker et al. (2021), to achieve sustainable organizational success, technology should pursue instrumental goals and guarantee individuals’ well-being in a simultaneous fashion.

Second, by integrating an examination of family and work systems, we provide a more complete view of the excessive mobile use phenomenon. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, previous research invoked perspectives based on individual characteristics, thus considering maladaptive use behaviors as an individual phenomenon or as something that refers only to the technological domain. Although previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings represent important stepping stones in shedding some light on the excessive use phenomenon, they do not take into consideration the role of nonwork domains in impacting excessive mobile use for work-related purposes, thus considering excessive use as a single domain phenomenon. We have shown that the occurrence of excessive mobile use for work purposes cannot be adequately understood without considering the environment in which such behavior is likely to occur. Because mobile technologies have enabled employees to stay connected to their work at any place and at any time (Kossek and Lautsch 2012), our study contributes to a better understanding of mobile technology in the realm of the work-family interface. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, resource drain theory allows us to consider excessive mobile use as a cross-domain phenomenon by outlining how the demands that originate in one domain (e.g., family) may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact behaviors that pertain to another domain (e.g., mobile use for workrelated purposes). Accounting for an antecedent of excessive mobile use that relates to the family domain allows scholars to achieve a more holistic understanding of this domain. Indeed, it conceptualizes mobile use for work purposes during nonwork hours as a phenomenon that should be investigated by taking both the family and work domains into consideration (Boswell and Olson-Buchanan 2007). Along these lines, we go further by considering the role of organizational climate as a boundary condition that can shed more light on the relationship between FWC and excessive mobile use. By doing so, we further highlight the interplay between the family and work domains. Indeed, our study shows that the relationship between

FWC and excessive mobile use is stronger for those employees who perceive themselves as working in a competitive environment. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is pivotal because it opens the door to developing a better understanding of the conditions under which the inter-role demands could contribute to excessive mobile use. Our results represent an answer to the call by Turel et al. (2011), who urged to integrate organizational behavior and work-family con<sup>fl</sup>ict models in studying maladaptive technology use.

In light of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we urge scholars to also explore how the constant reallocation of resources between work and nonwork domains may activate mediating mechanisms that may prevent individuals to truly feel the satisfaction that comes from complet ing their tasks (e.g., frustration), thus triggering mobile technology use during nonwork time. Moreover, future research might investigate the mechanisms through which technology-based violations in one domain (i.e., work or family) have prolonged effects that spill over into the other domain. For instance, by using resource-based theoretical perspectives, future research may consider the role of cognitive and relational mechanisms through which mobile intrusion affects individuals’ well-being and behaviors across domains, as well as the factors that alleviate the negative effects of such technology-based interference.

Third, our study contributes to previous research by highlighting the effects that FWC and an organizational competitive climate have on excessive mobile use, and in turn, on individual productivity and well being. In particular, relying on resource drain theory, we show how excessive mobile use may have a positive effect on productivity but impacts negatively on well-being. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings extend previous research on mobile technology by outlining the unintended consequences of mobile use after hours. Thus, our results shed more light on the tension between philosophies held by scholarship communities that put forth the negative consequences of after-hours mobile use and those that subscribe to the premise that more use is always better and should be encouraged as a means to maximize information technology (IT)-related bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts. Therefore, our study serves as a warning about the unwanted potential consequences of mobile tech nologies while recognizing their advantages and urges further research in the nascent <sup>fi</sup>eld of excessive use. Relying on the importance indicated by our research of considering both the work and the family domain in explaining phenomena related to the family-work interface, we urge future research to consider the relationship between productivity and well being in the long run. In particular, we encourage scholars to undertake panel studies to better investigate whether and how well-being might affect productivity in the long run. Indeed, in the long run a decrease of productivity may arise from the erosion of well-being, thus triggering a negative spiral for individuals’ work and personal outcomes.

In this regard, we encourage future researchers to delve deeper into the investigation of contextual factors that may represent a boundary condition in studying the role of mobile technology use during nonwork time. Indeed, previous studies on individual preferences in work-life domains (e.g., work-home integration preferences) show that contextual elements play a pivotal role in determining individuals after-hours use of mobile technologies. We have shown that competitive climate offers important insights for better interpreting how an organizational environment affects usage behavior during nonwork time. We call on future researchers to investigate work and family-related contextual elements which may alleviate the negative effects of mobile technologies pervasiveness. For example, future research could study whether social support at home may reduce the depletion of resources derived from after-hours mobile use or whether individual’s control on their activities may support a better allocation of their energies. This might enhance the advantages inherent to mobile technologies while limiting the downside effects in terms of well-being. We believe that taking into consideration the role of the family environment and job arrangements is pivotal in providing a better understanding of the “new normal” that is going to commence as we emerge from the COVID crisis.

Fourth, the study makes a methodological contribution to work-life balance and technostress research by going beyond cross-sectional research design and a single data source (Turel et al. 2011). By using data collection at two points in time and by incorporating dual data source in the form of a partners’ perspective in addition to the focal subjects’ perceptions, we demonstrate a study design aimed at a more realistic representation of the work-life interface phenomenon (Podsakoff et al. 2012, Burton-Jones and Lee 2017), and we encourage future IS research to continue along this pathway.

Furthermore, our research contributes to the advancement of resource drain theory in several ways. On one hand, the resource drain theory lens has been mainly adopted to explain the spillover effect of one single domain into the other (Courtright et al. 2016), falling short in considering the interplay of the antecedents and the effects in both domains. By simultaneously modeling both antecedents and consequences pertaining to the work and the family domains, we offer a more comprehensive and richer perspective of resource drain theory, urging researchers to go above and beyond the focus on how a single domain affects the other. Rather, our research stimulates scholars looking at the blurred boundaries between the work and the family environments to consider antecedents and consequences of both domains in a simultaneous fashion. For example, future research building on resource drain theory could go beyond the focal individuals by investigating cross-over effects into the family domain. For example, focal subjects’ excessive use of mobile technology may also affect the partners well-being, thus acting as a depleting mechanism that encompasses both employees and their partners. Although anecdotal evidence shows that the use of mobile technologies during nonwork time affects the relationships within the family environment, there is still a lack of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research in this domain. For instance, it may be that the divided attention of the focal individual between partners and the mobile technology may deplete the resources of partners in light of the fragmented interaction, thus increasing the sense of isolation, which increases negative consequences for partners in terms of their well-being and emotional states.

Moreover, our study contributes to the resource drain theory through integrating it into the IS realm. The resultant framework advances the scope of both resource drain theory and the IS literature by connecting two spheres that progressed distinctly. The integration of resource drain theory with IS research provides a more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained understanding of the unintended consequences of mobile devices’ pervasiveness, warning against the apparently effortless transitions between the work and the family domain. We encourage future research to rely on social psy chological resource-based theories (e.g., conservation of resources theory and its microlevel extensions) to examine different effects of the use of mobile technologies on individuals. For example, use of mobile technologies for synchronous interactions (e.g., calls) creates performative demands and therefore may deplete more resources in comparison with other asynchronous uses such as e-mails or chats. Similarly, individuals’ use of mobile devices for asynchronous media is likely to provide more control of how and when individuals deal with work demands during nonwork hours.

Moreover, despite how maladaptive mobile use evolved as a major social problem in the last years (Kwon et al. 2016), there is still a lack of studies rooted into resource-based psychological theories to explain the role of mobile technology in the work-family interface (Chen and Karahanna 2018). Integrating resource drain theory and IS research advances previous studies along this line and contributes to answering the call of previous scholars who stimulated a more focused approach in adopting resource-based organizational theories (Halbesleben et al. 2014). For instance, future studies can rely on resource drain theory to <sup>fi</sup>nd more sophisticated ways to deal with the ubiquitous nature of mobile technologies (such as turning off email servers), thus complementing and advancing popular practices that are designed to prevent employees dealing with work-related interactions during nonwork time. Although such practices may alleviate individuals’ resource depletion, they may also prevent individuals’ autonomy in dealing with their tasks, thus limiting the <sup>fl</sup>exibility provided by mobile technologies and increasing individuals sense of frustration in managing work and family demands.

Thus, although our study warns about the negative health consequences of excessive use of mobile devices during nonwork time, future IS research rooted in resource drain theory may investigate how organizations and individuals may embrace a more savvy and empowered reliance on mobile technologies. This could provide a better understanding of how individuals can leverage the potential of mobile technologies while minimizing the resource depletion that threatens their well-being. In other words, by leveraging resource-based theories rooted into cognitive psychology, future IS scholars may help individuals to be more in control of mobile technologies. Empowering and training individuals to control the use of mobile technologies is especially important in the post-COVID scenario, where hybrid work arrangement will continue to challenge the current policies because of the inevitable permeability of the work-life boundaries.

Building on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding family undermining, researchers may want to examine the effects of family undermining behaviors on work outcomes. It is quite possible that aggressive behaviors in the family because of excessive mobile use can have negative consequences within the organizational environment because the individuals tend to transfer their aggressiveness from the family to the organization. It seems reasonable to assume that these undermining behaviors will spill over to work environments as well. Relatedly, organizational researchers should examine the role of work climate in employees engagement in work tasks while at home.

## Managerial Implications

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research have several important managerial implications. Given the pervasiveness of mobile technologies and their ability to support individuals in performing tasks, it is important for organizations to gain a better understanding of the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and unexpected negative consequences of mobile use. This has gained salience from a managerial perspective in light of the new work-from-home phenomenon in the contemporary landscape because of COVID-19. Indeed, the pandemic increased the permeability between the work and the family environment, with almost 40% of employees who work remotely at least three days per week (Economist 2020). It is clear that this phenomenon is here to stay beyond COVID-19.

Because of the pivotal role played by mobile technologies in work from home (WFH) and generally reaping the advantages of remote work, managers need to pay particular attention to reshaping the way “work” is conceived and to alleviate unintended consequences of constant connection with the work environment. Our research contributes to the knowledge of this phenomenon by demonstrating that an excessive use of mobile technologies during nonwork time could increase perceptions of individual productivity, but it comes at a cost in terms of individual wellbeing. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings could serve to raise awareness among managers about this conundrum.

This research could help promote boundary management between home and work domains by encouraging a sustainable use of mobile technologies. Several pioneer ing <sup>fi</sup>rms are beginning to attempt boundary manage ment, but this is far from mainstream. For example, a few years ago, Volkswagen decided to turn off mobile e-mails after work hours. Similarly, Lidl banned internal email traf<sup>fi</sup>c between 6 p.m. and 7 a.m. to help employees to better recover and enjoy their leisure time. We suggest that it is important for more organizations to implement such initiatives by establishing policies about communication outside the business hours. Empirical support, such as ours, for value of boundary management policies can help bring about wider diffusion of such initiatives in an era where WFH is more the norm rather than occa sional practice.

Using interventions of training and policies development, organizations may be able to help individuals to manage family-work dynamics and smoothen the con <sup>fl</sup>icting resource demand between the two environments. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest negative well-being consequences of constant mobile connectivity, which can have downstream effects for organizational outcomes. Given this, it seems valuable for organizations to design training programs on use of mobile technologies outside the work boundaries or under remote work conditions. At this time, only 28% of the companies designed policies related to this aspect (Schrage et al. 2021), thus failing to support individuals in maintaining a savvy use of mobile technologies during nonwork time.

Work-family dynamics training should address implications of behaviors that might seem innocuous in the family setting (such as the use of the mobile phone for keeping up with the work environment) but could potentially and negatively affect the individual and work outcomes that are more serious over the long term. Our research provides important recommendations for individuals for de<sup>fi</sup>ning norms and rules about technology use within the family domain. For example, individuals could establish norms about when to use or when to ban the mobile for working purposes when spending time with the family.

Furthermore, our results address calls from managerial literature that urge scholars to identify contextual elements through which organizations may alleviate the negative impact on well-being of mobile excessive use (Harter 2014). Our results suggest that, in addition to offering technology-related policies and training, organizations should also pay attention to the internal climate, which we have shown to impact on individual outcomes. Indeed, our results show that, when individuals experience excessive mobile use, the extent to which this excessive use translates into a decrease in well-being and an increase in productivity also depends on whether individuals perceive an organizational climate to foster competition among employees. The COVID-19 pandemic has shined a light on the dark side of competitive climate, thus emphasizing the importance of developing a climate that enables effective mobile use when the family and the work domains are intertwined.

In the interest of engaged scholarship, the results of our research implying that organizations should invest in the development of a cooperative climate are in line with the evidence of a recent report by Deloitte (2020) that highlights the importance of collaborative behaviors climate in a situation where “the pandemic completely obliterated any barrier between work and home life” (Ben Waber, president of Humanyze) (Schrage et al. 2021, p. 6). Creating a cooperative climate may represent a way to support employees in dealing with the con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands between the work and home domains by encouraging employee engagement.

## Strengths and Limitations

Our research study has several strengths that are noteworthy. One major strength of our paper from a methodological standpoint can be traced back to the fact that we collected data from multiple sources at different points in time, thereby reducing potential common method variance issues (Podsakoff et al. 2012). This approach is particularly relevant because most empirical work on maladaptive use of technology has relied on single-source, cross-sectional data (Ferris et al. 2005). Thus, we attempted to overcome the common source bias criticism of previous research in the technology use domain. Second, by using the approach outlined by Preacher et al. (2007), all the steps encompassing the moderated mediation were estimated simultaneously, thus smoothing issues associated with piecemeal and causal step approaches for testing such models (Bauer et al. 2006).

These strengths of our study notwithstanding, as with any research, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings need to be interpreted in light of a few limitations. One limitation is that our sample was based on individuals who have a cohabitating partner, thus restricting the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings about technology excessive use to a part of the population. Future research should extend our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to a broad conceptualization of the home environment also including individuals who are not living with a partner but, for example, are sharing a residence, such as with close friends or housemates. While recognizing this aspect as a limitation, it allowed us to avoid a single source for collecting our data and to focus on family demands. Future research should also take into consideration the relationship between pri vate and work domains for single individuals and its effect on individual outcomes. Indeed, it would be crit ical to understand whether there are differences in the outcomes related to excessive mobile use for single individuals, cohabitant individuals, and individuals who have a family, thus opening up to differential interventions in both the organizational and the private domains. Another limitation could be related to the skewedness of our data: most respondents exhibited low excessive use levels. However, the skewedness in our study is comparable with other studies related to maladaptive use of technology (Caplan and High 2006, Boswell and Olson-Buchanan 2007, Turel et al. 2011). This pattern is common when measuring negative behaviors such as compulsive shopping (Ridgway et al. 2008) or abusive supervision (Yam et al. 2016). In addition, the purpose of our study was not to establish the level of excessive mobile use but rather to provide a better understanding of the triggers of such phenomena, as well as its consequences.

In conclusion, this research sought to develop a nuanced understanding of the drivers and outcomes of excessive mobile use for working purposes during nonwork hours. We believe that this research will help provide guidance in the contemporary work environments where mobile devices have become primary means of getting work done by providing organizations with a better understanding of contemporary work design issues.
