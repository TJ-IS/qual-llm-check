# Could Gamification Designs Enhance Online Learning Through Personalization? Lessons from a Field Experiment

Abstract. The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the importance of online learning. A learner autonomy is relatively high in online environments, learners must engage in selfregulated learning (SRL) to achieve optimal learning outcomes. Because most learners are unable to consistently engage in SRL, gami<sup>fi</sup>cation interventions are being implemented to improve SRL engagement; however, mixed results cast doubt on the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of this approach. Massively open online courses (MOOCs), a type of online learning environment, are currently experiencing rapid growth due to widespread adoption by many institutions. In MOOCs, there is no instructor intervention; hence, students have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty regulating their own learning and are easily distracted. Therefore, this study investigates whether mixed-research results regarding the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation can be attributed to lack of attention to individual learner traits during design. For this purpose, the study analyzes MOOCs as an instance of online learning by applying SRL theory and gami<sup>fi</sup>cation principles. We altered a traditional MOOC platform to provide different types of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback to facilitate learners’ SRL engagement. We then examined whether thi matched with goal orientation, an individual learner trait to in<sup>fl</sup>uence SRL and learning outcomes. Using learning-analytics tools, we tracked 760 college students’ SRL engage ment on a MOOC platform over <sup>fi</sup>ve weeks. As theorized, SRL engagement and learning outcomes of participants who had a strong performance-avoidance goal orientation increased with positively framed performance feedback that involved no social comparisons; however, the same feedback had a negative impact on participants with a strong mastery goal orientation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings add to SRL theory by demonstrating that gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs can enhance SRL engagement and learning outcomes in online learning, but with a caveat—this occurs only when there is a match with learner traits—con<sup>fi</sup>rming the gami<sup>fi</sup> cation principle stating that task improvements and meaningful engagement can only occur through thoughtful gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design.

## 1. Introduction

Online learning—also referred to as technologymediated learning (TML) and e-learning—is of deep interest to information systems (IS) researchers due to its extensive use for training employees and students on information technology (IT) platforms (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Gupta and Bostrom 2009, Santhanam et al. 2016). Massively open online courses (MOOCs) are a popular, widely used example of online learning. It was recently estimated that more than 101 million students across the globe learn through such platforms (Shah 2019). Furthermore, MOOCs are currently being integrated into regular university degree programs in innovative ways (Littenberg-Tobias and

Reich 2020). Because of the recent growth in the use of MOOCs and other online platforms among students, researchers are investing substantial efforts into identifying designs that can maximize learning success. Results from these studies, however, are mixed, and gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs do not always demonstrate enhanced learning outcomes (e.g., Henderikx et al. 2017, Reich and Ruiperez-Valiente´ 2019, and Huang et al. 2021). Using self-regulated learning theory and gami<sup>fi</sup>cation principles, we investigate whether differences in individual learner traits impact gami<sup>fi</sup>cation outcomes and could offer explanations for these mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Although learners participating in online learning environments may hold different motivations, personality traits, learning experiences, and strategies, multiple studies have demonstrated that despite these differences, learners must engage in self-regulated learning (SRL) strategies to achieve learning success (e.g., Santhanam et al. 2008, Kizilcec et al. 2017, Kim et al. 2018, and Jansen et al. 2020). In online learning environments, learners have substantial autonomy over their learning process. As a result, they may not be able to obtain similar levels of instructor-provided regulation and feedback or the encouragement and social learning affordances that are more readily available in face-to-face environments (Kennedy 2014, Alraimi et al. 2015). Therefore, many researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd that SRL—which refers to the practice of learners intentionally following and monitoring their goaldirected learning activities—holds added signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in online learning and is critical to its success (e.g., Zimmerman 2000, Santhanam et al. 2008, Eom 2015, and Wong et al. 2019). However, existing empirical studies have found that most learners are unable to engage in and sustain SRL strategies in online learning environments, resulting in subpar learning outcomes and high dropout rates (e.g., Santhanam et al. 2008, Wan et al. 2012, Chang et al. 2013, and Jansen et al. 2020). To tackle this problem, a variety of interventions, including gami<sup>fi</sup>cation, have been advanced to promote the SRL engagement of learners in online environments. However, the studies on gami<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness have generated con<sup>fl</sup>icting results that currently have no clear explanation (Hamari et al. 2014, Antonaci et al. 2019). Hence, we investigate the impact of the individual learner trait of goal orientation on SRL engagement in gami<sup>fi</sup>ed designs to determine whether it could provide an explanation for these con<sup>fl</sup>icting results.

According to social cognitive theory, SRL engagement requires learners to intentionally pursue goaldirected activities and monitor their own learning performance (Bandura 1991, Pintrich 2000, Zimmerman 2000). It is described in terms of distinct phases, with performance feedback playing an important role in facilitating these goal-directed activities (Butler and Winne 1995, Zimmerman 2000). Therefore, gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs that incorporate objects like badges and leaderboards are appropriate interventions for improving SRL engagement, as they can draw learners attention to learning goals and provide performance feedback in a fun manner (Santhanam et al. 2016, Antonaci et al. 2019, Sailer and Homner 2019). However, such gami<sup>fi</sup>ed designs have failed to deliver the expected improvements in learning outcomes, prompting calls for additional investigation (Ding et al. 2017, Kyewski and Kramer ¨ 2018). With the expectation of seeing learning improvements, gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs have been most frequently applied in the education sector (Majuri et al. 2018, Koivisto and Hamari 2019). Yet, research <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not indicate that gami<sup>fi</sup>cation has a consistent, positive impact on learning. This inconsistency must be addressed by scrutinizing each gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design element to identify how it can improve learner engagement and outcomes (Cheong et al. 2014, Park et al. 2019).

We focus on a key gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design element— performance feedback—which studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as integral to enhancing SRL, as it facilitates goaldirected actions (Butler and Winne 1995, Mory 2004, Hsieh and Cho 2011). However, the gami<sup>fi</sup>cation personalization principle states that a good match<sup>1</sup> between gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design elements and user traits is necessary to achieve meaningful engagement and improve task outcomes (Liu et al. 2017). In other words, the extent of match of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback to individual learner traits is likely to impact SRL and learning outcomes and could explain the inconsistent research results regarding gami<sup>fi</sup>ed designs in online learning. To better understand this effect, we adapted a MOOC platform to enhance SRL via different types of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback and examined its impact on goal orientation, the learner trait that is most relevant to SRL. Learner goal orientation is key to SRL engagement because learners must set learning goals, monitor their progress, and regulate their behaviors to achieve their goals (Pintrich 2000, Schunk and Zimmerman 2012). Furthermore, learner goal orientation in<sup>fl</sup>uences learners’ interpretations of performance feedback and their subsequent cognitive resource allocation; some studies have shown that goal orientation may in<sup>fl</sup>uence how learners respond to gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs (VandeWalle and Cummings 1997, Hakulinen and Auvinen 2014).

Per goal-orientation theory, learner goal orientations can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as mastery (i.e., developing competence by mastering challenging situations) or performance goal orientation (i.e., validating one’s competence by seeking positive judgments or avoiding negative judgments) (Dweck and Leggett 1988). The latter category can be further divided into performance-approach and performance-avoidance goal orientations (Elliot 1999). These learner traits may in<sup>fl</sup>uence how learners respond to gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback, which can be provided in several ways. For example, social comparison uses leaderboards that show a learner’s performance relative to their peers, whereas personal comparison shows a learner’s performance relative to their goals (Dom´ınguez et al. 2013, Christy and Fox 2014). Another method for providing gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback is message goal framing; that is, framing a message either positively or negatively relative to the goal (Levin et al. 1998, Van de Velde et al. 2010). A positively framed message emphasizes gains (e.g., steps to obtain an award or badge), whereas a negatively framed message highlights losses (e.g., penalties for not attaining a goal). According to goal-orientation theory, learners with high performance-avoidance goal orientation will likely perform better in response to gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback that uses positive framing and personal comparison, rather than negative framing and social comparison, because these learners do not like their poor performance to be revealed to peers. However, the strengths of these effects must be tested empirically.

Building on theoretical understandings of SRL, goal orientation, and gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design principles, we tested the match between learners’ goal-orientation trait and variations in gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback, such as the type of comparison (personal or social) and message framing (positive or negative), in an online learning environment. We then assessed the impact of matching on SRL engagement, learning ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency, and learning outcomes. Learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is particularly important in online contexts, as online learners can be easily distracted; moreover, because of work and other commitments, today’s learners cannot devote substantial amounts of time to learning (Cook et al. 2010, Khan and Madden 2016, Huang et al. 2021). Therefore, to comprehensively assess learning success, we also evaluated the impact of the match between learners’ goal orientations and gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback on learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (measured by the pace of learning with the SRL process), along with SRL engagement and learning outcomes.

We developed the gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback on a MOOC platform using it as an instance of online learning. We chose a MOOC platform because it is growing in popularity; there is also a noted lack of student engagement on MOOC platforms, and researchers have called for more studies to improve SRL processes in online learning contexts (Antonaci et al. 2017, Reich and Ruiperez-Valiente´ 2019, Wong et al. 2019). It should be noted that considerable research indicates that SRL engagement by learners enhances learning outcomes in all learning environments (Zimmerman and Schunk 2013, Winne 2019); however, because online learning environments are characterized by relatively high levels of learner autonomy and lower instructor or peer interactions, it is essential that learners engage in SRL in such environments. This is particularly true for MOOC online platforms, where there are no instructor interventions. In such contexts, learners feel isolated and face substantial challenges when practicing SRL (Kizilcec et al. 2017, Wong et al. 2019, Huang et al. 2021). Hence, it is an appropriate context for investigating the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs in online learning.

We recruited participants from students enrolled in an introductory course on management information systems (MIS) at a university in Hong Kong. Students were randomly assigned to groups that received different types of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback: per sonal versus social comparison and positive versus negative goal framing. For comparison, a control experiment was conducted with participants of simi lar backgrounds who were studying on the same online platform, but did not receive gami<sup>fi</sup>ed perform ance feedback. We made use of new learning analytics methods (Pardo et al. 2017, Kim et al. 2018) to capture participants’ online learning activities using their digital traces on the platform to measure their SRL engagement and learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. As expected, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that positively framed social comparison feedback matches well with participants with a strong performance-approach goal orientation and enhances these participants’ SRL engagement and learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Positively framed personal comparison feedback works effectively for participants with a strong performance-avoidance goal orientation. However, the same gamified feedback negatively impacts participants with a strong mastery goal orientation. As expected, we found that SRL is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant mediating factor leading to better learning outcomes. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings advance theoretical understandings of SRL in online learning and gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs and offer explanations for the inconsistent results observed in prior studies on gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs in MOOCs and other online platforms (e.g., Ding et al. 2017 and Kyewski and Kramer¨ 2018). We demonstrate that gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs must consider the trait of learner goal orientation to avoid negative impacts on task outcomes and personalize to achieve the best online learning outcomes.

## 2. Research Background and Hypotheses 2.1. SRL and Gamification in Online Learning

SRL engagement is a series of intentional goal-directed activities, strategies, and behaviors by learners that can facilitate their learning success (Zimmerman 2000). In social cognitive theory, SRL engagement is described in terms of activities that occur in the following three phases: forethought (in which learners set their learning task goals and plans); performance and volitional control (in which learners pay attention to their tasks using learning strategies such as note-taking, recording performance, and self-observing their learning progress to optimize their efforts); and self-reflection (in which learners re<sup>fl</sup>ect on and evaluate their learning progress against their goals and may adapt their learning strategies) (Zimmerman 2000). Self-re<sup>fl</sup>ection could lead to the revision of strategies that in<sup>fl</sup>uence forethought in subsequent learning; therefore, SRL engagement is described as a cyclical process. Research studies provide a library of activities in each of these phases that can be checked to measure the extent to which learners are engaging in SRL<sup>2</sup> (e.g., Butler and Winne 1995, Santhanam et al. 2008, and Schunk and Zimmerman 2012). Although learners may engage differently and put more or less effort into each activity or phase, the presence of more of these identi<sup>fi</sup>ed activities shows greater SRL engagement of the learner, whereas the presence of fewer activities indicates less SRL engagement.

In online learning, learners must exercise and maintain SRL engagement because they typically do not have autonomous control over their learning and generally receive little to no prompting from instructors (e.g., Santhanam et al. 2008, Wan et al. 2012, Kuo et al. 2014, and Eom 2015). Research suggests that learners may engage in various activities—such as goal setting, time or effort management, and cognitive strategies— that are related to the three phases of SRL, even though they are not aware of SRL (Hu and Gramling 2009). However, because all learners do not consistently and fully engage in SRL, major research efforts have been undertaken to increase the extent of SRL engagement in online learning (Santhanam et al. 2008, Bol and Garner 2011, Jansen et al. 2020). Interestingly, although learners’ SRL engagement is low in online environments, it is these IT platforms that provide affordances such as gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback through which learners can become aware of their progress and thus potentially revise their strategies to achieve learning goals. In fact, online learning platforms could be more effective than traditional classrooms for stimulating SRL because the technology artifacts can provide regular feedback, support all three SRL phases, and prompt learners to <sup>fi</sup>ne-tune their learning strategies (Narciss et al. 2007, Chang et al. 2013). Through the affordances of the technology artifacts, gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design elements that support performance feedback (e.g., points, badges, and leaderboards) are often used to boost learners’ activities and enhance their SRL and learning outcomes (Dicheva et al. 2015, Landers et al. 2015, Majuri et al. 2018).

Some gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs to improve SRL engagement have demonstrated a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on engaging learners; however, such effects are not universal, with some studies indicating no effect or even negative effects of the use of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation (e.g., Landers et al. 2017, Mekler et al. 2017, and Schobel et al. ¨ 2017). Most of these gami<sup>fi</sup>cation studies bundle multiple gami<sup>fi</sup>ca tion design elements together, treating gami<sup>fi</sup>cation as a generic uni<sup>fi</sup>ed construct and providing a conclusion as to whether it was successful. However, to advance the understanding of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design effectiveness, it is critical to systematically study the impact and nuances of each element for speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks (Landers et al. 2017, Liu et al. 2017, Schobel et al.¨ 2017, Kyewski and Kramer ¨ 2018, Majuri et al. 2018, Schmidt-Kraepelin et al. 2018). Given the mixed nature of previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we unpack and study one element—namely, gami<sup>fi</sup>ed per formance feedback—to better understand how it sup ports learners’ SRL engagement and learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency to improve learning outcomes.

To understand the perspectives and gaps in gami<sup>fi</sup>- cation studies in IS, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y reviewed the research published in the past <sup>fi</sup>ve years in the Senior Scholars’ Basket of Eight journals. Studies focusing on online platforms such as MOOCs are practically nonexistent, excepting for one recent paper (see Table A1 of Online Appendix A). A recent study highlights that procrastination by learners is high in MOOC online environments and proposes interventions to reduce these occurrences (Huang et al. 2021). Please note that procrastinations result in low SRL engagement (Wolters 2003, Cheng and Xie 2021). Another study demonstrates that it is particularly important to design timely and adequate performance feedback using technology artifacts, so as to address requirements for massi<sup>fi</sup>ed education (Piccoli et al. 2020). A study on gami<sup>fi</sup>ed e-learning concluded that a one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all approach is not appropriate and set forth possible study designs to address how the impact of each game element might be tested (Santhanam et al. 2016). A few other studies on online learning are summarized in Table A1 of Online Appendix A. Outside the domain of online learning, gami<sup>fi</sup>cation research in IS has been applied in several areas, including knowledge sharing, training in phishing prevention, and health and exercise management, indicating that gami<sup>fi</sup>cation is a useful approach to inducing user engagement (see Table A2 of Online Appendix A). A commentary on gami<sup>fi</sup>cation lists several principles for achieving gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design success and highlights the importance of matching gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design elements to user traits (Liu et al. 2017). In other related disciplines of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed learning (see Table A3 of Online Appendix A), some studies have documented the positive effect of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation on learning performance, but some have shown mixed or even negative effects. In general, researchers have concluded that gami<sup>fi</sup>cation cannot prompt all learners to deeply engage in learning unless gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design is person-speci<sup>fi</sup>c (van Roy and Zaman 2018). Taken together, although recent research speaks to the value of improving gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs in online learning, no studies have attempted to address the role of learner traits and individual gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design elements in improving SRL engagement, which is a key aspect of online learning (Pintrich 2000, Santhanam et al. 2008, Wan et al. 2012, Liu et al. 2017). We address this issue by studying whether the interaction between gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback and individual learner traits of goal orientation affects SRL engagement and learning performance in MOOCs.

## 2.2. Gamified Performance Feedback and Goal Orientation Consideration

On online learning platforms, gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback in the form of badges and leaderboards is quite popular. We built and extended these feedback options in an SRL theory-driven manner by altering the comparison type and framing of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance-feedback messages to study how they in<sup>fl</sup>uenced learners with different traits of goal orientation. Performance feedback can be delivered in different ways and in line with the gami<sup>fi</sup>cation principle, which states that comparison type and message format could be tailored to users to make the feedback more persuasive (Schmidt-Kraepelin et al. 2018, Zhou et al. 2020). One type of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback is social comparison. Through leaderboards, this type of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback highlights a learner’s progress relative to that of other learners (Dicheva et al. 2015, Sailer and Homner 2019). Individual learners’ goal orientations may impact how they respond. Per goal-orientation theory (Elliot and Church 1997), learners with a strong performanceapproach goal orientation may prefer to see social comparisons on leaderboards. However, those with a strong performance-avoidance goal orientation are anxious about showing ignorance in front of their peers and, thus, may prefer not to see their progress on the leaderboards. In particular, learners with a strong mastery goal orientation do not pay attention to scores. Studies indicate that some learners favor such social comparison on leaderboards, but others do not (Christy and Fox 2014, Lim and Yang 2015). If learners do not like or pay attention to leaderboards, the very purpose of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback is defeated.

Another type of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback is personal feedback that shows learners’ performance on the learning task as measured against a goal (Cheong et al. 2014, Mekler et al. 2017). This type of personal comparison feedback, which highlights personal accomplishments and progress without making peer comparisons (e.g., meeting a deadline and earning a badge), may be suitable for learners who do not like social comparisons. Game elements that show personal comparisons (e.g., posting the percentage of modules that the learner has completed) provide progressive information to users, with which they can evaluate their learning strategies, whereas social comparisons through game elements (e.g., leaderboards and loserboards) offer observational or vicarious reinforcements that indicate the af<sup>fi</sup>rmation or disaf<sup>fi</sup>rmation of behaviors exhibited by peers (Christy and Fox 2014, Landers et al. 2017).

Whether gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback is given in the form of personal or social comparisons, the framing of the message could be changed to affect how learners respond. Message-framing techniques— that is, the presentation of logically equivalent options in semantically different ways—can be used to persuade users to act on a particular message by framing it in either a positive or negative format (Levin et al. 1998, Krishnamurthy et al. 2001). One method of accomplishing this, known as goal framing, is to present messages relative to goals (Van de Velde et al. 2010). Positively goal-framed messages emphasize obtaining gains, whereas negatively goal-framed messages highlight losses that might accrue as a consequence of not achieving goals (Lindenberg and Steg 2013, Schmidt Kraepelin et al. 2018). Therefore, in positive goal framing, learners’ progress and achievements are highlighted, and positive consequences (e.g., providing a master badge for completing a learning module and highlighting the steps required to reach the next milestone) are emphasized with the expectation that they will generate positive actions among users (Roney and Lehman 2008). Conversely, negative goal framing emphasizes negative consequences (e.g., earning a loser badge and highlighting an incomplete learning module), with the expectation that users will want to avoid incurring such consequences in their learning. Whereas positive goal framing reminds users of the rewards associated with status and af<sup>fi</sup>rmation (Antin and Churchill 2011), negative goal framing uses informational feedback to prompt users to develop better learning strategies by highlighting penalties that could occur as a result of inaction (Krishnamurthy et al. 2001). The effectiveness of goal framing depends on the match between learners’ goal orientations and situationally induced goals (Sagiv and Schwartz 2000). As described above, our study addresses two dimensions of performance feedback (see Figure 1): type of comparison (social versus personal) and mes sage framing (positive versus negative goal framing).

According to goal-orientation theory, the individual trait of goal orientation indicates that learners possess highly different learning motivations that are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in their learning performance, particularly in online contexts (e.g., Ames 1992, Elliot et al. 2002, Zweig and Webster 2004, and Santhanam et al. 2008).

The trichotomous goal-orientation framework classi<sup>fi</sup>es individual goal orientations as mastery,<sup>3</sup> performanceapproach, or performance-avoidance (Elliot and Harackiewicz 1996, Elliot and Church 1997, Zweig and Webster 2004). Learners with a strong mastery goal orientation care primarily about competency and place less emphasis on scores and performance relative to others (Ames 1992). In contrast, learners with a performance goal orientation take a normative perspective. Those with a strong performance-approach goal orientation focus on attaining a favorable judgment of competency (Midgley et al. 2001), whereas those with a strong performance-avoidance goal orientation focus on avoiding being embarrassed in front of others (Darnon et al. 2007). Goal orientation is an important individual trait that is relevant to user interactions with technology, such as the use of web-based IS (Yi and Hwang 2003) and acceptance of e-learning systems (Santhanam et al. 2008, Wan et al. 2012).

## 2.3. Hypotheses Development

With SRL engagement, learners can actively seek to reduce a discrepancy between a goal state and their current level of performance by planning and monitoring their progress; therefore, gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback can be highly useful in this process (Cleary and Zimmerman 2012). To facilitate SRL engagement, gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback can be given using a social or personal comparison approach and positive or negative goal framing. However, because learners possess different goal orientations, their responses to gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback will vary. We expected that a match between gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback and learners’ traits would improve learners SRL engagement and increase their learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Henderikx et al. 2017). Because SRL engagement requires that learners pay complete attention to learning goals by screening out distractions and applying optimal learning strategies, their learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness should be high (Zimmerman 2000). Learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as measured by the pace of module completion does not imply shallow learning, as learners who engage in SRL undergo the cyclical strategic thinking process before, during, and after taking part in various learning activities, and they are consistent for maintaining pace with SRL (Jarvel ¨ a et al. ¨ 2016). This is particularly important in online environments (Kolfschoten et al. 2010).

Learners with a strong performance-approach goal orientation care about whether they are perceived as competent and tend to demonstrate the ability to earn favorable judgment from others (Elliot et al. 2002). Previous research shows that those learners strive for public approval by demonstrating their competence through achievements (Lee et al. 2003). To maintain their high achievements, they are likely to engage in rigorous and persistent study behaviors (Elliot and Church 1997). Therefore, gami<sup>fi</sup>ed and positively framed social comparison performance feedback that displays their achievements in front of others may motivate them to be more engaged. We thus posit:

Hypothesis 1 (a). Positively framed (positive) social comparison feedback encourages learners with a strong performance-approach goal orientation to engage in SRL;

and

Hypothesis 1 (b). Positively framed (positive) social comparison feedback helps learners with a strong performanceapproach goal orientation develop higher learning efficiency.

Learners with a strong performance-avoidance goal orientation worry about negative judgments and tend to avoid situations that show normative incompetence (Elliot and Harackiewicz 1996). With low selfperceived abilities and a strong fear of failure, these learners tend to avoid challenging or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult tasks (Dweck and Leggett 1988). Because they place excessive weight on the possibility of failure, uncertainty in their learning may make them even more concerned about their performance (Darnon et al. 2007). To alleviate such concerns, personal feedback with positive goal framing can help learners focus on positive aspects and provide them with clear guidance in learning. Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 2 (a). Positively framed (positive) personal comparison feedback encourages learners with a strong performance-avoidance goal orientation to engage in SRL;

and

Hypothesis 2 (b). Positively framed (positive) personal comparison feedback helps learners with a strong performanceavoidance goal orientation develop higher learning efficiency.

Learners with a strong mastery goal orientation are intrinsically motivated to improve their knowledge and skills and care less about scores and performance relative to others (Ames 1992). To achieve their mastery goals, learners are active in monitoring their learning progress and engage in cognitive learning strategies (Pintrich 2000). Feedback on their learning performance motivates them to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to further improve themselves. Negative feedback is valuable to these learners because it can help them identify ineffective learning and substandard learning performance (VandeWalle and Cummings 1997). Personal feedback with negative goal framing can pinpoint these individuals’ shortcomings. Such feedback can trigger individuals with strong mastery goal orientations to pay attention to the inadequacy of their learning strategies so that they can rectify them and improve their learning skills. Thus, we posit:

Hypothesis 3 (a). Negatively framed (negative) personal comparison feedback encourages learners with a strong mastery goal orientation to engage in SRL;

and

Hypothesis 3 (b). Negatively framed (negative) personal comparison feedback helps learners with a strong mastery goal orientation develop higher learning efficiency.

Learners’ engagement in SRL leads to better learning outcomes because users set learning goals; adopt strategies to achieve these goals; and go through the phases of forethought, performance and volitional control, and re<sup>fl</sup>ection in a motivated manner (Zimmerman 2000, Santhanam et al. 2008, Landers et al. 2015). The mediating role of SRL is important in the learning process. Therefore, we posit as a con<sup>fi</sup>rmation:

Hypothesis 4. SRL mediates the influence of the match between gamified performance feedback and goal orientation on learning outcomes.

We present our hypotheses and research model in Figure 2.

## 3. Research Method 3.1. Overview

To examine whether the match between learner goal orientations and gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback impacts SRL and learning outcomes in TML, we implemented a longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment to track participants’ SRL engagement and learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency over time. We chose a MOOC platform<sup>4</sup> as our test environment and measured learners’ activities over the course of <sup>fi</sup>ve weeks. The development of the design was theory-driven so that learners could cycle through the stages of forethought, performance and volitional control, and re<sup>fl</sup>ection during the SRL process (see Online Appendix B for screenshots). Our research participants were university students enrolled in an introductory MIS course, and the experiment was integrated as a class activity. As proposed in recent learning-analytics research, the digital traces of learning activities in the three SRL phases were captured and used to derive students’ SRL engagement scores. Self-regulated learning engagement, learning ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency, and learning outcomes served as dependent measures, whereas the gami<sup>fi</sup>ed treatments were considered independent measures. For hypothesis testing, we ran regressions and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the impact of the interaction terms of learner traits and gami<sup>fi</sup>ed treatment on the dependent measures. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive (negative) coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimate of the interaction term indicates that there is a match (mismatch) between a learner’s goal orientation and type of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feed back. We chose regression because it is suitable for testing moderators and accounts for heterogeneity among experimental subjects (Becker et al. 2013).

## 3.3. Goal-Orientation Classification

To identify participants’ goal orientation, we obtained the mastery, performance-approach, and performanceavoidance goal orientations<sup>6</sup> of each participant using the measures proposed by Midgley et al. Our pre-experiment survey (Online Appendix D) also collected participants’ IT expertise, demographic information, and knowledge on the course topic via a knowledge test. ## 3.5. Self-Regulated Learning Engagement and Learning Outcomes

According to Zimmerman (2000), SRL comprises three major phases: forethought, performance and volitional control, and self-re<sup>fl</sup>ection. We recorded and analyzed actual learners’ activities on the MOOC platform to measure their SRL engagement, rather than asking them to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their activities through a survey, as has been done in previous studies (e.g., Santhanam et al. 2008 and Wan et al. 2012). From the recorded user log, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed standard activities used in prior research to describe the three SRL phases, as shown in Table 1.

For the forethought phase, we focused on goal setting and planning, where we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the extent to which participants developed study plans (see Figure B2 in Online Appendix B). For performance and volitional control, indicators included proactive efforts related to self-control and self-observation (Zimmerman 2000, Santhanam et al. 2008). Hence, activities that showed attention and self-revision—such as reading lecture slides, watching videos, taking notes, seeking help in forums, and self-evaluation through volitional quiz-taking—were all included. At the end of each module, quizzes were available, through which participants could conduct self-evaluation on their learning performance.<sup>9</sup> For the re<sup>fl</sup>ection phase, participants were asked to write a self-re<sup>fl</sup>ection essay to evaluate their learning strategies and provide suggestions to improve their study plans. Engagement in these activities in each phase was calibrated to indicate learners’ SRL engagement.<sup>10</sup>

Table 1 shows the details of SRL, which provided the measure of SRL engagement. Learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, as captured by the pace of module completion with the SRL process, was measured by the time a participant took to complete a module and all three SRL phases. To determine learning outcomes, participants took a postexperiment test<sup>11</sup> (Online Appendix E) at the end of the self-study period. The test comprised knowledge and performance sections and was created in accordance with prior IS research on learning and training, which evaluated learners’ knowledge and skills in using speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies (Yi and Davis 2003, Santhanam et al. 2008).

3.6. Gamified Performance-Feedback Treatments Our two-by-two treatments included personal and social comparison feedback and positive and negative goal-framing feedback. Figure 1 shows the four types of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed feedback treatments: positive personal comparison, negative personal comparison, positive social comparison, and negative social comparison. In the positive personal (negative personal) feedback treatment group, participants were reminded of the percentage of tasks they had completed (or not yet completed) in each module. They also received tips for obtaining master badges (avoiding loser badges).<sup>12</sup> Table 2 lists all the badges that participants could potentially receive. For the positive social comparison (negative social comparison) treatment group, we constructed a leaderboard (loserboard) and helpfulness board (miser board). The leaderboard (loserboard) showed the names and scores of the highest (lowest) 20 daily cumulative scores.<sup>13</sup> We used a randomization strategy and repeated the procedure multiple times until the F-tests showed that the participants across the four treatment groups were similar. Details of the experimental procedure are shown in Figure 3. We assumed that stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) was valid where potential social interactions among participants had little effect on the treatments assigned to participants (Mithas et al. 2014).

Each treatment group received a speci<sup>fi</sup>c gami<sup>fi</sup>ed email, as shown in Online Appendix C. Both treatment and control groups received the following stand ard elements through the MOOC system: (a) a deadline for completing a module, (b) cumulative scores based on learning activities, (c) helpfulness scores based on the number of “up-votes” and “down-votes” received, and (d) master and loser badges obtained.

## 3.7. Propensity Score Matching

To alleviate sampling bias in the treatment and control groups (Dehejia and Wahba 2002), we conducted propensity score matching (PSM) to match each participant in the treatment group to another participant in the control group, such that the participants across groups were similar in terms of goal orientations, demographics, and pre-experiment knowledge test scores (see Online Appendix G). In conducting the PSM, sampling with replacement or without replacement could be applied. We chose the former primarily because it ensures sample independence (Raj and Khamis 1958). Previous IS research use the same approach to approximate the true population distribution (Bapna and Umyarov 2015).

After PSM, the treatment and control participant ratio was 1:1. The distribution of the propensity score as shown in the box and smooth histogram plots (Figures G1 and G2 in Online Appendix G) con<sup>fi</sup>rms that the matched control group closely resembles the treatment group. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test also shows that the distributions of the propensity score of the treatment and matched control groups are similar, with a p-value of 0.756. We reported the summary statistics of all participants in both treatment and control groups before and after PSM in Table G2 of Online Appendix G.

## 5. Discussion

Online learning platforms have been gami<sup>fi</sup>ed with the expectation of enhancing SRL engagement and improving learning outcomes. However, study results on gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design implementations are often mixed; hence, research must be undertaken to understand the impact of individual gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design elements (Dicheva et al. 2015, Seaborn and Fels 2015, Koivisto and Hamari 2019). Moreover, principles of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation suggest that a good match between design elements and individual traits is essential for fostering meaningful engagement and achieving task outcomes (Liu et al. 2017). Therefore, to offer one explanation for the mixed results from gami<sup>fi</sup>cation of online platforms, we tested the match between gami-<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback and the individual traits of learner goal orientations in an online learning platform. We found that when there was a match, gami<sup>fi</sup>- cation design was indeed effective at enhancing learners’ SRL engagement. In other cases, however, it led to the unintended consequence of a deterioration in SRL, defeating the very purpose of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the need to closely consider learners goal orientations when developing gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs in online learning environments and the importance of incorporating the personalization gami<sup>fi</sup>cation principle in designing performance feedback.

Research suggests that extrinsic motivators may crowd out intrinsic motivation in a task (Bénabou and Tirole 2006. Hanus and Fox 2015). Mastery goal-oriented learners are intrinsically motivated, learn out of curiosity, and pursue courses that are personally meaningful and interesting to them by engaging in deep learning strategies (Ames and Archer 1988, Elliot 1999, Senko and Miles 2008). In fact, they follow their own learning agenda; place effort into learning material they <sup>fi</sup>nd personally interesting, rather than that suggested by their teachers; evaluate their performance using self-referential standards; and may not receive the highest grades in the course—a pattern dissimilar from that of performance-approach goal-oriented learners (Senko and Miles 2008). Hence, for the mastery goal-oriented learners, even though the gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback was personal and there was no comparison with peer performance, reminders about external rewards (e.g., badges and indicators of how far they were from obtaining the next badge) may have frustrated them and caused them to lose engagement in the task. Prior research has found that praise and rewards may have no effect on, or even undermine, intrinsic motivation (Henderlong and Lepper 2002). We found that for learners with mastery orientation, the gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback did not enhance their SRL engagement. In fact, from a relatively signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive SRL engagement, the gami<sup>fi</sup>ed feedback led to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative change in SRL engagement. In a postexperiment survey, we invited learners to share their comments on the gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback. Some of the statements by learners with a strong mastery goal orientation who received positively framed personal comparison feedback support our expectations: “Give details of my learning process and results rather than those badges as I don't really know how I perform through those badges”; “Stop using badges. Motivate students in ways other than marks”; “Statistics emails don't say much. They just encouraged others to post senseless posts to get meaningless badges and points, but no real learning.”

On the other hand, the very same type of feedback, when provided to learners with a strong performanceapproach goal orientation, elicited the following responses: “I like the colorful badges. This motivated me to get high marks on KEEP”; “It’s good if I can know my ranking”; “Show the highest score among my classmates to inform me and thus motivate me”; “Give some information about the distance between top students”; “It would be more encouraging if I can compare my scores and rankings with my peers.” These qualitative statements support our premise that gami-<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback may be viewed differently by different learners, depending on their goal orientations and highlight the observed statistical differences in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the impact of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback on SRL engagement, learning outcomes and learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Gami<sup>fi</sup>cation interventions are being applied to enhance SRL with varying degrees of success because higher SRL leads to increased learning outcomes. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has yet examined learners detailed learning activities over a period of time to examine the impact of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation interventions. By closely tracking SRL engagement through a MOOC learning platform with digital tracing over a <sup>fi</sup>veweek time period, this study contributed to theoretical understandings of SRL by demonstrating how gami<sup>fi</sup>- cation designs can either enhance or diminish SRL engagement among different types of learners. We provide empirical evidence that SRL is an important mediator of personalized gami<sup>fi</sup>cation and learning performance outcomes. Unlike previous research on SRL in IS (e.g., Santhanam et al. 2008 and Wan et al. 2012), we used more granular methods—such as analyzing learners’ digital trace data captured by our system and learning analytics—rather than applying a cross-sectional survey design to measure SRL. Finally, in this study, we captured the pace of SRL engagement in learning and found that a match between game elements and learner goal orientations had a positive impact on learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

From a gami<sup>fi</sup>cation perspective, recent reviews show that there are many scholarly papers and reports on gami<sup>fi</sup>cation in practice; however, few studies have carefully examined the individual game elements and their impact on the psychological processes of individual users (Koivisto and Hamari 2019, Park et al. 2019). Moreover, few studies are theoretically anchored, and among those that are, social determination theory is the most applied theory in almost every context (Seaborn and Fels 2015). We applied SRL theory because it is most pertinent to online learning tasks, and we closely examined the impact of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed performance feedback on individual learners’ psychological processes over a period of time. We found that gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design enhanced SRL engagement and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved learning outcomes for some learners, but not for others. This result helps explain the mixed results of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs in online learning contexts, demonstrating that the overall gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design is not likely to lead to successful learning outcomes unless it is aligned with learner traits. Hence, unlike a one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all approach for quickly developing gami<sup>fi</sup>ed systems, the use of gami-<sup>fi</sup>cation principles, enhanced understanding of context, and careful design will lead to meaningful engagement and continued use of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed IS (Liu et al. 2017). Personalization in online learning will be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and add to the trend in designs of online platforms that call for more personalization and adaptation (Ho et al. 2011, Zhang et al. 2011, Ho and Bodoff 2014).

Online learning continues to be of considerable interest to IS researchers because it is used extensively in corporate training and education (e.g., George 2014, Panigrahi et al. 2018, and Huang et al. 2021). Because digital platform designs are increasingly used for corporate activities, research that takes a sociotechnical perspective on technology design is needed (Tiwana et al. 2010, Sarker et al. 2019). From a sociotechnical perspective, the interplay of social and technical elements closely conforms to the nomological net proposed by Benbasat and Zmud (2003), which describes how IT artifacts (e.g., gami<sup>fi</sup>cation) impact the context (i.e., online platforms) in which they are embedded. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the boundary conditions (i.e., a match between goal orientation and gami<sup>fi</sup>cation feature) under which online learning platforms can promote participants’ SRL behaviors and learning performance.

## 5.2. Implications for Practice

Although the adoption of gami<sup>fi</sup>ed IS has been growing in popularity, failed gami<sup>fi</sup>cation projects are estimated to cost U.S. businesses U.S. \$700 million over the last decade, casting concerns on gami<sup>fi</sup>cation designs and their use for heterogeneous work scenar ios (Maddox 2018). Rather than concluding that a gami<sup>fi</sup>cation design failed, it should be considered that it may have resulted in unmet goals for certain types of users; hence, a nuanced and thoughtful design could lead to successful outcomes. ## 6. Limitations, Future Research, and Concluding Remarks

As with any <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, our study has several limitations. First, we did not directly observe all the learning activities of individual participants, but relied on system-usage data for interpretation of SRL. Any activities outside the online platform, such as of<sup>fl</sup>ine discussions with peers, were not captured. Second, SUTVA may not be valid due to social interactions among participants. However, as our treatments contained personalized feedback, it is not likely that participants shared such private information with their friends. Moreover, some treatments were speci<sup>fi</sup>c to individuals (e.g., module progress) and may not have had any impact on others, even if they were shared. Third, we collected data from college students, but these <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not be easily generalizable to all learners, some of whom are working adults. In addition, whereas this study focused on one individual trait—goal orientation—because of its importance in SRL, future studies should address the impact of other individual traits. We tested our ideas in one instance of online learning—a MOOC, with studies indicating that observed behaviors generalize to other online environments (Almeda 2018). However, online learning can occur in different formats, ranging with some to no instructor intervention. Ou study results could be tested in other online environments, with other game elements and other individual traits.

Despite the growing popularity of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation, its failure rate is astonishingly high, and we <sup>fi</sup>nd that one way to alleviate this is to consider the match between personalized gami<sup>fi</sup>cation elements and user traits. COVID-19 has increased the reliance on and adoption of online education for many higher educational universities, and this study makes a timely contribution to make online learning more effective.
