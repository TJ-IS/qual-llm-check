# Moving Consumers from Free to Fee in Platform-Based Markets: An Empirical Study of Multiplayer Online Battle Arena Games

Abstract. Companies in platform-based business markets have widely embraced freemium business models where pro<sup>fi</sup>t primarily depends on a minority of paying customers. However, the key challenge of these models is transitioning participants from free users to paying consumers. To encourage paid consumption, companies often rely on product differentiation such as providing consumers who pay for products or services with enhanced features. However, limited research has addressed how such product differentiation may convert consumers from “free” to “fee.” Our research examines the multiplayer online battle arena (MOBA) game as a compelling example of a freemium platform-based business model. We contribute to the freemium literature by introducing three new MOBA-speci<sup>fi</sup>c differentiations—character competency, character variety, and character-appearance differentiation. We also extend consumption values theory into a dual-path model to unveil the underlying mechanisms through which product differentiation in<sup>fl</sup>uences in-game purchase. We empirically validate our dual-path model using data from a two-wave longitudinal experiment and three cross-sectional experiments. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support opposing mediating paths for product differentiation in character competency and variety and indi cate that these two types of differentiation can indeed undermine perceived game fairness. Conversely, character-appearance differentiation exerts only a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on players’ purchasing of in-game items. Consequently, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study have important potential implications for platform-based companies leveraging freemium business models that seek to increase their share of paying customers.

## 1. Introduction

Platform-based business models and markets are disrupting traditional consumer business models (e.g., Constantinides et al. 2018, Rietveld et al. 2019, Hagiu and Wright 2020), including the digital gaming market (Zhao et al. 2018, Huang et al. 2019, Rietveld et al. 2019, Haviv et al. 2020). Mobile video games have become one of the most prevalent leisure activities worldwide, transcending age and gender (Huang et al. 2019, Latinsky and Ueno 2021). As multiplayer online battle arena (MOBA) games have moved to mobile devices, this large global market has attracted highly dedicated gamers.

Although MOBA games can be highly pro<sup>fi</sup>table, they face unique challenges because they are highly competitive and rife with freeloading and <sup>fi</sup>ckle customers. MOBA games have widely adopted the freemium business model, in which game companies offer their basic products or services for free and generate revenue through the sale of value-added in-game items (Koch and Benlian 2017, Rietveld 2018). How ever, vendors using the freemium model struggle with the fundamental conundrum it poses for pro<sup>fi</sup>tability; namely, a minority of paying players <sup>fi</sup>nance the participation of the nonpaying majority (Fang et al. 2019). Converting free players into paying players, however, is an ongoing challenge for all free-to-play games, because offering a free version can reduce demand for the premium version (Nan et al. 2018). In free-to-play games, paying players may naturally group with paying players and vice versa; thus, it is likely that these two parallel worlds coexist in free-toplay games. Relatedly, several studies have veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the positive effects of peers on purchasing in the gaming context (Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Haviv et al. 2020). Understanding how to convert nonpaying players into paying ones may help remove the barriers between paying and nonpaying players in free-to-play games.

Vendors of free-to-play games typically leverage product differentiation (i.e., distinguishing or enhancing a product to make it more appealing to the target audience) to entice and convert nonplaying players into paying ones (Koch and Benlian 2017, Rietveld 2018). Product differentiation may help create demand for additional paid content and reduce the risk of free riders (Budd and Na 2000). Free-to-play games typically introduce differentiation through the monetary selling of in-game items that can enhance the competitiveness of game characters. In line with Wagner et al. (2014), we de<sup>fi</sup>ne product differentiation in the context of free-to-play MOBA games as the extent to which the attributes of a free game character differ from those of characters equipped with paid items. We explain below how these attributes can be aptly categorized in terms of competency, variety, and appearance; these are the character attributes gamers care about the most, because they enhance the gaming results (Hodges and Buckley 2018, Marder et al. 2019).

Studies of the critical role of product differentiation have drawn divergent conclusions regarding the relationship between the magnitude of product differentiation and in-game purchase behavior. The consumption value literature has long asserted that the inherent value of an item is a critical antecedent of purchase behavior (Sheth et al. 1991). Product differentiation is intended to facilitate various individual values, such as monetary, social, and enjoyment values. In free-to-play games, paid items are perceived to deliver monetary value (i.e., cost-effectiveness and a reasonable price) if the differentiation between free and paid items is high (Kim et al. 2011, Rezaei and Ghodsi 2014). The magnitude of product differentiation also positively relates to a player’s enjoyment—a key motivation for playing games required for achieving immersive gaming (Teng 2010, Lowry et al. 2013, Hussain et al. 2015). For example, the functional discrepancy between free and paid items increases enjoyment value when the use of paid items gives players the capacity to defeat opponents more easily. Thus, product differentiation positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences in-game purchasing by facilitating the various individual values of free-to-play games and thus satisfying players’ needs and motivations. In systems use that involves complex intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, ful<sup>fi</sup>lling such expectations and motivations is key to fostering immersive positive experiences and continuance (Li et al. 2015, Lowry et al. 2015a).

Conversely, other studies have claimed that the differentiation between free and paid items should be limited because such differentiation can undermine game fairness (GF). As a key psychological and economic principle, perceived fairness is important in determining perceptions of value, regardless of game type or context (Bolton et al. 2005, Dana et al. 2006, Ho and Su 2009). Game fairness is a key community value in freeto-play games, such that competency differentiation among game characters can negatively predict perceived game fairness (Lin and Sun 2011, Rong et al. 2018), thus hindering purchase behaviors in free-to-play games.<sup>2</sup>

These contradictory perspectives on the negative and positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of fairness values on consumer purchasing resonate with recent calls in leading interdisciplinary journals for in-depth investigation into the relationship between product differentiation and purchase behavior in general (Choe et al. 2018, Shi et al. 2019). Thus far, no theory-based empirical stud ies have attempted to reconcile these contradictory views of product differentiation vis-a-vis purchase \` behavior in the MOBA game market. To advance the product-differentiation literature in the context of free-to-play MOBA games, we embrace a values-based perspective and propose a theory-based research model that addresses the underlying individual and community-based values through which different types of MOBA game product differentiation occur. We raise the following general research question to guide our theoretical and empirical inquiry: How do the considerations of product differentiation, consumer values, and fairness contribute to converting nonplaying players into paying players in a free-to-play MOBA game context?

To address this question, we <sup>fi</sup>rst identify three types of product differentiation that are relevant to our context: character-competency differentiation (CD), character-variety differentiation (VD), and character-appearance differentiation (AD). We predict their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on converting nonpaying players into paying players through a contextualized application of consumption value theory (CVT). We identify the distinct roles of individual values and game fairness in product differentiation and the interrelationships between individual values and game fairness. We test the resulting dual-path model using a two-wave longitudinal experiment and three cross-sectional experiments. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support opposing mediating paths for product differentiation in character competency and variety, but not for differentiation in character appearance. Differentiation in character appearance does not appear to activate the negative path, and increasing the magnitude of character-appearance differentiation exerts only a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on in-game item purchasing behavior. We expand on these and other <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the discussion section.

## 2. Theoretical Foundation

Here, we review the determinants of consumer purchasing in freemium services and then explain how product differentiation and CVT work together to determine consumer purchasing outcomes. Finally, we explain the key individual and game fairness values that matter most to players of MOBA games, because it is the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment (or discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation) of these values that best determines whether players will convert from “free” into “fee.”

## 2.1. The Determinants of Consumer Purchasing in Freemium Services

Because in-game purchases are the main revenue resource for vendors that leverage freemium business model, extant research has sought to identify the reasons consumers pay for premium services. Hamari et al. (2017a) suggested that service quality explains why they do so. Consumers of online music or video applications often use premium services to skip advertisements (Bapna and Umyarov 2015; Li et al. 2020a, b). Wagner et al. (2014) suggested that users pay for premium services because they want to unlock additional features. Through a large-scale survey, Hamari et al. (2017b) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed unobstructed play, social interaction, and competition as the top reasons why free-to-play gamers purchase in-game content. Teng (2018) suggests that game loyalty positively predicts in-game purchase and value perceptions (i.e., social, enjoyment, and monetary value) are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant antecedents of game loyalty. Although the speci<sup>fi</sup>c conclusions of these studies differ, they all maintain that a consumer’s valuation of premium services determines the consumer’s willingness to purchase them and that valuation and purchasing have distinct domains.<sup>3</sup>

Another line of research has examined the antecedents of consumer purchasing in freemium services from the perspective of product differentiation. For example, free-to-play gamers often purchase in-game content because the premium content allows them to differentiate the appearance of their game characters from that of free players’ characters (Marder et al. 2019). Similarly, Wagner et al. (2014) suggested that the extent of functional differentiation between free and premium versions positively predicts consumers intention to pay for premium services. Likewise, marketing research has suggested that some consumers avoid change and others prefer variety (Sajeesh and Raju 2010). Thus, choice variety in freemium services is more likely to cater to heterogeneous consumer tastes than homogenous tastes.

The literature reviewed in this section examines the separate in<sup>fl</sup>uences of consumption values and product differentiation on consumer purchasing but does not consider the synergistic effects of these variables.

To address this research gap, in the next section, we explain how consumption values and product differentiation can jointly predict consumer purchasing.

## 2.2. Individual Values and the Community Value of Fairness of Paid In-Game Items

Recent studies have categorized value components of various kinds of consumer purchasing and systems use into several dimensions. For example, several studies have classi<sup>fi</sup>ed value components into the two dimensions of individual and community values (Doran 2010, Figueiredo and Scaraboto 2016, Hakala et al. 2017). In our study, individual values are the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of paid items to the players, and community values are the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of paid items to the game community. A complementary line of literature has further categorized individual values into utilitarian and hedonic values (Lowry et al. 2013, Chiu et al. 2014, Kuehnl et al. 2019). Research on players’ purchase behaviors in free-to-play games has classi<sup>fi</sup>ed enjoyment, socialization, and saving money as individual values and game fairness as a typical community value (Lowry et al. 2015b, Hamari et al. 2017b, Rong et al. 2018). Moreover, enjoyment and socialization can be regarded as hedonic values, whereas saving money can be viewed as a utilitarian value (Chiu et al. 2014).

2.2.1. Three Individual Values. Building on prior literature, we differentiate individual values from community values in the context of free-to-play MOBA games (Doran 2010, Figueiredo and Scaraboto 2016, Hakala et al. 2017). We identify three types of individual values that are salient in this context: enjoyment, monetary, and social identity value. Enjoyment value is a player’s overall enjoyment of the game, which can result from winning a game, recognition by peers, or socialization with peers, depending on the player’s motivation. Tseng (2011) found that the need for exploration and the need for aggression are motivations to play mobile games and that ful<sup>fi</sup>lling either need leads a player to experience enjoyment value. For example, aggressive players purchase in-game items to increase character strength and satisfy their need for competency and winning (Liu et al. 2013, Santhanam et al. 2016, Sepehr and Head 2018). By contrast, exploration players receive enjoyment from in-game socialization, such as sending paid items as gifts to peers (Tseng 2011).

Second, players may purchase in-game items due to perceived monetary value when paid in-game items are reasonably priced for the utility they produce. Recent economics research suggests that people naturally assess cost-effectiveness before purchasing a product (Myers 2019). In our context, high monetary value indicates that the utility gains from in-game items are perceived by players to outweigh the costs. Thus, when game operators provide a limited-time discount for in-game items, players are more likely to assess utility gains and will more likely want to purchase these “bargains.” Moreover, consumers place a high value on fairly receiving a bargain (Darke and Dahl 2003), and, thus, purchasing in-game items with high monetary value that are provided at a discount can be exhilarating and signal to players that the use of their money is wise and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (Kim et al. 2011).

Third, social identity value is a player’s perceived utility of a paid item based on the item’s ability to enhance the player’s social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Turel et al. 2010, Park and Lee 2011). A higher social identity value means a higher likelihood of being recognized by peers and a greater ease of establishing in-game relationships. Even if an in-game item has no functional purpose, players may purchase it to enhance the appearance of their characters because it is rare or can raise their virtual social status, thus providing social value (Marder et al. 2019). Enhancing one’s social identity is a well-known reason for consumer purchasing (Sweeney and Soutar 2001). By embellishing game characters with attractive paid items, players expect to attract more visitors—with whom they can potentially establish relationships and thus social identity value—to their home pages.

2.2.2. Value of Fairness. Players also embrace community values, or widely accepted principles or standards that members of a community seek to maintain (Prilleltensky 2001). We focus on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of fairness, a typical type of community value, on in-game purchase behaviors for two reasons. First, fairness is a key manifestation of community value in any social and economic system. One long-standing tenet of fairness is to promote collective wellness in a society (Prilleltensky 2001). Studies suggest that people dislike being treated in an unfair manner (Choi and Chen 2007, Ferguson et al. 2014, Hong et al. 2017). Fairness in<sup>fl</sup>uences all kinds of justice-related evaluations and subsequent behaviors, whether in a workplace (Fischer and Smith 2004, Lowry et al. 2015b), in a traditional physical community (Prilleltensky 2001), or regarding consumer behaviors (Mayser and von Wangenheim 2013, Lee et al. 2018). Relative to our context, consumers greatly value discounts or bargains as long as they are fairly given and earned (Darke and Dahl 2003). Second, fairness is one of the most important principles that free-to-play MOBA games strive to maintain. MOBA gamers compete directly against rivals, and each game has a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned ending, with a clear winner and loser. The competitive nature of MOBA games encourages game vendors to guarantee a fair in-game environment (Wu et al. 2016, Li et al. 2017).

## 2.3. Consumption Value Theory and Product Differentiation

Values, as enduring beliefs that a speci<sup>fi</sup>c mode of conduct is personally or socially preferable, provide a foundation for attitudes and guide behavior toward a future outcome (Rokeach 1973). CVT posits that a consumer’s decision to purchase is a function of multiple consumption values (Sheth et al. 1991). Consumption values re<sup>fl</sup>ect consumers’ overall assessments of the utility of a product or service based on their perceptions of what they give versus what they receive in return (Sheth et al. 1991). CVT posits that consumers form positive attitudes and preferences when the overall gain outweighs their cost/sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce and that these positive attitudes and preferences potentially motivate consumers’ future purchases (Sweeney and Soutar 2001).

Two additional propositions are axiomatic to CVT (Sheth et al. 1991, Sweeney and Soutar 2001, Turel et al. 2010). First, the types of consumption values and their relative importance vary by context, and thus each of these values makes a different contribution in speci<sup>fi</sup>c purchasing situations.<sup>4</sup> Second, the value components are not fully correlated, and the effects of multiple values on behavioral outcomes are partially additive (Sheth et al. 1991, Sweeney and Soutar 2001, Turel et al. 2010). Thus, CVT assumes that the effects of multiple value components must be considered concurrently as predictors of purchase behavior.

These propositions and associated assumptions inform our study’s contextualization of CVT to <sup>fi</sup>t the typical implementation context of free-to-play MOBA games, which are distinguished by two factors: (1) players compete directly against rivals in a gaming community, and (2) product-differentiation features are deployed to convert nonpaying players into paying players (Hamari et al. 2017a, Fang et al. 2019, Guo et al. 2019).

Following the contextualization guidelines developed by Hong et al. (2014), we contextualize CVT in three steps. In Step 1, we identify the context-relevant values and include them as the core of CVT. We incorporate three individual values—monetary, social, and enjoyment—and the key gaming community value of game fairness as the core CVT values. In Step 2, we identify distinct types of product differentiation as context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors for MOBA games. Finally, in Step 3, we model product differentiation as the direct antecedent of the core CVT values. In summary, we carefully contextualize CVT to incorporate product differentiation as the exogenous variable that in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer perceptions of value components in a MOBA game context.

Firms leveraging freemium business models often differentiate nonpaying consumers from premium ones through vertical and horizontal product differentiation (Jing 2016, Li et al. 2019). Unlike other subgenres of games (e.g., role-playing and social games), MOBA games do not provide prede<sup>fi</sup>ned plots and tasks. In MOBA games, each player controls a game character, and two teams of <sup>fi</sup>ve players compete against each other on one gaming map. Like e-sports, MOBA games are associated primarily with competition.<sup>5</sup> MOBA gamers thus care primarily about game characters, because such characters determine victory and the overall game experience. Thus, MOBA games differentiate nonpaying consumers from premium ones by distinguishing game characters.

Both vertical and horizontal differentiation inform the three types of product differentiation that are relevant in the free-to-play MOBA game platform–based market: character-competency differentiation, characterappearance differentiation, and character-variety differentiation. First, vertical product differentiation pertains to performance (or quality) differences between free and premium products (Caldieraro 2016, Choe et al. 2018), such as offering only a limited set of software features for free while requiring a fee for access to all features (Honhon and Pan 2017, Li et al. 2019). Although free and premium characters share some identical role skills and aspects of visual appearance, their inherent “quality” differs. Paid in-game items increase character strength and power to make game characters more competitive, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the essence of vertical product differentiation. Following Li et al. (2019) and Honhon and Pan (2017), we identify character-competency differentiation as a key form of vertical product differentiation in MOBA games. Character-competency differentiation refers to the extent to which a character’s functional attributes (e.g., health points, cooldown time, defense, attack damage) differ from those of the same character equipped with paid items.

Second, horizontal product differentiation represents the “feature” choices of product designs that satisfy heterogeneous customer preferences (Caldieraro 2016, Choe et al. 2018). Studies have viewed productvariety and product-appearance differentiation as important strategic tools <sup>fi</sup>rms use to horizontally differentiate themselves (Sajeesh and Raju 2010, Ren et al. 2011, Zeithammer and Thomadsen 2013, Qian et al. 2015). Some products are functionally better than others, whereas other kinds of product differentiation (e.g., color) are matters of individual taste. Unlike vertical differentiation, which focuses on quality differences, horizontal differentiation addresses consumer taste heterogeneity. Some MOBA gamers prefer to lead the attack, whereas others prefer to act as a guard in defending against opponents. Some want their characters to have an endearing appearance, whereas others prefer more menacing appearances. MOBA games thus provide game characters with a variety of appearances to satisfy these varied, horizontal preferences.

Based on horizontal product differentiation research, we introduce character-appearance and charactervariety differentiation to capture the nature of horizontal differentiation in free-to-play MOBA games. Characterappearance differentiation is the extent to which the variety of character “skins” available to free players differs from that available to paying players (Marder et al. 2019). Character-variety differentiation is the extent to which the set of characters available to free players differs from that available to paying players (Sajeesh and Raju 2010, Ren et al. 2011, Zeithammer and Thomadsen 2013).

CVT primarily emphasizes the positive effects of various value components but does not identify strategies <sup>fi</sup>rms may leverage to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer value perceptions. Studies have long suggested that the productdifferentiation strategies of the service vendor in<sup>fl</sup>uence the utility or value of products in freemium services (Hui et al. 2007, Lin et al. 2012, Cheng et al. 2015, Li et al. 2020a). In freemium services for products such as software, music, or videos, consumers can choose to pay for the premium service or to use the free service with either advertisements or limited functionality. Thus, the aim is for the perceived value of the premium membership to increase as the product differentiation (e.g., number of advertisements and degree of functional limitations in the free version) increases, such that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of premium membership outweigh its costs. If CVT holds in the context of free-to-play MOBA games, the product-differentiation strategy should indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uence in-game item purchase behavior through a player’s perceptions of multiple value components.

## 3. Research Model and Hypothesis Development

We contextualized our extension of CVT to free-to-play MOBA gaming using product-differentiation research. We thus develop a research model with hypotheses we can use to examine the determinants of purchase behavior in this context. First, we hypothesize that individual values—monetary, social, and enjoyment—and game fairness directly affect in-game purchase behavior (Hypotheses 1 and 2). We then examine the synergistic effect between individual values and game fairness (Hypothesis 3). Finally, we explain how product differentiation can create a con<sup>fl</sup>ict between an individual player’s values and game fairness (Hypotheses 4 and 5). Figure 1 depicts our model.

## 3.1. Integrated Individual Values and In-Game Item Purchasing

In our model, integrated individual values (IIVs) are operationalized as a second-order composite construct with three <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs: monetary, social, and enjoyment value. Monetary value is delivered if players perceive in-game items as cost-effective and reasonably priced (Park and Lee 2011). Typically, the more favorable the price of a digital item, the more likely players will be to purchase, because they believe purchasing the item is a wise use of their money (Kim et al. 2011). MOBA gamers also seek an enjoyable experience through the process of defeating rivals in an immersive environment. Paid items enhance the functional attributes of a game character and grant players in-game advantages, thereby increasing a player’s chances of experiencing joy from winning. Therefore, players who endorse a game’s enjoyment value are more likely to purchase in-game items.

Players may also buy in-game items to enhance their social image, because the purchase, display, and use of these items communicates a player’s symbolic meaning to others (Kim et al. 2011). For example, by purchasing in-game items, players can enhance the appearance of their game characters. The social identity value of such items relates to approval and selfimage improvement (Sweeney and Soutar 2001), which further in<sup>fl</sup>uence purchasing behavior. In summary, we predict the following.

Hypothesis 1. A player’s perceived integrated individual values of a MOBA game are positively related to the player’s in-game item purchasing.

## 3.2. Game Fairness and In-Game Item Purchasing

The most distinctive characteristic of MOBA games is competitiveness. Thus, game companies should strive to ensure a fair match between the two teams, because players expect fairness, as recent research has empirically established (Lebres et al. 2018, Rong et al. 2018, Font and Mahlmann 2019). Players are less attracted to games in which other players have inexplicable advantages. Hamari et al. (2017a) found that as a player’s perception of a game’s fairness decreases, the player is less willing to play the game and purchase in-game content. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. A player’s perceived game fairness is positively related to the player’s in-game item purchasing.

## 3.3. The Moderating Role of Game Fairness

We expect that integrated individual values and game fairness will generate synergistic effects in predicting players’ purchase behaviors. Like e-sports, MOBA games are associated primarily with competition (Mora-Cantallops and Sicilia 2018). Typical MOBA games, such as Arena of $V a l o r ^ { T M }$ and League of Legends<sup>TM</sup>, have professional leagues and tournaments. Game fairness is thus a key community value. Some MOBA games allow players to purchase functional items that grant in-game advantages. For instance, a more powerful sword offers the player better opportunities for defeating others. Yet, there is a growing disdain for the sale of functional items (Lin and Sun 2011, Rong et al. 2018, Marder et al. 2019). Although these items deliver great individual value, players are less likely to purchase them if they undermine game fairness (Li and Jain 2016). Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3. Game fairness positively moderates the relationship between integrated individual values and the player’s in-game item purchasing.

## 3.4. Product Differentiation and Perceived Value of Paid In-Game Items

In view of the role of game fairness and values in MOBA gamers’ purchasing, we now examine how game companies can better use the principles of prod uct differentiation to strengthen the various kinds of perceived values that MOBA gamers <sup>fi</sup>nd important. Doing so, however, involves a consideration of how such differentiation in<sup>fl</sup>uences fairness perceptions. A better empirical understanding of these causal links will enable us to provide clearer guidance on how product differentiation in MOBA games can strengthen in-game item purchase behavior. In this section, we thus address how character-competency, character-appearance, and character-variety differentiation can each in<sup>fl</sup>uence both integrated individual values and game fairness.

3.4.1. Differentiation and Values. We posit that in MOBA games, the more strongly in-game paid items foster character differentiation—based on (1) competency, (2) variety, and (3) appearance—the more such differentiation positively increases the integrated individual values of the paid items. Character-competency differentiation refers to the functional discrepancy between free and premium game characters. As character competency increases, premium game characters equipped with paid in-game items become more competitive and more likely to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the players’ need for winning and enjoyment, thus increasing their enjoyment values. This increase in competitiveness also helps players gain prominence among their peers and glean more social value. Thus, paid in-game items that can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve character competency tend to be regarded as cost-effective (Kim et al. 2011, Rezaei and Ghodsi 2014, Teng 2018). Conversely, if the functional attributes of characters equipped with paid in-game items are not distinct from those of free characters, users are more likely to feel the in-game items have poor monetary value. Thus, character-competency differentiation increases monetary value and enhances all three individual values.

Character-appearance differentiation is the extent to which the variety of character skins available to free players differs from that available to paying players such that paying players have access to a greater variety of character skins (Marder et al. 2019). Players often enhance the appearance of in-game characters using paid items (i.e., skins), enhancing the variety of character appearance for fun, thus increasing their enjoyment value. The increase in character-appearance differentiation may elevate in-game items’ monetary value if paid players have more skins from which to choose. This differentiation also plays a role in building players’ social identity value. Paid players often display the skins of their in-game characters on their personal page. The more skins they display, the higher the likelihood that their personal pages will attract attention from others. Paid in-game items thus help players develop relationships and improve their self-image and social identity, thus fostering social identity value.

Character-variety differentiation is the extent to which the set of characters available to free players differs from that available to paying players (Sajeesh and Raju 2010, Ren et al. 2011, Zeithammer and Thomadsen 2013). In most MOBA games, each character has different attributes and abilities and diverse ways to evolve. Because MOBA games typically feature small-team battles, the tactics for each matchup with competing teams grow exponentially with the increase in the total number of available game characters (Chen et al. 2018). As this distinction increases, premium players can explore more tactics and possibilities, increasing enjoyment value. Players are also apt to perceive the premium service as reasonably priced as this differentiation increases, leading to monetary value. Rare premium game characters can also improve players’ chances of being recognized among peers, thus providing social identity value (Marder et al. 2019). Thus, the stronger the character differentiation a MOBA game provides, the more enjoyment, monetary, and social identity values a player will perceive.

Hypothesis 4. A player’s perception of a game’s (a) character-competency, (b) character-variety, and (c) characterappearance differentiation are positively related to the player’s perception of the integrated individual values of the in-game paid items.

3.4.2. Differentiation and Fairness. Again, in-game item purchases create a conundrum for gaming companies: although these items may increase a player’s perceived integrated individual values, they can create a con<sup>fl</sup>icting perception of lack of fairness among other players. Game fairness is the concept according to which all players should be treated equally, and players’ results or rewards should match their performance (Wu et al. 2010, Lin and Sun 2011, Wu et al. 2016, Rong et al. 2018). As character-competency differentiation increases, so does the likelihood that premium characters equipped with paid in-game items will win. When this occurs, players may feel the game is unfair because winning will increasingly be based on wealth instead of skill. The increase of charactervariety differentiation and character-appearance differentiation suggests that more game characters and skins of game characters are available to paid players. Players may view the in-game resource allocation as a biased and inequitable distribution, which undermines the perception of fairness (Choi and Chen 2007, Ferguson et al. 2014). The use of product differentiation by MOBA games—allowing players to enhance character competency, alter character appearance, and use different characters by purchasing in-game items (Guo and Barnes 2009, Park and Lee 2011)—can thus make other players feel they have been placed at an unfair disadvantage, especially when they are trying to compete in good faith, that is, based on skill (Lin and Sun 2011).

Hypothesis 5. A player’s perceptions of a game’s (a) character-competency, (b) character-variety, and (c) characterappearance differentiation are negatively related to the player’s perception of game fairness.

## 4. Methodology

To test our research model, we <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted a study (Study 1) based on a cross-sectional design, which consisted of a main experiment followed by two supplemental experiments for a robustness check. The three cross-sectional experiments in Study 1 measured purchase intention (PI) as the proxy for actual purchase behaviors; because of legal and privacy restrictions, we were unable to collaborate with the gaming company to track actual in-game purchase behaviors. To further establish the validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from Study 1, we performed Study 2, a two-wave longitudinal experiment in which players’ actual purchase behaviors were observed. In Sections 4 and 5, we report the design, procedures, and results of Study 1. Sections 6 and 7 detail the additional information and results of Study 2.

## 4.1. Arena of Valor

We studied the MOBA game Arena of Valor, using active online players of the game for both studies, because it is the most popular and pro<sup>fi</sup>table mobile MOBA game worldwide (Suckley 2018). In typical MOBA games, two teams of <sup>fi</sup>ve players each compete against each other on one gaming map, beginning at opposite corners of the map (Figure 2). More than 50 characters exist in Arena of Valor, and each has different attributes and abilities that can evolve in diverse ways, substantially increasing the complexity of gaming possibilities.<sup>6</sup>

## 4.2. Study 1 Research Design

We adopted a scenario-based experiment that combines the methodological advantages of <sup>fi</sup>eld surveys and laboratory experiments (Lowry et al. 2013, Choi et al. 2016, Lowry et al. 2017). We manipulated product differentiation in game character competency, variety, and appearance through eight scenarios and then distributed an online questionnaire that measured value perception and purchase intention in each scenario. We deployed the experiment over the online survey platform operated by Sojump, a professional Chinese survey tool widely adopted for China-based studies (Sun et al. 2013, Shen et al. 2018). The platform enabled us to do the following: (1) implement manipulations using pictures of game characters along with a text description of the scenarios and (2) perform a truly random assignment of survey subjects.

## 4.3. Study 1 Procedures

The experimental procedures of Study 1 consisted of three stages. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, participants were invited to the study and provided with the institutionally required disclaimers, high-level study instructions, and the opportunity to provide informed consent to participate in the study. The study was approved for human subjects by the institutional review board of the lead author’s university. All participants agreed to the terms and fully participated with informed consent. They were noti<sup>fi</sup>ed that participation was voluntary and that only aggregate data containing no personally identi<sup>fi</sup>- able information would be used. Those who provided valid responses were encouraged to join a drawing where 50 winners were given a ¥35 (\$5) cash reward.

In the second stage, we asked the participants—all of whom were active players of Arena of Valor—for their demographic information and to respond to the preexperimental measure of their initial purchase intentions toward in-game items. This gave us a point of comparison for their postexperimental purchase intentions based on the manipulations they were given, further enabling us to establish the experimental effects. Finally, in the third stage, the participants were given speci<sup>fi</sup>c randomized experimental manipulations and asked to provide postexperimental survey responses to measure the effects of these manipulations.

## 4.4. Experimental Manipulations

For each category of differentiation, we developed “high” and “low” conditions consistent with a $2 \times 2 \times$ 2 experimental design with a fully randomized and crossed-between-subjects arrangement. The categories that were manipulated were the three differentiation strategies: (1) character competency (high/low), (2) character appearance (high/low), and (3) character variety (high/low). See Online Appendix C.

To increase the realism and potential ecological validity of our experiment, we made the crucial design decision to carefully follow Arena of Valor’s current approach to product differentiation as the baseline for our experimental manipulation—particularly for the character manipulations. To do so, we manipulated the high and low levels of product differentiation (i.e., character competency, variety, and appearance) by adjusting the differentiation from the baseline currently used in Arena of Valor. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the scenarios were manipulated by increasing or decreasing the differentiation by 25% from the current baseline to achieve the high and low conditions, respectively. Manipulations were operationalized through messages informing respondents that Arena of Valor had just released an update regarding the character distinction between free and premium players. In each scenario, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c details of the update were pictured for our respondents.

## 4.5. Pilot Testing and Final Participant Sampling and Procedures

Three pilot studies were used to contextualize, improve, and validate the manipulations and instrumentation used in this study.<sup>8</sup> To collect the <sup>fi</sup>nal data, we posted a link to the experiment on several Arena of Valor forums to solicit participation.<sup>9</sup> Upon visiting these forum, users either encountered a link to the study on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page or were given a pop-up message requesting their participation in the study with a link. ## 4.6. Questionnaire Development and Measures

Our measures were grouped into two survey parts. The <sup>fi</sup>rst was intended to measure players’ perceptions of Arena of Valor, which were not speci<sup>fi</sup>c to any scenario. In the second, respondents were presented with one of the eight scenarios and then with measures speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the scenario, and respondents were asked for their demographics. The second part also requested the respondents’ demographics. To ensure construct validity, we used items from existing scales whenever possible. All survey items were measured on a seven-point Likert-type scale, from one (strongly disagree) to seven (strongly agree). All measures were based on established measures in the literature and are carefully detailed in Online Appendix B,<sup>12</sup> and they were carefully translated and back-translated.<sup>13</sup> We controlled for age and gender, because these variables are key in<sup>fl</sup>uencers of consumer purchasing decisions (Chang and Cheng 2015, Law et al. 2016). We also controlled for player income, because disposable income is a key predictor of in-game item purchase behavior. In view of the <sup>fi</sup>nding by Guo and Barnes (2009) that experienced gamers were more likely than rookie gamers to purchase in-game items, we also controlled for game experience and hours spent playing per week.

## 5.3. Robustness Checks on the Manipulations Using Two Supplementary Experiments

We followed Arena of Valor’s current product-differentiation approach as the baseline for our experimental design, especially for the character manipulations. We then analyzed the same model using CB-SEM, but this time with the two new sets of data, and the results were consistent with those of our main experiment (Figures A.4 and A.5 in Online Appendix A). This is a notable robustness test given that both new experiments had much lower statistical power than the highly powered main experiment.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst checked the key identifying assumption of DID estimation: that the treatment and control groups exhibit a common trend in the absence of treatment (Meyer 1995). We thus compared the mean differences in four dependent variables (purchase behavior, purchase amount, integrated individual val ues, and game fairness) between the treatment and control groups in the pretreatment period (<sup>fi</sup>rst wave). ## 8. Discussion

In this research, we adapt CVT to the context of MOBA games to better understand how to convert free consumers into paying ones using productdifferentiation strategies while considering consumers’ values and perceptions of fairness. To establish an optimal <sup>fi</sup>t with the research context, we examine the effect of both individual values and game fairness on players’ purchase behavior. We empirically validate our dual-path model using data from a two-wave longitudinal experiment and three cross-sectional experiments. Our results largely support our theorization and thus have important implications for research, theory, and practice.

## 8.1. Summarizing Findings in Terms of Our Study’s Motivating Research Question

Our study is designed to answer a high-level research question: How do the considerations of product differentiation, consumer values, and fairness contribute to converting nonpaying players into paying players in a free-to-play MOBA game context? Our results suggest that using product differentiation with free players may have different effects on in-game purchase behaviors and that the effects depend on the type of product differentiation. In a MOBA game context, game characters are pivotal to the players’ expe rience; thus, we focused on product differentiation through the primary ways characters are manipulated. We show that all three types of product differentiation have strong positive indirect effects on in-game purchase behaviors. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that characterappearance differentiation has a positive indirect effect on in-game purchase behaviors through the mediators of perceived values that matter in a MOBA game context (i.e., monetary, social, and enjoyment value). Both character-competency and charactervariety differentiation not only exert an indirect effect through the mediator of perceived values but also have a direct inverted-U-shaped relationship with in-game purchase behaviors.

Beyond the perceived value mediators, another interesting factor associated with character-based product differentiation is perceived fairness, which also plays a key mediation role. But unlike the three key values, perceived fairness plays a contrarian role in that MOBA gamers universally want a fair gaming environment and become displeased if certain players can wield advantages by buying “super characters.” Thus, we show a strong positive statistical relationship between fairness and in-game purchase behaviors;

however, character differentiation through competency and differentiation through variety statistically diminish perceptions of fairness. However, players did not feel the same way about character differentiation through appearance—which is consistent with the fact that changes in appearance do not yield extra capabilities.

By considering both consumers’ values and their sense of fairness, when it comes to product differentiation through character, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm a dualpath mediation model with two contrasting factors; that is, the integrated individual values and game fairness play competing roles in mediating the relationships between product-differentiation strategies and in-game purchase behavior. Although both integrated individual values and game fairness facilitate in-game purchases, they have opposite mediation effects, such that product-differentiation strategies are found to enhance integrated individual values but undermine game fairness.

Moreover, in comparing the direct and mediated paths, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the two mediated paths through individual values and game fairness belong to distinct types of partial mediation. Integrated individual values complementarily mediate the relationship, such that the mediation reinforces the direct relationship of the three product-differentiation strategies. By contrast, game fairness competitively mediates the relationship, such that the mediation counteracts the direct effect of character-competency and character-variety differentiation. Because of the competitive nature of the mediation in game fairness, the overall effects of product differentiation in character competency and character variety on purchase behavior depend on the degree to which these two types of product differentiation compromise community fairness.

Finally, our data do not support a mediating role of game fairness for character-appearance differentiation; that is, the link between character-appearance differentiation and game fairness is nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant (Hypothesis 5(c)). A possible explanation for this is that the perception of game fairness depends on a given situation (Lin and Sun 2011). Some players may consider it acceptable for paying players to enjoy advantages from nonfunctional attributes but unfair for them to glean too much advantage from functional attributes. Because character-appearance differentiation provides no functional advantages and makes no difference in the outcomes of in-game competitions, it exerts little in<sup>fl</sup>uence on players’ perception of game fairness.

## 8.2. Contributions to Research and Theory

Our study makes several potential theoretical contributions to the broader literature. First, previous studies relying on CVT to investigate purchase behaviors have focused largely on various individual values (Kim et al. 2011, Park and Lee 2011, Porter et al. 2013, Chiu et al. 2014, Rietveld 2018). We extend the CVT literature by integrating product-differentiation strategies and juxtaposing individual values and game fairness as two distinct sets of mediators. No prior study has investigated purchase behavior in platformbased markets as the result of competing mediation from individual values and fairness. We con<sup>fi</sup>rm the existence of dual-path mediation, providing key insights into the mechanisms through which product differentiation in<sup>fl</sup>uences in-game purchases. Although both individual values and game fairness enhance in-game purchase behavior, they exert opposite mediating effects. Game fairness is a negative mediator that diminishes the positive effect of product differentiation intended by game vendors. Our proposed model thus opens a new venue for future research on purchase behavior in platform-based markets. Moreover, it is important for future studies applying CVT to separate the effect of individual values from that of the community value in question and jointly examine them to avoid con<sup>fl</sup>icting <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding consumer value perceptions.

Second, although product differentiation is widely adopted by companies employing freemium platformbased business models, exactly how it bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in-game purchase behavior remains debatable (Wagner et al. 2014, Koch and Benlian 2017, Choe et al. 2018, Rietveld 2018, Shi et al. 2019). Considering the inherent characteristics of free-to-play MOBA games, we shed new light on this debate by identifying three types of prod uct differentiation that are important in the MOBA game context. These distinct kinds of product differentiation are found to have a nonuniform in<sup>fl</sup>uence on purchase behavior. The forms of differentiation related to functional attributes (i.e., character competency and character variety) are found to exert both a positive and negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on purchase behavior, whereas character-appearance differentiation is found to in<sup>fl</sup>u ence purchase behavior only positively.

We also newly reveal two key paths by which differentiation in functional attributes in<sup>fl</sup>uences in-game purchase behavior. Vendors can both promote purchase behaviors directly and cultivate individual values. Differ entiation in functional attributes can hinder purchasing in two ways: via the mediated path through game fairness and through the direct, inverted-U-shaped quad ratic path. The former results from the erosion of game fairness, whereas the latter may arise from undermining the competitive nature of MOBA games. Our research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to unveil the underlying mechanisms of the two paths related to product differentiation.

## 8.3 Implications for Practice

If our <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold, this study can inform free-to-play MOBA game vendors regarding strategies for generating pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from product differentiation. Likewise, the results point to important design considerations and trade-offs, especially when it comes to balancing the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of differentiation on consumers’ values and its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on their perceptions of fairness.

First, the positive relationship between characterappearance differentiation and in-game purchases suggests a strategic opportunity to increase pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from the sale of decorative or nonfunctional items. To cater to players’ foundational psychological need for novelty and self-identity, we encourage MOBA game vendors to diversify the categories of decorative items and launch new, nonfunctional items frequently. A key part of this opportunity is to do so periodically and perhaps to provide limited functionality for a brief period (e.g., for holidays or special events), such that novelty is sustained. Periodic and unpredictable offerings could provide novel ways for players to address their ego and self-identity needs—similarly to how clothing companies must continually change their offerings to appeal to the latest trends. Recent research on intertemporal demand spillover effects found in video game platforms can reinforce the importance of timing product differentiation to stoke demand (Haviv et al. 2020). Nevertheless, game companies need to be cautious of the potential boundary conditions of charactercompetency and character-variety differentiation. If these types of differentiation move into territory perceived as unfair, a further increase in charactercompetency and character-variety differentiation could negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence purchase behaviors.

Second, it is imperative for practitioners to think of their platform-based business models in terms of a dynamic negotiation between free players, paying players, and game vendors. The key is optimizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to all three while minimizing the costs. Using character-variety and character-competency differentiation may please paying players, because it will give them considerable advantages over free players. Conversely, character-variety and character-competency differentiation will discourage free players as they struggle for equal opportunity to compete in the game. Many free players can push games to the top (or near the top) of ranking lists and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the game’s visibility, whereas paying players help generate pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for game vendors. Thus, there is likely a threshold beyond which competency differentiation and variety differentiation begin to undermine in-game purchases. Free-to-play MOBA game companies should be aware of this threshold and carefully consider how much differentiation in variety and competency between free and paying game characters can sustainably exist.

Third, this study highlights the potential effects of multiple individual values and game fairness on in-game purchase behavior. Consistent with previous related studies (Kim et al. 2011, Rezaei and Ghodsi 2014, Goncalves et al. 2016, Teng 2018), our study provides empirical support for the argument that monetary and enjoyment value are important factors in predicting the purchase behavior of free-to-play MOBA gamers. Game vendors can thus potentially boost in-game consumption by delivering monetary and enjoyment values. Contrary to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Rezaei and Ghodsi (2014), our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role of social identity value in in-game purchase behavior. Playing games is viewed not only as a way to pass time and a form of escapism, but also as an increasingly important means of socializing (Hamari and Keronen 2017). This is particularly true for MOBA games because they are team based. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that game vendors should develop the social identity value of paid in-game items. The positive role of game fairness should encourage game companies to infuse competitive game environments with a sense of fairness. For example, character growth, resource allocation, and speed of response to consumer complaints should likely be equal for free and paying players.

Fourth, because the character differentiation proposed in this study can be mapped to quality and taste differentiation, our results can likely be extended to general freemium services that rely on product differentiation to generate revenue. Marketers typically regard products as being differentiated by quality, such that a premium service/product is objectively better, or taste, such that a premium service/product is a better match with a consumer’s preferences (Zeithammer and Thomadsen 2013, Spiller and Belogolova 2017). In our study, character-competency differentiation corresponds to quality differentiation, whereas character-variety and character-appearance differentiation correspond to taste differentiation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that extending the magnitude of taste differentiation is an effective differentiation strategy; however, an upper limit should be set on quality differentiation, because excessive quality differentiation can undermine fairness perceptions, which can inhibit the freemium service’s revenue generation.

For instance, online video applications that adopt a freemium business model allow premium users to skip advertisements. To move free users to premium ones, online video applications increase the duration of the advertisements. For example, YouKou (YOKU on the New York Stock Exchange), a leading online video application in China, started to extend the duration of its advertisements from 30 seconds to 90 seconds during July 2017, and the duration keeps increasing. Although the ad duration does not affect functionality of the actual video, it makes accessing the video content more cumbersome and thus the ads become a part of (or roadblock to) the desired content, which can undermine the perceived quality of the content versus ad-free videos. Thus, expanding the extent of quality differentiation between freemium and premium services may undermine consumers fairness perceptions, which can hinder future <sup>fi</sup>rm revenue (Spiller and Belogolova 2017).

## 8.4. Limitations and Future Research Opportunities

Although this study offers new insights into what drives MOBA gamers’ in-game purchase behavior, it has several limitations, which highlight avenues for compelling future research. First, the inverted-Ushaped relationship between character-competency differentiation and in-game purchase behavior suggests that there is a threshold beyond which character-competency differentiation begins to undermine in-game purchase behavior. However, our study is not designed to identify the specific point at which the marginal contribution of character-competency differentiation decreased to zero. We believe such marginal analysis is a compelling research opportunity that would help game vendors establish sound upper limits on the functional attributes that can be enhanced by equipping characters with paid items. This would help practitioners address the triadic problem of optimizing the competing interests of free players, paying players, and the vendors themselves. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that excessive differentiation in functional attributes may lead to an unexpected backlash from players against the purchase of in-game items. Future studies should thus investigate the effect of product-differentiation strategies that separate functional attributes, which directly contribute to game performance, from nonfunctional attributes. Moreover, aside from character differentiation operationalized through visual attributes, future research could explore other differentiation approaches, such as those based on game characters’ audio features. Because this study examines the roles of three kinds of characterbased differentiation, caution is warranted before generalizing our conclusions to subgenres of games that do not rely as heavily on characters. Here, we believe that leading information systems research related to aesthetics, design artifacts, engagement, and affordances in gami<sup>fi</sup>ed systems can be especially helpful to expand research related to MOBA gaming design and differentiation (Liu et al. 2017, Suh et al. 2017, Lowry et al. 2020).

Second, we are inspired by a recently proposed theoretical model that examines the use of online platforms to explore new consumer products (Hagiu and Wright 2020). This study suggests several intriguing research directions related to platforms to apply to MOBA games research. For example, the authors theorized deeply about the effects of product experimentation with existing customers on attracting new ones, and they concluded that online platforms do not generally use this strategic lever suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently to their advantage. This bears special consideration for the MOBA game platform market, because better experimentation with product differentiation with existing paying players may help convert more nonpaying players into paying players. We recommend an expansion of this line of research into our unique platform context.

Third, many strategic considerations that have been applied to other consumer platform-based markets have not been considered for the MOBA game platform market, and they fall outside the scope of the present study. Nonetheless, such considerations are vital, especially given the intense competitiveness of the MOBA game market and the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of converting free players into paying players. The selective use of complements is a strategic lever that future research could consider. For example, the selective promotion of traditional console video games by complementors (i.e., video game console companies) has been shown to increase sales of video games in that market (Rietveld et al. 2019). The issue for MOBA game platforms is to <sup>fi</sup>nd appropriate complementors, especially because traditional console game providers are often hostile to this market. Given the social nature of MOBA games, complementors may be found through social media platforms. In such instances of strong complementary <sup>fi</sup>t, there is also an opportunity for platform integration to leverage the spillovers in com plementary markets, as aptly addressed by Li et al. (2017). We also commend the work of Constantinides et al. (2018) for identifying potential sources of inspiration for complementors and for developing other interesting research ideas related to underlying platform architectural and governance decisions that are seldom considered in our research context.

Fourth, our model was tested in the context of freeto-play MOBA games, which makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to generalize our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other game subgenres, especially in terms of the values that may be most impor tant. Players of different major online gaming genres (e.g., action, battle arena, sports, action adventure, role playing, adventure, racing, <sup>fi</sup>ghting, real-time strategy, simulation) and formats (time-based versus item based games) may harbor different motivations and associated values for in-game purchases. For example, compared with MOBA games, role-playing and social games are generally less competitive (Lin and Sun 2011, Wu et al. 2016, Mora-Cantallops and Sicilia 2018), which may counter the effect of fairness on purchase behavior. Moreover, we do not address how to motivate repurchase or continuance among paying players of MOBA games. Hence, future research could further examine the motivations of players on a variety of game platforms, showing similarities and differences.

Fifth, the distinct in<sup>fl</sup>uencing mechanisms of differentiation in functional versus nonfunctional attributes may also be extrapolated to nongaming businesses operating on platform-based freemium models, which have yet to be validated by future studies. It is important also to recognize that there are other kinds of freemium business models with various strategies in converting consumers to “fee,” such as cross-selling to other products (e.g., join.me, Solarwinds) or, in some cases, <sup>fi</sup>nding revenues outside of the immediate consumers (e.g., Google apps, Google search). Thus, differentiation needs to be further studied in such unique freemium contexts.

Sixth, this study does not directly address the effects of interpersonal and contextual differences, such as culture, gender, gaming ability, and the need for selfidentity or ego. Understanding the variance of in-game purchase decisions that derive from such interpersonal differences would be especially enlightening. It is particularly important to emphasize that these issues are likely multilevel in nature. For example, it is possible that cultural considerations could be infused into future research in the following ways, which could lead to several interaction effects: (1) national culture (e.g., China versus the United States versus Europe versus the Middle East), (2) game environment culture (e.g., aggressive and competitive versus cooperative and peaceful), (3) team culture (e.g., the culture of longstanding teams versus the culture of newly formed teams of strangers), and (4) individual-level culture (one’s personal cultural dispositions regarding collectivism versus individualism, masculinity versus femininity, uncertainty avoidance, risk taking, and so on; cf. Srite and Karahanna 2006, Taras et al. 2010, Lowry et al. 2011).

Finally, we examine only variables that have no effect or a negative effect on game fairness. It is thus unclear how increasing game fairness would in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer purchasing in the MOBA gaming context. Future studies could explore other antecedents of fairness and the role of elevated fairness in consumer purchasing. Moreover, fairness is a multidimensional concept that is applied in several different contexts from organizations, to groups, to consumers (Ho and Su 2009, Ferguson et al. 2014, Lowry et al. 2015b). Our measures capture different types of fairness, such as ability-oriented fairness and investment-oriented fairness. Different types of fairness could lead to different behaviors toward the purchase (Hong et al. 2017, Lee et al. 2018). Player characteristics may interact with different types of fairness. For example, nonpaying players might put more weight on ability-oriented fairness, whereas paying players may care more about investment-oriented fairness. We call on future stud ies to differentiate different types of fairness and clarify their distinct roles in predicting in-game purchase.

## 9. Conclusion

Integrating the product-differentiation literature and CVT, this research clari<sup>fi</sup>es the underlying mechanism through which product differentiation in<sup>fl</sup>uences in-game purchase behavior in a MOBA game context. Integrated individual values and game fairness are found to play competing roles in mediating the relationships between product-differentiation constructs and in-game purchase behavior. Both integrated individual values and game fairness exert positive effects on in-game purchases. Character-competency and character-variety differentiation facilitate integrated individual values but undermine game fairness. Character-appearance differentiation positively predicts integrated individual values, and its effect on game fairness is nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. This research provides a foundation for future research exploring how product differentiation in<sup>fl</sup>uences in-game purchases and for measuring the optimal magnitude of product differentiation.
