Abstract. Humans are increasingly interacting in and operating their daily lives through structured digital and virtual environments, mainly through apps that provide media for sharing photos, messaging, gaming, collaborating, or video watching. Most of these digital environments are offered under “freemium” pricing to facilitate adoption and network effects. In these settings, users’ early social interaction and experience often have a substan tial impact on their longer term behavior. On this background, we study the impact of an algorithmic system that matches new users to existing communities in an assortative manner. We devise a machine learning–based matching system that identi<sup>fi</sup>es users with high expected value and provides them the option to join highly active, in terms of engagement and expenditure, teams. We deploy this mechanism experimentally in a digital social game and <sup>fi</sup>nd that it signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases user engagement, spending, and socialization. This <sup>fi</sup>nding holds for more active communities and overall. Teams matched with low-activity new users are negatively impacted, leading to an overall more segregated social environ ment. We argue that social experience and social behavior in groups are likely mechanism that drive the impact of the matching system.

## 1. Introduction

Humans are increasingly interacting in and operating their daily lives through structured digital and virtual environments. It is estimated that they spend 20% of awake time on mobile devices. In 2020, there were almost 218 billion mobile app downloads worldwide, and spending in app stores amounted to \$143 billion (AppAnnie 2021). The freemium (free premium) pricing model is an important driver of this development. Under such pricing, apps can be downloaded and used free of charge, and app providers generate revenue from in-app purchases and advertisement. Many of the digital ecosystems created by these applications (e.g., social media, messaging, collaborating, dating, or gaming) provide a wide range of socialization functionalities (Alsen et al. 2016, Kwon et al. 2016). Indeed, social applications, especially gaming apps, account for most of the revenue and time spent on apps (AppAnnie 2021). This is not surprising as existing literature has already established social experiences as crucial to engagement and revenue generation in online freemium settings (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Kwon et al. 2016, Bapna et al. 2018).

Without entry barriers for new users, such as an up-front fee or price, successful freemium apps receive a large in<sup>fl</sup>ow of consumers. These users decide whether they should continue using the app or not based on their <sup>fi</sup>rst experiences in the environment, and only a small proportion remain active users. Most adopters use the app only for a few minutes and only for a couple of days: 1- and 30-day user retention rates are most often below 40% and 10%, respectively (Sifa et al. 2018, GameAnalytics 2019). Given this large in<sup>fl</sup>ow of users with highly heterogeneous valuations of the app and its premium upgrades (Sifa et al. 2018), an essential decision <sup>fi</sup>rms face is how to match these users into existing communities in order to maximize engagement, retention, community well-being, and expenditure. A successful matching method would heavily depend on the underlying mechanism that characterizes users and communities, that is, whether they exhibit complementarity or substitution effects in how peers affect each other’s engagement and spending. The underlying mechanism determines whether positive or negative assortative matching should be adopted to drive positive net impacts. Ex ante, it seems both viable to match users with high expected value into communities with low or high expected engagement and monetization to leverage positive effects they are bound to exert on peers’ premium purchasing (Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Bapna et al. 2018).

The main thrust of the present paper is to investigate the relative merit of either approach and understand the underlying mechanism of users’ cocreation of value to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Based on preliminary analysis of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c freemium environment that hosts our study, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the environment to have a potentially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant complementary nature. Thus, we devise an analytics-driven system that facilitates positive assortative matching of newly arriving users into existing communities. We take this system to a large-scale <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in a social gaming app to estimate the system’s causal effects on outcomes relevant for the management of online communities as well as to characterize and understand the underlying mechanisms that drive such outcomes.

The <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment indicates that a positive assortative matching system leads to net positive impact on engagement and other output measures. In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that new users who join active communities exhibit higher willingness to play and spend time with longer retention rates and spend more money. Teams matched with high-quality new users experience positive effects on engagement and revenue, whereas low-quality teams receiving low-quality new users are negatively affected. In line with the assertion of potentially high complementarities in the environment, the overall team-level net effect is positive when moving one new player from random to assortative matching.

The matching system (that results in recomposition of teams) generates positive net effects on engagement and revenue because the underlying production technology exhibits supermodularity, and the supermodularity is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally driven by increasing returns to scale at the team-level. This increasing-returns-to-scale characteristic is particularly valuable in a freemium environment in which the zero barrier to entry allows a larger user base and, hence, larger teams (i.e., higher return) relative to pricing mechanisms that charge an up-front fee. We must also note that social behavior (messaging) displays stronger effects than other outcomes in our reduced-form regressions, and the degree of supermodularity in messaging is much stronger than for the other outcomes. When matching players based solely on teams’ socialization score, we observe positive effects on all engagement metrics. We further conducted a mediation analysis with social behavior (messaging) as the mediator of the impact of the system on the other outcomes. Our results show that users’ messaging does seem to be a mediator. We believe these pieces of evidence strongly suggest that socialization as an intermediate outcome is channeling the impact of the new matching system to other outcomes.

The paper’s main contributions are twofold. First, it adds to the study of social and peer effects in the monetization of freemium offerings (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Bapna et al. 2018) by characterizing the underlying production technology and by identifying causal effects using a <sup>fi</sup>eld experimental approach. Second, it contributes to literature on assortative matching (Becker 1973) by studying user–group rather than user–user matches (Hitsch et al. 2010) and, to our knowledge, being the <sup>fi</sup>rst large-scale study in a real-world digital environment inducing exogenous variation in assortativity. Finally, we believe the present study also speaks to literature studying the consumption and production of social media (Levy 2021) by pinpointing how tacit algorithmic facilitation of assortativity (Carrell et al. 2013) can fuel polarization on social media (Bail et al. 2018; here deepening the divide between engaged and disenchanted social groups). On a substantive level, as freemium pricing and analytics-driven product management continue to proliferate in the sale of digital and virtual consumer goods, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings bear relevance for the design of digital marketplaces. Importantly, they indicate that the use of algorithmically facilitated assortativity can increase aggregate community engagement and <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts but have detrimental effects on marginal parts of the user population. In the authors’ view, the latter result raises ethical and societal concerns around purely private and pro<sup>fi</sup>tcentered management of, and is highly relevant in terms of algorithmic transparency on, digital social media (Chouldechova and Roth 2020).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides a review of related literature, and Section 3 presents the empirical environment. Section 4 introduces the analytics-driven matching system, and Section 5 lays out the experiment design and deployment, outcomes of interest, and hypotheses. Section 6 discusses the experimental results and underlying mechanisms. Section 7 concludes with a discussion of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. In the main body of the text, we use high-value (lowvalue) player and likely (unlikely) payer interchangeably to refer to new user classi<sup>fi</sup>cations.

## 2. Related Literature

Our paper stems from and contributes to two areas of research: the economics literature on assortative matching and research on social–digital environments operated under freemium pricing, including demand prediction in such settings.

The economic research on assortative matching starts with Becker (1973), who <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced the concept in his seminal analysis of the marriage market. He shows that assortative matching is ef<sup>fi</sup>cient if utility is transferable and technology is complementary. Subsequent theoretical work focuses on identifying suf<sup>fi</sup>cient conditions for assortative matching in a broader range of settings (Kremer 1993, Shimer and Smith 2000, Durlauf and Seshadri 2003, Legros and Newman 2007, Ahlin 2017, Chai et al. 2021). For example, Kremer (1993) proposes a model in which technology induces workers to sort into <sup>fi</sup>rms with individuals of the same skill level in equilibrium. Because of the complementarities between workers’ skill levels, <sup>fi</sup>rms with the highest quality workers place the most value on skilled workers and bid more for them. The empirical evidence supports this theory (Mendes et al. 2010, Torres et al. 2018).<sup>1</sup>

The literature also discusses the effects of assortative matching, focusing on team production compared with random or alternative forms of matching. The evidence from laboratory experiments mainly focuses on social dilemmas. The main <sup>fi</sup>nding in these studies is that assortative matching signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the average contribution of players (Page et al. 2005, Gunnthorsdottir et al. 2007). The evidence in these papers suggests that this higher production emerges from conditional cooperators being willing to maintain higher contributions in assortative matching settings relative to nonassortative ones as they are less often discouraged by interactions with free riders. This rationale very much underlies the analyticsdriven system that we propose and evaluate in this study and helps clarify how and why <sup>fi</sup>rms offering social media have incentives to facilitate user assortativity along various dimensions (Bail et al. 2018, Allcott et al. 2020, Levy 2021)

Whereas there is an abundance of studies of assortative matching using data from different kinds of markets and from the laboratory, a paucity of investigation on digital platforms and marketplaces remains. A notable exception is Hitsch et al. (2010), who use data from an online dating site to study sorting in online dating and marriage. They estimate the relative importance of individual characteristics in matching outcomes and <sup>fi</sup>nd that the digital platform exhibits patterns that resemble analog marriages, including assortative matching. They also study how much of said assortative matching can be attributed to preferences (relevant on both the dating site and in real life) and how much to search frictions (negligible on the dating site). Their results suggest preferences are an important cause, but that search frictions are a further key driver of assortative matching. Similarly, Ahmadpoor and Jones (2019) use digital records of academic coproduction to study team production and characterize production technology. Their work is particularly relevant for our paper. The authors propose a production function that allows decomposition of team output into the individual contributions of each member and apply it to the creation of knowledge in research teams, emphasizing the role of strategic complementarities and supermodularity in production technology, <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced by Topkis (1998).

Vis-a-vis this literature, the present paper studies\` user–group rather than user–user matching in a digital setting through tacit manipulation of the list of available teams recommended to newly arriving users. This approach leaves the user-side experience of freedom of choice and organic team formation untouched (Carrell et al. 2013) and holds search frictions constant (Hitsch et al. 2010). We then build on Ahmadpoor and Jones’ (2019) estimation approach to characterize production technology and mechanisms that help explain the impact of assortative matching in the studied setting. We believe that the speci<sup>fi</sup>c way we induce assortativity experimentally is further interesting to literature on the consumption and production of social media as it may represent a key algorithmic driver behind increasing levels of polarization reported across social media (Bail et al. 2018, Allcott et al. 2020, Levy 2021).

The second branch of literature, to which our study contributes, studies social digital environments. Similar to other social media that allow socialization of text, photos, video watching, and other activities, mobile applications for gaming often constitute social spaces that facilitate shared gameplay with friends, family, and also strangers (Alsen et al. 2016, Supercell 2018, Runge et al. 2022).

Most mobile apps with social components offer a free base version that can be enhanced via in-app purchases (or are monetized via advertising). This freemium pricing model is enabled by the near-zero marginal cost for production and distribution of digital goods (Niculescu and Wu 2014). It promises critical strategic advantages to <sup>fi</sup>rms in facilitating network effects and minimizing customer acquisition cost (Dou et al. 2013). It also poses novel problems; for example, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to decide how to deal with a large in<sup>fl</sup>ow of users with heterogeneous valuations (Ghose and Han 2014, Lambrecht and Misra 2017). Further, users’ valuations for goods are likely endogenous and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by users’ experience, in which social interaction plays an important role (Runge et al. 2022).

In particular, the simultaneous coexistence of users who have spent (vastly different; Sifa et al. 2018) amounts of money and users who use the free version introduces new contingencies in managing such products and services (Runge et al. 2022). Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson (2013) and Bapna and Umyarov (2015) show that social engagement and peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence are drivers of the conversion of users from free to premium. Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson (2013) show that, as users increase their community participation on a social radio app, their willingness to pay goes up. Additionally, community participation seems to be more important in driving willingness to pay than users content consumption. In a similar vein, Bapna and Umyarov (2015) use a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment to establish that paid subscribers exhibit positive peer effects on their nonsubscribed friends to make a payment. Bapna et al. (2018) take the reverse perspective and aim to study if and how making a purchase can increase social engagement with a freemium community. By means of observational methods, they <sup>fi</sup>nd that adopting a paid subscription leads users to be more socially engaged. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, paid subscribers appear to increase their content consumption, content organization, and community involvement. Extant literature, hence, asserts strong peer and social effects in adoption of premium content in freemium settings, using methods to approximate causal effects from observational data. It is not clear, however, how these effects simultaneously work together to create relevant <sup>fi</sup>rm outcomes, that is, content engagement, app retention, and monetization—a gap that the present study aims to start addressing.

Finally, our paper also draws on foundational research on future demand estimation, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally customer lifetime value (CLV), which is often used to value customers and users (Gupta et al. 2004). The prediction of CLV in the existing literature is commonly based on purchase history. There are two standard approaches to CLV. Under the <sup>fi</sup>rst one, users are grouped based on the recency, frequency, and monetary value of their purchase history (Verhoef et al. 2003). The second approach devises stochastic models of consumer purchasing, which, for example, use the Pareto distribution to determine retention and the negative binomial distribution to determine purchase frequencies (Fader et al. 2005). With the increased availability of data and computing power, more recently, analytics approaches have emerged by which the prediction of CLV relies on supervised learning with random forest (RF) algorithms (Vanderveld et al. 2016, Chamberlain et al. 2017). RFs are successfully applied to the prediction of purchase decisions (Sifa et al. 2015) and CLV (Sifa et al. 2018) in freemium mobile apps and are shown to perform well in the prediction of consumer behavior more generally (Lemmens and Croux 2006). We draw on this class of models in devising our analytics-driven matching system.

## 3. Environment

This section introduces the empirical environment for our study: a mobile puzzle game with a role-playing component. This game is played via an application (colloquially: “app”) for mobile devices that is available in the iOS (Apple) and Android (Google) app stores.

Team play and social coordination and collaboration are crucial to the app experience, and the application was part of the top-100 grossing charts throughout the time period when data for this study were collected. The application, hence, represents a leading social mobile app that is discovered by users organically via the app stores, family and friends, mentions in press and online forums, and digital advertising. Once in the game, users need to identify color patterns and align puzzle objects to form combinations that are rewarded with points (similar to, e.g., the famous puzzle game Candy Crush). The environment allows users to play solo campaigns and missions (a campaign consists of several missions) but is designed to encourage players to form a team with other players to defeat powerful virtual opponents.<sup>2</sup>

The unit of user activity or participation is a mission. A typical mission has the goal of defeating an opponent and can comprise varying numbers of pattern-<sup>fi</sup>nding puzzles. Upon succeeding in a mission, the entire team collects in-game rewards. Typi cally, one player chooses <sup>fi</sup>ve characters from a set of possible characters (up to 40) to participate in each mission. Using these characters, the goal then is to achieve maximum damage in each strike against the virtual opponents by being pro<sup>fi</sup>cient at pattern <sup>fi</sup>nding in puzzles and team coordination.

After a user has installed the app, the user is <sup>fi</sup>rst allowed to play solo missions to learn how the game works, but within a short period of time of playing, the user ranks up and reaches eligibility to join a team. The corresponding 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of time taken to reach eligibility are 22, 33, and 217 minutes. The distribution is highly skewed to the right so that the average time to eligibility is high with 474 minutes.

Once eligible, users see a list of teams that are available to join. All teams have names and some of them also have descriptions.<sup>3</sup> The set of teams that is offered in these lists is randomly chosen from all active teams.<sup>4</sup> There are about 9,000 teams in the game environment in total, and around 6,000 of them are actively involved in accepting new users. Their average size is 21 members (with median 25) with an upper limit of 30 members. Once users join a team, they are still able to play solo missions and campaigns, but now they can also start team missions with teammates and share rewards if the mission succeeds. Considering the in-game rewards, for a typical player, it is substantially more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient to work in a team than alone. There are several important resources and reward mechanisms in this environment:

In-game currency (IGC): These are objects that can be purchased with real money in the store of the game at a <sup>fi</sup>xed price or be earned as rewards after mission successes. Examples of IGC include gems and gold.<sup>5</sup> IGC can be used to purchase other resources and can be exchanged with other players as gifts as well. Around 6% of users become payers (purchase IGC in an in-app purchase) within 37 days after joining the game, and this is representative of freemium mobile apps more generally (Sifa et al. 2015, 2018; Runge et al. 2022).

IGC-purchased capital goods: Once a player acquires IGC in in-app purchases, the player is able to make in-game purchases. The in-game store includes capital goods (character cards and synergists) that make a user’s characters more powerful and give the user higher chances to complete dif<sup>fi</sup>cult solo missions and achieve higher contributions to team missions. These goods have different levels of durability. Some are targeted for speci<sup>fi</sup>c missions; others can be used across missions and do not expire.

Within the team, users can write messages with and make gifts of IGCs and other in-game objects (character cards, synergists, keys) to speci<sup>fi</sup>c other team members or to the team as a whole. Message space is not restricted; that is, any written message in natural language is allowed. Messaging often involves explaining game concepts and strategies to the new users (learning) and agreements for exchanging capital goods (trading) to equip characters before going on a mission.

The environment is built to encourage participation in teams. The main type of player activity, called the weekly event, is designed so that there exists strategic complementarity with respect to players’ strengths and action. These weekly events are themed campaigns that run on a weekly basis and are designed such that users can experience new tasks and challenges every week. These events can vary in quality and popularity as well as in user engagement levels but consistently generate the majority of user engagement and revenue in the game.

With a variety of solo and team missions, the environment can serve a rich variety of players with different skills and different levels of willingness to spend real money. Overall, in-app purchases provide a more advanced experience to premium users compared with that of free users. Similarly, teams that spend more tend to exhibit more engagement, perform better in their missions, and receive higher rewards when completing missions.

Despite the complementarity of skills and willingness to spend in the environment, the platform does not facilitate nor actively promote that players of higher skill or willingness to purchase form teams among themselves (seek positive assortative matching). Although players can move to other teams that <sup>fi</sup>t them better, information on team characteristics and quality is limited and scattered. Teams can only be searched by team name; thus, for a new user who needs to make a team choice for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, seeking additional information would be very, and likely prohibitively, costly. For existing users who have participated in joint missions and have access to the team rankings (which are displayed on a “leaderboard” visible to all players who joined a team), it is also hard to <sup>fi</sup>nd a speci<sup>fi</sup>c team name from thousands of teams on the board. The vast majority of users, hence, rely on the team recommendation page provided by the game when joining a new team instead of performing an active search for a team that matches their preferences. Strictly speaking, new users’ selection of teams of different quality, for example, with high or low activity, occurs at random.

## 4. Matching System

This section describes the analytics-driven system for assortative matching. A preliminary analysis indicates that the environment exhibits potentially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant complementarities. That is, players with higher engagement and monetization potential bene<sup>fi</sup>t from joining a more skilled and engaged team. However, at the time of design of this study, the <sup>fi</sup>rm relied on users’ self-organization by showing random lists of teams to all new users, leading to possibly high search cost and low match value. It seems that the <sup>fi</sup>rm could increase realized match value by assortatively proposing an existing community to users. This would also lower search cost and, hence, the requirements on supermodularity of team production for assortative matching to be effective (Shimer and Smith 2000). Importantly, it also maintains the perception of selfselection and endogenous team formation on the side of users, which could otherwise impact team production separately (Hitsch et al. 2010, Carrell et al. 2013, Chen and Gong 2018).

Our matching mechanism is based on targeting potential high-value users and proposing to them to join highvalue teams. Individuals with higher potential are given a list of high-activity teams (in terms of engagement and revenue) from which to choose as opposed to the random set they were given before. The user is ultimately the one that decides which team to join.

The analytics system that sustains the mechanism can be decomposed into two classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems: one for the users and one for the teams. Users are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed between likely and unlikely payers.<sup>6</sup> Teams are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as high or low activity. With these two com ponents, the system is able to promote those users with a high probability to be engaged and a payer to enter more engaged and active teams.

## 4.1. Team Classification

To implement the team classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, we leverage institutional expertise from product managers to determine what constitutes a highly active user community.

In collaboration with managers, we devise a team-level activity score building on the following variables: (1) the number of active players (team members who open and engage with the game), (2) the number of missions played as a team, (3) the volume of gifts (gems, stamina, hero cards) exchanged between team members, (4) the number of messages exchanged between team members, and (5) total team revenue de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the sum of all members’ expenditure in the game. We generate these variables on a daily basis and calculate a 14-day moving average for each of them. We then aggregate the individual inputs into an overall activity score using a geometric mean.

To conduct a simple test of the viability of this team classi<sup>fi</sup>cation in terms of capturing future engagement with and revenue from the game, we sort all teams on a set of arbitrarily chosen days and measure what share of revenue during the next 30 days originates from high- versus low-activity teams. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the top 30% of teams capture at least 80% of revenue in the next month. We set the activity threshold for the team classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model at this 30th percentile such that high-type teams can be expected to drive at least 80% of next month’s revenue and, hence, represent the most productive social groupings in the environment.

This approach ensures that the high-activity teams not only capture the majority of game revenue, but also meet a minimum level of engagement and social behavior, such as gifting and messaging. That is, we argue this method is likely to identify teams with engaging social experiences for users while simultaneously accounting for revenue generation as the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s main goal.

## 4.2. User Classification

For the user-side classi<sup>fi</sup>er, we use supervised learning techniques to predict users’ CLV based on input features derived from behavioral traces commonly collected by mobile application developers. There are several technical challenges associated with this exercise. First, because team recommendation typically occurs relatively early in a user’s in-app experience, these behavioral traces are limited, and there is virtually no purchase history. To address this challenge, we rely on machine learning methods that <sup>fl</sup>exibly accept nonpurchase-related data (Vanderveld et al. 2016), putting them at an advantage here vis-a-vis\` classical methods for CLV prediction in the marketing literature (Fader et al. 2005). Second, there is strong class imbalance as only a small share of users actually become payers in the future (Weiss 2004, Nieborg, 2015, Sifa et al. 2018). In order to alleviate the latter problem, we apply a synthetic oversampling technique (SMOTE) to generate synthetic instances from the class of actual payers (Chawla et al. 2002, Torgo et al. 2013). SMOTE generates synthetic instances using convex mixings of a random paying user with the user’s nearest neighbors. A key bene<sup>fi</sup>t of this approach, in addition to reducing class imbalance in the training data, is a regularization effect from the random noise added via the convex mixings. For a detailed exposition of the viability, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and implementation of this approach, please refer to Sifa et al. (2018).

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne purchase amount in dollars (CLV) as the outcome variable to be predicted by different regression techniques. The outcome variable is constructed by summing all purchase amounts in the 30 days after the user becomes eligible to join a team. Input features for the regression include both demographic variables (users’ language, type of device, and country) as well as data characterizing early behavior in the game, tracked in the game provider’s servers from app install up to the moment of team eligibility. Behavioral data include the time (in minutes) users take to reach each game rank before team eligibility, number of sessions and solo missions played and won, highest rank achieved, and revenue generated before eligibility. Table 1 summarizes these user characteristics. The training data set for our user prediction model includes one month of new installs who reach team eligibility within seven days after joining the game. It comprises around 52,000 individuals.

During training, we perform a <sup>fi</sup>vefold cross-validation (CV) to avoid model over<sup>fi</sup>tting. On each training set during the CV process, we conduct SMOTE oversampling of the minority class of future paying users by factor two<sup>7</sup> and perform feature selection and hyperparameter tuning to minimize root mean squared error.

## 4.3. Implementation of the Assortative Matching System

The XGBoost model deployed to production continuously makes 30-day expected revenue predictions for new users when they arrive at the team recommendation page. It retrains itself daily on the newest available training data and automatically performs feature selection and parameter tuning.

After obtaining a prediction for each new user entering the game, we classify users into two categories: likely and unlikely payers. On the team side, we have high- and low-activity teams correspondingly. The number of available team spots in each category (i.e., supply of “free seats” with a maximum of 30 seats each team) is <sup>fi</sup>xed from the supply side because we determine the number of high-activity teams up to the point that they capture 80% of future 30-day revenue (see Section 4.1). In order to equilibrate supply and demand in the matching market, the threshold that separates a likely versus unlikely payer on a given day depends on the number of daily available spots in high-activity teams the day before. The assignment algorithm for day t runs at the start of day t (when day t 1 has completed) and works as follows: (1) Sort all teams based on their activity score on day t 1 in descending order. (2) Select the top 30% of teams from this list; these are the high-activity teams for day t. (3) Calculate the number of available spots in these high-activity teams. (4) Sort all new users who came into the game on day t 1 based on their predicted 30-day revenue in descending order. (5) Identify the threshold above which players from the sorted list in 4 can be offered a spot in a high activity team (based on availability in 3), assuming that all eligible players choose a spot. (6) Use the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed threshold to inform which new players are offered high- versus low-activity teams on day t.

Whereas users are still free to choose which speci<sup>fi</sup>c team they join, this algorithm ensures that higher value users join high-activity teams. The mechanism is designed to clear the matching market such that highactivity teams driving the majority (80%) of future revenue receive the highest quality users available. This design ensures that the system is robust to longer term <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in the in<sup>fl</sup>ux of new users that are common in this environment and that high-activity team spots do not go unused.<sup>9</sup> The thresholds for separating high- and low-activity teams and likely and unlikely payers are automatically refreshed every night based on the most recent supply and demand information collected at the end of the day. Similarly, both the user- and team-level models are retrained on the most recent data available in an overnight job. Throughout implementation of the system, roughly 70% of new users are categorized as likely payers, and the rest are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as unlikely payers, and hence, about 70% of new users are recommended highactivity teams.<sup>10</sup>

Prior to releasing the system in the live game environment, we perform an of<sup>fl</sup>ine evaluation to gauge its expected impact on user behavior outcomes. We leverage a propensity score matching regression on historical observations (without assortative matching, historical team matching is effectively random as described) to estimate the treatment effects between paired observations that share similar high-value propensity scores but join different types of teams (through choice from the randomly selected list of teams shown). We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, for likely payers, joining high-activity teams leads to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more engagement in sending messages and playing game missions. For unlikely payers, joining high-activity teams leads to nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts for the majority of the outcome variables except messaging for which we note a positive effect. Section A in the online appendix describes our approach and the results in more detail. Overall, this of<sup>fl</sup>ine evaluation corroborates that we can expect an aggregate positive impact on engagement and monetization from our system.

## 5. Experiment Design

Based on the positive results of our preliminary assessment (see Section A in the online appendix), the collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rm authorized the deployment of our matching system to production. To evaluate the mechanism, we perform a time-wise experiment in which the system is turned on and off in three-day intervals over six weeks. During the on period, users and teams are matched assortatively as detailed in Section 4. During the off period, users and teams are matched randomly following the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s prior policy.

This approach has several important advantages. First, teams’ activity in this game environment occurs mostly during weekly events. This is by design; the game encourages users to work together in these challenges. However, these events are heterogeneous. Events can vary in quality, popularity, dif<sup>fi</sup>culty, engagement, and revenue. Additionally, there might be weekly seasonal effects in terms of new users downloading the game being different on different days of the week and the weekend. Running the experiment in three-day intervals and for a six-week period ensures that the types of weekly events and the days of the week are approximately evenly split between treatment and control moments. Second, the result of the assignment is nearly equivalent to that of a randomization at the user-level. This is because users’ arrival time is arguably independent of the arbitrary time intervals in which the system is on or off. If, instead, we randomized across users, teams would receive users both assortatively and randomly. Our approach introduces time separation between random and assortative user in<sup>fl</sup>ow to teams, a fact that we use in the analysis to identify team-level effects. 11

## 5.1. Outcomes

We use the following outcomes to evaluate the matching system’s impact on the game environment. These variables are de<sup>fi</sup>ned at the individual level. Their team-level analogs are constructed by aggregating the user-level variables. All outcomes are log-transformed when used for regression analysis:

Revenue: the cumulative amount of U.S. dollars the user(s) spent (for individual analysis) or the total amount by team members (for team-level analysis).

Number of messages: total number of within-team messages sent by the user (for individual-level analysis) or the total among team members (for team-level analysis).

Weekly event mission starts: total number of weekly event mission starts by the user (for individual-level analysis) or the total of team members (for team-level analysis).

Weekly event mission successes: total number of weekly event mission successes by the user (for individual-level analysis) or the total of team members (for team-level analysis).

Retention: Indicator variable of whether the user is still active 14 days after joining their <sup>fi</sup>rst team.

## 5.2. Hypotheses

Here, we provide a precise statement of the hypotheses regarding the impact of the system on the outcomes mentioned. We also offer these hypotheses’ conceptual rationale.

Hypothesis 1. Adding likely payers into highly active teams positively impacts new players’ engagement, output, and spending.

Hypothesis 2. Similarly, adding likely payers into highly active teams positively impacts existing players’ and teams outcomes.

Hypothesis 3. Matching unlikely payers into low-activity teams has a negative impact on new players’ engagement, output, and spending.

Hypothesis 4. Similarly, adding unlikely payers into lowactivity teams has a negative impact on existing players and teams’ outcomes.

These four general hypotheses follow the broad tenets of our study: that the team’s quality directly impacts the performance, engagement, and spending of the newly joined team members. Conversely, the quality of the freshly joined players has an impact on the team’s outcomes. Notice these are statements of in<sup>fl</sup>uence between the quality of teams and players. However, these hypoth eses do not necessarily imply that changing the system from random assignment to assortative improves the performance of the whole environment. That is why we formulate the following hypotheses regarding the net effect of implementing the matching system.

Hypothesis 5. The overall net effect of the assortative matching system is positive on engagement, output, and spending for new players.

Hypothesis 6. The overall net effect of the assortative matching system is positive on engagement, output, and spending for incumbent players of the existing teams.

These two last hypotheses may seem mechanical when construed as a direct result of the <sup>fi</sup>rst set of hypotheses. However, we believe that spelling them out is important as the total net effects are critical to the central premise of this study.

If the matching system has positive net effects, this indicates that the technology exhibits supermodularity or strategic complementarities. However, supermodularity can emerge in different ways. In particular, both returns to scale and the degree of technical complementarity can contribute to supermodularity. The following hypotheses state our conjecture regarding these properties of the production function of this environment.

Hypothesis 7. The estimation of a production function for teams’ outcomes yields both strategic and technical comple mentarities as well as returns to scale

We also know that the possible strategic complementarities can operate at different levels, namely, in the rules of the environment and in the interaction among players. However, this information does not identify or characterize the mechanisms activated by the matching system. To investigate these mechanisms, we <sup>fi</sup>rst desegregate the team quality score into the component mainly associated with social interactions (proxied by volume of messaging and gifting) and the other factors. This breakdown of the score allows us to assess what would happen if the assortative matching was based solely on the social behavior of teams. Would the movement from a team with mild social activity to another with more social activity cause the same impact on new team members? Our conjecture, re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the following hypothesis, is that socialization is an essential mechanism through which the matching system operates and impacts our outcomes.

Hypothesis 8. For both types of players, likely and unlikely payers, being matched with a highly social team has an impact comparable to being matched with a highactivity team (based on the whole activity score).

Suppose socialization is the primary driver of the results. In that case, we can consider in-game social behavior as an intermediate outcome. We should also observe that the quantitative analysis corresponding to Hypotheses 1–4 should yield a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on messaging. We summarize this idea in the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 9. The direct impact of the matching system is more prominent in socializing behavior than in other outcomes.

We proxy socializing behavior using messages sent by users. Ultimately, if social behavior acts as an intermediate variable, we should <sup>fi</sup>nd that the total effects of assortative matching on the other outcomes are mediated by messaging. This conjecture is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 10. The effect of the matching system for outcomes not related to socialization is mediated by in-game social behavior.

## 6. Results and Discussion

This section analyzes the effects of the proposed assortative matching system at the user- and team-level. In Section 6.1, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present the direct effects of the system based on the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation described. Overall, results paint a positive picture of the system’s impact on user engagement, outputs, and monetization. These results suggest that assortative matching systems can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve user experiences and <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in freemium settings.

In the primary reduced-form analysis, we use a cross-sectional approach; the individual analysis uses outcomes from when users <sup>fi</sup>rst join a team until

14 days later. For team-level analysis, we consider outcomes measured for the last 14 days of the experiment. We use these time intervals to estimate the impact of the system in the short run. Also, for both types of analysis, the user classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is based on the score measured when the user becomes eligible to join a team. Similarly, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation variable for the team is the one constructed at the beginning of the experiment (for the team-level analysis) or the moment the player joins the group (for the user-level analysis).

We also study several possible mechanisms that drive these results. We focus on characterizing the technology that rules this environment and the role of sociality as an essential intermediate outcome and mechanism.

## 6.2. Mechanisms

In this section, we present an empirical exploration of the mechanisms that drive our results.

6.2.1. Technology. Because the matching system has positive net effects, this might indicate that the environment exhibits supermodularity. However, both returns to scale and the degree of technical complementarity can contribute to supermodularity. In this section, we characterize the speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of the technology that yield these overall strategic complementarities.

We characterize users’ personal and team production via a simple yet <sup>fl</sup>exible model of production technology. We then present the estimation of said model using observational pre-experiment data. In this technology, personal production is embedded inside a team production function. For the personal production, we use a constant elasticity of substitution (CES) function that combines three personal input variables: (1) time spent as a proxy of labor; (2) in-game currency expenditure in tools, productivity boosters, and synergists as a proxy of capital; (3) and user rank as a proxy of skill. In Equation (3), these variables are denoted by $x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , x _ { 3 } ,$ , respectively, and their weighting coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are the corresponding α’s. These personal inputs are powered to $\delta ,$ a parameter capturing the degree of substitution between individual inputs.

The personal output is denoted by $y _ { i } .$ Notice this is the same as player i’s contribution to the team, and it is not directly observed. For the team production, we also specify a CES function with $\rho$ as the substitution parameter and σ denoting the team-level returns to scale. This technology is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Equation (3).

$$
y _ {g, t} = \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {g, t}} \left[ \underbrace {\left(\alpha_ {1} x _ {1 , i , t} ^ {\delta} + \alpha_ {2} x _ {2 , i , t} ^ {\delta} + \alpha_ {3} x _ {3 , i , t} ^ {\delta}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{\delta}}} _ {y _ {i, t}} \right] ^ {\rho}\right) ^ {\frac {\sigma}{\rho}},\tag{3}
$$

where i denotes the player, g denotes the team, and $n _ { g }$ is the size of team g. For $y _ { g } ,$ , team outputs, we consider (1) revenue, capturing within-game spending; (2) number of messages, capturing team social behavior; (3) number of started projects (mission starts), capturing aggregate effort; and (4) number of reached goals (mission successes), capturing aggregate performance.

We estimate the model using weekly data collected for the six weeks that precede the experiment when the matching is random.<sup>13</sup> All outcome variables are log-transformed. Parameters δ and $\rho$ are restricted to be less than or equal to one.

Table 4 presents the estimated parameters. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, for all outcomes, δ estimates are in the 0:05, 1 range. In the team production level, we <sup>fi</sup>nd substantial increasing returns to scale (σ estimates are in the 1, 1:8 range). We also <sup>fi</sup>nd perfect technical substitution among team members for three out of four outcomes with the sole exception of the number of messages exchanged between team members, which displays imperfect substitution (ρ is estimated to be approximately equal to 0.4 for the latter and equal to one in all other outcomes). σ estimates are in the 1:04, 1:75 range.

By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, there is supermodularity with respect to the personal production if the following holds: $\partial ^ { 2 } y _ { i } / ( \partial y _ { i } \partial y _ { j } ) > 0 .$ . In this technology, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed if and only if $\sigma > \rho .$ . Furthermore, this condition is actually suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for global strategic complementarities in the sense that they hold even between players’ inputs, that is, between $x _ { k , i }$ and $x _ { k ^ { \prime } , i ^ { \prime } }$ for $k , k ^ { \prime } \in \left\{ 1 , 2 , 3 \right\}$ . Overall, it seems then that the main drivers of these complementarities are the increasing returns to scale at the team-level $( \sigma > 1 )$ . These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are in line with the expectation formulated in Hypothesis 7.

We note two insights of particular relevance stemming from this estimation. First, as we observe increasing returns to scale at the team-level, it appears that freemium pricing does bene<sup>fi</sup>t production by allowing a larger user base and, hence, larger teams relative to pricing mechanisms that would charge an up-front fee. Second, if the returns to scale are strong enough that they create supermodularity, further gains can be achieved by deploying a positive assortative matching mechanism. This is because better quality or higher-activity players have a higher impact on similar players than on lower quality, less active ones.

Next, we explore the question of whether technology is invariant across types of teams and over time. For this purpose, we estimate the same production function for high- and low-activity teams separately. Furthermore, for each type of team, we estimate separate regressions for two different time intervals: before and during the experiment, each with a length of six weeks. We also compute a factor-neutral productivity term associated to each of these regressions.

The results are presented in Tables 10–12 in Section E in the online appendix and brie<sup>fl</sup>y discussed here. We observe differences in the production technology between high- and low-activity teams. Whereas the former display increasing returns to scale across the board (all outcomes and both periods), low-activity teams exhibit increasing returns to scale in both periods only for mission-related outcomes and decreasing ones for revenue and messages. Despite this pattern, supermodularity is consistently present except for revenue in low-quality teams. We must also note that the degree of supermodularity in social behavior (proxied by messages) is in all cases much stronger than for the other outcomes. For instance, the ratio $\sigma / \rho$ is three times larger in messages than in other outcomes among high-activity teams. Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the main features of the underlying technology do not change substantially over time conditional on team quality. The productivity factor associated to messages increases for both types of teams. For mission-related metrics, the productivity decreases in low-activity teams and remains relatively constant in high-activity teams. Note that revenue’s productivity factor size is substantially larger during the experiment. We advise caution in interpreting these results. The distribution of spending is highly skewed, which, combined with the nonlinear quality of the estimation method, may generate nonrobust estimates.

Now, what do these results tell us about the sources of gains from the matching system? The net effect estimates are relevant because the new matching has forces with opposite signs, and consequently, the shares of different types of players and teams and their reactions to changes in the environment play a role. With this in mind, the aggregate effects can be partially traced down to these sources or mechanisms. First, because of the team recomposition associated with the matching system, old and new players could behave differently. Second, the technology that aggregates players’ inputs into outputs could change over time. Third, the recomposition of teams itself could drive changes in outcomes.

Our experiment and the conceptual framework do not perfectly distinguish the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second sources. This is because the production function combines the game’s rules (<sup>fi</sup>xed and representative of this institutional setting) and the output behavior that emerges through interaction. Player’s input behavior (i.e., the x’s in the production function equation) can be a separate source of change. This would contemplate the possibility of players reacting to their teammates’ behavior (others’ x’s). We test this conjecture in two ways (see Section F in the online appendix). First, we implement a standard peer-effect regression in which one’s behavior depends on the other team members average behavior, <sup>fi</sup>nding that individual behavio has a very low sensitivity to others’ (see Table 13 in Section F in the online appendix). Second, we run the user-level main speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (Equation (1)) with play ers’ inputs as dependent variables. Here, we observe that inputs are unaffected by switching on the assortative matching system. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that changes in input behavior resulting from peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence are not the main mechanism through which the matching system delivers its impact. Additionally, as previously mentioned, we observe that, conditional on the quality of the team, the underlying technology changes over time but not substantially.

Given this overall picture, we argue that the positive net changes in observed outcomes are largely attributable to the recomposition associated with the matching system. The gains from this recomposition are, in turn, facilitated by a technology with supermodularity.

6.2.2. Team Sociality. In this section, we study social behavior as an intermediate outcome and possible mechanism driving the effects on <sup>fi</sup>nal outcomes. In previous sections (reduced-form main results as well as in the production technology estimation), we document that social behavior (messaging) displays stronger effects than other outcomes when switching on the system (validating Hypothesis 9), and that the production of social output (messaging) exhibits a stronger supermodularity. We believe that these pieces of evidence set social behavior aside from other outcomes and suggest that socialization as an intermediate outcome may be channeling the impact of the matching system to other outcomes.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst explore what would happen if we matched players based solely on a socialization score for teams. In particular, we estimate the effect of moving a player from a team with low socialization to another that is highly social. Table 5 presents the results of this exercise. The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is similar to the user-level estimate replacing the high- and low-activity dummy with the dummy variable indicating that the team is highly social. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that results are very similar to those obtained when using the high- and low-activity dummy, which lends support to Hypothesis 8. In particular, we observe a positive effect for all outcomes except for revenue. This effect, furthermore, does not depend on whether the player is a likely or unlikely payer. Indeed, for likely payers, the effect is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant on all outcomes but revenue (with gains ranging from 17% to 29%) with messages experiencing the highest increase. Notably, this latter effect is very similar in magnitude to that of moving from a low- to high-activity team, which also amounts to 29% (as seen in the lower part of Table 2). The effect of joining a team with higher sociality is similar for unlikely payers, who also experience a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase in messages and mission-related outcomes (between 18% and 27%) and no signi<sup>fi</sup> cant change in revenue. However, the impact on retention turns insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant albeit remaining close in magnitude to that for likely payers.

We also conduct a regression analysis in which we study the interaction between the high-sociality team dummy and the dummy variable corresponding to the rest of the components of the team activity score (henceforth technical component). The model estimated is reported in Table 15 in Section G in the online appendix. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the effect of higher sociality depends strongly on the team’s position regarding the remaining score. In particular, when the team scores high in the remaining components of the score, unlikely payers experience no effect on any outcomes as all estimates are statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. Meanwhile, likely payers actually see signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reductions in all variables. We interpret these results as suggesting that the two components of the score (social and technical factors) are strong substitutes. For example, when a player is on a team that is already strong in the technical component, moving the player from a low-sociality to a highly social team only reduces spending by that player, presumably because the player learns more easily that others are already spending on this team.

Furthermore, we analyze whether teams are differ ently impacted by new players depending on whether the team sociality score is low or high. Regression results are reported in Table 16 in Section G in the online appendix. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the effects of new likely and unlikely payers are similar for teams of both low and high sociality. As in the main results, likely and unlikely payers have generally positive and negative effects, respectively.

Results so far suggest that socialization serves to channel the effect of assortative matching on the other outcomes. We explicitly evaluate this hypothesis by conducting a mediation analysis of the effect of assortative matching on revenue and output-related outcomes with messages as the mediator variable. We follow the standard approach in mediation analysis by estimating a structural equation model in which one equation regresses each outcome against the matching SystemON<sub>i</sub> binary variable and the other equation adds the messaging intensity. The former equation estimates the total effect of the matching system, compounding the direct and indirect effect through the mediator. The latter measures only the direct effect of the system. Put differently, we decompose the total effect into its direct and indirect (mediated by social behavior) components.

## 7. Conclusion

This study provides <sup>fi</sup>eld experimental evidence of the impact of algorithmically facilitated assortative matching between new users and existing communities in a digital freemium environment. We develop a machine learning–based system to classify users and social groups (teams) in terms of their future engagement and monetization and match them assortatively in a binary fashion, that is, high to high and low to low. We test this matching system in a time-based <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in a popular mobile game. Our premium player prediction model using behavioral and demographic features collected at an early stage of user lifetime in the app performs 60% better than random guessing. Our team classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model is able to identify the top 30% of high-activity teams that contribute 80% of the app’s revenue in the following month.

Results from the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment show that assortative matching of users and social groupings results in increased team output, engagement, and revenue. A reduced-form analysis suggests that, per user added, our system has an average net positive effect across teams of about 5% (p < 0.05) on team revenue and on messages sent within teams and of about 4.2% (p < 0.05) and 4.7% (p < 0.01) on game rounds started and won, two atomic units of engagement in this environment. At the user-level, the assortative matching system deployed generates a positive effect on all outcomes for the average player with in-game social behavior being impacted most. Indeed, messages sent and game rounds started and won increase by about 13.2%, 10.1%, and 9.2% (all $p s \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ , respectively, whereas revenue increases by about 2.7% (p < 0.05). We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the system leads to better retention of the average player with a 0.9% $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ increased proba bility of staying active two weeks after joining a group.

Further analysis suggests that these results are mainly driven by increased socialization among team members. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of heterogeneous effects across different quartiles of team and user quality, which sug gests that <sup>fi</sup>rms could potentially obtain better results by using a <sup>fi</sup>ner classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of teams and players than the binary system used in this study. As the environment under study shares many commonalities with apps and digital social media in general, we believe the insights obtained extend to and can be usefully applied in other contexts. In particular, the results extend to freemium digital products for which users cocreate outputs through collaboration and coordination in online communities. They overall paint an attractive picture for the use of assortative matching by pro<sup>fi</sup>t-oriented <sup>fi</sup>rms to improve engagement and monetization of social interaction–based freemium software.

Our paper is, to our knowledge, the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>eld experimental study of assortative matching in a digital environment. It extends a seminal paper by Hitsch et al. (2010) who study user-to-user matching in online dating. It is delineated from said paper by designing an algorithmic system for assortative matching, test ing this system in a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, and investigating user–team versus user–user matches. The present study also contributes to the analysis of freemium platforms and monetization (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Bapna et al. 2018) by characterizing production technology in this setting and establishing that team production is supermodular. This <sup>fi</sup>nding, in turn, implies that increases in engagement and monetization can be obtained by assortative matching. On the level of managerial implications, a vital issue for <sup>fi</sup>rms operating freemium software is matching newly arriving users with highly heterogeneous valuations for free and premium experiences into existing communities. Many <sup>fi</sup>rms rely on users’ self-organization (Supercell 2018), but this approach can entail a high search cost for users (Bapna et al. 2016). The present paper shows how <sup>fi</sup>rms can identify (high-value) premium users to provide them with personalized, engaging social experiences to foster revenue generation.

Whereas pro<sup>fi</sup>table, the algorithmic introduction of new users of similar behavior as the existing team members creates a separating path between teams of different qualities. Highly engaged teams maintain and grow their high-activity environment, and existing high-quality team members spend and play more. As, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, high-quality teams and players account for the majority of engagement and revenue, overall effects of the matching system are positive. The matching of low-quality players and teams, however, has a negative impact on low-quality teams, further destabilizing the team environment and discouraging the development of these players and their communities. We believe this result to be concerning from a wider societal vantage point as it highlights a risk of increased polarization from tacit algorithmic facilitation of assortativity in the overall online community between low- and highactivity teams and players. Algorithmic mechanisms of a similar character may be at work in many curation and recommendation algorithms on digital platforms and social media, for example, “People you may know” (Dewey 2015) or “Posts you may be interested in” (Bail et al. 2018, Levy 2021). Along these lines, the present study bears relevant insights for the study of the production and consumption of social media. An attractive extension may be to identify optimal levels of assortativity in matching or other ways of matching altogether to facilitate long-term cohesion of the overall online community and contrast these with the pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing policy.

Summarizing, assortative matching of newly arriving users to existing freemium communities can be useful to improve <sup>fi</sup>rm-side outcomes and retain a larger number of valuable new users on digital marketplaces. However, from a wider societal perspective, the type of matching system designed and studied here can have detrimental effects on poorer, weaker, or more marginal segments of the user population, leading to higher inequality and polarization in the digital environment. Such inequality can possibly adversely impact wider welfare, for example, when considering online dating platforms (Hitsch et al. 2010) for which highly assortative algorithms might preclude some users from <sup>fi</sup>nding a mate altogether. Because an increasing, already sizable, portion of human life is being spent in digital environments, we believe that designers of social interaction systems and policy makers should be aware of the trade-offs between net private bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that can be achieved by positive-assortative matching systems and their potential costs for weaker segments of users. We also believe that understanding these trade-offs of machine learning–powered matching systems should be a priority in the future research on fairness in machine learning (Chouldechova and Roth 2020).

Andrews et al. (2012), however, argue that those results are due to limited mobility bias, not the lack of assortativity.

<sup>2</sup> The industry collaborator chose to remain anonymous. For confidentiality reasons, we use general terms, such as teams, characters, opponents, and campaigns/missions, instead of game-specific terms throughout and do not identify the game.

<sup>3</sup> Here are some typical team names (description): Midnight Elementals ( … ready to bring the power of the elements to destroy the competition … ), nflesh (no description), Bald dwarfs ( … a team determined to be one of the greatest … ).

<sup>4</sup> If all members of a team have not logged into the game for a consecutive 14 days, the team is considered inactive and removed from the team pool.

<sup>5</sup> In the actual environment, IGCs have different names.

<sup>6</sup> We use the term “likely” (as opposed to “actual” on the user side) for the following reasons: First, team matching happens at a very early stage of players’ lifetime: for the majority, about 30 minutes after install. At this point in time, players usually have made zero in-game purchases. Second, users who actually convert to payers 3 days after install are still a small percentage of all users.

<sup>7</sup> We searched across factors two to five and found factor two to achieve the highest mean performance.

<sup>8</sup> Using a time-independent holdout set provides an unbiased evaluation of our user classification model’s prediction power. Similar to the training data set, the test data set uses one month of new installs, independent of the one month of data used in the training set, and contains around 100,000 individuals. This approach further tests if stationarity can reasonably be assumed in our setting.

<sup>9</sup> The number of available team spots in general is higher than the number of players looking for a team, so each player is guaranteed a team spot, albeit possibly in a team with low activity.

<sup>10</sup> For existing users who are looking to switch teams, the system only recommends high-activity team environments. The underlying rationale is that existing users actively switching teams are looking for a more engaged and active social environment. Designing the system in this way further ensures that a user who is unhappy with the user’s first team match receives a high-quality match on a second attempt. The user signals through this second attempt (Bapn et al. 2016) that the user is a high-value user and has higher intent to engage than predicted by our user-level model.

<sup>11</sup> Whereas it would be ideal to control users’ assignment at the team-level, this alternative was not feasible because of software engineering constraints.

<sup>12</sup> Please note that all outcomes at the exception of retention are logtransformed. Tables consistently report nonexponentiated results. In the text, we simply rely on linear approximation to interpret these as percentage changes.

<sup>13</sup> Broad results on supermodularity and returns to scale hold for both daily and weekly levels of aggregation.
