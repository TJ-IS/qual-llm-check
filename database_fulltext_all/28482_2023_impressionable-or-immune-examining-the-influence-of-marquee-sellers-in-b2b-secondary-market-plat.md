# Impressionable or Immune? Examining the Influence of Marquee Sellers in B2B Secondary Market Platforms for IT Products

Abstract. Although digital platforms have become mainstream, there still remain some unanswered questions pertaining to managing platform ecosystems. One such unexplored question pertains to the effect of marquee sellers on the platform—marquee sellers arguably attract other sellers and buyers to the platform, thereby enhancing the platform’s value. In this paper, we study how adding a marquee seller to a business-to-business (B2B) secondary-market platform for IT products affects other sellers, in terms of the prices they obtain for comparable products. Using proprietary data on B2B secondary-market auctions obtained from a platform provider, we show that the entry of a marquee seller has a positive effect on the prices obtained by other sellers on the platform, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting a referenceprice effect. We further show that this positive effect on <sup>fi</sup>nal prices is moderated by the extent to which bidders are active on multiple seller sites on the same platform and the extent to which bidders participate in the marquee seller site. We explain these effect using theory in multihoming and involvement, in terms of how reference prices are set. Our work extends the platforms literature by considering the speci<sup>fi</sup>c in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a marquee seller on other sellers, thereby informing platform owners on the implications of the advice to add more marquee sellers to platforms. We also contribute to the literature on secondary markets for durable, used IT products, which are instrumental in reducing elec tronic waste and mitigating the environmental damage done by electronic products in land<sup>fi</sup>lls.

## Introduction

Double-sided digital platforms are increasingly ubiquitous in the economy today, giving rise to a body of literature enhancing our understanding of how they operate and the drivers of performance (Rochet and Tirole 2003, Farrell and Klemperer 2007, Wilbur 2008, Evans 2009, Eisenmann et al. 2011). The importance of network effects and their implications for switching costs and pricing remain salient within much of this work (Zhu et al. 2021). However, there remain speci<sup>fi</sup>c arguments provided in the theoretical or managerial work on platforms that remain empirically untested. Amongst these, one recurring theme pertains to building same-side and cross-side networks through the acquisition of marquee players (Eisenmann et al. 2006, Edelman 2015). Stated plainly, platform owners can bene<sup>fi</sup>t by attracting high-visibility, high-quality sell ers or buyers because they increase same-side partici pation on the platform, while also attracting greater cross-side participation through network effects. However, the economic impact of adding marquee sellers or buyers to the platform and its differential impact across sellers’ prices has not been clearly established— we address this particular gap in this paper. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, we study how the addition of a marquee seller on a two-sided platform affects prices obtained by other existing sellers for similar products.

The impact of adding sellers to a platform has been shown to reduce prices, on average, on platforms (Wright

2004, Chandra and Collard-Wexler 2009, Zhu et al. 2021) through the increase in competition and the associated increase in supply. By lowering average prices, the new entry of sellers can also kickstart negative same-side network effects, to the point where sellers potentially leave the market entirely (Caillaud and Jullien 2003, Rochet and Tirole 2003, Chen and Tse 2008). However, the addition of a marquee seller may spur different dynamics on the platform. First, marquee sellers may better attract users (or consumers) on the cross-side of the platform, thereby offsetting the increase in supply and reducing the extent to which prices fall on the platform (Eisenmann et al. 2006). Second, absent any price-externality effects, they may attract additional high-quality sellers to the platform, who, by offering higher-quality products that command a higher price, increase the average price of a product sold on the platform. Finally, in the presence of price-externality effects, they may induce higher valuations for products sold on the platform by virtue of reference-price effects because they provide a new set of price and quality anchors for users on the platform (Monroe 1973, Rao and Monroe 1989). That is, all else equal, marquee sellers may help raise prices for other sellers on the platform, thus increasing value for platform owners as well.

In this paper, we argue and show that prices faced by other sellers increase for similar products after the entry of a highly visible marquee seller who commands high prices for its products. Although supply does increase upon entry, we do not observe a concurrent increase in the number of buyers associated with the observed higher prices. Similarly, we do not observe new sellers entering the platform either. Thus, through a series of tests, we show that the effect of the marquee seller on prices faced by other sellers is largely explained by the reference-price effect, suggesting that beyond network effects, it is important to consider how price anchors and reference prices are also in<sup>fl</sup>uential in platform settings.

We locate our study of marquee sellers in the context of business-to-business (B2B) secondary-market auctions for IT products, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally mobile devices. The B2B auction market is one form of secondary markets in which pallets of equipment are sold to preregistered business buyers through an online auction portal (Scott 2019). These markets process the resale of products that have passed through the primary market in some form, either as customer returns, unsold inventory, or from buyback programs where old devices are turned in for discounts on newer devices (Lee and Whang 2002, Tibben-Lembke 2004, Elmaghraby et al. 2018). For our study, we use data from a leading online intermediary platform that specializes in running online auctions for big-box retailers, thereby allowing them to liquidate their excess inventory through independent and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally created retailer storefronts. In this context, our research site is the platform provider, connecting big-box retailers on the one side to B2B buyers/ bidders on the other side. These B2B bidders are themselves resellers, including <sup>fl</sup>ea-market operators and independent stores that vary in bidding activity and purchase volumes. Consistent with the literature, the platform provider has worked toward adding more retailers to the platform because this encourages growth on the buyer side by virtue of cross-side network effects (Eisenmann et al. 2006, Bakos and Katsamakas 2008). The addition of one such marquee retailer to the platform forms the basis of our study.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we exploit the exogenous entry of AT&T, a marquee seller in the mobile industry in the United States, to the platform to study prices obtained by other sellers of comparable products. We focus on auctioned pallets of iPhone mobile devices in our anal ysis. Our sample contains auction data of iPhone pallets offered in the United States spanning the period from January 2014 to July 2017, which includes the time period when AT&T entered the platform as a seller. The sample contains data of 21,284 bids in 3,605 unique auctions and includes 1,241 unique B2B bidders. As a starting point, we <sup>fi</sup>rst establish that the prices obtained by AT&T’s pallets, all else equal, are higher than those obtained by others, thereby indicating that there is a premium attached to the retailer. Subsequently, we consider prices for other sellers of mobile phone pallets and see clear evidence of reference-price effects—the higher prices associated with comparable AT&T auctions act as price anchors and lead to increased prices obtained by other sellers postentry.

Beyond this direct effect, we also consider heterogeneity in terms of where and how these reference-price effects manifest. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we consider two moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uences here. First, we consider the role of multihoming bidders—that is, those bidders who bid for pallets in multiple marketplaces (retailer storefronts for auctions) versus those who participate in only one particular marketplace (retailer auction site). Prior research shows that multihoming consumers manifest different behavior relative to single-homers (Gabszewicz and Wauthy 2004, Landsman and Stre mersch 2011). Relative to single-homers, multihomers are exposed to a wider range of prices on the platform, and therefore the addition of a single new price anchor, even from a marquee seller, may have a smaller or no effect. Second, we consider the impact of involvement on multihomers (Petty and Cacioppo 1979)—that is, when multihoming bidders bid on AT&T auctions, thereby becoming active in the marquee seller’s marketplace. Prior work argues that involvement with a task can reduce the extent to which the new anchor is assimilated, thereby leading to less in<sup>fl</sup>uence from reference prices. Using this reasoning, bidders who actively bid across multiple retailer storefronts, including AT&T auctions, may not be as readily in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by AT&T’s prices. We see clear evidence for the moderating role of involved multihoming in our analysis.

Our work contributes to the literature in multiple ways. First and foremost, we address the gap in the digital platforms literature pertaining to the ostensible effect of marquee sellers—we focus on the crossside price effects of such sellers. Using a referenceprices framework, we show how marquee sellers can affect prices for other sellers, while controlling for increased supply and demand. Second, we highlight the role of multihoming and involvement, in terms of their moderating effects. Our results go beyond the implications of adding users per se in two-sided platforms. We show that adding new sellers to the platform can have more nuanced effects on prices obtained by other sellers on the platform, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant externality that has not been empirically documented within the literature.

Our work also contributes to research on secondary markets for durable IT products (Elmaghraby et al. 2018). Secondary markets for IT products are important from a societal perspective because they help reduce electronic waste (e-waste) (Tibben-Lembke 2004), direct supply of durable computing equipment to lesserserved parts of the economy, and help extract greater value from functional and usable IT equipment. Our work here helps further understanding of how platforms that enable such secondary markets work, while also providing guidelines for making them more effective. In order to contextualize our work, we <sup>fi</sup>rst describe the B2B liquidation-market auction setting before proposing research hypotheses.

## Research Context

Our work here is set in the B2B liquidation-market auctions for pallets of mobile devices, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally iPhone models. The research site is a leading online intermediary platform that effectively acts as a market-maker, hosting a platform on which big-box retailers create their own independent auction markets to liquidate their excess inventory. In contrast to prior research in secondary markets, where auction sites took possession of merchandise (Pilehvar et al. 2017), our research site only hosts and manages the auction process. Retailers who operate on the auction platform create their own virtual “shopfronts,” where the auctions are hosted. Each pallet contains a bundle of products that are typically of the same category (e.g., mobile phones, laptops, etc.), but not necessarily identical. Depending on the retailer, the pallet composition can vary with respect to the category and number of items included. Neither the research site nor the bidder determines what goes into a pallet. Retailers typically form the pallets and list them online for an auction as rapidly as possible, so as to liquidate their excess inventory as soon as possible (Pilehvar et al. 2017).

In the speci<sup>fi</sup>c case of mobile phones, pallets are typically formed of devices that are of the same operating system, brand, and generation (for instance, Android, Samsung, and Galaxy) with occasional exceptions. Because we focus on iPhones, pallets are typically formed of a number of iPhones of the same generation (e.g., iPhone 6 or iPhone 7), but with varying feature sets. This information is provided in the auction page by each retailer, so as to facilitate bidding by the B2B buyers.

In order to maintain a consistent look and feel for the platform’s bidders, the auction site generates a consistent user interface (shopfront) for different retailers. Although the structure of the user interface is similar across different retailers’ sites, information attributes provided may vary from one auction to another based on the information available to the retailer. All auctions use the second-price auction format, where winners pay the second-highest bid plus the minimum bid increment (Pilehvar et al. 2017).<sup>1</sup> To prevent sniping, the platform automatically extends the end time of the auction if they receive a last-minute bid—that is, the popcorn bidding rule. All bidders are business buyers who must pass through a separate registration process for bidding privileges on each individual retailer’s private auction site in which they wish to bid. They can register across multiple retailer sites; no restrictions are placed on their bidding activities therein. In our data set, all auctions received bids, and no pallets went unsold.<sup>2</sup>

In the context of platforms, the literature has brie<sup>fl</sup>y discussed and provided some de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of what constitutes a marquee seller or user, although a clear and well-accepted de<sup>fi</sup>nition is still lacking. In one such perspective, marquee sellers are those who offer highquality products or premium content in the market, rendering other sellers less attractive (Bedre-Defolie and Nitsche 2020). In multisided platforms (MSPs), marquee sellers are those whose participation adds value by attracting a higher number of new users to the platform (Rochet and Tirole 2003, Eisenmann et al. 2006). Among other attributes, marquee users have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as potential opinion leaders, bring high-quality transactions to the MSP, or serve as very active users (Stummer et al. 2018). Marquee users can also be remarkably big buyers, such as the U.S. government, or high-pro<sup>fi</sup>le entities, such as anchor stores in malls (Eisenmann et al. 2006).

Based upon this literature focused on platforms, and pulling together the different threads that identify marquee players, we can identify the essential aspects of a marquee seller as follows: sellers who offer products that generate high surplus for buyers, through high quality, high visibility, and enhanced brand.

Accordingly, in the mobile phone industry, AT&T is a recognized and leading seller of smartphones in the primary market, with a high reputation and brand name. Hence, treating AT&T as a marquee seller here is consistent with the literature (Johnston 2003, Edelman 2015).

In order to ensure some homogeneity, as well as retain parsimony in our analysis, we study the two largest independent sellers on the platform: Seller A and Seller B (we do not disclose names on request from our research site). Sellers A and B are resellers of a wide range of electronic products in the primary market and are generally not associated with or considered in<sup>fl</sup>uential sellers of smartphones in the primary market. Focusing on two of the largest retailers, outside of the marquee seller (AT&T), allows us to measure the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reference prices on bidding behavior more accurately—in our data set, all multihomers participated in either Seller A or Seller B’s auctions. Although there are more sellers on the platform, they rarely offer pallets of iPhones, compared with Sellers A and B. In the time period of our study, more than 85% of the iPhone pallets offered on the platform came from these two retailers, while other retailers made up the remaining collectively. Therefore, we focus on Sellers A and B, in addition to AT&T as the marquee seller.<sup>3</sup> We now move to reviewing the literature and proposing research hypotheses set in this auction context.

## Literature Review and Research Hypotheses

Our work is positioned at the intersection of three streams of research. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stream studies online two-sided platform markets, whereas the second pertains to research studying the effects of reference prices on consumers’ willingness-to-pay. The third stream focuses on how involvement affects bidder behavior. We review these streams of work next.

## Two-Sided Platforms and Marquee Participants

Two-sided platforms involve two distinct types of users, each of whom obtains value from interacting with users of the opposite type (Rochet and Tirole 2003, Parker and Van Alstyne 2005). In recent years, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant body of literature has sought to understand the strategies required for digital platforms to scale and compete, including studying optimal pricing strategies (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005), business models (Parker et al. 2017, Tian et al. 2018), interactions among competing platforms (Koh and Fichman 2014, Niculescu et al. 2018, Song et al. 2018), matching ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency between buyers and sellers (Hong and Pavlou 2017), multihoming across competing platforms (Rochet and Tirole 2003, Eisenmann et al. 2006, Li and Zhu 2021), platforms’ investment decisions (Anderson et al. 2014), and competition with new entrants to a platform (Wright 2004, Chandra and Collard-Wexler 2009, Zhu et al. 2021).

One factor that has often been suggested to allow a platform to gain traction has pertained to the presence of so-called marquee participants. The effect of marquee participants is based on the observation that “all users of two-sided networks are not created equal. The participation of ‘marquee users’ can be especially important for attracting participants to the other side of the network” (Eisenmann et al. 2006, p. 6). Edelman (2015) argues that the presence of marquee sellers sig nals credibility to buyers, thereby enhancing crossside effects. Indeed, in recent work, Zhang et al. (2021) study the implications of inviting marquee sellers on the supply offered by smaller and lesser-known incumbents in the marketplace who vary in quality. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that the marquee seller’s quality can have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for the welfare of incumbent sellers by in<sup>fl</sup>uencing their supply of products on the platform, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally by making incumbent sellers of similar quality better off relative to dissimilar-quality incumbents.

How does the presence of a marquee seller in<sup>fl</sup>uence prices and buyer behavior on the cross-side? This remains relatively underexplored in the research literature. Prior work has argued that the addition of any sellers on the platform can induce, through same-side network effects, greater supply on the seller side (Zhu et al. 2021), which tends to reduce prices on average (Chandra and Collard-Wexler 2009). Through crossside network effects, adding sellers to the platform can attract more buyers, but the presence of lower transaction costs and low switching costs within the platform environment can reduce prices. Thus, the entry of new sellers and the downward pressure on prices can even result in sellers choosing to leave the market (Caillaud and Jullien 2003, Rochet and Tirole 2003, Chen and Tse 2008). When a marquee seller joins the platform, the cross-side positive network effects kick in because this signals credibility and makes the platform more appealing to buyers on the other side (Edelman 2015).

However, beyond new buyers entering the platform and changing demand, we argue that the marquee seller’s prices serve as new reference prices on the platform, affecting other sellers’ prices in the shortvterm. On platforms where there is considerable uncertainty about the underlying product, bidders or buyers are likely to condition their valuations on information gleaned from other concurrently sold products (Pilehvar et al. 2017). Price information on other comparable products (including those provided by the marquee seller) provide external information cues and operate as anchors, offering benchmark pri ces for the focal products (McGee and Sawyerr 2003,

Mazumdar et al. 2005). Thus, the marquee seller entering the platform not only kickstarts positive cross-side network effects in the medium to long-term, but also immediately provides higher price anchors that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence prices obtained by other sellers already on the platform through the pathway of reference prices. We brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe the literature on reference prices next.

## Reference Prices and Bidders Heterogeneity

Reference prices are benchmarks against which the price of a product or service is judged (Mazumdar et al. 2005). They may be internal to the individual or externally observed within the broader environment. For products of a similar category, externally observed prices provide an alternative standard for the price of the product that is rooted in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context. Buyers make decisions by comparing prices to a set of reference prices that provide external anchors for valuation (Yadav and Seiders 1998, Kamins et al. 2004, Elmaghraby et al. 2018). External reference prices become more in<sup>fl</sup>uential when uncertainty is more salient (Simonson and Drolet 2004).

In the B2B secondary-market context, where we focus our study, buyers face signi<sup>fi</sup>cant uncertainty about the actual contents of the pallets for which they bid (Elmaghraby et al. 2018). In the presence of such uncertainty, bidders are more likely to depend on external information sources, such as the prices observed across the platform for similar products (McGee and Sawyerr 2003, Pilehvar et al. 2017). In the platform we study, we exploit the entry of AT&T—a company with a strong brand name in the mobile phone industry. Upon joining the platform, AT&T’s auction prices are higher, as expected of marquee sellers with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant brands (Rao and Monroe 1989, Teas and Agarwal 2000).<sup>4</sup> Because these prices are also incorporated into the external reference prices for bidders, other sellers of comparable products gain as a result. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1. The reference-price effect from the entry of AT&T positively impacts final prices in other marketplaces for comparable products.

Although Hypothesis 1 argues for an overall effect of AT&T’s entry at the level of an individual product (pallet in this case), we now consider how the marque seller in<sup>fl</sup>uences individual bids by bidders, in addition to <sup>fi</sup>nal prices. Bidders on platforms are heterogeneous with respect to their multihoming activity (Koh and Fichman 2014); they vary in terms of the number of individual retailer marketplaces in which they are registered. We can thus segment bidders into singlehomers—that is, those who operate in one retailer’s marketplace only—and multihomers—that is, those who participate in multiple retailer marketplaces on the platform. We note that this de<sup>fi</sup>nition of multihoming varies slightly from the one commonly observed in the literature. We refer to multihomers as those who choose to register and bid in auctions offered on different retailer storefronts, whereas the de<sup>fi</sup>nition adopted in the literature pertains to users operating on different platforms (Armstrong 2006, Eisenmann et al. 2006, Landsman and Stremersch 2011). Because each retailer operates independently and bidders are expected to register separately for each retailer, we adopt the notion of multihoming, while acknowledging the nuanced differences (Zhu et al. 2021).

In this setting, therefore, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne single-homers as B2B bidders who register and participate (i.e., bid) in a single retailer’s marketplace. By virtue of registering on a seller’s marketplace on the platform, bidders are able to issue bids and receive instant updates (via email) regarding auctions (e.g., current prices and number of placed bids) offered by that speci<sup>fi</sup>c retailer and observe the price dynamics of various auctions in that marketplace. Hence, single-homers observe detailed auction price dynamics in a single marketplace, their home marketplace. Single-homers may also observe aggregate prices in other marketplaces, in which they are not registered (see Figure 1), but do not receive email updates, pallet updates, or detailed information about bid dynamics from these markets.<sup>5</sup>

Multihomers, on the other hand, register at and bid in more than one seller’s marketplace, thereby receiving updates and bid information from each of these individual marketplaces. An advantage of multihoming is that bidders observe a wider range of prices on the platform for concurrent and similar products with little incremental effort, and so are more aware of pri ces from multiple sellers’ marketplaces relative to single-homers. Reference-price effects on individuals exposed to price anchors from multiple marketplaces tend to be aggregate effects, which have greater inertia because they are less likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by any single piece of information (Rajendran and Tellis 1994). Hence, the marginal impact of AT&T’s entry is likely to be lower for multihomers than single-homers because they are exposed to more price signals than single-homers. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2. The reference-price effect on bidders’ willing ness-to-pay is greater for single-homers than multihomers.

## Bidders’ Involvement

To what extent does bidding on AT&T auctions in<sup>fl</sup>uence the reference-price effects we hypothesized about above? To address this question, we study how bidders and <sup>fi</sup>nal prices are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced in the bidders home markets—the auctions hosted by Seller A and/ or Seller B. We classify bidders by virtue of their participation in auctions offered by AT&T into two categories: The <sup>fi</sup>rst set never participate (i.e., never bid on a pallet) in AT&T auctions—that is, they passively observe prices from the marquee seller without any further involvement. This set of bidders can include both single-homers (i.e., buyers who bid in either Seller A or B’s marketplaces) and multihomers (i.e., buyers who bid in both Seller A and B’s marketplaces). The second set of bidders includes multihomers who actively participate (bid) in AT&T auctions and are, thus, aware of the prices and the product types sold by the marquee seller by virtue of their participation activity. We refer to the <sup>fi</sup>rst set as passive bidders, whereas the second set is referred to as active bidders. To understand how participation in AT&T auctions may in<sup>fl</sup>uence how bidders incorporate the potential reference prices set by AT&T auctions in the bids submitted in their home markets, we invoke research in involvement theory (Lee et al. 1999, Chandrashekaran and Grewal 2003, Lo et al. 2013). Recall that reference prices represent price anchors for bidders as they consider suitable bids for the focal pallet —although these external reference prices are easily available, they may not be fully representative of the appropriate value that bidders should ascribe to the focal product. In other words, external reference prices may not necessarily represent the bidder’s value for focal product (Nunes and Boatwright 2004). Products sold by marquee sellers may include a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant premium for the brand, all else being equal, which need not apply to products sold by nonmarquee sellers. Involvement in auctions posted by the marquee seller can, interestingly, lessen the extent to which these price anchors are in<sup>fl</sup>uential on bidders.

Research shows that involvement can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence decision makers’ susceptibility to a variety of common judgment and decision biases (Lee et al. 1999, Chandrashekaran and Grewal 2003, Lo et al. 2013). Involvement here is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree of personal relevance arising from the underlying task, such as a purchase decision. Thus, the act of making a deliberate purchase decision can induce involvement, which leads to greater reasoned and deliberative decision making and less bias (Petty et al. 1983, Zaichkowsky 1985). Applying this logic, we argue that bidding in AT&T auctions in<sup>fl</sup>uences bidders by inducing them to process information from the marquee seller more diligently and fully (Cacioppo and Petty 1979, Richins et al. 1992, Meyers-Levy and Peracchio 1996). Those who do not participate in these auctions are more likely to use simple heuristics in applying these price anchors to their home markets, leading to a more pronounced reference-price effect (Chandrashekaran and Grewal 2003).

In summary, we posit that involved or active individuals scrutinize the information and process central aspects of the price anchors more diligently, speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally evaluating how and why reference prices may vary from their own willingness-to-pay and bids. In contrast, uninvolved or passive individuals tend to process the same information less rigorously, depending upon super<sup>fi</sup>cial aspects—for example, the mere presence of a reference price and other contextual and semantic cues (Chandrashekaran and Grewal 2003). We thus hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. The reference-price effect on bidders’ willingness-to-pay is greater for passive bidders, relative to active bidders.

## Data and Methodology

We use a proprietary data set obtained through a formal research collaboration with the platform provider. We focus only on pure pallets of iPhones, so as to minimize extraneous variation in the pallets and concentrate on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects of interest around the entry of AT&T. The pallets typically carry one speci<sup>fi</sup>c generation of iPhone devices, although individual models can vary. However, there is variation across pallets in terms of the physical condition of the phones, generation, and other attributes of the devices (such as memory size and carrier). This information is provided, albeit in a condensed and nonspeci<sup>fi</sup>c manner, in the auction for each pallet (see Figure 1). As discussed above, because all pallets are sold as-is, it is largely up to the bidder to evaluate the value of the pallet and bid accordingly. It is here that external price anchors are in<sup>fl</sup>uential.

Our data set includes 21,284 bids issued in 3,605 unique auctions for iPhone pallets across the time period January 2014 to July 2017. As mentioned earlier, these auctions are hosted by two retailers, Sellers A and B, with each retailer hosting its own shopfront on the platform, and include 1,241 unique bidders.<sup>6</sup> For each auction, we collect information pertaining to the retailer offering the pallet, the number of units in each pallet, the <sup>fi</sup>nal price of the auction, the number of bidders who participated in the auction, and the date and time of the auction. Additional information on the products in the pallet includes physical condition of the devices, device models and memory sizes, and the telecom carrier associated with the device. A description of the variables in our data set and summary statistics and correlation tables are provided in the online appendix in Tables A1–A4.

Our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy is offered by the exogenous entry of AT&T as the marquee seller to the platform. The platform introduced AT&T as a new seller in January 2017 and started hosting auctions for AT&T iPhone pallets. Auction <sup>fi</sup>nal prices for AT&T pallets were clearly higher than observed on the platform for the two other retailers, as would be expected by virtue of AT&T’s marquee status (see Table A5 in the online appendix for a sample of the price differences). Because our focus is not on AT&T auctions, but, rather, prices obtained for auctions offered by Sellers A and B, the entry of AT&T can be viewed as exogenous for these retailers (entry of retailers on the platform site is the result of private negotiations between the interested parties, and, therefore, the decision of which retailers will enter and when is typically not revealed to other sellers). Therefore, in our analysis, we restrict the sample to only Sellers A and B auctions. We conduct our analysis in stages, as described below.

As a <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we aim to address the baseline research question—does the entry of AT&T lead to higher prices for comparable Sellers A and B auctions, all else being equal? Recall that in this analysis, we con trol for same-side network effects because there are no other new retailers joining or leaving the platform as a seller during our observation window. We use the auction as the unit of analysis here, whereas the dependent variable of interest is the auction <sup>fi</sup>nal price, normalized by the number of devices in the pallet—that is, <sup>fi</sup>nal price per unit—consistent with prior work (Pilehvar et al. 2017, Elmaghraby et al. 2018). The main independ ent variable is the entry of AT&T, captured as a binary variable. The average <sup>fi</sup>nal price per unit in our sample is \$175 (standard deviation (sd) \$125), whereas the average pallet has 73 devices.

After we establish the baseline results, we then move to testing our hypotheses pertaining to how individual bidders may respond to the marquee seller. These analyses are performed at the bid-level for indi vidual bidders. The bid-level analysis quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the difference in the magnitude of the effects among different populations of bidders based on their multihoming and participation activities, thereby addressing Hypotheses 2 and 3. The dependent variable is the value of each bidder’s <sup>fi</sup>nal submitted bid in an auction. The main independent variables are whether a bidder is a singlehomer or multihomer and whether she is an active or passive bidder. Recall that we de<sup>fi</sup>ne active bidders as multihomers that participate in AT&T auctions. The total number of single-homers in our sample is 530, whereas multihomers number $7 1 1 . ^ { 7 } \mathrm { ~ O f ~ }$ these multihomers, 115 have participated in AT&T auctions and are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as active bidders (Figure 3). To further establish that these bidders continue to bid on AT&T’s site—that is, to ensure their active status—we track their bid activity on AT&T auctions for the seven months postentry in our observation period. The proportion of bidders who remain active by bidding in auctions on a monthly basis on AT&T’s site remains high (approximately 80%), as shown in Figure A7 in the online appendix. All active bidders continue to bid on AT&T auctions for multiple months after their <sup>fi</sup>rst observed bid, thereby ensuring their involvement with the AT&T marketplace.<sup>8</sup> Although excluded from the sample for the main analyses in this paper, AT&T initiated 946 unique auctions in the period from January to July of 2017 (roughly 135 auctions per month).

## Econometric Results and Discussion Baseline Results

We start with proposing an econometric model to capture the effect of AT&T’s entry on prices obtained by Sellers A and B on the platform, at the auction level. We model the <sup>fi</sup>nal prices per unit, with the auction as the unit of analysis, as shown below:

$$
F P _ {j k} = \delta_ {o} + \delta_ {1} A t t \_ e n t r y _ {j k} + \gamma X _ {j k} + \tau_ {j} + \lambda_ {k} + \epsilon_ {j k}.\tag{1}
$$

In Equation (1), $F P _ { j k }$ is the <sup>fi</sup>nal price per unit of auction j in marketplace for seller $\bar { k , { \bf \bar { k } } } = \{ { \bf \bar { A } } , { \bf B } \}$ . The main independent variable is Att\_entry, which is a dummy variable that equals one if the auction was posted on the platform after the entry of AT&T, and zero otherwise.

We also include $X _ { j k }$ as a vector of control variables, such as the number of bidders in the auction, number of iPhones (units) in the pallet, and the physical condition, generation, carrier, and memory capacity of the phones. To account for the possible unobserved heterogeneity among sellers, we include marketplace <sup>fi</sup>xed effects (λ<sub>k</sub>). We also control for seasonality by including time (month-year) <sup>fi</sup>xed effects in the model (τ ). Finally, $\epsilon _ { j k }$ are independent and identically distributed random errors. We expect the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of Att\_entry to be positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, showing that there is a systematic increase in <sup>fi</sup>nal prices obtained postentry. Recall that in our setting, the treatment of AT&T’s entry is received by all iPhone resellers (including Sellers A and B) because AT&T’s entry is observed across the platform. In this sense, there is no untreated (control) group per se. The results are shown in Table 1.

Column (1) of Table 1 shows that there is, indeed, a positive effect on <sup>fi</sup>nal prices obtained after the entry of AT&T to the platform (33.63, p < 0.01). Postentry, <sup>fi</sup>nal prices in both Sellers A and B auctions increased, on average, by \$33.6 per unit, ceteris paribus. To con-<sup>fi</sup>rm that this effect is associated with entry of AT&T and the reference-price mechanism, we need to rule out the possible confounding effects of the changing supply of pallets in the two marketplaces—that is, are there fewer or more pallets listed on the platform by Seller A or B as a result of AT&T’s entry? In addition, the entry of a new seller like AT&T could result in positive network effects by attracting new participants/ bidders to the network, resulting in a possible demand increase on the platform. The increase in the number of bidders on the platform could further lead to a rise in within-auction competition among bidders, resulting in higher auction prices. We test for these confounding effects below.

First, we consider the effects of changing supply offered by Sellers A and B on the platform. We com pare the monthly supply of pallets for seven months prior to the entry of AT&T to supply in the seven months postentry. On average, the monthly supply pre-entry was 140 pallets, whereas postentry was roughly 190, across both retailers. We run an ANOVA, where the dependent variable is the monthly supply for each seller, whereas the independent variable is the AT&T entry dummy variable. The analysis shows that the differences in the pre-entry and postentry period are marginally signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p = 0 . 0 7 )$ , with postentry supply being higher than pre-entry. Thus, the marginally higher supply of pallets, when combined with the increased supply from AT&T, should reduce prices (Bapna et al. 2009, Pilehvar et al. 2017), rather than increase prices.

We now consider the possibility of new bidders joining the platform. The total number of unique bidders who bid on auctions hosted by Sellers A and B on the platform before and after AT&T’s entry does not change signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly—that is, there are 486 unique bidders in the seven-month pre-entry period and 435 unique bidders in the seven-month post period, of which 89.7% participated in the seven-month pre-entry period. Furthermore, we consider the ratio of supply to demand on the platform for Sellers A and B iPhone pallets before and after entry. Supply here is the number of pallets, whereas demand is the total number of unique participating bidders on these auctions. Although there are many buyers registered on the platform, we are most interested in those buyers who actively pursue iPhone pallets. In the seven months prior to entry, the supply-todemand ratio is 66.7, whereas the postentry period ratio is 75.4. This increase is due to the increased number of auctions posted postentry, as noted above, rather than an increase in buyers. Again, given the increased supply relative to demand, we expect <sup>fi</sup>nal prices to reduce and not increase, as observed in simultaneous auctions (Bapna et al. 2009, Pilehvar et al. 2017).

## Placebo Testing

Although we treat the entry of AT&T on the platform as exogenous to bidders, additional placebo testing that probes the extent to which the estimated treatment effects are truly valid is warranted (Athey and Imbens 2017, Burtch et al. 2018). In this section, we apply a series of these placebo tests to our context. We <sup>fi</sup>rst consider placebo testing that involves randomly perturbing the “treatment”—that is, the date of AT&T’s entry on the platform—and then examining the resulting treatment effects (Burtch et al. 2018). These placebo tests should ideally yield insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant results because the treatments are false by design. We generate 200 of these randomly assigned placebo dates between February 1 and November 30, 2016 (prior to the observed entry of AT&T on the platform) and use these randomly generated dates to denote the “treated” periods. We conduct the same analysis as shown in Table 1 and plot the resulting coef<sup>fi</sup>cients obtained for the AT&T entry variable in Figure 6. The mean value of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients obtained across the 200 regressions is <sub>−</sub>8.90 (sd <sub>-</sub> 7.84), which is statistically indistinguishable from zero. Note that the original treatment effect we obtained was 33.63 (p < 0.01). This placebo test indicates that the treatment effect we estimate is robust and unlikely to occur from pure chance.

When we plot the 95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals for the 200 coef<sup>fi</sup>cients obtained above, shown in Figure 7, we see that 30.5% of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are statistically significant, with an average effect size of 15.51. Furthermore, these coef<sup>fi</sup>cients largely occurred in placebo regressions where the entry was set in the second half of 2016, suggesting that there may have been an alternative event that occurred during this period. We note that the new iPhone 7 model was introduced by Apple to the primary market on September 16th, 2016. It is possible that the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coef<sup>fi</sup>cients we observed in the placebo test re<sup>fl</sup>ected this event. To account for this potential effect, we created a new con trol variable that took values of one after this date (named “iPhone7\_intro”), and zero before. We reran the placebo tests after including this variable in the analysis, with the results plotted in Figures 8 and 9. The mean value of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient obtained across this new run of 200 placebo treatments reduced to 4.76 (sd 7.89), with less than 5% of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 5% levels, suggesting that the introduction of the new iPhone 7 may have been generating the observed effects.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Platforms play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role throughout the economy, as they minimize transaction costs between market sides. Platforms-based businesses have demonstrated remarkable growth and achieved high <sup>fi</sup>nancial valuations. Scholars have shown signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest by studying multiple factors that impact prices on two-sided platforms, including competition with network effects and switching costs (Rochet and Tirole 2003, Eisenmann et al. 2011), interactions among competing platforms (Koh and Fichman 2014, Niculescu et al. 2018, Song et al. 2018), and competition with new entrants to a platform (Wright 2004, Chandra and Collard-Wexler 2009, Zhu et al. 2021).

In this paper, we investigate how the entry of a new seller with a marquee brand name impacts prices of other sellers for comparable products on the platform. We study this question in the context of B2B secondarymarket auction platforms for IT products, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, mobile devices. We are able to observe the exogenous addition of a high-visibility and high-quality marquee seller to the platform and evaluate its effects on other sellers in the platform. In particular, we exploit the exogenous entry of AT&T, a marquee seller, on the platform as a seller of iPhone devices and study how this entry affects prices obtained by other sellers. The use of proprietary data, combined with an identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy made possible by the entry of AT&T, allows us to provide clear evidence for how reference prices and the level of bidder involvement could in<sup>fl</sup>uence bidders’ willingness to pay in two-sided platforms.

Our work contributes to the literature in multiple ways. First, we extend the literature studying the twosided platform models (Armstrong 2006, Rochet and Tirole 2003, Parker and Van Alstyne 2005). By relying on reference-prices theory, we show that the entry of marquee sellers can positively impact prices of other sellers for comparable products on the platform. We argue and provide evidence that bidders on the platform view AT&T prices as new external anchors that positively impact their willingness-to-pay, resulting in higher auction prices on the platform. We also show that the price effects resulting from the entry of AT&T vary based upon bidders’ multihoming activity on the platform. Relying on involvement theory, we further show that, by virtue of involvement in the source of the effect, bidders are relatively less prone to the reference-price effects. The literature on platforms has argued for the effects of network effects in terms of bringing consumers and sellers to the platform, but there is little work considering the role of price dynamics on the platform as a result of reference prices. In most contexts, teasing out the speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects of adding new sellers or buyers to a platform can be tricky, given the multiple confounding effects that have to be accounted for. In contrast, our analysis is able to use the entry of AT&T, and the associated reference prices, to show how these dynamics affect other sellers on the platform, an externality that has not been clearly established in the literature.

Beyond network effects resulting from the addition of new users to one side of the platform, our work extends this literature by showing that the value of new users depends on their brand quality, which can have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive impact on prices of other users on the platform. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be extended to other industries as well. For instance, in the lodging industry sharing economy, Airbnb has of<sup>fi</sup>cially opened up its platform to allow hotel listings on its site.<sup>13</sup> When a hotel with a marquee brand name lists on the site, local or lower-quality hosts could view the hotel’s prices as new external anchors on the platform. These hosts would potentially increase their prices closer to the new hotel’s prices, resulting in an overall price increase on the platform. These dynamics have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for both the platform owner and other local hotels that may now see interesting externalities in their prices from the marquee seller. Edelman (2015) also argues that marquee sellers may be convinced to exclusively operate on a platform, thereby ensuring exclusivity. It is unclear whether Airbnb will be able to create such a scenario with marquee hotel chains, but the effects of such exclusivity would clearly impact other hosts and hotels on the platform, an interesting research question for future research on supply-side strategies in platforms.

Second, our paper contributes to the context of secondary markets for durable IT products (Tibben-Lembke 2004, Elmaghraby et al. 2018). Enhancing the effectiveness of secondary markets for these products is desirable because they retain value, but also understanding the price dynamics of the B2B market is critical in ensuring the long-run ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of secondary markets for durable IT products. Furthermore, ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and viable secondary markets for IT products have ecological and market value. From an ecological perspective, e-waste that ends up in land<sup>fi</sup>lls causes signi<sup>fi</sup>cant environmental damage, which can be avoided by extending the life of these products (Elmaghraby et al. 2018). The IT industry is renowned for its ability to produce, market, and sell IT equipment, which has justi<sup>fi</sup>ably led to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant economic value. However, the ecological implications of these developments have only started to receive attention in recent years (Tibben-Lembke 2004). If the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts offered by digital platforms can help mitigate some of the high transaction costs that exist in secondary markets for IT products, the payoff for the environment would be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Our work contributes by highlighting how the recruitment of marquee sellers into the secondary-market platform can be used to enhance the prices obtained on these platforms, thereby helping the environment as well. A deeper dive into the link between secondary markets and the environment is out of the scope of this paper, but interested readers are referred to the 2019 Sustainability Report issued by the Consumer Technology Association.<sup>14</sup>

Our work here is subject to certain limitations. First, we conjecture that the underlying difference in the magnitude of the effects between single-homers and multihomers is due to the amount of price information that bidders observe on the platform. However, because of the available data in the market, we cannot directly observe bidder clicks to track how much information bidders are observing on the platform. Second, we do not have visibility into the subsequent resale market to understand how demand in the business-to-consumer (B2C) market could impact bidders willingness-to-pay in the B2B auctions. However, we can account for these using time <sup>fi</sup>xed effects because the broad trends in the B2C market are observed by all players in the B2B market. Third, there may be a case where bidders register in AT&T, but never issue a bid. Our approach to classifying bidders would assign these bidders to the passive category, whereas they would actually receive most of the auction information and price dynamics from AT&T auctions. Because we do not have visibility into such behavior in the absence of any bidding behavior, this remains a limitation.

Finally, we note that our arguments about reference prices relies on the reasonable assumption that bidders are watching other auctions on the platform and, even in the case of passive bidders, are responding to auction dynamics within their own home markets. Of course, it is possible that bidders vary in how much attention they are paying to the platform environment and that in the limiting case, there could exist bidders who do not pay any attention to other bids or auctions (thereby entirely unin<sup>fl</sup>uenced by any reference prices). Given the B2B context, where all bidders are representing active resellers, and the auction mechanism, it is unlikely that bidders here are entirely uninformed about market trends and are limited in their participation on the platform. This remains a maintained assumption in our analysis, but one that is largely justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the B2B context and our discussions with managers at our research site.

There are several managerial implications that emerge from our research. For secondary-market platform providers, we show that the entry of a seller with a marquee brand name has a positive impact on prices of other sellers on the platform. It is imperative for the platform’s provider to understand such price effects to create guidance when targeting new sellers to join the platform. In other words, it is essential to understand how the entry of a major seller could impact average prices on the entire platform. Our paper suggests that platform owners should consider and target sellers with marquee brand names, as they can result in a “sugar rush” for prices of other sellers on the platform. As the overall prices increase on the platform, platform owners can bene<sup>fi</sup>t by generating higher revenue gains. On the opposite side, adding a relatively cheaper seller could negatively pull prices down on the platform. However, these effects can also be mitigated over time through the twin forces of involvement and multihoming. Therefore, the advice to recruit marquee sellers has to be viewed keeping these opposing dynamics in mind. Beyond the network effects per se, intraplatform pricing dynamics continue to be challenging for platform owners because the decentralized model of double-sided platforms tend to take many of these decisions away from platform owners. Being aware of these dynamics, manifesting through reference prices and their dissipation over time, is critical in ensuring that sellers and buyers are appropriately recruited and managed on the platform. We hope that our work here sparks off more work on studying pricing dynamics on platforms, in addition to the more commonly studied elements of network effects and winner-take-all dynamics.
