# On the Same Page? What Users Benefit from a Desktop View on Mobile Devices

Lior Fink,<sup>a,</sup>\* Daniele Papismedov<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. The presentation of online content is routinely adapted to the smaller screens of mobile devices by implementing an information architecture (IA) that hierarchically restructures the information being presented to users, in conjunction with layout and size adaptations. Despite the understanding that users of mobile devices are likely to experience lower usability and higher load without such adaptations, a recent trend is to offer these users the ability to opt out of content adaptation and revert to a desktop view. The prevailing justi<sup>fi</sup>- cation for this trend is the existence of websites that fail to provide an optimal mobile experi ence. In this work, we show that a desktop view may bene<sup>fi</sup>t mobile users by increasing the accuracy of their decisions. Focusing on the difference between a desktop IA, where information is presented on a single page, and a mobile IA, where information is hierarchically distributed across pages, we hypothesize that mobile users exhibit lower decision accuracy when a mobile IA is implemented. We theoretically anchor this hypothesis in the effortaccuracy framework, which views decision behavior as contingent on the trade-off between effort and accuracy. This hypothesis is supported in two laboratory experiments in which participants are faced with the multiattribute choice task of making an online hotel reservation. The experiments also highlight a boundary condition for the negative effect of the mobile IA on decision accuracy, depending on whether the mobile IA is congruent with user preferences. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that content adaptation on mobile devices deserve greater research attention, particularly from the perspective of adaptive decision behavior.

## 1. Introduction

The widespread use of mobile devices has motivated providers of online services to adapt the presentation of information on their websites to the smaller screens with which mobile users interact. Responsive web design, which optimizes the layout of a web page for the user’s screen dimensions and orientation to provide an optimal viewing experience, has consequently become ubiquitous (Marcotte 2011, Nogueira et al. 2019). Although content adaptation is commonly implemented by modifying the layout and size of information items to <sup>fi</sup>t mobile screens, these smaller screens generally cannot effectively present the same information that can be conveniently presented on larger desktop screens. Therefore, an integral part of content adaptation, in particular when a large volume of information needs to be presented, is to apply an information architecture (IA) that hierarchically restructures the information presented on a single page for desktop use to be presented on multiple pages for mobile use (McVicar 2012, Kardys 2014).

Content adaptation has become the norm because of the understanding that mobile users are likely to experience lower usability and higher information load unless the presentation of information is adapted to the smaller screen sizes of their mobile devices (Lee and Benbasat 2003, Schlick et al. 2009, Chen et al. 2014). However, despite this understanding, a recent trend is to offer users of mobile devices the ability to opt out of content adaptation and revert to a desktop view, in a way similar to the <sup>fl</sup>exibility users had to choose their preferred view before responsive design became the norm. A prominent example is such an option (“desktop site”) in the settings of the popular browser Google Chrome. Some websites, such as tha of the Association for Information Systems eLibrary (https://aisel.aisnet.org), allow mobile users to switch back and forth from mobile view to desktop view via a <sup>fl</sup>oating button. A common rationale for the availability of this option is the need to interact with websites that do not provide an optimal experience on mobile devices (e.g., Advit 2020, Unadkat 2020). In this paper, we highlight a potential bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the desktop view on mobile devices that, to the best of our knowledge, has yet to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed or empirically demonstrated. Focusing on the use of a hierarchica IA in content adaptation, we suggest that the presentation of the same information on a single page (labeled here as “desktop IA”) rather than on multiple pages (labeled here as “mobile IA”) may cause mobile users to make more accurate decisions. Although a mobile IA merely spreads the same information over multiple pages and although mobile users can navigate across pages to acquire this information, the higher effort involved in such behavior may cause mobile users to base their decisions on less information relative to a situation in which a desktop IA presents the same information on a single page. From a theoretical standpoint, our explanation draws on the effort-accuracy framework, which views decision behavior as contingent on a compromise between the desire to minimize cognitive effort and the desire to make an accurate decision (Shugan 1980, Russo and Dosher 1983, Thomas 1983, Johnson and Payne 1985, Creyer et al. 1990, Stone and Schkade 1991, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995). The underlying mechanism is that a mobile IA requires users to expend more cognitive effort than that required by a desktop IA to attain the same level of accuracy. In search of a better balance between effort and accuracy, users with a mobile IA reduce effort by employing decision strategies that are more heuristic in nature, saving cognitive effort at the cost of less accurate decisions.

Given this focus, we seek to answer the following research question. Do users of mobile devices make more accurate decisions with a desktop IA than with a mobile IA? This question is addressed in two laboratory experiments. The decision-making task in the experiments is the classical multiattribute choice task, wherein a consumer is asked to choose an alternative (brand) on the basis of an information matrix of alternatives and attributes (Jacoby et al. 1974, Payne 1976, Malhotra 1982, Keller and Staelin 1987, Creyer et al. 1990, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995, Chu and Spires 2000). Because decisions in this task are made on the basis of individual preferences (Gigerenzer 2004), decision accuracy is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which users make decisions that are consistent with their preferences. The experimental platform is an online website, developed speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for this work, that simulates the process of booking a hotel room online. The mobile IA is <sup>fi</sup>xed across participants in the <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment and manipulated in the second experiment as either congruent or incongruent with user preferences. In both experiments, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that decision accuracy is lower with a mobile IA. We suggest that an effort-accuracy mechanism is at play by showing that cognitive effort is also lower with a mobile IA, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by partial information search, shorter decision time, and lower perceived information load when the information about alternatives and attributes is presented on multiple pages. Although it could be expected that users would exert more effort when the same information is distributed across pages, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that they exert less effort. Whereas we do not observe a behavior of higher effort to maintain accuracy with the congruent mobile IA, we do observe such behavior with the incongruent mobile IA, suggesting the existence of a boundary condition for the decrease in decision accuracy arising from the mobile IA.

This work contributes to both research and practice in several ways. First, an important contribution is extending the effort-accuracy literature on the consequences of information display to include IA as an important concept for understanding contingent deci sion behavior. Against the focus of this literature on the content and format of the information being dis played (e.g., Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Vessey 1994, Creyer and Ross 1997, Hess et al. 2005, Jiang and Punj 2010, Mertens et al. 2020), we demonstrate that the effort-accuracy equilibrium at the basis of decision behavior may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a simple restructuring of the same information in the same format across pages. Moreover, we show that the equilibrium can shift in different directions contingent on whether the resulting structure is congruent with user preferences, proposing that this variable is consequential for understanding adaptive decision behavior. Second, this work advances the literature on mobile use by highlighting a possible negative consequence of content adaptation related to the hierarchical IA commonly applied to allow large volumes of information to be conveniently presented on smaller mobile screens. Although research on mobile use has justi<sup>fi</sup> ably paid greater attention to the aspects of ubiquity and usability that characterize the use of mobile devices (Lee and Benbasat 2003, Venkatesh and Ramesh 2006, Bang et al. 2013, Xu et al. 2017), content adaptation has generally been treated as a natural by-product of mobile device use rather than as a key feature by itself. A question that has remained largely unanswered is the prices that users pay for the convenience of content adaptation, and this work takes an important step in addressing this question, despite its narrow focus on the hierarchical IA commonly applied in content adaptation. Finally, from a practical standpoint, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of both experiments suggest that the increasing availability of the option to switch to a desktop view on mobile devices is a positive evolution that should be encouraged, particularly in tasks in which decision accuracy is important to users.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Mobile Devices, Adaptation, and IA

Considerable attention has been devoted in recent years to the study of the behavioral consequences of mobile device use (Pousttchi et al. 2015, Lee and Gopal 2016, Ghose 2017, Lurie et al. 2018, Jung et al. 2019), in particular the consequences for consumer behavior (Wang et al. 2015, Raphaeli et al. 2017, Rodr´ıguez-Torrico et al. 2017, Lee et al. 2020) and user-generated content (Burtch and Hong 2014, Piccoli and Ott 2014, Melumad et al. 2019, Ransbotham et al. 2019). Research has highlighted two distinguishing characteristics of mobile devices relative to desktops; users are mobile (ubiquity) and interact with smaller screens (usability) (Lee and Benbasat 2003, Bang et al. 2013, Xu et al. 2017). Although ubiquity is considered advantageous for users, lower usability is considered a disadvantage of mobile devices (Kim et al. 2011, Napoli and Obar 2014). Several studies empirically investigated the negative consequences of using smaller screens. Ghose et al. (2013) showed that ranking effects were higher (i.e., links that appeared at the top of the screen were more likely to be clicked) on mobile devices, suggesting that smaller screen sizes increased information search costs. Smaller screens were also shown to impair reading comprehension (Singh et al. 2011), survey completion (Lambert and Miller 2015), video-based learning (Maniar et al. 2008), and navigation (Dillemuth 2009).

Given these negative consequences, human-computer interaction (HCI) research has been interested in how the display of information on mobile devices can be adapted to their smaller screens to improve usability and performance (Lee and Benbasat 2003, Sweeney and Crestani 2006, Adipat et al. 2011, Raptis et al. 2013, Hoehle and Venkatesh 2015). In the cell phone era, when screens were smaller and restricted to line-based interfaces, HCI researchers primarily explored how menus should be structured given the limited number of lines users could see without scrolling. Given this limitation, it was important to understand the trade-off between the breadth (amount of information per page) and depth (number of pages) of menus (Chae and Kim 2004). Menus had to be adapted to avoid the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant deterioration in performance when using mobile phonesized interfaces relative to desktop interfaces (Jones et al. 2003), and adaptive menus were shown to have a larger positive impact on user performance and satisfaction with small screens compared with large screens (Findlater and McGrenere 2008). To maximize the utility of the limited space, simplicity was repeatedly emphasized as an important design principle for mobile interfaces (Choi and Lee 2012, Lee et al. 2015).

Although the more advanced and larger screens of the smartphone era allow users to view website con tent in the same way it is presented on desktop screens, it has quickly become accepted that content adaptation is necessary to improve usability and reduce the information load on users (Lee and Benba sat 2003, Schlick et al. 2009, Chen et al. 2014). Content adaptation is commonly realized through the imple mentation of responsive web design, which adapt the presentation of a web page for the user’s screen dimensions and orientation to provide an optima viewing experience (Marcotte 2011, Nogueira et al. 2019). Content adaptation is a broad concept that encompasses all aspects of the presentation, including the layout of information items on the screen, as wel as their size and shape. Naturally, however, if the amount of information per page is unchanged despite the smaller screen, users will have to interact with smaller text and images or to scroll excessively. There fore, particularly when large volumes of information need to be presented, content adaptation is realized by implementing an IA that hierarchically restructures the information presented on a single page for desktop use to be presented on multiple pages for mobile use (Kim et al. 2005, Fling 2009, Hoivik 2013, Jogoo et al. 2019). Generally, an IA is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a blueprin and navigational aid to the content of information rich systems” (Toms 2002, p. 855). The mobile IA commonly implemented in practice are hierarchica tree (a standard site structure with an index page and a series of subpages), nested doll (leads users in a lin ear fashion from broad overview pages to detai pages), hub and spoke (a central index from which users will navigate out), bento box (brings more detailed content directly to the index screen by using dynamic components to display portions of related content), and <sup>fi</sup>ltered view (allows the user to navigate within a set of data by selecting <sup>fi</sup>lter options to create an alternative view) (McVicar 2012, Kardys 2014). Common to all these mobile IAs is the restructuring of information from a <sup>fl</sup>at, single-page structure (suitable for larger screens of desktops) to a hierarchical, multi page structure (suitable for smaller screens of mobile devices). Typically, mobile IAs do not reduce the amount of information available to mobile users but rather, divide the information in a hierarchical man ner across pages to enhance usability (Kim et al. 2005). An illustration of the differences between mobile and desktop IAs is presented in Figure 1.

## 2.2. Effort-Accuracy Framework: How Information Display Affects Decision Behavior

A mainstream approach in decision research has been to investigate the extent to which information processing in decision making is contingent on characteristics of the task and context, consistent with considerable evidence showing the sensitivity of judgment and choice to seemingly minor changes in such characteristics (Einhorn and Hogarth 1981, Payne 1982, Payne et al. 1993). A central theoretical framework that explains adaptive decision behavior is the effortaccuracy framework, which views decision behavior as contingent on a compromise between the desire to minimize cognitive effort and the desire to maximize decision accuracy (Shugan 1980, Russo and Dosher 1983, Thomas 1983, Johnson and Payne 1985, Creyer et al. 1990, Stone and Schkade 1991, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995). This framework suggests that decision makers unnecessarily aim at maximizing the accuracy of their decisions because of the considerable cognitive effort involved in such a decision strategy. Instead, the balance between the effort and accuracy resulting from different decision strategies determines which strategy is employed by the decision maker. Although the literature describes numerous decision strategies, including lexicographic, satis<sup>fi</sup>cing, and elimination by aspects strategies (Payne et al. 1988), it generally distinguishes between normative strategies, which arrive at the optimal decision in a rational manner, and heuristic strategies, which save cognitive effort by using simplifying shortcuts that lead to suboptimal decisions (Gigerenzer 2004). The effortaccuracy framework suggests that a heuristic strategy may be preferred over a systematic (normative)

Among the task and context characteristics identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed as responsible for contingent decision behavior, information display has repeatedly been shown to affect decision behavior (Payne 1982). A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant stream of research has empirically investigated the effects of various information display characteristics on decision outcomes that re<sup>fl</sup>ect effort and accuracy. The fundamental approach of such research has been to demonstrate that variance in how alternatives are presented in decision tasks leads to variance in decision behavior. Early decision-making studies showed that different presentations of gambles resulted in different choices, although the gambles were equivalent in terms of <sup>fi</sup>nal outcomes and probabilities (Payne and Braunstein 1971, Aschenbrenner 1978). Studies that observed decision behavior in multiattribute choice tasks focused on two information display characteristics: the organization of information items into either a matrix or a list (Bettman and Kakkar 1977, Bettman and Zins 1979, Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995) and the form of information items as either numbers or words (Stone and Schkade 1991, Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Jiang and Punj 2010). Additional characteristics examined in these studies were the sequence of information items as either sorted or random (Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Jiang and Punj 2010), vividness of information (Hess et al. 2005), salience of attributes (Jiang and Punj 2010), and presentation of information as either numbers or graphs (Kreiner and Gamliel 2017). Information systems researchers have similarly relied on the effort-accuracy framework to study the effects of information display characteristics, such as the format of graphs (bar charts) as matrix, alternative based, or attribute based (Jarvenpaa 1989); the presentation of information as either graph or table (Vessey 1994); and the format of information as matrix or list (Hong et al. 2004). Finally, the effects of information display on decision behavior have inspired studies on the consequences of decision aids (Todd and Benbasat 1994, Chu and Spires 2000, Haubl and Trifts¨ 2000, Swaminathan 2003, Hess et al. 2005, Lee and Benbasat 2011, Xu et al. 2014), value indices (Creyer and Ross 1997), and attribute translations (Mertens et al. 2020).

Common to these studies is the underlying reasoning that speci<sup>fi</sup>c information displays require decision makers to invest more effort than that required by alternative information displays in order to attain the same level of accuracy. In search of a better balance between effort and accuracy, decision makers frequently reduce effort dramatically by employing decision strategies that are more heuristic in nature and that involve partial information processing. Accuracy consequently suffers, but the decrease in accuracy may be less dramatic than the decrease in effort, implying that a better balance between the two outcomes is achieved at lower levels. In the case of verbal versus numerical information, for example, calculating differences within attributes is likely to be more effortful with words than with numbers (Stone and Schkade 1991). If so, then strategies that make heavy use of differences are less likely to be employed when the information is verbal, resulting in lower effort but also, lower accuracy. We extend this reasoning to study how decision behavior is affected by the IA, which has become an important information display characteristic because of the ubiquity of mobile devices. In so doing, this work extends the effort-accuracy framework while contributing to the literature on mobile use.

## 3. Research Hypothesis

As noted, a fundamental element of content adaptation, beyond modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of layout, size, and orientation to <sup>fi</sup>t mobile screens, is the implementation of an IA that segments information that can <sup>fi</sup>t in a single desktop page into multiple smaller pages (Kim et al. 2005, Hoivik 2013, Charlton 2015, Downs 2019, Jogoo et al. 2019). Although there are various approaches to desktop page segmentation for mobile devices (Roudaki et al. 2015), this segmentation typically follows a hierarchical structure (as illustrated in Figure 1), where primary information is presented <sup>fi</sup>rst on an overview page, from which users can navigate to pages with secondary detailed information (McVicar 2012, Kardys 2014). A common guideline in industry is that with mobile “less is more” and that “how many results you display and how much information you provide about each result is the difference between information overload and a good user experience” (Cerdan and Daher 2020). Industrial user experience research prescribes that the number of items on product lists should be between 50 and 100 for desktop sites and between 15 and 30 for mobile sites (Hugo 2020).

The focus of this work is on the behavioral consequences of this speci<sup>fi</sup>c difference between desktop and mobile IAs. Namely, we empirically investigate how decision accuracy, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the performance of users in decision-making tasks, is affected by the hierarchical segmentation of a larger desktop page into smaller mobile pages. Such segmentation requires users to navigate through multiple pages to acquire the same information they can acquire on a single page in the absence of any segmentation. Importantly, mobile users are not exposed to less information following the implementation of a mobile IA. However, the presentation of less information per page requires more pages and greater between-page navigation (Chae and Kim 2004). Trading within-page browsing for between-page browsing leads to an increase in task complexity (Adipat et al. 2011). In a study about the implications of a small display for map use and spatial knowledge acquisition, Dillemuth (2009) demonstrated that when the user can view only a discrete section of a map at any time instead of seeing the entire area of interest, performance is hindered with respect to accuracy. Schlick et al. (2009) discussed the con<sup>fl</sup>icting visual and cognitive demands for mobile displays, suggesting that there is a trade-off between visibility and cognitive performance; the smaller the amount of information presented on each screen is, the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it is for users to orientate themselves properly. Xu et al. (2017) maintained that the large screens of desktops provide an edge over mobile devices in that they allow users to maintain a global perspective on their shopping tasks by facilitating the comparison of product characteristics of multiple goods.

From a cognitive standpoint, the distribution of information across pages implies that users can only integrate the complete information in their minds instead of being able to view it on a single page. Users can only view a segment of this information at a time while storing information segments they already viewed in their working memory. This necessity to engage in information storing and retrieval to mentally construct the complete information instead of having this information readily visible on a single page implies that mobile users need to exert more cognitive effort following the implementation of a mobile IA. The additional effort can be considerable if users aim at high decision accuracy (i.e., they seek to make optimal decisions) and need to acquire a large amount of information. Even then, their efforts may not yield the desired outcome because of the very limited capacity of working memory (Miller 1956, Luck and Vogel 1997), which is critical to the ability of users to mentally integrate the acquired information. Information integration efforts may also be hindered by the tendency of decision makers to discount or ignore information that has to be stored in memory, inferred from the explicit display, or transformed (Slovic 1972).

Users of mobile devices may, therefore, need to “adapt their information acquisition and decision strategies to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the amount of information that can be accessed simultaneously” (Lurie et al. 2010, p. 459). The effort-accuracy framework suggests that the need to exert greater cognitive effort to maintain the same level of accuracy because of the segmentation of information across pages will motivate users to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of their decision behavior by employing decision strategies that better balance effort and accuracy. Although the framework considers decision strategies as re<sup>fl</sup>ecting a spectrum of responses to diverse contextual demands, it prescribes that a better balance between effort and accuracy can be achieved by using decision strategies that are more heuristic in nature and require the processing of less information. We build on this framework to propose that users respond to the need arising from the mobile IA to exert greater effort for the same accuracy by employing decision strategies that sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce accuracy to improve the balance between effort and accuracy. Although accuracy is lower, the decrease in accuracy is less dramatic than the decrease in effort, implying a better balance at lower levels of both. For example, similar to <sup>fi</sup>ndings when information is verbal rather than numerical (Stone and Schkade 1991), users may respond to the segmentation of information across pages by preferring decision strategies that rely less heavily on compensatory processing (i.e., attributes can compensate for one another), which is generally associated with normative decision behavior. Such heuristic strategies, like the lexicographic strategy, base decisions on information about few or even a single attribute, reducing processing demands by ignoring potentially relevant information (Payne et al. 1988). Common to such heuristic strategies is that they primarily serve the purpose of reducing effort by allowing decisions to be made more quickly and based on less information relative to the normative standard (Gigerenzer 2004, Shah and Oppenheimer 2008). Consequently, because it is challenging to directly observe that heuristics are being employed, the information-processing approach to decision making has emphasized the need to monitor information search behavior and response time to conclude that effort-reducing mechanisms are at play (Payne and Bettman 2004). Consistent with this line of reasoning, we suggest that the implementation of a mobile IA requires mobile users to adapt their decision behavior to a context that entails an inferior trade-off between effort and accuracy. Mobile users are able to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of their decision behavior in this more cognitively demanding context by favoring strategies that are more heuristic in nature, implying that decisions are made more quickly on the basis of less information. Because the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of reduced effort comes at the cost of lower accuracy, we hypothesize that decision accuracy is lower with a mobile IA than with a desktop IA.

Hypothesis 1. Mobile users exhibit lower decision accuracy when a mobile IA is implemented than when a desktop IA is implemented.

## 4. Experiment 1

We tested our hypothesis by relying to the extent possible on methodologies, procedures, and measures from the decision-making literature. Accordingly, we asked users to perform a decision-making task in a laboratory setting, which controlled for environmental conditions that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the behavior of mobile users. The decision-making task was the classical multiattribute choice task, in which a consumer is asked to choose an alternative on the basis of a predetermined information matrix of alternatives and attributes (Jacoby et al. 1974, Payne 1976, Malhotra 1982, Keller and Staelin 1987, Creyer et al. 1990, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995, Chu and Spires 2000). The com mon dependent variable (DV) in multiattribute choice is decision accuracy, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which consumers make decisions that are consistent with their preferences. The <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment aimed at testing the hypothesis with a <sup>fi</sup>xed mobile IA, before testing in the second experiment whether user behavior was affected by variance in the mobile IA.

## 4.1. Participants and Design

Sixty-three undergraduate engineering students (66.7% female, mean age of 23.98 years with a standard deviation of 1.14 years) at a leading Israeli university participated in experiment 1 as users of mobile devices in return for extra credit. The experiment used a within-subjects design, as each participant performed two multiattribute choice tasks: one with a desktop IA and another with a mobile IA. The order of the tasks was counterbalanced, implying that participants were randomly assigned to whether a mobile

IA was implemented in the <sup>fi</sup>rst or second task. The within-subjects design was consistent with the common approach in previous studies to ask participants to perform several multiattribute choice tasks that varied in the amount or display of information (Payne 1976, Creyer et al. 1990, Stone and Schkade 1991, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995). Keller and Staelin (1987) con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that it is possible to ask each participant to complete several multiattribute choice tasks without introducing bias.

The experimental platform was an online website developed speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for this study. The website simulated the process of booking a hotel room online. While participants were asked to use the website to book a hotel room, they were practically performing a multiattribute choice task with a predetermined information matrix of alternatives and attributes. For the decision-making process to be as realistic as possible, we performed two preliminary steps, designed to choose the set of attributes and set of alternatives for the choice tasks. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step was a survey of 36 respondents (from the same target population of undergraduate students), aimed at identifying which attributes have the greatest value to users in choosing a hotel room. The survey presented 16 common attributes of a hotel double room and asked respondents to rate the importance of each attribute to them on a seven-point Likert-type scale. The eight attributes chosen for the study were those that received the highest importance means in the survey. In descending order of importance, the eight attributes were price per night, distance from city center, location score, general score, free access to Wi-Fi, cleanliness score, inclusion of a free breakfast, and number of stars (the three score attributes were based on consumer reviews).

The second preliminary step was to generate a set of alternatives that would be as realistic as possible and that would re<sup>fl</sup>ect real-world trade-offs among attributes. The alternatives presented to participants were based on search results from Booking.com for a one-night double room in London. We sorted the search results by price, randomly sampled 1 of each 16 results (to representatively sample the results), and collected data about the eight attributes of interest for each sampled alternative. This procedure yielded a matrix of 110 alternatives by eight attributes. To avoid having a biased sample because of speci<sup>fi</sup>c observations, we next averaged the attribute values for each block of <sup>fi</sup>ve sequential alternatives, yielding a matrix of 22 alternatives by eight (averaged) attributes. These 22 alternatives were then divided into two sets, with odd alternatives in one set and even alternatives in another set, thereby ensuring that the two sets for the two within-subjects tasks were different yet equivalent.<sup>1</sup>

## 4.2. Procedure

The experiment was performed in a university laboratory. Participants were asked to access the website developed for the study with their own smartphones. Upon entering the website, the preferences of partici pants were elicited by asking them to <sup>fi</sup>rst evaluate the relative importance (weight) of the eight attributes for them by dividing a total of 100 points among the attributes (they could not proceed if the sum of eight weights was different from 100). For each attribute that received a positive weight (above zero) from a participant, the participant was asked to select a preferred value from a range of possible real-world values. We asked for preferred values because people unnecessarily prefer the highest or lowest values (e.g., they unnecessarily prefer the cheapest hotel room). This procedure of preference elicitation was consistent with those used in previous decision-making studies (e.g., Jacoby et al. 1974, Malhotra 1982, Keller and Staelin 1987).

Next, participants were asked to perform two sequential multiattribute choice tasks, one with a desktop IA and another with a mobile IA (counterbalanced to prevent sequence effects). In each task, participants were presented with an “overview page” that included a list of 11 alternatives of possible hotel rooms (the alternatives were randomly ordered within the list), from which they were asked to choose their preferred alternative. The alternatives were clickable, leading to a “detail page” for each alternative with the complete information (all eight attributes) for that alternative. Participants could choose their preferred alternative from any page, including the overview page and detail pages. The design of the website was relatively simple but nevertheless, aimed at simulating similar real-life websites to the extent possible.

Mobile IA was operationalized by implementing a simple hierarchical structure, which is at the basis of most mobile IAs implemented in practice, as illustrated in Figure 1. Consistent with how products and services are listed when searching on popular commercial websites, the mobile IA was implemented by hierarchically restructuring the information about the attributes for each alternative. In the desktop IA, information about all eight attributes was presented on the overview page, implying that the complete information (11 alternatives by eight attributes) was available to participants on a single page. Participants could access detail pages to isolate the information about speci<sup>fi</sup>c alternatives, but no new information was available on those pages. In the mobile IA, information about only three of the eight attributes—price per night, distance from city center, and number of stars (the two most important attributes and the least important attribute according to the preliminary survey)—was presented on the overview page, implying that partial information (11 alternatives by three attributes) was available to participants on the overview page.<sup>2</sup> In this case, accessing a detail page for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c alternative could allow participants to acquire information about all eight attributes for that alternative. Although complete information was available on the overview page with the desktop IA, the mobile IA required participants to go back and forth from the overview page to all 11 detail pages to acquire the complete information. Snapshot examples of overview pages with different IAs and a detail page are presented in Figure 2. The potential implications of this study from a design science perspective are discussed in Online Appendix A.

Consistent with the literature (Shugan 1980, Russo and Dosher 1983, Vessey 1994), the DV of decision accuracy was actually measured as decision inaccuracy (i.e., error) de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the distance (in number of superior alternatives) of the observed choice (the alternative chosen by the participant) from the normative choice (the best alternative given the preferences elicited from the participant at the beginning of the experiment). To rank the 11 alternatives for each participant, we applied the weighted additive (WADD) strategy, which maximizes the sum of the products of attribute values and relative weights.<sup>3</sup> Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we computed an overall distance score for each alternative from the “ideal” choice (not included in the alternative set) given the participant’s attribute weights and preferred values elicited at the beginning of the experiment. For each alternative, we summed the eight products of multiplying the attribute weight by the absolute difference (standardized across attributes) between attribute value and preferred value to obtain an overall distance score from the “ideal” choice. Thus, alternatives with larger distances were those in which there were larger differences from preferred values in relatively important attributes. Then, the 11 alternatives could be ranked for each participant from best (smallest distance) to worst (largest distance). To minimize the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of actual attribute values, we measured decision inaccuracy as the number of alternatives in the alternative set that were superior (closer to preferences) to that chosen for the speci<sup>fi</sup>c participant (i.e., the superior alternatives “missed” by the participant). Consequently, decision inaccuracy was an integer that ranged from 0 (the best alternative was chosen) to 10 (the worst alternative was chosen). Consistent with our methodological approach, we followed the literature as closely as possible in measuring the distance between observed and normative choices (Jacoby et al. 1974, Malhotra 1982, Keller and Staelin 1987).

To validate that an effort-accuracy mechanism could be at the basis of the decision behavior we observed, we employed three measures of effort common in the decision-making literature. In a comprehensive analysis of effort in choice, Bettman et al. (1990) advocated the use of both behavioral and cognitive models of effort. Consequently, we assessed the effort of participants through both objective behavioral measures and subjective cognitive measures (Jiang and Punj 2010). First, effort is frequently measured by observing actual search behavior, such as the amount of available information searched (i.e., number or percentage of information items accessed) for a particular decision (Payne 1976, Creyer et al. 1990, Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Chu and Spires 2000, Haubl and¨

Therefore, we measured information search as the number of detail pages that the participant accessed before making a choice (a speci<sup>fi</sup>c page could be counted more than once if accessed more than once). Second, another common behavioral measure of effort is the time it takes to make a decision (Creyer et al. 1990, Stone and Schkade 1991, Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995, Payne and Bettman 2004). Consequently, we measured decision time as the time elapsed (in seconds) from the presentation of the overview page until the participant made a choice. Third, to capture effort from a subjective standpoint, self-report measures of perceived information load are advocated in the literature to better capture the effort actually experienced by decision makers rather than the volume of information they are required to process. Therefore, we measured perceived information load with an item that asked the participant to provide an estimate (on a seven-point Likert-type scale) of “the extent to which information load was experienced during the choice task,” consistent with previous studies that used a single similar self-report item to measure the effort experienced by decision makers (Bettman and Kakkar 1977, Milord and Perry 1977, Bettman et al. 1990, Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Fennema and Kleinmuntz 1995, Majchrzak et al. 2005, Jiang and Punj 2010).

Finally, data about participant gender and age were collected at the end of the experiment to control for these variables in data analysis. The participant was also asked about the device usually used to perform similar tasks to control for mobile literacy. To control for differences in participant preferences, we computed for each participant the standard deviation (STD) of the eight attribute weights provided by the participant at the preference elicitation stage. A low preference STD value suggested that the participant perceived the attributes as relatively equal in importance, whereas a high value suggested that some attributes were perceived as considerably more important than others.

## 5. Experiment 2

In experiment 1, the three attributes presented on the overview page for participants using the mobile IA were <sup>fi</sup>xed. These attributes were relatively informative to participants, as they included the two most important attributes, on average, according to the preliminary survey (price per night and distance from city center). Experiment 2 aimed at replicating experiment 1 while varying the degree to which the attributes presented on the overview page with the mobile IA were congruent with user preferences. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we were interested in examining whether an incongruent mobile IA (i.e., the most informative attributes were presented only on detail pages) would cause participants to pursue a different equilibrium of accuracy and effort than that found for the congruent mobile IA (i.e., the most informative attributes were presented on the overview page). In so doing, we examined whether participants with the mobile IA would be consistent in their less effortful and accurate behavior even when the more important attributes for them were less immediately accessible and required navigation to detail pages. Our aim was to create a situation in which heuristic processing was less effective and to examine whether participants would consequently change their decision behavior, thereby seeking to uncover in experiment 2 a boundary condition for the decision behavior observed in experiment 1.

## 5.1. Participants, Design, and Procedure

Eighty-four undergraduate engineering students (42.9% female, mean age of 25.29 years with a standard deviation of 1.68 years) who did not participate in experiment 1 participated in experiment 2 in return for extra credit. Experiment 2 replicated experiment 1 in all methodological aspects, including instruments, design, and procedure, but with the congruence of the mobile IA to user preferences as a between-subjects variable. This manipulation had no impact on the task performed with the desktop IA because the overview page in that condition presented the complete information (11 alternatives by eight attributes). By contrast, this manipulation in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the three attributes presented on the overview page with the mobile IA. In the congruent mobile IA condition, the overview page included information about the three attributes that were rated by the participant as most important (received the highest weights in the preference elicitation stage). In the incongruent mobile IA condition, the overview page included information about the three attributes that were rated by the participant as least important (received the lowest weights). Participants were randomly assigned to either congruent or incongruent mobile IA condition.

## 5.2. Results and Discussion

Similar to experiment 1, the unit of analysis in experiment 2 was the decision made by a participant in a task (N 168). We again tested Hypothesis 1 by estimating the same mixed-effects Poisson regression model with decision inaccuracy as the outcome variable. Although this model included the same <sup>fi</sup>xed and random effects, the use of three different IAs— desktop, congruent mobile, and incongruent mobile— implied that two coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, instead of one, were estimated to test the effects of the IA on decision inaccuracy. The regression results, presented in Table 2, showed that both congruent and incongruent mobile IAs signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased decision inaccuracy (i.e., decreased decision accuracy) relative to the desktop IA. Therefore, we concluded that Hypothesis 1 was supported irrespective of whether the three most or least important attributes to the participant were presented on the overview page with the mobile IA.<sup>5</sup> Additionally, the results showed that higher decision inaccuracy was found when the task was the <sup>fi</sup>rst, consistent with a learning effect, and when the participant had a tendency to use mobile devices for such tasks. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggested that mobile literacy could be associated with more heuristic processing.

Similar to experiment 1, we again examined whether effort also varied as a result of IA manipulations by estimating three mixed-effects OLS regression models with information search, decision time, and perceived information load as outcome variables. The results for all models are presented in Table 2, and estimated marginal means are depicted in Figure 4. The results showed that although information search with both congruent and incongruent mobile IAs was higher than that with the desktop IA, information search was much higher in the incongruent case. This <sup>fi</sup>nding was consistent with an architecture that presented less informative attributes on the overview page, requiring participants to navigate to detail pages more excessively. Presumably because of this excessive information search, participants with the incongruent mobile IA needed more time to reach a decision and experienced higher information load relative to participants with the congruent mobile IA. Although less effort was observed with the congruent mobile IA than with the desktop IA, consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of experiment 1, effort levels with the incongruent mobile IA were similar to those with the desktop IA (information search with the desktop IA was naturally lower because the complete information was available on the overview page). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggested that decision behavior with the mobile IA was sensitive to the congruence of the attributes presented on the overview page with user preferences, despite the <sup>fi</sup>xed hierarchical structure. Evidently, participants sought to compensate for the incongruence of the mobile IA with their preferences by increasing their effort. As can be seen in Figure 4, the incongruent mobile IA was similar in (higher) effort to the desktop IA but similar in (lower) accuracy to the congruent mobile IA. From an effort-accuracy perspective, the incongruent mobile IA implied the worst structure among the three conditions because information was segmented across pages and the overview page was uninformative. Consequently, the effort-accuracy tradeoff was also the worst. Participants could address this trade-off by maintaining their effort and allowing accuracy to deteriorate further or by increasing their effort to avoid an additional decrease in accuracy. Our results suggested that participants preferred the latter course, probably to maintain a reasonable level of accuracy in their decisions.

## 6. General Discussion

The two experiments provide empirical evidence in support of our hypothesis, con<sup>fi</sup>rming that the decision accuracy of mobile users is lower when a mobile IA is implemented than when a desktop IA is implemented. Both experiments, with separate samples, consistently show that the common practice in content adaptation of hierarchically segmenting information about alternatives and attributes, where information about some of the attributes is presented on detail pages rather than on the overview page, leads to less accurate decisions. This outcome is observed irrespective of whether the attributes presented on the overview page are <sup>fi</sup>xed (experiment 1) or vary based on user preferences (experiment 2). Although congruence of the mobile IA with user preferences is manipulated in experiment 2 as two extremes (i.e., most versus least congruent), decision accuracy remains similar and lower than that with the desktop IA, consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of experiment 1.

We argue that the mechanism underlying the effect of information structure on decision accuracy is grounded in the effort-accuracy framework. The fact that the same information presented on a single page with the desktop IA is hierarchically distributed across pages with the mobile IA implies that users must exert more cognitive effort to collect and integrate the same amount of information if they wish to maintain the accuracy of their decisions. Against this inferior effort-accuracy trade-off, users can either increase effort to meet the demands of the hierarchical structure or resort to less systematic decision behavior, restoring the balance between effort and accuracy but at lower levels. Both in experiment 1 and under the congruent condition in experiment 2, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the mobile IA triggers decision behavior characterized by lower levels of effort and accuracy. The balance between effort and accuracy in the two experiments is discussed in Online Appendix B.

Something different yet consistent with an effortaccuracy mechanism seems to be happening when the mobile IA becomes incongruent with user preferences. Relative to the desktop IA, the incongruent IA demands even greater effort than the congruent IA because the information distributed to detail pages is that most valuable rather than least valuable to users. Evidently, the incongruent IA drives users to prioritize accuracy over effort. Instead of paying in accuracy to avoid an increase in effort, similar to how users behave in the congruent case (experiment 2) and <sup>fi</sup>xed case (experiment 1), users pay in effort to avoid a decrease in accuracy. Relative to users with the congruent IA, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that users with the incongruent IA search for more information, need more time to reach a decision, and experience higher information load, whereas their decision accuracy is similar. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the adaptive nature of decision behavior and the two possible routes engendered by the effort-accuracy mechanism—either to reduce accuracy to avoid an increase in effort or to increase effort to avoid a decrease in accuracy. An interesting question here is why the former route is observed in the congruent case and the latter route is observed in the incongruent case. The answer, we suggest, is that decision makers generally prefer the former more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and resource-preserving route, as evident from the prevalence of heuristic decision making (Gigerenzer 2004, Shah and Oppenheimer 2008), unless decisions do not even meet the criterion of being satisfactory (Simon 1956; 1979), in which case decision makers turn to the latter route. The incongruent IA may lead users to make highly inaccurate decisions if they mainly rely on the overview page to reach a decision, as they seem to do when using the congruent IA. Therefore, users are motivated to change their decision behavior and invest more effort in navigating to detail pages, thereby averting the otherwise inevitable loss of accuracy. Despite the plausibility of this explanation, its post hoc nature calls for its validation in future research.

The <sup>fi</sup>nding of two con<sup>fl</sup>icting routes via which mobile users respond to the various IAs strengthens the conclusion that our results are best explained by the effort-accuracy framework rather than by alternative theories of decision behavior. Particularly, it is challenging for alternative theories to explain why the desktop and incongruent IAs motivate users to prioritize accuracy, whereas the congruent IA motivates users to prioritize effort, unless the relation between effort and accuracy is at the basis of the explanatory mechanism. This point can be demonstrated by highlighting the gaps involved in drawing on existing theories to provide alternative explanations for our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. On the one hand, as implied, satis<sup>fi</sup>cing behavior can explain why users exert greater effort with the incongruent IA relative to the congruent IA. Basically, satis<sup>fi</sup>cing is a heuristic strategy that entails searching through the available alternatives until an alternative passes the thresholds (“aspiration levels”) for all attributes (Simon 1956). Such a strategy demands greater effort when the mobile IA is incongruent because of the segmentation of information about attributes that are important to the user. Although satis<sup>fi</sup>cing can explain the observed differences in decision behavior between the two mobile IAs, it should <sup>fi</sup>nd it challenging to account for the highe effort and accuracy with the desktop IA relative to the congruent IA. If users engage in satis<sup>fi</sup>cing behav ior, they are expected to demonstrate such behavior not only when information is segmented across pages but also, when the information is presented on a sin gle page. Because it should be less effortful (e.g., less time consuming) for users to identify a satis<sup>fi</sup>cing alternative with the desktop IA, satis<sup>fi</sup>cing theory predicts lower effort and comparable accuracy in this condition rather than the higher effort and accuracy we observe. On the other hand, theories that conside decision behavior as shaped by limited pools of resour ces can explain the observed differences between desk top and congruent IAs, but such theories should <sup>fi</sup>nd it challenging to account for the higher effort with the incongruent IA relative to the congruent IA. Two such theories are capacity theory of attention (Kahneman 1973) and ego depletion theory (Baumeister et al. 1998), which address the decision-making consequences of limited attention and volition, respectively. These two theories propose that resource pools are depleted more rapidly when a task imposes greater demands, pre scribing lower effort with the incongruent IA relative to the congruent IA, consistent with the lower effort observed with the congruent IA relative to the desktop IA. Additional theories similarly predict a monotonous decrease in effort across the desktop, congruent, and incongruent IAs because of the monotonous increase in the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of information collection and integration. For instance, cognitive load theory (Sweller 1988) sug gests that as the quality of information presentation deteriorates, cognitive load increases, and working memory limitations become more detrimental to per formance. Notwithstanding this prediction, we observe greater effort and similar accuracy (i.e., per formance) with the incongruent IA relative to the con gruent IA (see Online Appendix B). Our observation of both effort minimization and accuracy maximiza tion contingent on IA variations accentuates the importance of considering the effort-accuracy trade off in explaining decision behavior. Again, because of the post hoc nature of this theoretical analysis, there is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant merit in future research that can discrim inate among theories that generate similar predictions of decision behavior.

This work makes two major research contributions. First, we contribute to the effort-accuracy framework by extending the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of information display to include the concept of IA, which received little to no attention in this literature. Studies on how decision behavior is contingent on information display have largely focused on the behavioral consequences of variations in the content and format of displayed information (e.g., Schkade and Kleinmuntz 1994, Vessey 1994, Creyer and Ross 1997, Hess et al. 2005, Jiang and Punj 2010, Mertens et al. 2020). Conversely, we demonstrate that decision behavior is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a simple segmentation across pages of the same information in the same format. Moreover, we show that simple manipulations of whether more informative attributes appear higher or lower in the hierarchy (i.e., congruent or incongruent mobile IA) result in two different patterns of decision behavior, where effort is preferred over accuracy in the former case and accuracy is preferred over effort in the latter case. In so doing, this work starts to bridge the gap between the vast knowledge existing about the implications of information display for adaptive decision behavior and the lack of knowledge about the effects of contemporary information displays on decision making.

Second, we contribute to the literature on mobile use by presenting a more balanced account of the consequences of content adaptation on mobile devices. Focusing on the hierarchical segmentation of information, frequently implemented to allow large amounts of information to be conveniently displayed on smaller mobile screens, we demonstrate that content adaptation designed to bene<sup>fi</sup>t users may come at the cost of less accurate decisions. Content adaptation has become a natural by-product of mobile device use based on an assumption that mobile users are otherwise likely to experience lower usability and higher information load (Lee and Benbasat 2003, Schlick et al. 2009, Chen et al. 2014). The present study does not aim to invalidate this assumption but rather, to highlight a possible downside of its ubiquitous implementation. Beyond showing that decision accuracy decreases when information is hierarchically distributed across pages, we propose that this effect is grounded in an effort-accuracy mechanism, which drives users to prioritize effort (minimization) over accuracy (maximization) when the collection and integration of information becomes more effortful. However, we demonstrate that the tendency of users to exert less effort with the mobile IA is not independent of its quality, which we instantiate in experiment 2 as congruence with user preferences. We observe that users decrease their effort as long as the mobile IA provides an informative structure (i.e., congruent mobile IA). If this is not the case (i.e., incongruent mobile IA), users increase rather than decrease their effort, arguably as a compensatory mechanism to maintain a satisfactory level of decision accuracy. This interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding highlights a possible boundary condition in mobile behavior contingent on characteristics of the mobile IA, suggesting that subtle changes in how information is structured across pages may lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variations in the decision behavior of mobile users.

Several practical implications arise from our <sup>fi</sup>nd ings. An important implication is the need to increase the availability of the desktop view on mobile devices. Currently, there is little justi<sup>fi</sup>cation in online or academic sources as to why the option of desktop view has been introduced in browsers and websites beyond noting that it is effective when interacting with the few remaining websites that fail to provide an optimal sur<sup>fi</sup>ng experience on mobile devices (e.g., Advit 2020, Unadkat 2020). Such justi<sup>fi</sup>cation suggests that this option is expected to gradually phase out as websites become better at adapting to mobile devices. We suggest that a desktop view may have at least one advantage over a mobile view, particularly when al information is presented on a single page instead of being distributed across pages. The advantage is that users are likely to make more accurate decisions, which may come at the cost of higher cognitive bur den. Given this advantage, we suggest that the option to switch to a desktop view should be promoted and encouraged, particularly in situations in which users may be highly sensitive to the quality of their decisions. Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may motivate providers of online services to consider more carefully the IA in adapting the presentation of their offerings to mobile devices. Because effort-accuracy trade-offs are underlying decision behavior, providers are advised to consider the level of effort users need to expend if they wish to make accurate decisions and whether effort can be reduced without losing signi<sup>fi</sup> cant accuracy. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that overview pages of search results should be hierarchically structured on mobile devices in a manner that maximizes the amount of information effectively displayed on the overview page while minimizing the amount of information distributed to additional pages. When such an approach is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to implement, offering users of mobile devices the option to switch to a desk top view is likely to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the accuracy of thei decisions.

The main limitations of this work stem from our methodological approach. By employing a controlled experimental approach, which is the dominant approach in empirical decision-making research, this study trades external validity for internal validity. The primary advantages of this approach are the controlled setting and the ability to attribute causality to the observed effects. Its primary disadvantage is the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty to represent the complexity of real-world settings given the need to focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c differences

while holding all else equal. We compare mobile and desktop views on mobile devices by focusing on the IA and on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c hierarchical structure. The choice of IA as the concept of interest rests on the notion that IA is more critical than layout, size, or shape to understanding the distinctions between mobile and desktop views from a decision-making standpoint. We operationalize the mobile IA via the implementation of the most fundamental hierarchical structure common to existing mobile IAs. Given our <sup>fi</sup>nding that a simple segmentation of information across pages signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces decision accuracy, it is reasonable to assume that additional variance in the layout, size, or shape of information items will exacerbate the effect. For instance, if the size of items is increased in the mobile view to improve readability, consistent with common responsive design practices, more information will probably have to be distributed from the overview page to detail pages to avoid excessive scrolling, resulting in even lower decision accuracy. It is also reasonable to assume that implementing the mobile IA on a single page, where users can access detailed information either by scrolling down the page or by using links to navigate within the page, is unlikely to improve decision accuracy because the collection and integration of information are not expected to be less cognitively effortful than in the multipage mobile IA. Therefore, the effect we observe may be considered as a lower bound for the effect of content adaptation on decision accuracy. The methodological decision to present three attributes on the overview page in the mobile IA based on an exploratory analysis of popular hotel booking websites may similarly limit the external validity of this work. It is sensible to assume that increasing this number of attributes will mitigate the differences in decision accuracy, as the overview page in the mobile IA will become more similar to that in the desktop IA. The practical implication is that responsive designs that minimize the differences between desktop and mobile IAs are likely to maintain decision accuracy, but then, usability is likely to suffer. As for our operationalization of the desktop IA, we assume that the entire overview page is devoted to the presentation of alternatives and attributes (i.e., search results) to ensure that only the IA varies across experimental conditions. However, responsive designs sometimes take advantage of larger desktop screens to include in the desktop view additional information and functionality (e.g., search <sup>fi</sup>lters) which effectively reduce the space available for search results with the desktop IA on mobile screens relative to the mobile IA. This is another aspect in which we prioritize the inference of causality over the complete representation of real-world settings. Given the complexity of our research setting, we were unable to estimate the sensitivity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to changes in these methodological choices. Therefore, caution should be exercised in generalizing our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to markedly different settings.

These methodological choices, nonetheless, together with our key <sup>fi</sup>ndings open new avenues to study the decision-making consequences of content adaptation on mobile devices. First, it should be interesting to study in future research whether more elaborate mobile IAs, alone or in concert with other content adaptations, have similar effects on the decision accuracy of mobile users. Second, it should also be interesting to identify variables that, similar to congruence with user preferences, serve as boundary conditions of these effects. Third, our understanding of adaptive decision behavior can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from greater attention to the trade-off between effort and accuracy in the context of mobile use. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our post hoc observation that users shift from prioritizing effort to prioritizing accuracy when the IA is incongruent with their preferences calls for more attention to the thresholds of maximum effort and minimum accuracy that may constrain the trade-off between the two. Finally, although our experiments randomly assign users to either mobile IA or desktop IA, recent practices in industry allow users to opt out of content adaptation. Future research that compares settings in which content adaptation is exogenous or endogenous can inform both research and practice about the behavioral consequences of affording mobile users more freedom to decide on their preferred view.

In conclusion, we repeatedly demonstrate in two experiments that an adaptation technique designed to bene<sup>fi</sup>t mobile users may paradoxically impair their ability to make accurate decisions. Although the implications of this study are limited by methodological choices made to control for the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variation characterizing real-life settings, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that greater research attention should be devoted to the prices that users pay for the convenience of content adaptation.
