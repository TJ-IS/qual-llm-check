# The Attraction Effect in Crowdfunding

Abstract. At the core of reward-based crowdfunding (RBC) is the reward menu. Carefully constructed reward menus can impact fundraising success signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. For example, an oft-discussed approach to in<sup>fl</sup>uencing purchasing decisions is introducing an irrelevant option into a consumer’s choice set—a decoy option—making another, often more valuable, option more attractive. This phenomenon is also known as the attraction effect. Prior research, however, has found mixed evidence for its occurrence in applied settings because it has largely employed simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed attribute presentation—with only numeric attributes such as ratings—and hypothetical choices, which had no economic consequences. Hence, researchers questioned the practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the attraction effect. In this paper, we examine whether the attraction effect will manifest in an applied setting such as the crowd funding context, where product attributes are both numerical (e.g., price) and nonnumerical (e.g., content of a product), choices have economic consequences, and organization offer several menu options to consumers. We draw upon the salience theory and propose four hypotheses, suggesting that introducing an irrelevant decoy option in choice sets, created digitally in reward menus, may lead backers to choose higher-priced options. We conducted seven online experiments and a <sup>fi</sup>eld study on Kickstarter (in total, n 3,998 participants), with increasing levels of similarity with RBC. We found that the attraction effect signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shifted backers’ preferences from a low-priced to a high-priced reward by 18.8% to 28.2% in different settings, highlighting the substantial potential of properly designed digital reward menus to in<sup>fl</sup>uence funders’ choices. Given that researchers have expressed skepticism about the attraction effect in applied settings, our results are particu larly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting that information systems design can in<sup>fl</sup>uence choice behavior. This research makes targeted contributions to the literature on crowdfunding, the attraction effect, and digital design.

## 1. Introduction

Making choices among multiple options is a fundamental aspect of the consumer purchasing process. As digital markets have become ubiquitous, consumers have increasingly purchased products and services online, where choices are plentiful (Xu et al. 2020). This provides online vendors with ingenious opportunities to present a given choice set to consumers. In a recent study, however, behavioral economists Benartzi and Bhargava (2020) argued that contemporary <sup>fi</sup>rms underestimate the impact of digital design on behavioral outcomes, including consumption, investments, and privacy (see also Acquisti 2009). They suggested that the design and presentation of options in online choice menus can affect a consumer’s eventual choice signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Online menus present a vital opportunity to shape consumer experiences and buying behavior.

A popular approach to in<sup>fl</sup>uencing purchasing deci sions is introducing an irrelevant option into a con sumer’s choice set, making an original, often more expensive option, more attractive. The attraction effect (also known as the decoy effect or the asymmetric dominance effect; see Huber et al. 1982; 2014) explains how consumer preferences change between two options—the so-called competitor and target options— when a third option, the decoy is added. The effect occurs when the decoy option is dominated by the target option, making the target option more attractive. Although the original options do not dominate each other, the decoy’s introduction makes the target more attractive at the expense of the competitor. Hence, the attraction effect violates the assumptions of rational choice theory, which suggests that consumers have well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned and stable preferences.<sup>1</sup>

Although the attraction effect has been studied extensively, empirical research has found mixed evidence for its occurrence in applied settings. Research on the attraction effect has been criticized on three grounds (e.g., Frederick et al. 2014). First, prior research has largely employed simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed designs by representing all choice attributes purely numerically. Often, the attributes have been represented by ratings (e.g., 6.9 out of 10). In many practical settings, however, product attributes are often nonnumerical (e.g., different reward types in reward-based crowdfunding (RBC)). Second, prior research has often chosen simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed, “context-free” representations. In many cases, these studies describe the options in only textual form, without presenting them in a realistic decision context (e.g., the look and feel of a real website). Furthermore, studies utilizing data from real organizations are rare in the literature. Thus, the applicability of prior studies, using simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed stimuli, has been questioned (Yang and Lynn 2014). Third, most previous studies have used hypothetical instead of economically consequential choices. Participants’ choices in most of these studies had no economic consequences. In light of these limitations, some scholars have cast doubt on the practical relevance of the attraction effect (Frederick et al. 2014, Yang and Lynn 2014).

In this study, we use RBC, which typically employs internet-enabled digital market platforms, to examine the attraction effect. To our knowledge, the attraction effect has not been examined in digital markets, which are used by billions and have widespread impacts on the economy and the society. Two aspects of digital markets suggest that it is critical to examine the attraction effect in this context. First, in traditional marketplaces, choice sets often have to be created by the customer and are therefore unstructured, which makes product comparisons more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (e.g., customers have to walk around in stores and check the attributes of various products). In digital markets, however, choice sets are often well de<sup>fi</sup>ned, which makes comparisons easier (e.g., Kickstarter presents different rewards in ascending order). We posit that online menus can enable easier comparisons across product offerings. Given that choice options in digital marketplaces consist of nonnumerical and numerical attributes, we believe it is particularly important to examine the attraction effect in digital markets, such as RBC. Second, as discussed above, consumer participation in digital markets entails economically consequential choices. However, much of the literature examining the attraction effect has examined consumer choices that are hypothetical.

In RBC, backers can choose among several rewards, which usually contain different versions of the to-befunded product. These rewards are organized in a digital reward menu, which is essentially a choice set. The design of these digital reward menus is fundamental to RBC campaigns. Considering that key characteristics of digital markets—attribute type (numeric versus nonnumeric) and choice type (hypothetical versus consequential)—have been largely underexplored, we extend current research in information systems (IS) and the attraction effect by considering both as moderating factors. We examine three research questions in this paper: (1) Can the attraction effect manifest in a crowdfunding context? (2) Can the attraction effect be observed for both numeric and nonnumeric attributes? (3) Does the attraction effect differ for hypothetical and consequential choices in a crowdfunding context? To examine the attraction effect in RBC, we draw on the salience theory from behavioral economics (Bordalo et al. 2012). It attempts to explain preference changes in context-dependent choices, in particular, when alternatives are added to existing choice sets. The theory suggests that introducing a decoy option changes the reference option in a choice set, making the pricier and higher-quality option more attractive.

RBC provides an apt context in which to examine the attraction effect in digital markets. RBC has emerged as a prominent funding vehicle for develop ing innovative products and services and has experienced robust annual growth, garnering signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention from researchers, practitioners, and policy makers (Mollick 2014; Burtch et al. 2018; Hong et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2018; 2021; Cornelius and Gokpinar 2020; Yang et al. 2020). At Kickstarter, one of the larg est RBC platforms, 20.5 million backers have supported more than 211,000 projects, contributing USD 6.2 billion to projects (Kickstarter 2021). These numbers suggest that RBC is emerging as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant source of alternative funding for entrepreneurs. Besides being economically relevant, RBC is a useful context also from theoretical perspectives. In particular, RBC allows us to (1) use options that participants can clearly encode (i.e., the rewards), (2) implement real economic consequences (i.e., provide participants with a budget), and (3) use actual data from funding campaigns employed by real organizations.

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a series of experiments, which enabled us to overcome some challenges that arise from using observational data (Tams et al. 2018). Given the dynamics of RBC platforms (e.g., changing funding progress, new users contributing, and other developments), it would be impossible to run several conditions that differ only in the treatment. In this situation, experimental research is apt to study the effect in isolation because, in experimental research, one can control for the environment (for the advantages of economic experiments in IS research, see Gupta et al. 2018). The drawback of experiments, however, is that each experiment often addresses only a certain aspect. To overcome this issue, we ran a series of eight studies with a total of 3,998 participants, focusing on different aspects in each study. We gradually increased the degree of realism, thereby moving from internal to ecological validity. First, to better understand the effect and test the salience theory in the context of RBC, we conducted a controlled experiment with only numerical attributes to isolate the attraction effect (Study 1). Subsequently, we tested the attraction effect using nonnumerical attributes (Study 2) and consequential choices (Study 3). Finally, in collaboration with a Swiss luxury watchmaker, we manipulated the reward menu of a real crowdfunding campaign on Kickstarter (Studies 4 and 5). We found that the attraction effect signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shifts backers’ preferences from a low-priced competitor to a high-priced target reward. The results are robust across the experiments and independent of attribute presentation (i.e., numerical or nonnumerical) but depend modestly on choice type (hypothetical or consequential).

This study makes three key contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the attraction-effect literature by demonstrating that the effect also works in digital markets with nonnumerical attributes and consequential choices. Second, we contribute to the IS and crowdfunding literatures—particularly on the role of digital design on in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individual-level purchasing decisions. Although many researchers have studied RBC success factors, little research has examined the core of RBC: the reward menu. We <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap and show that the way the digital reward menu is composed can in<sup>fl</sup>uence backers’ decisions. We emphasize that the digital reward menu is instrumental because the use of the technology allows the marketers and entrepreneurs to design choices for consumers that would not be possible otherwise. Third, we contribute to the behavioral-economics literature by not only testing but also extending the salience theory of Bordalo et al. (2013) in digital markets. Whereas they demonstrated the theory’s prediction for numerical attribute values (“Quality and price are measured in dollars”; Bordalo et al. 2013, p. 807), we show that the theory also applies for nonnumerical attribute values. This <sup>fi</sup>nding could lead to a reframing and extension of the current theory, facilitating its application in digital rewards menus applicable in online markets.

## 2. Related Work

We draw upon two distinct literature streams: (1) the attraction effect and (2) crowdfunding, with a focus on success factors. The former has largely appeared in psychology, behavioral economics, and marketing, and the latter has appeared in IS, marketing, entrepreneurship, and operations management. We have chosen crowdfunding as a study context because it is a wellstudied digital market and is theoretically and practically relevant. Furthermore, the attraction effect can easily be incorporated into crowdfunding reward menus because these are often standardized and well structured.

## 2.1. Attraction Effect

The attraction effect increases the attractiveness of one option (the target) when a new, inferior option (the decoy) is added to a choice set. In RBC, for example, a choice set consists of several rewards. To make the attraction effect work, a decoy reward should be simila to the target reward but inferior in at least one attribute—for example, price or quality—to make the target reward look more attractive. It is important to remember that the decoy reward is dominated by the target reward but not the competitor reward. More formally, let us assume a reward menu with two options $C = \{ r _ { c } , r _ { t } \} - \mathsf { a }$ cheap competitor reward $r _ { c }$ and an expensive target reward r —which is extended by a decoy reward $r _ { d } ,$ , so that we get $C _ { d } = \{ r _ { c } , r _ { t } , r _ { d } \}$ . Let us further assume that $r _ { d } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ and $r _ { t } ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ quality are the same, but that $r _ { d } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ price is slightly higher—less attractive— than $r _ { t } ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s } .$ . Given the same quality but lower price, $r _ { t }$ appears to be more attractive than $r _ { d } ,$ thus increasing the probability that the relatively high-priced, high quality target reward $r _ { t }$ will now be chosen.<sup>2</sup> Formally, the probability of choosing the target reward $r _ { t }$ is highe if $r _ { t }$ is presented along with a similar but less attractive decoy reward: $P ( r _ { t } \mid \{ r _ { c } , r _ { t } , r _ { d } \} ) > P ( r _ { t } \mid \{ r _ { c } , r _ { t } \} )$ .

To implement the attraction effect, Huber et al. (1982) suggested different placement strategies for the decoy option. (See Figure 1 for the various placements in the attribute space. The two dark circles show the original product offerings.) For example, to make the target option more attractive, a decoy should be inferior in either price (the range strategy, R; see Figure 1) or quality (the frequency strategy, F). Combinations of both (the range-frequency strategy, RF) or extremes (e.g., the extreme-range-increasing strategy, $R ^ { * } )$ are also possible, but used very infrequently in research. In this paper, we will make use of the range and the frequency strategies.

Research has shown that the attraction effect in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer choice for selections of restaurants, beers, cars, and tour packages, among other things (e.g., Huber et al. 1982, Heath and Chatterjee 1995, Josiam and Hobson 1995, Hedgcock et al. 2009, Frederick et al. 2014). Scholars have studied boundary conditions, such as the effect of hypothetical and consequential choices (Lichters et al. 2017), and the effects of attribute representation, including quantitative representation using numbers (Huber et al. 1982, Simonson 1989, Frederick et al. 2014), perceptual presentation using different visual representations (Trueblood 2012, Trueblood et al. 2013, Trueblood and Pettibone 2017), and categorical representation using different product types (Ha et al. 2009).

Although the literature has offered evidence of the attraction effect, scholars question its robustness (Frederick et al. 2014, Yang and Lynn 2014). Critics have argued that the attraction effect has been shown only in stylized product representations, “when stimuli [are] represented numerically, but not otherwise” (Frederick et al. 2014, p. 488), resulting in skepticism among researchers about the practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the effect. The situation in practical settings such as RBC and other digital markets differs from that of many typical attraction-effect experiments (Lichters et al. 2015) for three reasons. First, attributes in these settings can be represented in multiple ways. For instance, rewards in RBC may involve both numerical representation (i.e., the price) and nonnumerical representation (e.g., different contents of a product) of attributes. Second, choices have economic consequences. Third, choices are made in realistic environments (i.e., on a website). These differences make RBC an interesting context in which to study the attraction effect and its applicability.

## 2.2. Crowdfunding Success Factors

Our study builds on the extensive literature on crowdfunding research, in particular, prior RBC research. Researchers have a good understanding of drivers that in<sup>fl</sup>uence crowdfunding success. We classify these drivers into factors related to founders, backers, and project-design characteristics. Success factors that relate to the project founders include the founders social network (Lin et al. 2013, Mollick 2014, Agrawa et al. 2015, Koch and Siering 2019, Chen et al. 2020, Murray et al. 2020), communication with backers (Mollick 2014, Xiao et al. 2014, Koch and Siering 2019), team composition (Frydrych et al. 2014), and project experience (Koch and Siering 2019). Factors that relate to the project backers include peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Burtch 2011, Herzenstein et al. 2011, Bapna 2019, Kim and Hann 2019, Murray et al. 2020), backing history (Kim and Hann 2019, Koch and Siering 2019), culture and geography (Burtch et al. 2014, Agrawal et al. 2015), information disclosure and privacy (Burtch et al. 2015, 2016), input from customers (Cornelius and Gokpinar 2020), and prosocial motives (Dai and Zhang 2019).

Our study is closely related to the literature investigating success factors related to project design characteristics that allow creators to customize campaigns. A frequently investigated design factor is the funding goal, which should not be set too high (Frydrych et al. 2014, Mollick 2014, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Koch and Siering 2019, Chen et al. 2020) because proj ects that are close to the funding goal tend to receive more contributions (Dai and Zhang 2019). Funding duration is another frequently investigated success factor, although the results are equivocal. Whereas some studies have argued that long funding durations signal founder incompetence and therefore undermine success (e.g., Mollick 2014, Koch and Siering 2019), others have maintained that long funding durations lead to extended exposure and better project performance (see Burtch et al. 2013). Finally, pitch framing is associated with campaign success. Visual representation measured by the number of videos (Mollick 2014) or images (Chen et al. 2020) has a positive impact on fundraising success, as do the length, tone, and readability of a proj ect’s description (Kaminski and Hopp 2019).

In RBC, project creators can con<sup>fi</sup>gure individual rewards in terms of their price and quality levels and combine rewards for a reward menu. Given RBC’s popularity and its core idea of reward selection, it is striking that the composition of reward menus has received little attention. A few notable studies have dealt with rewards or the reward menu. For example, Hu et al. (2015) examined crowdfunding product and pricing decisions by proposing menu design options that are incentive compatible, making a backer better off selecting one particular option rather than others. Along these lines, other researchers have argued that successful projects list fewer rewards (Xiao et al. 2014), and the optimal number of rewards follows an inverted-U shape (Du et al. 2019). Jiang et al. (2021) suggest that rewards can offer utilitarian, sociomaterial, and participatory values and it is important to understand the mechanisms through which these awards incentivize backers to support a project. They also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the value RBC backers receive from a project can be substitutive, and their combination can have an inverted-U relationship with the project performance. Furthermore, backers tend to prefer limited rewards (Weinmann et al. 2017, Yang et al. 2020), which can in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decisions even when the rewards are sold out because they remain visible on the campaign’s web page (Adam et al. 2019). For example, Yang et al. (2020) suggest that the scarcity effect can be used to attract project backers, but its effect is not uniform across different stages of the funding process; the depleted reward tiers are effective only during certain times.

These studies are valuable contributions to understanding how founders can design reward menus to maximize funding success. Our research differs in several ways. First, we study the attraction effect, which is very prominent in the decision-making literature, but has been questioned to work in practical settings such as digital markets. Second, whereas prior research has examined rewards and reward menus from the perspective of crowdfunding campaign performance or pricing and product decisions, we investigate how the design of the digital reward menu impacts the individual consumer-level decision to purchase a product. Rather than aggregating individual-level effects or considering the actions of other individuals (Xiao et al. 2014, Hu et al. 2015, Yang et al. 2020, Jiang et al. 2021), our experimental focus stays at the individual level. Third, we examine how a typical backer’s purchase decision can change with just the introduction of a decoy product. We argue that changes in the digital reward menu via the introduction of a decoy product changes the salience of different attributes, leading to changes in the purchase decision. Finally, we use a multiple-methods approach— experiments and a <sup>fi</sup>eld study—to study causal relationships in reward menus. Prior research has mainly used archival data, making it hard to draw causal conclusions.

## 3. Theory and Hypotheses

A fundamental feature of decision making is thinking in context and identifying the best option based on the most salient aspects of the choice context. Given the impact choice attributes and their salience can have on consumer decision making, we develop our arguments based on the salience theory, also known as the theory of context-dependent choice (Bordalo et al. 2012). Salience occurs when one product attribute stands out. The salience of attributes determines the attention a decision maker pays to these attributes and the weight she gives them.

As Bordalo et al. (2013) showed, a consumer’s attention goes to products with higher quality to price ratios, that is, products that offer a better “value for money.” In essence, when a new option is introduced into a choice set, it can alter the value-for-money metric for the original products, thereby changing the consumer’s choice. We apply this key reasoning in the salience theory to examine the attraction effect in RBC. We <sup>fi</sup>rst present the general tenets of the theory (see Table 1 for notations used).

The theory considers a consumer who faces a choice set $C = \{ ( q _ { 1 } , \overset { \cdot } { p } _ { 1 } ) , \dots , ( q _ { N } , p _ { N } ) \}$ with n>1 goods, whereby each good k has a quality of $q _ { k } > 0$ and a price of $p _ { k } > 0 ,$ with both measured in monetary units (Bordalo et al. 2013). Absent any salience distortions, a rational consumer places equal weights on quality and price. For good $k ,$ a consumer assigns the following utility: $\boldsymbol { u } _ { k } = \boldsymbol { q } _ { k } - \boldsymbol { p } _ { k }$ . A salient thinker deviates from this equation. She perceives one attribute as salient (quality or price) and places a higher weight on it. For good $k ,$ the degree of salience is determined by comparing the attribute levels of one attribute, either $q _ { k }$ or $p _ { k } ,$ to its reference attribute level, which is the average level of an attribute in a choice set. For example, the average quality level is $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { q } = \frac { q _ { 1 } + q _ { 2 } } { 2 } } \end{array}$ , and the average price level is $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { p } = \frac { p _ { 1 } + { p _ { 2 } } } { 2 } } \end{array}$ . (We assume a two-options choice set: competitor and target option.)

A salience function $\sigma ( x , y )$ determines an attribute’s salience. It must satisfy the conditions of ordering and homogeneity of degree zero. Ordering means that if an interval x, y is contained in a larger interval $[ x ^ { \prime } , y ^ { \prime } ]$ , then $\sigma ( x , y ) < \sigma ( x ^ { \prime } , y ^ { \prime } ) ;$ ; homogeneity of degree zero means $\sigma ( \alpha x , \alpha y ) = \sigma ( x , y )$ for any $\alpha > 0 ,$ with $\sigma ( 0 , 0 ) = 0$ Furthermore, the salience function is assumed to be symmetric; thus, $\sigma ( a _ { 1 } , \bar { a } ) = \sigma ( a _ { 2 } , \bar { a } )$ for $p ,$ $q ,$ which means that any attribute is equally salient (given two products). An example of a salience function that satis<sup>fi</sup>es these assumptions is $\begin{array} { r } { \sigma ( a , \bar { a } ) = \frac { | a - \bar { a } | } { \bar { a } } . } \end{array}$ . It measures the salience of an attribute a as the proportional difference from that attribute’s average value a¯ (see Bordalo et al. 2016). In essence, an attribute a $( = q$ or $p )$ is compared with its reference attribute ${ \bar { a } } .$ . The salience of quality is then given by $\sigma ( q _ { k } , \bar { q } )$ , whereas the salience of price is given by $\sigma ( \boldsymbol { p } _ { k } , \bar { \boldsymbol { p } } )$ . Quality is salient if $\sigma ( q _ { k } , \bar { \bar { q } } ) > \sigma ( p _ { k } , \bar { \bar { p } } )$ and price is salient if $\sigma ( q _ { k } , \bar { q } ) < \sigma ( p _ { k } , \bar { p } )$ . To determine which of the two attributes in a choice set (quality or price) is salient, one must consider the quality–price ratios—in particular, a good k’s quality–price ratio $\frac { q _ { k } } { p _ { k } }$ compared with the reference good’s quality–price ratio $\begin{array} { r } { { \frac { \bar { q } } { \bar { p } } } . \mathrm { ~ A ~ } } \end{array}$ good $k ^ { \prime } s$ higher quality (or lower price) is salient if $\frac { q _ { k } } { p _ { k } } > \frac { \bar { q } } { \bar { p } } .$ This equation is central to salient thinking: “a good deal is attractive because it draws a consumer’s attention to its advantage (high quality or low price) relative to its competitors” (Bordalo et al. 2013, p. 809).

Hypothesis 2. Adding a decoy reward that is slightly less attractive than the target (high) reward’s quality increases the likelihood of backers choosing the target (high) reward.

The theory of Bordalo et al. (2013) assumes that both quality and price are measured quantitatively. The majority of attraction effect experiments ful<sup>fi</sup>ll this criterion and use numerical attributes. Frederick et al. (2014) showed that the attraction effect occurs only with numerical attribute values. They could not demonstrate the effect in experiments employing nonnumerical attributes. Other researchers have also argued that nonnumerical attributes can diminish the attraction effect (Malkoc et al. 2013). In RBC, rewards usually consist of a combination of numerical attributes, such as the price, and nonnumerical attributes, such as different content versions of a product received in return for a contribution. Other digital markets share this characteristic— products and services in these markets constitute both numerical and nonnumerical attributes. Ha et al. (2009) argued that the combination of numerical and nonnumerical attributes renders it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for consumers to form quality-to-price ratios and compare goods, suggesting that it can attenuate the attraction effect. Banerjee and Chatterjee (2020) suggest that nonnumerical attributes often stimulate compensatory decision making where consumers indulge in attribute related tradeoffs and attempt to pick the right mix of attributes.

Attribute salience, then, may lose its prominence, rendering the attraction effect ineffective. Based on the arguments provided by this stream of literature, we would also expect a weaker effect when using nonnumerical attribute values.

Hypothesis 3. The attraction effect in RBC is weaker when using nonnumerical attribute values than when using numerical attribute values.

A shortcoming of many attraction-effect experiments is their design as hypothetical choice experiments: decisions have no economic consequences. In reality, decisions have economic consequences. There is an element of risk involved in real crowdfunding situations because the consumer may not get the product she wants if the campaign fails or, worse yet, she may lose her contribution (Hu et al. 2015, Yang et al. 2020). Lichters et al. (2017), for example, found that the attraction effect is stronger when binding choices with economic consequences are used. The salience theory would suggest that compared with decision makers in riskless (hypothetical) situations, decision makers facing economic (risky) consequences will pay closer attention to the choices, features, and context. The attraction effect in RBC is thus likely to be stronger when there are economic consequences. We therefore hypothesize that choice type (hypothetical/consequential) moderates the attraction effect.

Hypothesis 4. The attraction effect in RBC is stronger when choices have economic consequences than when they have hypothetical consequences.

In summary, we expect both decoys—whether we manipulate price or quality—to increase the likelihood of choosing the target (Hypotheses 1 and 2). We also expect the effect to be moderated by attribute type, such that the effect is stronger for numerical attributes (Hypothesis 3). Finally, we expect the effect to be moderated by choice type, such that the effect is stronger for consequential choices.

## 4. Overview of Studies

Much RBC research has used secondary and <sup>fi</sup>eld data, which limits researchers’ ability to manipulate effects. Given RBC’s dynamics, it is nearly impossible to run two identical campaigns in parallel, with participants being randomly assigned to control and treatment conditions; hence, researchers rely on controlled (laboratory) studies. We use a multiple-methods approach, using experiments and a <sup>fi</sup>eld study to test the salience theory. In total, we conducted eight studies, including replications, to systematically assess how the attraction effect in<sup>fl</sup>uences backers’ reward choices (see Table 3).

## 4.1. Purpose of the Studies

In Study 1, we tested Hypotheses 1 and 2 by using the example presented in the theory section. Participants could select rewards with numerical attributes for both quality and price (they could pledge for different numbers of tracks on a music album). Note that it is common in economics to measure the quality attribute numerically (see, e.g., Bordalo et al. 2013, who measured the quality of wines in dollars). In Study 2, participants could select more realistic rewards that had not only numeric attribute values but also nonnumerical ones (they could pledge for different versions of a music album—a digital download or a CD). Studies 1 and 2 differed only in attribute representation; we used either numerical (i.e., number of tracks) or nonnumerical attribute values (i.e., versions of a music album). Comparing the results of these two experiments helped us test Hypothesis 3. Furthermore, in a replication, we tested all conditions at once (Study 6).

## 5. Study 1: Quantitative Reward Attributes

## 5.1. Overview

In Experiment 1, we used numerical attribute values for a reward’s price and quality, allowing us to test the Bordalo et al. (2013) theory, which was developed for numerical values only. ## 7. Study 3: Introducing Economic Consequences 7.1. Overview

Experiments 1 and 2 did not have associated economic consequences for participants. Following Smith’s (1976) induced value theory, we now link participants’ actions to monetary payoffs. Therefore, in Experiment 3, we introduced economic consequences. We took our lead from practices used in many prominent crowdfunding platforms—provision-point systems (Burtch et al. 2018). For example, in Kickstarter, projects receive funding only if a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned funding goal is reached—all or nothing. If a project does not reach its goal, backers get their pledged money back, but they do not receive the rewards they pledged for. In contrast, in Indiegogo, projects receive all funds, and backers can lose their money if a project is unsuccessful—a keep-it-all mechanism. Experiment 3 mimicked risky choices from practice by adding economic consequences to the experimental setup.

## 7.2. Material and Procedure

We used the same material and conditions as in Experiment 2 (see Table 5) but added economic consequences. We simulated the uncertainty associated with RBC by implementing a version of the keep-it-all funding mechanism: participants were told that projects would be successful only if a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned funding goal was reached. Given that only 50% of all music campaigns turn out to be successful (Kickstarter 2021), we used a random number generator with a 50% success probability that determined after the experiment whether the campaign was successful (Simons et al. 2017).

## 7.3. Payout

Following induced value theory (Smith 1976), participants’ actions were directly linked to their payouts. They received an endowment of 25 coins that they could invest for rewards $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , download and/or CD). Because we were unable to transfer real rewards to the participants, we opted for a bonus payment: if a campaign was successful (as determined by the random number generator after the experiment), we doubled and paid out the pledged amount (one coin equaled $\bar { \mathrm { G B P } 0 . 0 1 ) }$ ). However, if a campaign was unsuccessful, participants lost their money. For example, if a project was successful and participants had backed it with 20 coins, their bonus payment was calculated as follows: 25 coins (endowment) minus 20 coins (pledge) plus 40 coins (reward 2 pledge) equals 45 coins. If a project was unsuccessful, participants lost their pledged money, and their bonus payment was only 5 coins: 25 coins (endowment) minus 20 coins (pledge). This procedure ensured that choices had economic consequen ces. It also ensured that we accounted for the delivery risk in RBC, which refers to backers contributing to a campaign but not receiving the reward in return.

## 8. Studies 4 and 5: Real-World Campaign 8.1. Overview

In the previous experiments, we saw strong evidence for the attraction effect. We used experimental data that have obvious bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, such as establishing a causal relationship and controlling confounds, but lack ecological validity, potentially limiting their external applicability. Backers in a real campaign may behave differently than participants in our experiments, for the following reasons. First, we used low-stakes payouts. Participants may have behaved differently if they were faced with high-stakes decisions. Second, we used a simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed payment structure. Participants may have engaged in gambling, in particular, in Experiment 3, where we used a random number generator for the payout. Third, we neglected a no-buy option. Participants may have preferred not to pledge. Fourth, we used experiential products such as music, where rewards contained the same content but differed in the type of delivery, that is, a hardcover or digital version. Participants may have acted differently for a different class of products. Finally, we presented only competitor, target, and decoy rewards but concealed others that are common in crowdfunding, such as limited rewards.

## 8.2. Setting

To address these issues, we collaborated with a partner, a Swiss luxury watchmaker. The company produces high-end watches made from rocket material. Having successfully completed several crowdfunding campaigns, our partner was open to the idea of experimentation and allowed us to manipulate the reward menu for their next campaign. The collaboration with our partner allowed us to address many of the previously mentioned concerns: we were able to use real-world data from a real campaign, where backers were involved in high-stakes decisions (addressing the <sup>fi</sup>rst issue, no high stakes) and received actual rewards (addressing the second issue, arti<sup>fi</sup>cial payout structure); however, they could also decide not to pledge (addressing the third issue, the missing opt-out option). In addition, we used a product other than music or books (addressing the fourth issue, other product), and presented rewards other than only the competitor, target, and decoy (addressing the <sup>fi</sup>fth issue, other rewards).

The campaign was rather successful. Our partner’s goal was to raise CHF 60,000 but 727 backers pushed it to CHF 495,557. In this campaign, our partner offered three products: (1) a keychain astronaut for CHF 49, (2) a mechanical watch for CHF 579, and (3) a high-end automatic watch for CHF 999. For this study, we focused on the keychain astronaut and the high-end automatic watch, for which we added a decoy option. This setting allowed us to introduce a decoy option in the reward menu and test it in a real-world setting. Although running a <sup>fi</sup>eld study offered us many advantages (as described above), it also rendered a disadvantage, in that we lacked a control group. Hence, before going live, we pretested the attraction effect in the new context using a controlled experiment. We expected that including a decoy option leads to more participants choosing a higher-priced watch bundle. Next, we describe the pilot (Study 4).

We argue that an RBC context provides unique opportunities to extend previous insights. In crowdfunding, attributes can be nonnumerical, which prior research has suggested may mitigate the attraction effect. In contrast, choices in crowdfunding are mostly consequential, which may amplify the effect. We <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effect across all studies, demonstrating that the attraction effect is in<sup>fl</sup>uential in RBC. We con<sup>fi</sup>rm this using seven online experiments (including replications) and a <sup>fi</sup>eld study on Kickstarter. In combination, these studies help us overcome the challenges of studying the attraction effect on real crowdfunding platforms. On platforms, it is almost impossible to study the effect in isolation. Given the dynamics of crowdfunding platforms, it would be impossible to run several conditions that differ only in the treatment. Experiments, however, offer a systematic examination in a controlled setting (Gupta et al. 2018). In our experiments, we provide initial evidence that the attraction effect is operative with both numerical and nonnumerical attribute values. In addition, we show that it is stronger with consequential choices. Given that choices in crowdfunding platforms are consequential and that rewards are both numerical (price) and nonnumerical (quality), this study lays the groundwork for other studies to explore the possibility that the attraction effect may work in other digital marketplaces.

A possible explanation for the attraction effect working in crowdfunding may lie in the key properties of digital markets. Digital markets enable easier comparison of different attributes across diverse products and services (Bakos 1997, Smith et al. 2000). These markets allow sellers to present product-attribute information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and succinctly (Xu et al. 2020). The rich communication made possible through the representation capability of these markets can allow buyers to compare product attributes ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently (Overby 2008). Essentially, digital markets enable sellers to communicate product features—numerical and nonnumerical—without excessive information exchange and processing, thereby lowering the complexity of product description (Mishra et al. 2007), which in turn enables buyers to compare features in different products easily and without incurring large transaction costs. In fact, our results suggest that consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd nonnumerical attributes as easy to compare as numerical attributes, which may partially explain why the attraction effect works in digital environments for all attributes. In other words, the central argument used in prior research, which maintains that a combination of numerical and nonnumerical attribute values make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for consumers to compare features and form price-to-value ratios, may not apply to digital markets.

## 9.2. Limitations and Future Research

This study has limitations, providing opportunities for future re<sup>fi</sup>nements and extensions. First, our experimental approach has some drawbacks. Although experiments allow us to address the challenges of archival empirical research—isolate effects, control for noise, and show causal relations—they often lack a certain degree of realism. We addressed this issue by running a series of experiments that dealt with various crowdfunding aspects in sequence (e.g., funding mechanism, reward structure, website design, and an actual crowdfunding campaign). In combination, our experiments provide robust evidence for the attraction effect in digital markets. Note that we did not consider the funding dynamics associated with crowdfunding. In our experiments, every participant was presented with the same situation. In real campaigns, these situations change constantly. Furthermore, the funding goal, total pledges, and the number of backers were not displayed, and participants could not access the FAQs, use the comment option, or access the project status via updates. We imposed these constraints to isolate the attraction effect and its impact on consumer decision making in crowdfunding. In contrast, in our Kickstarter <sup>fi</sup>eld study, we captured the dynamics associated with crowdfunding, but we were unable to run two identical campaigns (i.e., because of the dynamic nature of crowdfunding). Here, our partner allowed us to manipulate the reward menu, but we lacked a control group. Hence, we conducted a pretest to study the effect with control groups. We found that results from our online experiments and the <sup>fi</sup>eld study were aligned. Individually, both online experiments and the <sup>fi</sup>eld study have limitations, but in combination, they provide strong evidence for the attraction effect in digital markets. With our approach, we follow the recent trend in the IS literature of using a mixed methods approach by combining online experiments and a <sup>fi</sup>eld study to provide robust evidence of causal effects and external validity (e.g., Hong et al. 2017, Yi et al. 2017).

Second, we used established guidelines to enhance the attraction effect.<sup>17</sup> One guideline suggests to allow for the possibility to evaluate the product, which we were not able to implement. In Studies 1–3, we used a <sup>fi</sup>ctitious music album, which would be hard to evaluate. In Studies 4 and 5, backers pledged for watches, which are to be developed and produced. In Study 8, we used a book project, where we did not see the possibility of providing participants the opportunity to evaluate the book’s quality before pledging. Future studies may provide an evaluation option to see whether it in<sup>fl</sup>uences results.

Third, this study did not consider founder and funder characteristics because these have been examined elsewhere and were not our focus. Future research could examine whether those characteristics, in isolation or in combination, affect the attraction effect in crowdfunding. Attraction effects might be weaker or stronger depending on certain founder, backer, or project characteristics.

Fourth, some RBC projects incentivize potential backers with perks, such as meet-and-greet and book signing events, and including names of supporters in picture credits. Our theorizing and empirical analysis do not account for these aspects. We also examine the differences only between no economic consequence and economic consequence, whereas we reckon that there may be <sup>fi</sup>ner variations of this aspect in the real world. Finally, we hasten to add that we examined a scenario where a product has only two attributes, price and quality, but in other buying scenarios, many more product attributes may need to be considered, potentially limiting the applicability of the attraction effect in contexts with more than two attributes.

## 9.3. Research Contributions and Implications

The purpose of our study was to understand the attraction effect in a digital market context such as RBC. Our study makes several key contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the attractioneffect literature by providing strong evidence of the effect in an applied setting. Studies of the attraction effect have produced ambiguous results. As a result, scholars have questioned whether the attraction effect works in practice (Frederick et al. 2014). We show that the effect works in applied digital situations—namely, crowdfunding—where product attributes can be numerical and nonnumerical. Because rich attribute representation enabled by digital markets renders comparison of both numeric and nonnumeric attributes easy, the use of the attraction effect to examine the impact of menu design on consumer choices can enrich the theory.

Second, we contribute to the behavioral economics literature by not only testing the Bordalo et al. (2013) salience theory but extending it. To our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to employ the salience theory to investigate a digital marketplace. Our results are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant because digital marketplaces go beyond the original setup considered by Bordalo et al. (2013). In the original theory, the authors indicated that both price and quality are measured numerically, but that may not always be true in digital marketplaces. We show that the theory also works with a nonnumerical attribute representation. This is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant result, suggesting a much wider applicability of the salience theory than recognized in the literature. Our study can motivate subsequent research in this domain.

Third, we contribute to the growing literature on traditional crowdfunding. We provide evidence demonstrating that the design of the digital reward menu— the core of RBC—constitutes a largely untapped yet signi<sup>fi</sup>cant opportunity to improve fundraising. This approach is novel because previous studies have usually examined only drivers of crowdfunding success that can be considered environmental factors (for exceptions, see Yang et al. 2020, Jiang et al. 2021). We, in contrast, investigated the decision to support a project by looking at behavioral insights at an individual level when designing reward menus. Our research approach of using behavioral insights in design—that is, “digital nudging” (Schneider et al. 2018) and “persuasive technology/design” (Oinas-Kukkonen and Harjumaa 2009)—paves the way for future studies to investigate the attraction effect and other context effects in a more nuanced manner in crowdfunding and other online markets.

Finally, we contribute to the literature on information systems. The analysis of digital markets is one of the core topics in contemporary IS research. IS researchers have been studying digital markets for many years; however, this literature takes a marketing or econometric perspective on the subject, estimating various population average effects such as sales, conversions, and contributions. We join this literature with this paper but show that IS design—here, the design of reward menus in the context of crowdfunding—has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on user decisions. Our research, in essence, provides one potential mechanism for how digital menu designs can in<sup>fl</sup>uence crowdfunding campaign success, as shown in the prior literature (Yang et al. 2020, Jiang et al. 2021). We posit that this success comes through individual consumer-level decisions, which are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by digital designs. Our results also establish the strategic importance of the digital design by demonstrating that menu designs can shift consumers’ preferences by as much as 28.2%. This paper thus draws attention to the information systems design aspects to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer decision making, which is an understudied aspect in the literature (Benartzi and Bhargava 2020, Bhargava et al. 2000). We add to this literature by showing the IS design can have a lasting effect on consumer decision making.

## 9.4. Implications for Practice

Online crowdfunding markets entail the participation of both project creators and project backers. Digital market operators act as facilitators and providers of the institutional framework for the exchange transactions to take place. Our results thus have important implications for project creators, project backers, and digital market operators.

For project creators, the key outcomes are to meet their funding goals and to launch their ventures successfully. To do so, they need to nudge backers toward more valuable products. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the attraction effect may help lead backers to higherpriced rewards. In addition, our study has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed further boundary conditions and guidelines. Project creators should feel free to use both numerical and nonnumerical attributes. Nonnumerical attributes are intuitive and widespread in daily life, so our <sup>fi</sup>ndings—that both numerical and nonnumerical attributes enable the attraction effect—give project creators more <sup>fl</sup>exibility in designing the digital reward menu. Our results also give project creators a sense of what proportion of the backers can be convinced to switch to a higher-priced version of the digital product, which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence revenue during product release. Finally, for the attraction effect to work, backers must be able to compare different products in a choice set and assess “value for money” for each. Project creators must present backers with a few carefully selected reward options; many tiers of rewards may overwhelm backers and prevent them from detecting dominance relationships.

Our results also have implications for project backers. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of cognitive biases on decision making in a crowdfunding environment. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they suggest that backers can be nudged toward the high-priced reward by project creators inserting decoy options. To make informed, unbiased decisions, backers should examine reward menus carefully. Backers have an interest in projects being realized, so it would be in their interest for project creators to collect suf<sup>fi</sup>cient funds to realize their projects. Thus, nudging backers to higher-priced rewards would also be in the backers’ interest. In a recent study, Loewenstein et al. (2015) found that nudging is effective as long as people believe that the attempted nudge is the best option for them. In the RBC context, backers may be attached to the proposed project; thus, they want to make it happen, increasing their willingness to contribute. The attraction effect would be a vehicle to achieve higher contributions.

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have implications for digital market providers. Their business model is largely based on collecting fees for successful campaigns. (Kickstarter charges 5% of the funds raised.) Hence, it serves providers’ interests if project creators collect more funds. To help them do so, providers can supply creators with guidelines and handbooks. Kickstarter crafted a creator handbook that walks creators “through everything from planning your shipping to communicating with backers,”<sup>18</sup> including a section on “building rewards.” Markets could adapt their guidelines for project creators and add sections on reward-menu design, with a focus on cognitive biases. Kickstarter recommends using scarce rewards but offers no other guidelines for designing reward menus.

Although this study focused on crowdfunding markets, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be transferred to other digital marketplaces. A characteristic that digital marketplaces share is that they offer customers a choice set of options. Our implications should be relevant not only for reward menus in crowdfunding but for choice set in digital marketplaces. For example, Uber offers choice sets with different car options; they could use a decoy option to make electric vehicles more attractive. Likewise, airlines present different <sup>fl</sup>ight itineraries; they could use a decoy option to increase carbon offsets. Hence, our implications may hold for other contexts, and future research could investigate those contexts and boundaries in digital marketplaces.

## 9.5. Conclusion

The results of our studies suggest that inserting decoy options into reward menus can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence backers’ choices. In particular, inserting a price or quality decoy shifts backers’ preferences from low-priced competitor rewards to high-priced target rewards. This effect is independent of the attribute type (numerical or nonnumerical attributes) but depends on the choice type (hypothetical or consequential). Taken together, these results indicate a choice bias in digital choices menus: Information systems design can in<sup>fl</sup>uence choice behavior, and small modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to a digital choice set can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shift customers’ preferences.
