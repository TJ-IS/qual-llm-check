# Pushing Yourself Harder: The Effects of Mobile Touch Modes on Users’ Self-Regulation

Abstract. Many mobile applications use push noti<sup>fi</sup>cations and reminders to explicitly educate, remind, and motivate users to perform healthy behaviors. However, users do not always act according to these explicit digital interventions. Our study investigates whether users’ self-regulation can be implicitly facilitated with a proper mobile interaction design. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigate the impacts of two touch modes that are supported by forcebased interaction technology, that is, pressing and tapping. Drawing on the theory of embodied cognition, which suggests that people automatically infer meanings from their bodily actions, we conjecture that pressing, compared with tapping, enhances selfregulation because the action of pressing on the touchscreen embodies resolute approach motivation toward goals. We test our hypotheses in three experiments. The <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment investigates beverage choices on a mobile app; the second experiment examines goal setting on a <sup>fi</sup>tness app; and the third experiment focuses on personal hygiene learning on a mobile education app. The results from the three experiments show that pressing actions can improve users’ self-regulation in selecting a healthier but less tasty beverage (Study 1), setting higher exercise goals and performing more physical exercise (Study 2), and reducing lapses in maintaining personal hygiene (Study 3). In addition, such effects were more salient among users with a higher level of health knowledge and a promotion-focused health orientation. This study contributes to healthcare IT research by showing that mobile interaction can be leveraged to nudge users toward enhanced self-regulation.

## 1. Introduction

People pay increasing attention to pursuing a healthy lifestyle (Ipsos 2020). However, good intentions to maintain a healthy lifestyle might not always lead to persistent healthy behavior. Suppose you are in a cafe´ and see a high-calorie cake vividly displayed on the iPad menu. Can you resist the temptation and order a whole-wheat bagel instead? Indeed, healthy behavior, that is, activity undertaken for the purpose of maintaining or enhancing one’s health, such as following a healthy diet and engaging in regular exercise (Loef and Walach 2012), often requires self-regulation. To achieve long-term well-being, people may have to perform actions that are temporarily dif<sup>fi</sup>cult or displeasing but bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in the long run (Mann et al. 2013). Hence, it is meaningful for researchers and practitioners to understand how to leverage technologies to enhance users’ self-regulation.

Health-related decisions are now routinely made through mobile devices. For example, an increasing number of restaurants allow users to order food on tablets or smartphones, which brings consumers’ dietary decisions to touchscreens. People are also increasingly using mobile health applications, such as Nike and Calorie Counter, to plan and track their healthrelated behavior. Accordingly, much research is devoted to designing and evaluating digital health interventions that aim to explicitly motivate and remind users to maintain a healthy lifestyle. In particular, explicit interventions, such as noti<sup>fi</sup>cations and reminders, delivered by portable mobile devices can be contextualized, personalized, and pushed to users with the right timing (Free et al. 2013). However, their effectiveness is often impaired by users’ negligence and even psychological reactance to information with an obvious persuasive intent (Pinder et al. 2015).

Our study, thus, aims to devise implicit ways to enhance users’ self-regulation with digital technologies. Consistent with the nudge literature (Thaler and Sunstein 2008, Weinmann et al. 2016), which advocates eliciting bene<sup>fi</sup>cial behavior change without coercion, this study investigates how to leverage mobile interaction to nudge users toward enhanced self-regulation in their pursuit of health-related goals. One major characteristic of mobile interaction, compared with the traditional interaction modality (e.g., mouse and keyboard), is the use of natural touch gestures (Liu et al. 2019, Luo et al. 2019). This modality shift results in changing consumer perception and purchase behavior in implicit ways. For example, prior research <sup>fi</sup>nds that touch-based interaction, compared with mouse-based interaction, can elicit a greater sense of ownership and increase consumers purchase intention on online shopping websites (Brasel and Gips 2014). Other studies also show that touchbased interaction facilitates the choice of an affect-laden alternative over a cognitively superior one (Shen et al. 2016, Liu and Jiang 2020).

Despite past research examining touch-based interaction, how different touch gestures can affect users’ cognition and behavior remains largely unknown. According to the theory of embodied cognition, different bodily actions can embody various meanings and, thus, shape our thoughts and behaviors (Niedenthal 2007, Barsalou 2008, Krishna and Schwarz 2013). For example, prior research reveals that incidental bodily actions, such as covering or turning a page for rejected alternatives, elicit a sense of “choice closure,” thus reducing consumers regret and increasing their choice satisfaction (Gu et al. 2013). Therefore, we conjecture that bodily interaction with devices can subliminally trigger associated mental representations and thereby can be designed to elicit positive behavior change.

We test the conjecture by investigating the effects of two touch modes, that is, pressing and tapping, enabled by force-based interaction technology. Whereas tapping is performed with a gentle force exertion, one needs to apply extra force to carry out pressing actions. Many prevalent devices have begun to enable the detection of users’ touch force with either pressure-sensitive hardware (e.g., pressure-sensitive touchscreens, touchpads, and controllers) or advanced algorithms (e.g., identifying changes in touch surface area) (Heo and Lee 2011, Quinn 2019). For instance, pressure-sensitive touchscreens have become a highlighted feature of certain trendy smartphone models (e.g., Xiaomi Black Shark 4). Similarly, different touch modes enabled by force-based interaction in haptic touchpads are broadly embraced by major laptop manufacturers (e.g., Apple, IBM, and Microsoft) (Rubino 2021). Recently, the Tesla Model S has also included force-sensitive touch controls on the center console (Lambert 2021).

As a fundamental way of interacting with the physical world, force is often applied when we make an effort to achieve goals (Talmy 1988, White 2012). For example, we regularly tighten our muscles and clench our <sup>fi</sup>sts to gather strength and express determination; we invest more physical effort in acquiring something we genuinely want. These life experiences contribute subliminally to the development of body–mind associations between force exertion and intense approach motivation toward goals. For this reason, touch modes (i.e., pressing and tapping) that vary in required strength may convey different embodied information and, thus, implicitly affect users’ judgments, deci sions, and behaviors. This study, therefore, examines whether touch modes can affect users’ self-regulation in making health-related decisions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to test the embodiment effect of bodily actions in interaction with mobile devices on users’ decision making and healthy behaviors as prior studies focus mainly on the usabil ity aspects of mobile interaction (Marshall et al. 2013). By highlighting the role of bodily interactions with devices in shaping users’ cognition, this study offers a new perspective on the application of embodied cognition theory in the human–computer interaction (HCI) context.

In addition, this study investigates when the embodi ment effects of touch modes may vary. According to embodied cognition theory, although people may automatically infer purposes and intentions from bodily actions, how they interpret and use the embodied information may depend on their existing knowledge, atti tudes, and conceptions (Wilson 2002, Zhang and Risen 2014). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, prior research suggests that the effectiveness of health interventions is often affected by users’ health-related knowledge (Kenkel 1991, Johnson and Hariharan 2017) and health-related regulatory orientation (Gomez et al. 2013). Whereas health knowledge denotes awareness of facts and information about health development, health orientation re<sup>fl</sup>ects an innate motivation toward health development (i.e., either seeking health improvement or avoiding health hazards). By revealing the contingent factors of the embodied self regulation effects, that is, health knowledge and health orientation, this research deepens our knowledge of how touch modes can be designed to enhance selfregulation among different groups of users. It also enriches our theoretical understanding of how people infer the embodied meanings conveyed through bodily actions and incorporate them into decision making.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Study 1 focused on users’ self-regulation in maintaining a healthy diet (i.e., choosing a healthy beverage over an unhealthy one). It explored the main effect of touch mode (pressing versus tapping) on selfregulation and the moderating role of health knowledge. Study 2 was conducted in the context of <sup>fi</sup>tness app usage. As setting challenging exercise goals and exerting oneself to reach the goals require self-regulation, we used goal setting and actual physical exercise as indicators of self-regulation. We investigated whether touch mode (pressing versus tapping) affected users’ goal setting and actual physical exercise and how the effects differed among users with different health orientations. Study 3 examined how touch mode in<sup>fl</sup>uenced users self-regulation in adherence to hygiene practices. We performed a mediation analysis to corroborate the role of approach motivation in explaining the embodied selfregulation effect. In addition to pressing and tapping, Study 3 also considered a holding condition. Unlike pressing, which is triggered by a <sup>fi</sup>rm force exertion on the touchscreen, holding occurs when users tap and hold the touchscreen for an extended period. The inclusion of the holding condition enabled us to effectively rule out contact duration as an alternative explanation of the effects of the touch modes.

The remaining sections of this paper are organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst review the related literature and develop our hypotheses. We then describe our research methods and results. The three experiments provide converging evidence showing that pressing (compared with tapping) can enhance users’ self-regulation in maintaining a healthy diet, performing physical exercise, and engaging in hygiene behaviors. Additionally, the results show that the impact of touch modes is particularly evident for users with more health knowledge and a more promotion-focused health orientation. We conclude by discussing how the current work expands research on digital health interventions and the implications for the effective design of mobile interfaces.

## 2. Related Literature

## 2.1. Digital Health Interventions

In recent years, digital health interventions, that is, interventions designed to improve users’ health status via digital technologies, such as websites, text messages, and mobile applications, have increasingly attracted research attention (Loef and Walach 2012, Murray et al. 2016, Chen et al. 2019). Based on theories such as the health belief model (Janz and Becker 1984) and protection motivation theory (Rippetoe and Rogers 1987), previous studies reveal that healthy behavior can be instigated by drawing users’ attention to health through explicit digital interventions (Chatterjee and Price 2009, Agarwal et al. 2010). For example, persuasive messages and noti<sup>fi</sup>cations can increase users’ awareness of healthy behavior and remind them to maintain a healthy lifestyle (Bickmore et al. 2007, Liu et al. 2019). Self-monitoring features provide visualizations of performance and, thus, increase users’ self-improvement motivation to engage persistently in physical exercise (Etkin 2016). In addition, by facilitating interpersonal communication, social features in <sup>fi</sup>tness applications enable users to provide informational and emotional support to one another and, thus, motivate them to engage in healthy behavior (Frost and Massagli 2008, Choi and Jiang 2018, Arigo et al. 2020). Collectively, these interventions explicitly educate, per suade, and motivate people to sustain a healthy lifestyle.

However, explicit interventions do not always result in actual behavior change (Pinder et al. 2015) and may even cause unintended negative consequences, for example, annoyance and even aversion (Fogarty and Youngs 2000, Yi et al. 2019). It is also found that explicit interventions may undermine users’ intrinsic motivation by making activities (e.g., physical exercise) feel more like work and, thus, less enjoyable (Etkin 2016). Accord ingly, some researchers advocate nudging users implicitly (Bargh and Morsella 2008, Thaler and Sunstein 2008). In particular, prior research in psychology and behavioral economics suggests that incidental, ambient, and periph eral cues can activate a user’s preexisting “instinctive” cognitive paths and, thus, nudge the user to perform bene<sup>fi</sup>cial behaviors (Bargh 2006, Goes 2013). Therefore, human judgments and decisions may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by factors that are not “rationally” related to the focal task. For example, seemingly task-irrelevant cues, for example, auditory cues, can subliminally affect consumers’ food choices and consumption amounts (Elder and Mohr 2016). Indeed, recent advances in health psychology encourage practitioners to implicitly promote healthy behavior (Sheeran et al. 2013, Hollands et al. 2016). However, few empirical studies investigate how digital tech nologies can be leveraged for such implicit interventions (Pinder et al. 2018).

In summary, the digital health intervention literature suggests that healthy behavior can be promoted with both explicit and implicit approaches. Although prior research focuses primarily on eliciting healthy behavior change by explicitly enhancing users’ awareness, motivations, and intentions, it would be interesting to consider leveraging digital technologies to unobtrusively and implicitly nudge behavior change. This study, therefore, complements this stream of literature by investigating the potential of touch modes as a subliminal intervention to nudge healthy behavior. In the next section, we review the literature on self-regulation to provide the premise upon which our research model is developed. Afterward, we discuss the literature on embodied cognition to develop the theoretical connection between body movement and cognition.

## 2.2. Self-Regulation

Good health is generally considered desirable, but many people <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to consistently engage in healthy behaviors because they are typically challenging or even painful. For example, to follow a healthy diet, people must give up delicious foods that often contain high levels of sugar and calories. They also need to push themselves toward their physical limits and exert themselves through strenuous physical exercise to achieve better health. Thus, adopting and maintaining healthy behavior often require selfregulation (Carver and Scheier 2001). In other words, to achieve long-term well-being, people need to not only forgo temptations, but also overcome temporal dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and inertia (Vohs and Baumeister 2004). Indeed, many unsuccessful health-related attempts, such as failed adherence to medication regimens (Horne and Weinman 2002) and failure to maintain a healthy diet (Hagger et al. 2006), along with unhealthy behaviors, such as alcohol addiction (Murgraff et al. 2000), originate from people’s inability to regulate their own behavior.

Self-regulation is a process by which people intentionally alter their responses, including thoughts, emotions, impulses, and behaviors, based on certain standards and goals (Mischel et al. 2011). It is often viewed as a dynamic process that includes multiple activities, such as identifying goals, formulating plans for the pursuit of those goals, executing the plans, and maintaining the pursuit despite distractions (Inzlicht et al. 2021). In general, self-regulation occurs when there is a con<sup>fl</sup>ict or gap between an individual’s current state and an individual’s (either explicit or latent) goal, and the individual needs to maintain or alter the individual’s behavior to achieve the goal. Hence, self-regulation is characterized by a broad set of perceptual and process measures re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the effort exerted to achieve goals (e.g., willpower) (Latham and Locke 1991, Gailliot et al. 2007, Mann et al. 2013, Enkavi et al. 2019). As self-regulation requires people to take actions to move toward goals, it is also deemed to be related to a number of behavioral outcomes in different contexts, such as maintaining a healthy diet, engaging in physical exercise, and adhering to hygiene behaviors (Fulham and Mullan 2011, Hung and Labroo 2011, Critcher and Ferguson 2016, Enkavi et al. 2019).

Scholars largely agree that people’s self-control ability is crucial to developing self-regulation (Carver and Scheier 2001). However, improving self-control ability can be a daunting task because it often entails repetitive physical self-control exercises or numerous mental practices (Sultan et al. 2012). Even when a fundamental improvement of self-regulation is not immediately achievable, scholars suggest that self-regulation can be induced by temporarily boosting motivational resources (Metcalfe and Mischel 1999, Baumeister et al. 2007). That is, external stimuli that increase an individual’s motivational intensity for goal attainment can effectively enhance an individual’s self-regulation in goal pursuit (Hagger et al. 2006). For example, it is found that an individual’s genuine interest and values in achieving a goal can powerfully suppress goal-disruptive temptations and increase resilience against impulsivity (Milyavskaya et al. 2015).

In summary, although self-regulation is crucial for achieving well-being in the long run, it is often attenuated, if not entirely subdued, by unpleasant experiences in performing healthy behaviors. Evidence suggests that self-regulation can be boosted via the activation of intense approach motivation toward goals (Carver and Scheier 2001, Werner and Milyavskaya 2019). Our study, thus, investigates how different touch modes can be used to enhance self-regulation subliminally in health related contexts.

## 2.3. Embodied Cognition Theory

Whereas previous psychology studies accumulate an extensive understanding of how cognition affects behavior, recent studies on embodied cognition reveal that cognitive activities (e.g., judgments and decision making), which are often assumed to be effortful, deliberate, or intentional, can at times be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the senses and movements of the body (Niedenthal et al. 2005, Barsalou 2008). For instance, research <sup>fi</sup>nds that the experience of physical warmth (e.g., holding a warm cup of coffee in social interaction) can lead people to judge a stranger to be friendlier (Williams et al. 2009); physically approaching (versus avoiding) leads to more positive attitudes toward a product (Labroo and Nielsen 2009); and washing one’s hands after a tough product decision can also wash away postdecisional dissonance and, thus, lead to higher product satisfaction (Lee and Schwarz 2010). These and many other examples demonstrate that cognition is not completely based on amodal abstractions, but is deeply rooted in one’s experience in interacting with the physical environment.

According to embodied cognition theory, the reason for these phenomena is that our bodily interactions with the environment can activate memory associated with prior experience. As individuals interact with the environment (e.g., approaching and reaching for a product they like in a physical store), various states across modalities are captured and stored in their memory in a multimodal representation. Therefore, people can establish automatic associations between bodily states (e.g., actions of reaching out) and cognition (e.g., positive attitudes) in relation to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c environment (e.g., shopping at a store). More importantly, these body–mind associations are <sup>fi</sup>rmly embedded in their memory and can be subliminally activated when similar bodily states are experienced regardless of whether the original environment is still involved. Hence, when people are induced to perform actions of reaching (e.g., arm <sup>fl</sup>exion) during product evaluation in another environment (e.g., in a laboratory setting), they are likely to subliminally develop positive attitudes toward the focal product. The reason is that arm <sup>fl</sup>exion (e.g., the motor action of bringing an object toward oneself) activates memories of having acquired desired objects in the past, and people subconsciously associate such positive attitudes with the new product (Cacioppo et al. 1993, Van den Bergh et al. 2011).

Prior research suggests that the effect of embodied cognition is often subtle and typically evident when one’s judgment or decision does not require substantial deliberation (Reimann et al. 2012). Furthermore, some evidence suggests that the activation of body–mind associations can vary across different environments (Meier et al. 2012, Reimann et al. 2012, Krishna and Schwarz 2013, Brinol et al.˜ 2017). For example, in a context in which an individual actively initiates an action, force exertion action can trigger the memory of active pursuit, but in another context, in which an individual defends oneself against a threat, force exertion can be associated with a memory of protection and rejection (Talmy 1988, White 2012, Wolff et al. 2014). As this study examines users’ voluntary usage of health-related mobile applications, we focus on the former context.

In addition, the impact of bodily actions on judgment and decision making may be subject to people’s preexisting knowledge and attitudes toward the decision. For example, affectionate gestures (e.g., hugging a product during evaluation) can provide embodied cues of “liking and attachment,” which can further induce strong positive attitudes toward a product. Such an effect is found to be stronger among consumers who feel lonely (Hadi and Valenzuela 2014) because lonely individuals tend to have a greater need to attach and, thus, are especially sensitive to the embodied information (i.e., liking) engendered by affectionate gestures. This rationale is consistent with emerging theoretical work that highlights the potential boundary conditions in embodiment effects (Meier et al. 2012, Brinol et al.˜ 2017); however, there is a paucity of empirical evidence on such a contingent mechanism (Kwon and Adaval 2018).

Drawing on embodied cognition theory, HCI research employs the body–mind association to advance the usability of mobile interactions (Dourish 2004, Kirsh 2013). For example, because the swiping gesture is associated mainly with disposal activities in everyday life, app designers often adopt it for users to perform deletion or removal (Mauney et al. 2010). Regarding force-based interaction, HCI scholars utilize varying levels of touch force for a variety of interaction cases, such as altering the thickness of a sketch in drawing applications, controlling <sup>fi</sup>repower in games, and activating pop-up content (Heo and Lee 2011, Hurtienne et al. 2015, Antoine et al. 2017). However, past research focuses primarily on employing force-based interaction to enhance system usability. The effects of force-based interaction on users’ decisions and subsequent behaviors are underexplored. In addition, some studies call for a contingent view of embodiment effects, but systematic investigation remains scarce (Meier et al. 2012, Kwon and Adaval 2018). This study, thus, aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll these research gaps by examining how touch modes affect user decisions and self-regulation behavior in health-related contexts and testing the moderating factors that may affect how people interpret and use embodied information.

## 3. Hypothesis Development

Drawing from embodied cognition theory, we argue that, in making health-related decisions on mobile devices, different touch modes (e.g., pressing versus tapping) can lead to different embodied effects on regulatory behavior. In addition, we examine how users health knowledge and health orientation moderate the embodiment effects of touch modes. The research model is presented in Figure 1.

## 3.1. Touch Modes and Self-Regulation

Force exertion is repetitively performed in the common daily environment (White 2012). For instance, we regularly tighten our muscles and clench our <sup>fi</sup>sts to gather strength and express determination; we invest more physical effort in acquiring something we truly want; we use the phrases “push yourself harder” and “exert yourself” to encourage ourselves to pursue higher goals. Therefore, people can naturally form body–mind associations between force exertion and eagerness as well as the urge to move toward goal achievement when they voluntarily initiate an action. Indeed, past academic research con<sup>fi</sup>rms that force exertion is often associated with resolute approach motivation in goal pursuit even when the goals may be latent or vague (Talmy 1988, Ackerman et al. 2010, Wolff et al. 2014).

The embodied cognition literature extensively documents how our bodies can profoundly affect our minds (Niedenthal et al. 2005, Barsalou 2008). Because people form body–mind associations with life experiences, some bodily actions are so closely linked to certain thoughts and emotions that the mere execution of an action may activate thoughts or feelings associated with the action (Hostetter and Alibali 2008, Goldin-Meadow and Beilock 2010). Drawing on the close association between force exertion and active pursuit, we postulate that, when an individual voluntarily initiates a force exertion, the memory of investing effort to pursue goals is implicitly evoked. Following this logic, when users exert physical force on a touchscreen in making healthrelated decisions, their memory of past pursuit of health goals can be subliminally activated, and they experience intense feelings of eagerness in pursuing health improvement. Thus, pressing during interaction with health apps engenders stronger approach motivation toward health goal attainment. Accordingly, users will be more willing to regulate their behaviors to achieve those goals (Harmon-Jones et al. 2011).

In summary, we argue that, pressing, as compared with tapping, elevates health app users’ eagerness to achieve their health goals and likely leads to stronger self-regulation in health-related behavior (e.g., staying healthy and maintaining an active lifestyle), particularly when users consider trade-offs in decision making (e.g., deciding between dieting versus bingeing or working out in a gym versus loa<sup>fi</sup>ng). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. Compared with tapping, pressing in mobile interaction can enhance users’ self-regulation in health goal pursuits.

## 3.2. Moderation Effects of Health Knowledge and Health Orientation on Self-Regulation

Past research examining embodied cognition reveals that the effect of body–mind association on self-regulation can be affected by individual differences (Reimann et al. 2012, Zhang and Risen 2014). This study focuses on the moderating roles of health knowledge and health orientation in shaping the effects of force-based interactions on self-regulation toward maintaining healthy behaviors.

3.2.1. Health Knowledge. Health knowledge re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which an individual is aware of the health impact of the individual’s behavior (Johnson and Hariharan 2017). People who possess more health knowledge tend to feel more responsible for their health and well-being as they are not only acquainted with the importance of healthy behavior but also well versed in the negative consequences of unhealthy behavior (Plank and Gould 1990, Newsom et al. 2005, Iversen and Kraft 2006). As a result, they are particularly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by behavioral nudges that promote healthy behaviors (Hong 2011, Stevenson 2016). In particular, health knowledge increases the awareness and the accessibility of individuals’ health-related goals, enhancing the alignment of their health behaviors with their goals (Iversen and Kraft 2006). Hence, when users make health-related decisions, for those with a higher level of health knowledge, the effects of pressing on strengthening self-regulation in pursuit of goals (i.e., well-being) is facilitated by their health knowledge.

In contrast, users who are less knowledgeable about the health consequences of their behaviors are often less sensitive, if not averse, to health interventions. According to embodied cognition theory, people tend to interpret and use embodied information in a manner that is consistent with their preexisting beliefs and attitudes. Lower health knowledge may result in underestimating health risks, thus making health goals vaguer and less accessible. In this case, among users with a lower level of health knowledge, the advantageous effect of pressing compared with tapping in inducing meaningful goal pursuit and augmenting self-regulation is less evident. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2a. The effect of touch modes on self-regulation in health goal pursuit is moderated by health knowledge; that is, the effect of pressing on enhancing self-regulation is stronger among users with a higher level of health knowledge than among users with a lower level of health knowledge.

3.2.2. Health Orientation. We expect individuals’ health orientation to play a vital role in shaping self-regulation toward maintaining healthy behaviors. Regulatory focus theory posits two regulatory orientations that govern goal pursuit: promotion- and prevention-focused orientation (Higgins 1998). Whereas promotion-focused orientation is associated with growth and involves striving for ideals, aspirations, and rewards through accomplishment, prevention-focused orientation is associated with the need for safety and involves ful<sup>fi</sup>lling duties and obligations through responsible behaviors. In the healthcare context, people with promotion-focused health orientation are often eager to improve their bodily state and health condition, whereas people with preventionfocused health orientation are typically more focused on avoiding health issues and diseases (Keller 2006, Gomez et al. 2013). Accordingly, we conjecture that the effect of touch modes on self-regulation can vary based on users health orientation.

When users press to make health-related decisions, the embodied information (i.e., the association between force exertion and approach motivation) increases their eagerness to pursue health-related goals. Because users with a promotion-focused orientation typically have a strong urge to improve their health condition (Gomez et al. 2013), the embodied information becomes closely aligned with their existing attitudes and beliefs, hence further bolstering their motivation to maintain healthy behaviors. Furthermore, users with a promotionfocused orientation typically have a high readiness to respond to goal-achievement opportunities. Consequently, when eagerness for health improvement is activated through pressing, these users can become especially willing to adopt behaviors to achieve health goals. Hence, promotion-focused orientation strengthens the effects of pressing on self-regulation in maintaining goal-consistent behaviors.

In contrast, prevention-focused orientation often leads people to emphasize preventing negative outcomes (Higgins 1998, Cesario et al. 2004). Although the ultimate goal is to stay healthy, users with a prevention-focused orientation often emphasize preventing negative outcomes associated with self-regulation failure and ful<sup>fi</sup>lling basic health needs. Thus, users who are oriented toward the prevention bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of healthy behavior, for example, reducing the risk of chronic diseases by exercising more, inherently have an avoidance tendency and are less willing to push themselves in strenuous endeavors (e.g., intensive exercise). Although pressing signals intense approach motivation for active pursuit, such embodied information is less meaningful for preventionfocused people. Accordingly, these users are less likely to incorporate the intense approach motivation embodied in pressing into their judgment and decision making. Thus, the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of pressing (versus tapping) on self-regulation is less salient among users with a prevention-focused health orientation. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows.

Hypothesis 2b. The effect of touch modes on self-regulation in health goal pursuit is moderated by health orientation; that is, the effect of pressing on enhancing self-regulation is stronger among users with a promotion-focused health orientation than among users with a prevention-focused health orientation.

In summary, our theoretical development explains how touch modes may in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ self-regulation in pursuing health-related goals. We also theorize that these effects can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by users’ health knowledge and health orientation. We conducted three experiments to test our hypotheses and the underlying theoretical mechanisms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in Study 1, we examine whether touch modes can affect users willpower to resist unhealthy but tempting beverages as well as their actual choice. We also investigate how users’ health knowledge moderates these effects. In Study 2, we examine whether touch modes can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ exercise goals and exercise amount as well as the extent to which these effects are contingent on users’ health orientation. We further conducted sup plementary Study 3 in the context of hygiene education to demonstrate that pressing can reduce violations of hygiene guidelines and provide direct evidence to explain the embodiment effects of touch modes on selfregulation.

## 4. Study 1: Touch Modes and Beverage Choice

When making food choices, people often need to consider the trade-off between healthiness and tastiness because many healthy foods, for example, salads and whole grains, are plain in <sup>fl</sup>avor, whereas many delicious foods, for example, cakes and fried chicken, are less healthy. Resisting the temptation of pleasurable, indulgent foods in favor of healthy but less tasty foods is often challenging and requires self-regulation. Thus, in Study 1, we investigated whether touch modes can affect users’ self-regulation to maintain a healthy diet. Two variables were used as indicators of self-regulation. Because self-regulation typically requires strong willpower, that is, the mental resolve to resist temptations and persist in goal pursuit (Mischel 1996, Gailliot et al. 2007), we measured users’ willpower as a subjective indicator of self-regulation (Baumeister et al. 2007). Moreover, we captured their actual product choices as an objective indicator of self-regulation and hypothesized that pressing, compared with tapping, would lead to a greater likelihood of choosing a healthy option.

## 4.1. Experimental Design and Procedure

A <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment was conducted at a public university in Southeast Asia. The experiment administrators randomly approached students passing a university cafeteria and asked whether they were willing to taste new beverages for free. Participants were presented with beverage information on a smartphone and were asked to use the smartphone to select the beverage they most wanted to drink. They were informed that they would receive the beverage they chose along with cash in the local currency equivalent to US\$4 as a reward for their participation.

In the mobile app, we provided four beverage options (two healthier but less tasty options and two less healthy but tastier options) of similar prices. To further demonstrate the appropriateness of our beverage stimulus, we conducted a pretest online among 40 participants from the same university. The pretest results showed that the two types of beverages did not differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in overall consumer attitudes and familiarity to consumers but did differ in perceived healthiness<sup>1</sup> $( M _ { h e a l t h } = 5 . 3 6$ versus $M _ { u n h e a l t h } =$ $2 . 8 7 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and perceived tastiness $( M _ { h e a l t h } = 3 . 9 4$ versus $M _ { u n h e a l t h } = 5 . \bar { 3 } 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . That is, healthy beverages (e.g., ginseng–date tea) were perceived as less tasty, whereas unhealthy beverages (e.g., sparkling water with passionfruit <sup>fl</sup>avor) were perceived as tasty. Thus, participants had to make a choice by considering the trade-off between tastiness and healthiness. None of the participants had consumed the four beverages previously; hence, their choices were unlikely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by past preference. We had also conducted a pilot test<sup>2</sup> to verify the experimental procedure and ensure that the mobile app worked as expected.

One hundred twenty participants volunteered to participate in our experiment and were randomly assigned to the tapping or pressing condition with 60 subjects in each group. At the beginning of the task, participants were shown four beverage options displayed on a provided smartphone. The four beverages were divided into two categories labeled “healthy options” and “tasty options.” The order of the two categories was counterbalanced. The participants inspected each of the four beverage options by either tapping or pressing. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in the tapping condition, on the beverage option page, participants tapped on beverage images to show the preview pop-ups (i.e., a short description with the beverage image). After examining the short description, participants tapped on the beverage image on the pop-up to navigate to the detailed information page, which provided both health information (e.g., calorie and sugar levels) and taste information (e.g., taste description and rating) of the beverage. In the pressing condition, on the beverage option page, participants pressed on beverage images to show the preview pop-ups. Only when the pressure was above a certain threshold, which was consistent with the prevailing industry standard advocated by Apple, would the preview pop-up be displayed. Participants then pressed on the beverage image on the pop-up to retrieve the detailed information page. Screenshots of the mobile app are provided in Online Appendix I. After inspecting the four beverage options, participants informed the experiment administrators of their choices. In addition, participants were asked to complete a postexperiment survey that included questions about their demographic information and measurement items of the research variables. The measurement items are listed in Appendix A.

## 4.3. Discussion of Results

Consistent with our predictions, the results reveal that pressing, compared with tapping, augmented participants’ willpower to resist temptation. That is, when considering trade-offs between healthiness and tastiness, pressing actions strengthened participants’ approach motivation toward latent health-related goals and, thus, enhanced their mental resolve to resist temptations. We also observed the interaction effect between touch modes and health knowledge on participants’ willpower. Specifically, pressing increased willpower among participants with a higher level of health knowledge; however, such an effect was not evident among those who had a lower level of health knowledge. Our rationale is consistent with the embodiment literature (Landau et al. 2010) that health knowledge can amplify the accessibility of healthrelated goals; hence, the approach motivation embodied in pressing is more meaningful among people with health knowledge.

Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that touch modes not only affected willpower, but also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced beverage choices. Compared with participants who performed tapping actions, those who performed pressing actions were more likely to choose healthy beverages. However, we did not observe an interaction effect on beverage choices between touch modes and health knowledge. The reason is likely that beverage choices, as a behavioral indicator of self-regulation, may not be as sensitive as subjective willpower.

## 6. Study 3: Touch Modes and Health Education

The key objective of Study 3 is to acquire empirical evidence to uncover how touch modes enhance selfregulation. In other words, we aim to show that pressing leads to higher self-regulation than tapping because pressing induces stronger approach motivation toward goal achievement. Study 3 was conducted in the context of health-related education.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the recent disruptions (e.g., of<sup>fi</sup>ce closures and self-isolation) caused by COVID-19 have necessitated the massive dissemination of hygieneand health-related education to the public. However, it is found that, after attending health-related education, individuals often do not entirely adhere to personal hygiene behaviors and at times violate standard hygiene practices (von Lengerke et al. 2015). Hence, in Study 3, we aimed to test whether pressing actions on a mobile personal hygiene education app could lead to adherence to personal hygiene practice. In particular, we used the participants’ determination to adhere to good hygiene practice as a subjective indicator of intended self-regulation. We observed participants behaviors after their interaction with the app and reverse-coded their violations of hygiene guidelines as an objective indicator of self-regulation. To test the underlying mechanism of the touch mode effect, we measured users’ approach motivation and directly tested whether it mediated the effect of touch mode on users’ self-regulation. In addition, pressing might be seen as different from tapping not just in the level of force exertion but also in contact duration (e.g., pressing might lead to a longer contact duration than tapping). To rule out the confounding effect of contact duration, we included a “holding” condition in Study 3. The holding condition required a user to hold the user’s <sup>fi</sup>nger on a screen object for an extended period to activate a certain function. We designed the holding condition based on the prevailing industry standard on iOS.<sup>6</sup>

## 6.1. Experimental Design and Procedure

We recruited participants from a public university in Southeast Asia, and 141 participants took part in Study 3. Upon arrival, they were told that the experiment consisted of two tasks: personal hygiene education and hygiene product recommendation. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions (i.e., tapping, pressing, and holding) to perform task 1, and in each condition, we manipulated the touch modes by varying the extent of touch force and contact duration (for holding only) required for interactions. After a short training, participants were presented with an experimental app (the personal hygiene education app) with eight personal hygiene topics with each on a separate screen (see Online Appendix I). After reading each topic, participants were required to tap (or press or hold, depending on their assigned condition) a button that exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed their commitment to maintaining a hygiene practice (e.g., “I will upload my personal hygiene”) to proceed from one screen to the next. At the completion of the personal hygiene education, participants were presented with an online survey on the smartphone. The survey contained measurement items that captured our research variables, that is, the inten sity of approach motivation and determination to adhere to good hygiene practice (see Appendix A).

The experiment administrator then announced the end of task 1 and accompanied the participants to a nearby seminar room for task $2 , ^ { 7 }$ in which they were informed that the university had set aside a budget (approximately US\$30,000) to purchase personal hygiene products (reusable masks, hand sanitizer, and alcohol swabs) for 1,000 needy students. The university wanted their recommendations on some short-listed products. Participants were then given a smartphone<sup>8</sup> through which they could examine information on the shortlisted disinfectant products. They were asked to consider the available budget in making their recommendations (i.e., the speci<sup>fi</sup>c brand and amounts of masks, sanitizer, and swabs). Upon completing the second task, participants were thanked and received their participation compensation (approximately US\$10).<sup>9</sup>

The purpose of task 2 was to establish a context in which we could observe participants’ actual hygienerelated self-regulation behavior after receiving the hygiene education in task 1. In particular, we used personal hygiene lapses to indicate actual self-regulation behavior. To quantify personal hygiene lapses, two experiment administrators were instructed to observe the participants and independently identify four observed behaviors that breached the personal hygiene guidelines that participants had learned in task 1. These personal hygiene lapses included violation of social distancing, handshaking, using a shared phone without proper sanitization, and face touching. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a waiting-in-line scenario was used to observe violations of social distancing (see Online Appendix II). Handshaking was generally discouraged in the pandemic context because physical contact could facilitate interpersonal transmission of diseases.<sup>10</sup> At the beginning of task 2, an experiment administrator was instructed to welcome each participant by offering a handshake. Handshaking was considered to have been performed when the participant responded to the offer. In addition, participants were informed that the experimental smartphone in task 2 had been used by earlier participants. The guideline on disinfecting the surfaces of a shared object was deemed to have been breached if a participant did not sanitize the smartphone. The participants had learned in task 1 to avoid face touching even though they felt uncomfortable wearing a mask because viruses could easily be transmitted indirectly through a hand that touches the outer surface of a contaminated mask.<sup>11</sup> In task 2, face touching was recorded if a participant touched the participant’s face with the hands. Overall, a participant could receive a score of zero through four for personal hygiene lapses (see Appendix A).

## 6.3. Discussion of Results

Our results largely support our hypotheses and theoretical arguments. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that participants who performed pressing actions on the app (compared with tapping or holding) reported the highest self-regulation intention and were more likely to follow the guidelines. Furthermore, consistent with our theorization, the effect was mediated by approach motivational intensity.

As discussed earlier, pressing may require a longer contact duration, which could confound our reasoning that touch modes affect self-regulation because of force exertion. Study 3 addresses this concern by demonstrat ing that pressing does lead to stronger self-regulation compared with tapping and holding, whereas there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between holding and tapping in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing self-regulation. Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings clearly suggest that the driver of the effect of touch modes on self-regulation is indeed force exertion, not contact duration.

## 7. General Discussion

This study investigates how touch modes in mobile applications in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ self-regulation in using health-related mobile apps. In Study 1, we examined how touch modes affected consumer willpower and choice. We found that, when users were required to press to acquire health-related product information, their willpower was strengthened, and they became more likely to choose a healthy option. We also observed that the effect of touch modes on willpower was more prominent among users with a higher level of health knowledge, which was consistent with our conjecture. In Study 2, we investigated how touch modes affected users with different health orientations in the gym environment. We observed that, compared with tapping actions for setting goals, pressing actions led users to set more challenging goals (i.e., users were more willing to exert themselves to exercise more in the pressing condition). In addition, this effect was more salient among users who were primed with a promotion-focused health orientation. Moreover, pressing increased the exercise amount among promotion-focused users but not among prevention-focused users. In Study 3, we found that pressing to make a commitment led to higher adherence determination and reduced personal hygiene lapses. Collectively, the three experiments provide largely converging evidence that pressing in making healthrelated decisions can increase users’ self-regulation in pursuing health-related goals, especially for people with a higher level of health knowledge or a more promotion-focused health orientation.

## 7.1. Theoretical Implications

This paper extends the current literature in a number of ways. First, we contribute to the healthcare IT literature by highlighting the importance of self-regulation and revealing factors that can subliminally facilitate self regulation. Many existing digital health intervention studies focus on eliciting healthy behavior by drawing people’s conscious attention to health (Chatterjee and Price 2009). However, even when people are clearly aware of explicit health interventions, they may fail to consistently perform healthy behavior when facing temptations or physical challenges. In recent years, increasing evidence suggests that success in pursuing health goals hinges largely on self-regulation (Mann et al. 2013). This evidence inspired research on the design and evaluation of health interventions that aim to enhance users’ self-regulation subliminally (Murray et al. 2016). Our study, thus, contributes to the design science literature by demonstrating how touch mode design can be utilized as an effective implicit health intervention. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, implementing force-based interaction and allowing users to press and exert force on a touchscreen can implicitly enhance their selfregulation in the pursuit of health-related goals because the touch mode of pressing embodies stronger approach motivation toward a latent health goal. We further suggest that force-based interaction designs be strategically applied to other design contexts, well beyond healthrelated practices, particularly when users can be highly susceptible to temptations and urges. In a broader sense, as our other bodily actions (not limited to touches) can also automatically activate associated mental representations, mobile interaction designers can consider leveraging various types of bodily actions (e.g., gestures and postures) to nudge behavior change.

In addition, whereas the traditional information systems literature suggests that users’ decision making often determines <sup>fi</sup>nal user actions and behaviors, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that decisions can also be subliminally in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by user actions and behaviors. In particular, our study contributes to the HCI literature by effectively applying embodied cognition theory to the context of force-based interaction. Conventional interfaces are shifting from computer mice to touchscreens (Brasel and Gips 2014), creating new ways to interact with screen content. In the past, embodied cognition has informed a stream of research on embodied interaction, that is, drawing on natura body movements and body–mind associations to design mobile, wearable, and other body-controlled user interfaces (Dourish 2004, Marshall et al. 2013). However, these prior studies focus exclusively on using body–mind associations to improve usability. For example, it is shown that body–mind consistency (e.g., moving one’s hand up to increase volume and moving one’s hand down to decrease volume) improve ease of use and makes an interaction experience more enjoyable (Hurtienne and Israel 2007). By empirically demonstrating the role of touch modes in provoking approach motivation in goal pursuit and enhancing self-regulation, our study reveals that the bodily actions that users perform to interact with digital systems can also trigger thoughts about prior experiences, and the activated memories can further affect their judgments, decisions, and behaviors. Thus, embodied interaction research should consider not only the system usability aspect, but also the behavioral impacts of bodily interaction. This study also complements the emerging touch interface literature, which emphasizes the comparison between touch interface and other interaction modalities (e.g., mouse and voice) (Brengman et al. 2019, Madzharov 2019). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, for touch interfaces, the way people interact with digital content (e.g., by pressing hard or gently tapping) matters. Our results invite future research exploring the embodied mean ings of other touch modes and gestures (e.g., swiping, tilting, shaking, and pinching) and their impacts on users’ cognition and behavior.

Finally, we advance embodied cognition theory by suggesting a contingent perspective to understand the embodiment effect of touch modes. Whereas past embodied cognition literature emphasizes the consistency of body–mind association across conditions, recent studies suggest that meanings delivered by bodily actions and physical objects can vary across people and situations; thus, various prerequisites are needed to understand embodiment effects (Schwarz 2011, Meier et al. 2012). One such prerequisite is the learned association between bodily actions and related concepts (Van den Bergh et al. 2011). For example, Lee and Schwarz (2012) <sup>fi</sup>nd that incidenta exposure to <sup>fi</sup>sh odor induces suspicion and undermines cooperation in economic exchanges in a trust game, but the effect existed only for users with prior knowledge that led them to associate a <sup>fi</sup>shy smell with untrustworthiness. In our study, we identify users’ preexisting thoughts and attitudes, that is, health knowledge and health orientation, as prerequi sites of embodiment effects. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, users with higher levels of health knowledge and more promotionfocused health orientation tend to perceive the embodied meanings of pressing, that is, approach motivation, as more relevant in the health-related decision-making context. Hence, they are more likely to internalize the information and, thus, be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by force-based interaction design. Our results provide strong empirical evidence that users’ preexisting thoughts and attitudes affect how they interpret and use embodied information activated by their bodily actions, thereby in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the strength of embodiment effects. This <sup>fi</sup>nding echoes prior theoretical papers in suggesting context speci<sup>fi</sup>city in meanings of bodily actions and encourages future research to explore other boundary condi tions of embodiment effects (Brinol et al.˜ 2017).

## 7.2. Practical Implications

Our work also makes important contributions to practice. By examining the embodiment effects of touch modes in three experiments, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest a subtle but powerful way to bolster self-regulation in maintaining healthy behavior by leveraging forcebased interaction technology. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that pressing actions engender a preference for a healthy beverage over a tasty beverage, lead to more challenging exercise goals and more exercise, and reduce personal hygiene lapses after receiving hygiene education. Hence, digital health product designers may consider implementing force-based interaction and designing interaction with pressing gestures to facilitate users self-regulation and attainment of health-related goals. It is likely that the effect of touch modes on selfregulation is applicable not only to health contexts, but also to other scenarios that can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from self regulation, such as working and learning. For example, implementing force-based interaction in productivity and mobile learning applications may also enhance users’ selfregulation and potentially result in better outcomes.

Our study also reveals that health knowledge and health orientation moderate the effect of touch modes on self-regulation. It suggests that implementing forcebased interaction to enhance self-regulation is more effective among users with more health knowledge and a promotion-focused health orientation. This <sup>fi</sup>nding underscores the importance of customer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling. Whereas traditional market surveys can help acquire customers’ health knowledge and health orientation, survey administration is not only laborious and costly, but also not entirely effective in obtaining a full assessment of customers. We suggest that retailers can draw on behavioral analytics (e.g., online search history and interaction data on mobile apps) for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient customer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling to effectively deploy force-based interaction in marketing health-related products. In addition, mobile app designers can enhance the effectiveness of force-based interaction in driving self-regulation by providing relevant health-related content to enhance users’ health knowledge and induce promotion-focused health orientation

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research Directions

We acknowledge that this study has some limitations. First, our experiments investigate whether touch modes can affect users’ self-regulation behavior shortly after they perform an action on mobile devices. However, prior literature suggests that achieving health goals may require persistence over time (Muraven et al. 1999). As our experiments were conducted within a relatively short time window, we encourage future research to adopt a longitudinal study of self-regulation and investigate changes in long-term self-regulation behavior.

Second, it is important to note that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not be generalized to a decision task that involve substantial deliberation. Prior literature <sup>fi</sup>nds that embodied cognition can affect human judgment and decision making when the cognitive resources involved in a task are low (Wilson 2002). It is possible that the subliminal effect of touch modes may be overshadowed by other important decision trade-offs in critical tasks that require users’ substantial deliberation. Hence, when users are involved in serious tasks, fo example, deciding to purchase a car, they may rely more on the conscious system by taking their real needs and product information into consideration, leaving the embodiment effect less evident. Therefore, our study’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings are best applied to tasks that are relatively simple and do not entail much deliberation. Indeed, such tasks are not rare. For example, food selections and goal setting are common decisions, and they have substantial impacts on one’s general wellness.

Furthermore, embodied cognition theory posits that the associated memory activated by bodily actions is contingent on the environment in which the actions occur. Future studies can consider investigating the embodied meanings of force exertion in other environments. For example, pressing may lead to more aggressive and frequent stock trading on mobile platforms because force exertion can embody the meaning of proactive pursuit. It is also possible that the effects of force exertion can potentially be explained by mechanisms other than embodied cognition in some settings. For example, in some hedonic contexts (e.g., immersive social media and virtual reality games), touch-pressure sensing, as a relatively novel interaction mode, is likely to enhance users’ cognitive engagement and perception of novelty. Hence, it would be interesting to further explore how force-based interaction can affect user behavior via other psychological mechanisms in different usage contexts.

With the prevalence of novel digital devices, such as wearables, virtual reality (VR), and augmented reality headsets, new HCI technologies are increasingly utilizing the physical body for interaction. We contend that bodily actions in interacting with these digital systems can trigger thoughts about prior experiences and associated experiences, affecting individuals’ judgments, decisions, and behavior. To this end, we recommend that future studies consider the embodied meanings of bodily actions in interacting with those new technological gadgets. For example, following the success of Nintendo’s Ring-Con and metaverse applications on VR platforms, designers may consider leveraging the enriched information conveyed by gesture-based and full-body controllers to nudge positive behavior change in augmented and immersive environments.

## 8. Conclusion

Using multiple experiments, this study examines embodied cognition through force-based interaction on mobile devices. Our results show that pressing actions, compared with tapping actions, enhance selfregulation. Furthermore, we reveal that the effect of pressing on self-regulation is especially salient among people with a high level of health knowledge and a promotion-focused health orientation compared with those with a low level of health knowledge and a prevention-focused orientation. This study provides theoretical explanations for the underlying mechanism of embodied cognition and proposes actionable insights into the design of mobile interfaces and digital health interventions.
