# Personalized Ranking at a Mobile App Distribution Platform

Abstract. The ease of customer data collection has enabled the widespread personalization of content and services in digital platforms. We examine personalization in a hitherto unaddressed context: that of mobile app distribution. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we develop a comprehensive framework for the personalized ranking of app impressions, leveraging revealed preferences embedded in consumer clickstream data. To improve platform revenues, the framework jointly accounts for consumer utility and cost per action (CPA) margin, which is the revenue earned by the platform per app installation. To this end, we specify a structural model of click and installation choices, jointly estimated as a function of a comprehensive set of numerical (screen rank, quality, and popularity) and textual (titles, descriptions, and reviews) covariates. Our novel data set is at the granular user-impression level and uniquely includes app CPA margins paid to the platform. We conduct a series of policy experiments to quantify the value of personalization. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we show that a personalized hybrid margin and utility margin ranking scheme outperforms other personalized methods, including those based on utilities alone or a combination of utilities and margins. Overall, our analysis demonstrates how platforms can leverage routine consumer click stream data to personalize the ranking of app impressions, thereby more effectively monetizing mobile app distribution.

## 1. Introduction

The market for mobile apps has seen exponential growth over the last two decades with 218 billion apps downloaded in 2020 contributing to sales of \$143 billion (App Annie 2021). In addition to the handful of native app stores (most notably Google Play and Apple App Store), third-party app distribution platforms have emerged, including the one we study here. More than 100 billion app downloads are expected through such platforms, which will generate annual revenues of \$36 billion by 2021, and both downloads and revenues are growing at an annual rate of 12% (App Annie 2017).<sup>1</sup> Third-party platforms typically run a cost-per-action (CPA) business model, whereby they are compensated by app developers at a precontracted rate for each app installed. The revenues collected by the platforms, as with product search engines in general, depend on what app impressions are shown to users and in what order (e.g., Ghose and Yang 2009, Agarwal et al. 2011). Yet third-party app distribution platforms are generally naïve in terms of app ranking strategies (Dogtiev 2019), resorting to such simple metrics as click-through rates (CTR), conversion rates, and CPA margins and ignoring fundamental drivers of user valuation for the apps. Indeed, prior work (e.g., Ghose et al. 2014, De los Santos and Koulayev 2017) shows that accounting for average consumer utilities in the ranking of impressions can yield higher platform revenues. We build on this to show how personalized rankings—that is, rankings based on individual utilities rather than the average utility—can further increase both platform pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and consumer utilities.

Personalization is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the capability to match content, products, and services to individual users based on the knowledge of their past behaviors and revealed preferences (Hagen et al. 1999). It is studied in a variety of contexts, including search engines (Yao and Mela 2011, Yoganarasimhan 2020), recommender systems (Ansari et al. 2000, Bodapati 2008), personalized pricing (Zhang and Wedel 2009), targeted marketing (Ansari and Mela 2003, Arora and Henderson 2007, Goldfarb and Tucker 2011), and more (see Adomavicius et al. 2008 for a survey). Users bene<sup>fi</sup>t from personalization because it reduces their search costs, helping them <sup>fi</sup>nd more relevant offerings at the right place and time. Higher customer satisfaction translates into higher customer retention and conversion rates, greater willingness to pay, and generally higher average revenues per user per unit of time. In a sense, personalization is an effort to turn customer heterogeneity into incremental revenue opportunities through the appropriate matching of customer revealed preferences to suitably tailored content and offerings. It is helpful to distinguish between personalization and customization—terms that are often used synonymously but are subtly different (Davis 2018, Muhamad 2019). A succinct distinction between the two is discussed by Muhamad (2019): “While customization is initiated by the user, personalization is done for the user.” In other words, when users proactively con<sup>fi</sup>gure products to meet their preferences, that is customization; when the platform ranks the preferred products for individuals as studied in this research, that is in the realm of personalization.

We now place our instance of personalization within the framework of Adomavicius et al. (2008), which highlights four key dimensions of the type of personalization: offering (personalization of recommended products, content, or price), approach (provider-, consumer-, or market-centric), granularity (individual or segment-based), and intrusiveness (intrusive or nonintrusive). Within this framework, our offering is the personalization of recommended products (i.e., apps) using a consumercentric approach. In terms of granularity, our analysis is at the individual level (as in Balabanovic and´ Shoham 1997) rather than the segment level as the analysis of sponsored search advertising by Yao and Mela (2011) in an anonymous search engine.<sup>2</sup> Finally, ours is a case of nonintrusive personalization, in the sense that the personalized app impressions are presented passively on the offer wall and do not interfere with the user’s browsing process.

Indeed, the premise of our consumer-centric ranking scheme is that revealed preferences embedded in clickstream data can be analyzed to recover consumers’ preferences and, in turn, used to personalize the ranking of app impressions. When clickstream data are collected by all manner of product search engines, the mobile app distribution context has relatively more frequent click and conversion interactions because of the numerous free apps available for download.<sup>3</sup> Over time, this translates into substantial volumes of click and install choices (i.e., revealed preferences) from heterogeneous users. This offers an actionable opportunity for the platform to learn about the nature of app demand and then leverage that knowledge for more effective ranking and monetization. When ranking design has drawn attention in prior research (see Table 1), we ask a set of hitherto unaddressed questions: (1) How are consumer utilities from app click and install in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by app and impression characteristics? (2) How should the platform balance the drivers of these utilities with app revenue margins and design a personalized ranking of impressions? (3) How do alternative personalized ranking schemes compare in terms of contributions to platform revenues?

To address these questions, we obtain a unique data set of click and download data from a marketing agency that manages the home screen for one of the prominent mobile networks in the United States. The agency hosts an offer wall that serves as an app distribution platform. The user impression–level data contain app impressions along with the corresponding click and install responses. We estimate a structural model of click and download utilities as a function of a broad set of numerical and textual app characteristics using a two-stage bivariate probit model in a hierarchical Bayes framework. The numerical variables include screen ranks, star ratings, review volume, and the number of downloads; the textual features consist of informativeness, ambiguity, and sentiment extracted from titles, descriptions, and reviews, using suitable natural language processing methods.

The parameter estimates provide a number of insights. First, consistent with prior work (e.g., Ghose and Yang 2009), we con<sup>fi</sup>rm salient ranking and quality effects. Quantitatively, moving an app impression one slot upward in the screen is associated with a 4.26% higher click-through rate; having one additional star in rating boosts click and install performances by 2.91% and 1.37%, respectively. Second, consumers generally prefer more informative app titles so that a 1% increase in the length of app titles is associated with a 2.12% increase in CTR. Yet one additional synonym of a word in an app title results in a 3.15% decrease in CTR, indicating that title ambiguity deters clicks. App titles having restricted words (e.g., kill, shoot, and ruin) generally draw more attention, whereas positive descriptions recruit more installs. Finally, we notice that consumers are more heterogeneous in their response to textual rather than numerical variables.

We leverage our structural parameter estimates to conduct a series of policy experiments to evaluate the incremental value of personalization. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we compare the mean utility based ranking (Ghose et al. 2014) with personalized ranking at the individual level. We show that personalization enhances the platform’s expected revenues substantially. To further improve platform pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, we propose a novel personalized ranking scheme, namely, the hybrid margin and utility margin (HMUM) ranking. The hybrid ranking approach alloys two ranking methods and exquisitely balances download performance with revenue margins. We demonstrate that this hybrid ranking outperforms alternative ranking strategies based on either utilities or a combination of utilities and margins, especially when personalization is incorporated. It is noteworthy that, whereas prior research tends to focus on picking “a” best algorithm from among alternatives, it tends to discard the potential of a hybrid approach that combines multiple schemes in an optimal ranking scenario.<sup>4</sup> The proposed ranking scheme presents a personalized list of app impressions to each user, which consists of two sections. The top few positions on the screen are populated with the user’s most favorite apps sorted in decreasing order of CPA margins. The user is likely to click on these highutility impressions anyway, irrespective of precise screen ranking, making app CPA margins the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinant of incremental revenues. The lower section consists of the remaining app impressions sorted in decreasing order of the product of utility and margin (i.e., utility margin), again, on a personalized basis. The resulting hybrid ranking scheme is designed to balance the forces that drive individual user utility versus revenue margins in a manner that enhances platform revenues along with customer value. Further contributions of our work to the literature and practice are discussed in the concluding section.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature and describes the conceptual framework for our empirical analysis. We detail the research context and data in Section 3. Section 4 describes our empirical model and estimation strategy. In Section 5, we describe our results and robustness checks. A series of policy experiments are conducted to compare alternative ranking methods in Section 6. Section 7 concludes this research with our contributions and managerial implications. ## 2. Literature Review and Framework

At a high level, this work contributes to the emerging literature on the mobile app economy. Although prior studies focus on how aggregate demands are affected by app and consumer characteristics (e.g., Ghose and Han 2014, Han et al. 2016, Li et al. 2020), our work takes a “micro” perspective on app consumption, focusing on personalized ranking designed for third-party app distribution platforms. In so doing, we contribute to two interrelated areas of prior work: (i) ranking of search impressions and consumer choice thereof and (ii) the personalization of online content and services. We discuss each in turn and conclude with our concep tual framework.

## 2.1. Ranking

Our modeling of consumer click and conversion utilities builds on prior work that studies online consumer choice of search results as summarized in Table 1. What is common across these studies is that consumers choose from a ranked list of information, content, or product offerings. Further, the outcomes of interest are a combination of clicks and conversions. We highlight whether these studies explicitly consider ranking design, and if so, what is the basis for screen ranking? A consistent theme in the literature is that screen rank plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in consumer propensity to click. For example, in an online shopping context, Brynjolfsson et al. (2010) show that consumers tend to choose product offerings on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page. In sponsored search, Ghose and Yang (2009) <sup>fi</sup>nd that low-ranked sponsored listings attract more clicks and conversions using keyword campaign data from an advertiser. In a similar setting, Agarwal et al. (2011) notice that the click-through rate decreases with position, but the top positions are not necessarily the most pro<sup>fi</sup>table for an advertiser to bid because of higher placement costs.

These studies consider screen ranking as a covariate for explaining online click and conversion decisions. In contrast, other works focus on the design of ranking systems themselves (Ghose et al. 2012, 2014; De los Santos and Koulayev 2017; Ursu 2018; Choi and Mela 2019). In a hotel search setting, Ghose et al. (2012) propose a ranking system that sorts products in decreasing order of the average utility for all users (i.e., their “best value” ranking). They <sup>fi</sup>nd that con sumers prefer such a ranking over price- or ratingbased rankings. Ghose et al. (2014) further show that such average utility-based ranking can increase platform expected revenues signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. In this direction, the mean utility-based ranking is shown to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the platform by Ursu (2018) in her <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments on Expedia. She further <sup>fi</sup>nds that ranking affects consumer clicks only and not purchase decisions. Finally, De los Santos and Koulayev (2017) extend the mean utility-based ranking by considering price sensitivity at the segment level. This scheme outperforms naïve ranking by price or popularity alone. We also leverage structural modeling (i.e., structural estimation followed by counterfactual analyses), but our study departs from prior work by examining incremental revenues from a personalized ranking of utilities at the individual level. Another point of departure is that prior studies are not privy to revenue-sharing details between the platform and advertisers.<sup>5</sup> By contrast, the actual CPA margins are observed in our context, enabling us to explicitly study the interplay between consumer utility drivers and platform revenues.

It is also worth noting that the existing literature tends to propose a single performance metric to rank all products. Such a one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all approach may incur undesirable outcomes, especially when utilitybased methods maximize consumer utilities at the expense of a platform’s revenues. A hybrid algorithm that blends rankings based on utilities and margins might help generate higher overall revenues. Choi and Mela (2019) showcase this approach in an online marketplace, which is monetized through sales commissions and advertising revenues. Their hybrid scheme optimizes overall platform revenues, wherein the top 5 slots are reserved for cost-per-click auctions to enhance advertising revenues with the lower positions sorted in terms of expected sales revenues. A similar logic is applicable in our analysis of a hybrid ranking method, but two key differences are worth noting. We balance the factors that affect consumer utilities and platform revenues, whereas Choi and Mela (2019) trade off advertising and sales revenues. In addition, our analysis quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the value of both hybridization and personalization, whereas the prior study proposes a nonpersonalized hybrid approach.

## 2.2. Personalization

Personalization is the ability to provide tailored content and services to individuals based on their revealed preferences in past behaviors (Hagen et al. 1999). Personalization is studied in different contexts with attention paid to the impacts of personalization on consumer surplus on the one hand and seller pro<sup>fi</sup>ts on the other. As a representative example, Rossi et al. (1996) study target couponing by designing various coupon face values for heterogeneous consumers. The results show that target couponing generates revenues 2.5 times higher than blanket couponing. Ansari and Mela (2003) propose personalized marketing emails, <sup>fi</sup>nding that the contenttargeting approach improves click-through rates. Also, prior studies examine recommender systems, which are designed to predict users’ preferences using their own past choices or similar users’ (e.g., Ansari et al. 2000, Bodapati 2008). In a personalized search setting, Yoganarasimhan (2020) adopts a machine-learning approach to implement personalization that maximizes consumer utilities.<sup>6</sup>

Personalized online engagements, however, may harm platform pro<sup>fi</sup>ts when increasing consumer surplus. Zhang and Wedel (2009) investigate the effects of personalized promotions at different levels of granularity. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that individual-speci<sup>fi</sup>c personalization does not always result in a higher payoff than segment-speci<sup>fi</sup>c personalization or no personalization at all. Similarly, in the targeted advertising con text, Goldfarb and Tucker (2011) show that obtrusive ads aggravate privacy concerns and thereby lead to lower purchase intent. Finally, Ghose et al. (2014) <sup>fi</sup>nd a negative effect of “active personalization” on platform revenues in a randomized experiment on a mock hotel search engine.<sup>7</sup> These studies suggest the possibility that higher personalization may create bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for consumers at the expense of a platform. An underlying reason might be that consumer preferences are not always aligned with revenue margins. Our goal, in part, is to craft exquisite personalization that creates incremental value for both consumers and the platform in the app distribution context.

## 2.3. Conceptual Framework

We illustrate the conceptual framework for our empirical analysis in Figure 1. The ultimate goal is to study personalized ranking on platform revenues, jointly considering consumer utilities and CPA margins. Consumer latent utilities are structurally modeled in a two-stage decision-making process of click and conversion. Covariates used to estimate the latent utility functions include a variety of impression and app characteristics, including screen rank, quality, popularity, and other variables motivated by prior research. We recover the utility function at the individual level by specifying full user heterogeneity in a hierarchical Bayes framework. Finally, we leverage the structural parameter estimates, in conjunction with data on platform revenue margins, to conduct a series of counterfactual policy experiments to evaluate a set of nonpersonalized and personalized ranking schemes. These experiments demonstrate the incremental value of personalization and shed light on the nature of the most fruitful personalized ranking scheme, which aligns consumer preferences with enhanced platform revenues.

To characterize the nature of the optimal ranking scheme, we develop a simple analytical model (see the online appendix), which considers app consumption via an offer wall. The model incorporates search (or exploration) costs, different app qualities, and user heterogeneity in taste for quality.<sup>8</sup> We solve the model to compare platform revenues under the optimal personalized with nonpersonalized ranking schemes. One of the key results is that a hybrid ranking scheme could be most effective. Extrapolating from the analytical model, the logic for a hybrid personalized ranking scheme in a real-world platform is as follows. The top of the list should include the highest utility apps for a given user, sorted in decreasing order of revenue margins. Because the user is likely to click on all these high-utility impressions (i.e., the utility differences among them are negligible), ranking is the most signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant determinant affecting click performance. As such, the platform maximizes revenues from these slots ranked by margins. The remaining spots are sorted in decreasing order of the product of utility and margin (utility margin) to align consumer download utility with revenue margins. The resulting hybrid scheme demonstrates higher platform revenues relative to alternative rankings based on utilities or margins alone.

Though we gain useful guidance from the analytica model, the primary engine that drives our empirical analysis is a structural utility model that considers a two-stage choice process (i.e., click and install) at the individual level. Our high-resolution model grants two bene<sup>fi</sup>ts with respect to ranking design. First, we are able to characterize each consumer’s preference precisely, whereas prior literature (e.g., Ghose et al. 2012, 2014; De los Santos and Koulayev 2017) has to average out preference heterogeneity. It is also worth noting that our two-stage modeling better re<sup>fl</sup>ects users’ decision making compared with simultaneous equations that ignore the conditional dependence of clicks and conversions. Second, we specify how to balance the recovered utility functions with CPA margins to yield more effective personalized ranking. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, in order to maximize expected revenues at the individual level, we explicitly balance conversion performances with CPA margins by hybridizing ranking schemes. In this regard, the structural parameter estimates allow us to infer a consumer’s favorite apps, which are ranked in terms of margins, yet the consumer is most likely to click on them. The remainder of the list is sorted on the basis of both utility and margin. Indeed, this is the logic behind our hybrid personalized ranking scheme based on the context and data described next.

## 3. Empirical Context and Data

We obtain a unique data set from a marketing agency that monetizes the home screen for one of the major mobile service operators in the United States. The home screen provides common features, including local and web search, content display (e.g., news, weather, and horoscope), and a mobile app offer wall. We focus on the role of this service provider as an app distribution platform. Note that third-party app distri bution platforms, such as this one, account for some 32% of overall app installs (App Annie 2017).

Our clickstream data are derived from server logs collected during the <sup>fi</sup>rst three weeks of July 2015. The logs capture the impressions served to each user along with their clicks and installs. All impressions are organized in chronological order at the user-impression level, enabling us to recover the screen rank of each impression and the total body of impressions displayed during a given session. In addition to mobile clickstream data, we also consider app characteristics from both numerical (screen rank, review volume and valence, the number of downloads) and textual (app titles, descriptions, and reviews) data.

Recent research emphasizes the importance of supplementing quantitative data with textual variables, such as readability (e.g., Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011) and sentiment (e.g., Goh et al. 2013). To this end, we consider a pair of objective and subjective measures. Informativeness captures how much objective information a text contains, whereas sentiment measures consumers’ subjective feelings expressed through the text. We follow prior literature to include three constructs for informativeness, including length (e.g., Ghose and Han 2014), word frequency (e.g., Yamamoto and Church 2001), and ambiguity (e.g., Klapdor et al. 2014). These are supplemented with the polarity of sentiment (e.g., Archak et al. 2011).

We apply multiple text-analytic tools to operationalize these two types of textual variables. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we construct informativeness and ambiguity measures for objective information and extract sentiments from the text for subjective opinions as described.<sup>10</sup> Informativeness is operationalized based on word frequency and the length of texts. Word frequency characterizes how speci<sup>fi</sup>c a word is. The more frequently a word is used, the less speci<sup>fi</sup>c information it conveys. The overall informativeness of a text is taken to be negatively related to the average word frequencies.<sup>11</sup> Thus, we follow Leech et al. (2001) to compute the frequency of each word in a text using the corpus-based word frequency and compute the average. Also, longer texts generally contain more information than shorter ones. We count words in the texts to approximate the richness of information; an ambiguous description could mislead consumers and result in higher search and cognitive costs. Applying logic similar to Klapdor et al. (2014), we measure ambiguity at the word level by calculating the number of semantic interpretations that a word possesses.<sup>12</sup> We use the average of word ambiguities as a measure of the overall ambiguity of each text object. Finally, to capture the sentiments of text, we adopt a polarity classi<sup>fi</sup>er. We let the polarity range from zero to one, wherein the two extremes indicate completely negative and completely positive sentiments, respectively. We implement the classi<sup>fi</sup>er in a logistic regression model and estimate it using an L1-penalized maximum likelihood estimator.<sup>13</sup> App titles, descriptions, and reviews are run through the trained model to obtain the corresponding sentiments.

## 4. Empirical Model and Estimation

We develop a two-stage bivariate probit model of consumer click and conversion decisions in a random utility framework. Because of the interdependence of the click and conversion decisions, our modeling framework considers that a consumer, given an app impression, <sup>fi</sup>rst decides whether to click on the impression; if the consumer clicks, the consumer then decides whether to install the app. A consumer clicks on an app impression (respectively, installs an app) only if clicking (installing) provides higher expected utility than not doing so. In the click stage, we denote the observed response for consumer i clicking on impression j as

$$
y _ {i j} ^ {c l i c k} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \text { if   click; } \\ 0, & \text { otherwise. } \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

Similarly, we specify the observed outcome in the install stage as

$$
y _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \text {if install;} \\ 0, & \text {otherwise.} \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

These outcome variables are related to the covariates in a hierarchical Bayes speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, which accounts for unobserved heterogeneity at the individual level.

## 4.1. Click and Install Decisions

Following random utility theory (McFadden 1974), we specify consumer i’s expected utility from clicking on impression j as

$$
\begin{array}{l} u _ {i j} ^ {\text { click }} = u _ {i j} ^ {\text { click } *} + \varepsilon_ {i j} = \\ \beta_ {i 0} + \beta_ {i 1} \text { Rank } _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 2} \text { TitleFreq } _ {j} + \beta_ {i 3} \text { TitleLen } _ {j} + \beta_ {i 4} \text { TitleAmb } _ {j} \\ + \beta_ {i 5} \text { TitleSent } _ {j} + \beta_ {i 6} \text { Rating } _ {j} + \beta_ {i 7} \text { Game } _ {j} \\ + \beta_ {i 8} \ln (\text { NumCompApp } _ {i j}) + \varepsilon_ {i j}. \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

Rank refers to the screen ranking of impression j. The consumer evaluates app impressions in the click stage based on app titles and star ratings.<sup>14</sup> The corresponding textual constructs consist of TitleFreq, TitleLen, Title-Amb, and TitleSent, and Rating captures the impact of star ratings. Game serves as an additional control for app categories and takes the value one for games and zero for nongames. NumComp $4 p p _ { i j }$ captures the number of competing apps for the focal impression $j . ^ { 1 5 }$ Leveraging the nature of a hierarchical model, we specify our model with full heterogeneity as

$$
\pmb {\beta} _ {i} = \overline {{\pmb {\beta}}} + \pmb {\zeta} _ {i},\tag{4}
$$

where $\zeta _ { i } \sim \mathrm { M V N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \Sigma ^ { \zeta } ) . ~ \beta _ { i 0 }$ is the individual-speci<sup>fi</sup>c intercept, which allows for variation in the baseline utility of click. The individual-speci<sup>fi</sup>c slope coef<sup>fi</sup>cients model the heterogeneous effects of the covariates. They together help recover the utility function at the individual level.

Assuming the idiosyncratic errors, $\varepsilon _ { i j } ,$ follow a standard normal distribution, we have a binary probit speci-<sup>fi</sup>cation:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (y _ {i j} ^ {c l i c k} = 1) = \operatorname * {P r} (u _ {i j} ^ {c l i c k ^ {*}} + \varepsilon_ {i j} > 0) = \Phi (u _ {i j} ^ {c l i c k ^ {*}}),\tag{5}
$$

where Φ denotes the cumulative density function (CDF) of a standard normal distribution. A consumer clicks on an app impression $( \mathrm { i . e . , } y _ { i j } ^ { c l i c k } = 1 )$ ) if the latent expected utility of clicking $u _ { i j } ^ { c l i c k } > 0 ;$ the consumer does not click otherwise.

After clicking on an impression, the consumer is redirected to the app landing page. The consumer acquires additional information (such as the detailed description, etc.) and decides whether to install the app. Consumer i’s expected utility from installing app j is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & u _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l} = u _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l ^ {*}} + \eta_ {i j} \\ & = \gamma_ {i 0} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i1}} D e s c F r e q _ {j} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i2}} D e s c L e n _ {j} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i3}} D e s c A m b _ {j} \\ & \quad + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i4}} D e s c S e n t _ {j} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i5}} R e v F r e q _ {j} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i6}} R e v L e n _ {j} \\ & \quad + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i7}} R e v A m b _ {j} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i8}} R e v S e n t _ {j} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i9}} R a t i n g _ {j} \\ & \quad + \gamma_ {i 1 0} \ln (N u m R a t i n g s _ {j}) + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i11}} P o p u l a r i t y _ {j} + \gamma_ {\mathrm{i12}} G a m e _ {j} + \eta_ {i j}. \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

In the install stage, app descriptions are available. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two constructs, DescFreq and DescLen, measure the description informativeness, and the second two, Desc-Amb and DescSent, capture the ambiguity and sentiment. Besides star ratings, NumRatings captures the number of ratings. Popularity is a dummy variable indicating whether the number of installs is above or below the mean. We specify the full individual-speci<sup>fi</sup>c heterogeneity at the install stage as

$$
\pmb {\gamma} _ {i} = \overline {{\pmb {\gamma}}} + \pmb {\xi} _ {i},\tag{7}
$$

where $\pmb { \xi } _ { i } \sim \mathrm { M V N } ( \mathbf { 0 } , \Sigma ^ { \xi } )$ , so $\gamma _ { i }$ captures the individual deviations from the baseline expected utility. We further assume that the errors, $\eta _ { i j } ,$ are independent and identically distributed as the standard normal distribution, resulting in the following probit model speci<sup>fi</sup>- cation:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} (y _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l} = 1 | y _ {i j} ^ {c l i c k} = 1) = \operatorname * {P r} (u _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l ^ {*}} + \eta_ {i j} > 0) \\ = \Phi (u _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l ^ {*}}). \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

## 4.2. Interdependence Between Click and Install Decisions

We have to formally model the interdependence between the click and install decisions. Although the two have already been considered in terms of conditional probabilities, we still need to specify the correlation between the errors, $\varepsilon _ { i j }$ and $\eta _ { i j } ,$ in the click and install models, respectively. Allowing for such a correlation reduces the likelihood of bias resulting from unobservables that affect both decisions simultaneously. We model the error terms as

$$
\binom{\varepsilon_ {i j}}{\eta_ {i j}} \sim \mathrm{BVN} \biggl (\left( \begin{array}{c c} 0 \\ 0 \end{array} \right) & \left( \begin{array}{c c} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{array} \right) \biggr),\tag{9}
$$

where BVN denotes a bivariate normal distribution. $\rho$ captures the correlation between the two sets of errors, and the variance of errors is set at one to maintain the binary probit speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

Three scenarios are possible in the two-stage choice context, namely, (i) not clicking, (ii) clicking but not installing, and (iii) clicking and installing. Based on the proposed speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of the errors, we derive the likelihood of the observed data and formulate the three cases as

$$
\text {(i) Not clicking:Pr} (y _ {i j} ^ {\text { click }} = 0) = 1 - \Phi (u _ {i j} ^ {\text { click } *});\tag{10}
$$

ii Clicking but not installing : Pr $( y _ { i j } ^ { c l i c k } = 1 , y _ { i j } ^ { i n s t a l l } = 0 )$

$$
= \Phi_ {\mathrm{BVN}} (u _ {i j} ^ {c l i c k ^ {*}}, - u _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l ^ {*}}, \rho);\tag{11}
$$

iii Clicking and installing : Pr $( y _ { i j } ^ { c l i c k } = 1 , y _ { i j } ^ { i n s t a l l } = 1 )$

$$
= \Phi_ {\mathrm{BVN}} (u _ {i j} ^ {c l i c k ^ {*}}, u _ {i j} ^ {i n s t a l l ^ {*}}, \rho),\tag{12}
$$

where Φ denotes the CDF of a bivariate normal distribution. Accordingly, we have the joint likelihood function as

$$
\begin{array}{l} L \left(Y ^ {\text {click}}, Y ^ {\text {install}}\right) = \prod_ {i} \prod_ {j} (1 - \Phi \left(u _ {i j} ^ {\text {click} ^ {*}}\right)) ^ {1 - y _ {i j} ^ {\text {click}}} \\ * \left(\Phi_ {\mathrm{BVN}} \left(u _ {i j} ^ {\text {click} ^ {*}}, - u _ {i j} ^ {\text {install} ^ {*}}, \rho\right)\right) ^ {y _ {i j} ^ {\text {click}} * \left(1 - y _ {i j} ^ {\text {install}}\right)} \\ * \left(\Phi_ {\mathrm{BVN}} \left(u _ {i j} ^ {\text {click} ^ {*}}, u _ {i j} ^ {\text {install} ^ {*}}, \rho\right) ^ {y _ {i j} ^ {\text {click}} * y _ {i j} ^ {\text {install}}}. \right. \end{array} \tag {13}
$$

## 4.3. Estimation

We utilize a hierarchical Bayes approach to estimate the model because of the nature of the parameter hierarchy. Following the preceding hierarchical model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, we develop a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedure to make recursive parameter draws from the corresponding full conditional distributions as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \beta_ {i}, \gamma_ {i} | Y, \rho , \Sigma^ {\zeta}, \Sigma^ {\xi} \\ \Sigma^ {\zeta}, \Sigma^ {\xi} | \beta_ {i}, \gamma_ {i} \\ \rho | \beta_ {i}, \gamma_ {i}, Y. \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

We implement the random walk Metropolis–Hastings algorithm because the posterior distributions do not have closed-form speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Our adaptive estimation strategy consists of two steps to search the parameter spaces more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we run a pilot MCMC for 50,000 iterations and discard the <sup>fi</sup>rst 20,000 draws as the burn-in samples. We next utilize the remaining 30,000 draws to compute the empirical posterior means and covariance matrices. In the second step, we run a separate Metropolis–Hastings sampler using the posterior means and covariance matrices as the initial values and covariance parameters for the proposal distributions. We run the sampler for 50,000 iterations and keep every 20th draw to approximate the posteriors. To test the convergence of the MCMC procedure, we perform the Gelman–Rubin diagnostic that compares the estimated between- and within-chain variances for each parameter (Gelman and Rubin 1992). We ensure convergence by running three parallel chains with different initial values and random seeds. The diagnostic concludes that the chains converge. The resulting potential scale reduction factors (PSRF) are all below 1.08—well below the suggested threshold $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \mathrm { P S R F } = 1 . 2 )$ for the assessment of convergence (Brooks and Gelman 1998).

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst robustness check, we consider whether the results are different for gaming versus nongaming apps as the former constitutes 70% of our data. Some apps recruit enormous installs (e.g., Facebook and Twitter), boosting the average volume of reviews to more than 600,000. These popular apps may naturally differ from the vast majority of gaming apps. To ensure that the results are not skewed by the presence of these extremely popular apps, we separately estimate the results for game and nongame subsamples. Next, we consider an alternative approach for constructing the textual covariates. In the main model, we operationalize the textual information of app titles and descriptions using word frequency, the length of texts, ambiguity, and sentiment measures. Alternatively, we now consider the entropy of topics to measure the amount of information contained in the texts. Intuitively, a higher entropy implies that more diverse topics are covered in the text. We apply latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) to extract topics from app descriptions. Because LDA treats each description as a mixture of various topics, it is generally expected that a description covering multiple topics tends to contain more diverse information. In other words, the highe the topic dispersion of a description, the more informative it is. Thus, we substitute topic dispersion for both word frequency and description length as an alternative measure of informativeness. In the analysis, we consider 10 topics and characterize each description as a weighted vector of the topics $\hat { \mathbf { \sigma } } _ { i } = ( \delta _ { 1 j } , \delta _ { 2 j } , \ldots \hat { \delta _ { 1 0 j } } ) .$ 17 Then, we compute topic dispersion using the entropy measure, as follows:

$$

## 7. Conclusions

We develop a comprehensive framework to leverage clickstream data for more effective mobile app distribution monetization through a personalized ranking in the context of third-party app distribution platforms. Such platforms account for 32% of all app downloads, so this setting is of considerable interest in its own right. Besides, these platforms also serve as advertising and retail channels for app developers in partnership with telecom companies and online communities. In this regard, these platforms help developers to market apps and consumers to explore apps by maintaining a variety of app categories.

Our empirical analysis proceeds in two stages. First, we implement a two-stage click and conversion model in a hierarchical Bayesian framework that incorporates individual heterogeneity. Second, we recover individ ual utility parameter estimates and use them in a counterfactual analysis through suitable policy experiments to study several nonpersonalized and personalized ranking methods. To summarize the results of the click and conversion models, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that our set of numerical and textual covariates are collectively highly significant in explaining consumer choice on the platform. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that screen rank is a critical determinant of which app impressions consumers choose to click on; moving an impression up one slot on the screen is associated with a 4.26% increase in click-through rates. The valence of reviews is also important, more so with respect to which apps are installed than which impressions are clicked on. Also, there exist signi<sup>fi</sup>cant substitution effects among the competing apps in the same browsing session. Interestingly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the popularity of apps does not explain download behavior. The reason, we suspect, could be that the most popular apps are downloaded already, nullifying the incremental impact of this factor on conversions.

Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that users pay attention to textual information. In the click stage, the informativeness of app titles has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive impact, whereas ambiguity and sentiment have a negative relationship with CTR. Consumers are persuaded to click on the titles consisting of less frequently used and less ambiguous words. App titles with more restricted words (e.g., kill, shot, and ruin) generally catch more eyeballs. In the conversion stage, only the sentiment of descriptions positively affects consumer decisions. We also notice that review text features are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant for explaining app installs. A likely explanation is that users tend not to read detailed review texts on their cramped mobile screens, relying on numerical summaries instead.

In the policy experiments, we manipulate the positions of app impressions using the recovered consumer utility parameters and calculate the expected platform revenues based on the actual CPA margins. The results indicate that a personalized ranking method accounting for utilities and margins outperforms all other ranking methods we consider. Remarkably, personalized rankings outperform nonpersonalized counterparts ranging from 4.92% to 16.73% in which the proposed hybrid approach is more effective than other personalized alternatives. In terms of implementation, personalized ranking could be deployed via a nightly batch process, wherein the utility functions are reestimated using all the available clickstream data in the archive. These updated utility function parameters are used to determine the personalized rankings in advance for each user such that the impressions are presented with a minimal real-time computational burden.

These results contribute to the literature in several ways. First, as Table 1 summarizes, this study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine app install monetization, adding to the range of contexts studied in prior work, including search engines and the downloads of music and movies. Further, we deploy a comprehensive set of both numerical and textual covariates. Second, we are able to implement a unique study of personalization in the app monetization context because of the <sup>fi</sup>ne granular clickstream data at the user–impression level. Thus, we model individual heterogeneity, which allows us to examine a personalized ranking through suitable policy experiments; in contrast, prior studies (Ghose et al. 2012, 2014; Choi and Mela 2019) discuss earlier estimate drivers of weekly aggregate click and conversion rates. At the same time, we have access to each app’s CPA margin—we have not seen this in any prior work—which enables us to analyze the precise revenue impact of alternate ranking strategies. The optimal hybrid scheme that we propose balances drivers of consumer utilities with revenue margins. Finally, with respect to understanding the consumer utility for mobile apps, we focus on the microeconomy of an app distribution platform, whereas prior work focuses on market-wide demand drivers (e.g., Ghose and Han 2014).

Our work also provides actionable managerial implications. The majority of content distribution platforms, such as the one we study, exercise simple monetization heuristics. Our empirical analysis provides executable guidance on how such platforms can better monetize app distribution using clickstream data. These data, combined with CPA margins, help deploy ranking schemes that are substantially more pro<sup>fi</sup>table than extant algorithms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the platforms can personalize rankings to individual users in an optimal way, generating higher platform revenues along with higher consumer surplus at the same time. Such personalized rankings are increasingly feasible to implement in real time as more data processing and analytical capabilities are available to mobile service operators. We expect that our hybrid personalized ranking is also applicable in other contexts as long as consumer utility and revenue margins matter, such as movie or music distribution and product search engines. Indeed, our empirical framework provides step-by-step guidance on how to implement such a novel personalized ranking method.

Our analysis also points to the importance of considering textual content (i.e., titles and descriptions) for both platforms and developers, shaping titles, descrip tions, and sentiments for more effective monetization. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the textual variables suggests that platforms could use app titles and descriptions to promote new apps that do not yet have enough ratings and review data. Finally, marketers can learn to leverage the preference heterogeneity on textual information to perform targeting. For instance, the platform can engage users who are used to reading descriptions by providing additional product details in texts. Similarly, phrasing in various sentiments can be utilized to target speci<sup>fi</sup>c consumers given the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant heterogeneous preference on sentiment.

Our study, as is other empirical work, is constrained by data limitations. First, we cannot collect consumer demographics because of privacy protections. In this regard, we manage to implement a hierarchical model to account for unobserved consumer heterogeneity. Platforms with access to such demographics can further sharpen personalized rankings given a lower bound on revenue improvements via the personalization we show. Second, we have not evaluated the proposed ranking strategy in a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment; instead, we exploit the individual heterogeneity estimates through policy experiments. However, it would be a fruitful exercise for further research to conduct experiments to obtain more robust insights into the impact of personalization. Third, we do not estimate the temporal effects on users’ click and install decisions from a dynamic perspective. We precisely model the stage-dependency between the click and install decisions with full heterogeneity at the individual level. This approach enables us to study consumer heterogeneity and personalization in the ranking design as the main focus of this paper. As expected, incorporating dynamic components into the model could incur substantial dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in empirical identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and computational burden given the existing estimation challenges. We, thus, leave this area for future research. Overall, our analysis provides a platform with executable actions to enhance app distribution monetization along with potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to multiple stakeholders in a mobile ecosystem.
