# Brand Crisis and Customer Relationship Management on Social Media: Evidence from a Natural Experiment from the Airline Industry

Abstract. In this study, we investigate the effect of a brand crisis on the customer relationship management (CRM) efforts of brands on social media. Despite the opportunities social media offers to brands to connect and engage with customers, it is still unclear how a brand crisis can change a brand’s social CRM efforts. Social media platforms can amplify the negative consequences of a brand crisis. However, the unique and public nature of social media platforms can offer new means for brands to handle a brand crisis and publicly manage their customer relationships. Compared with traditional CRM, social media enables brands to communicate with their customers publicly, which can strengthen their relationships with customers and maintain higher levels of customer engagement. Leveraging a natural experiment setting, we investigate the impact of the United Airlines crisis on three dimensions of social CRM efforts: informativeness, timeliness, and attentiveness. Contrary to traditional CRM efforts and recommendations, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the brand crisi increases informativeness efforts but reduces timeliness and attentiveness efforts.

## 1. Introduction

A brand crisis, such as the Delta Airlines computer system outage in $2 0 1 6 , ^ { 1 }$ causes tremendous threats to brands. Not only does it lead to <sup>fi</sup>nancial losses (Chen et al. 2009), but also a brand crisis can damage brand trust, hurting its image and reputation (Siomkos and Kurzbard 1994, Rhee and Haunschild 2006). Acknowledging the depth of the potential damage caused by a brand crisis, researchers dedicate substantial attention to assessing the impact of a brand crisis (e.g., Dawar and Pillutla 2000, Cleeren et al. 2008, Chen et al. 2009) and developing appropriate brand response strategies to mitigate it (e.g., Dawar 1998, Lei et al. 2012, Cleeren et al. 2013). One such strategy is based on effective customer relationship management (CRM).

CRM aims to create value for customers and brands with better customer satisfaction and brand performance (e.g., Verhoef 2003, Reinartz et al. 2004, Zineldin

2005). Researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd that effective CRM strategies, such as redress, can strongly affect customers’ postcomplaint behavior and mitigate potential losses (McCollough et al. 2000, Davidow 2003).

Most recently, social media platforms, such as Twitter and Facebook, have been increasingly creating new opportunities for successful CRM activities. Compared with traditional CRM, social media–based CRM enables brands to have easy and direct communication with their customers, which can strengthen brand–customer relationships and maintain higher customer engagement levels (Tsimonis and Dimitriadis 2014, Kumar and Qiu 2022). Furthermore, the pub lic nature of social media offers an innovative way for brands to publicly engage and manage their customer relationships, which can increase brand awareness and customer loyalty (Sashi 2012, Bapna et al. 2019) and enable easy access to prospective customers (Malthouse et al. 2013). These advantages, in turn, enhance brand image and, subsequently, <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Luo et al. 2013, Kumar et al. 2018). However, public customer engagement on social media does not come without risk. For example, negative word of mouth on social media stemming from poor customer engagement or brand response can disseminate rapidly and reach a large audience, especially if the customer has a strong social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Trusov et al. 2009, Qiu et al. 2018).

Whereas the role of CRM in a brand crisis is wellestablished, it is unclear about social media–based CRM efforts during a brand crisis given the risks of brand–customer public engagement on social media. In fact, despite social media’s increasing importance and popularity in customer management, it is reported that social media marketing managers lack the proper guidelines to respond to and mitigate online <sup>fi</sup>restorms (Herhausen et al. 2019). Meanwhile, research on social media–based CRM is also in its infancy (Ma et al. 2015, Gunarathne et al. 2018, Gans et al. 2021), let alone on the role of social CRM during a brand crisis. A notable exception is a recent study by He et al. (2018) that investigates how nonfocal brands, which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as brands that offer products and services similar to a focal brand suffering a productharm crisis, adjust two types of marketing efforts on social media following aviation crises: offensive and defensive marketing. However, little is known about how a focal brand adjusts its own CRM efforts on social media after a brand crisis. Therefore, in this paper, we attempt to <sup>fi</sup>ll the gap in the literature by asking the following: how does a brand crisis affect the brand’s social CRM efforts?

In this work, we examine a brand’s social CRM efforts by exploiting a natural experiment setting on United Airlines’ brand crisis. On April 9, 2017, United Airlines faced a brand crisis after a video showed a passenger being removed from one of the airlines <sup>fl</sup>ights by an airport’s aviation security of<sup>fi</sup>cials, resulting in physical injuries.<sup>2</sup> The video went viral on social media, causing both online and off-line <sup>fi</sup>restorms. In response to the crisis, the airline issued an apology statement on April 11, 2017, which was posted to the airline’s of<sup>fi</sup>cial Twitter account and website. There are several reasons why we chose this crisis in this study. First and foremost, although the incident occurred on a United Airlines <sup>fl</sup>ight, there is no indication that the airline’s social media management team could have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced any decision to remove the passenger from the plane. In addition, given that this crisis was unexpected, this does not give the social media management team enough time to prepare, allocate resources, and/or implement new marketing efforts. Therefore, this incident can be treated as an exogenous event to the airline’s social media team, allowing a study of the impact of a brand crisis on social CRM efforts using a natural experiment setting. Additionally, in conceptualizing customer-based brand equity theory, Keller (1993) describes that consumers can derive both functional and symbolic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts or personal values from a brand’s product or service. Functional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are those that customers derive from the product-related attributes, which typically relate to the performance of the product or service. Symbolic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, on the other hand, are the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that customers attach to the non product attributes, and this can be related to consumers’ social approvals and re<sup>fl</sup>ect emotions induced by the brand. Keller (1993) additionally describes brand image as including these types of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and attributes that customers associate with the brands. In examining different response strategies to brand crises, Dutta and Pullig (2011) conceptualize a brand crisis into two general types: (a) a performance-based crisis, such as a product-harm crisis, that affects customers con<sup>fi</sup>dence in derived functional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts—for example, He et al. (2018) examine the effect of a productharm crisis on marketing strategies on social media in the context of aviation crashes—and (b) a values-based crisis, which involves social or ethical issues rather than a defective product. A values-based crisis can affect customers’ con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the symbolic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from the brand as it does not include productrelated attributes that are attached to the functional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from the brand.

It is worth noting that values-based crises, although rare, are becoming more frequent and create a high risk to brands because of the nature of social media. A social media post or message from a dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customer can hurt a brand’s reputation, and this risk can increase if that customer has a stronger social in<sup>fl</sup>u ence, such as having a large number of followers on Twitter (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Trusov et al. 2009). Social media is accelerating the spread of information when a crisis happens, which, in turn, reaches a wider range of users. In recent years, we have witnessed more brands going through a similar type of crisis, especially in the aviation industry. There are currently 18 major airline carriers in the United States with more than \$1 billion in revenue.<sup>3</sup> In 2017, following the crisis in this study, Southwest Airlines, which is a leading airline in the United States, had a similar incident. A video went viral on social media of a passenger being removed from one of its <sup>fl</sup>ights by Transportation Authority of<sup>fi</sup>cers.<sup>4</sup> Despite being rare, given that these incidents occurred in the past and could potentially occur in the future, a brand has to consider how to adjust its social CRM efforts to miti gate the negative impact of the brand crisis. Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings and the implications we draw from the results in this study can provide insights for brands who might experience similar crises in adjust ing their social CRM efforts.

To characterize a brand’s social CRM efforts, we focus on the three most important dimensions inspired by prior CRM literature (Smith et al. 1999,

Davidow 2003): (1) informativeness (refers to a brand’s effort in providing more explanation in its responses to customers), (2) timeliness (refers to the speed at which a brand responds to a customer), and (3) attentiveness (refers to the degree of a brand’s interaction with a customer). We create a unique panel data set from more than 1.5 million customer service–related tweets and construct Twitter conversation threads that were exchanged between customers and United Airlines’ social media customer service agents between November 2016 and July 2017.

Using a difference-in-differences (DID) estimation, our analyses show several interesting results. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the brand crisis increases the informativeness efforts and decreases the timeliness and attentiveness efforts of customer service agents on social media. The key implication of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings is that brands on social media become more conservative and careful in their response efforts, which contradicts prior literature on social media that suggests faster response and higher engagement levels with customers. We further empirically examine the effectiveness of these changes in social CRM efforts and conduct a mediation analysis to further understand the possible underlying mechanism that drives the overall effect of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction, an outcome measure on social media. The results shed some light on the effectiveness of social CRM efforts following a brand crisis. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the mediation analysis show that these efforts have a minimal effect in attenuating the negative effect of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction, which indicates that the conservative approach is not the ideal approach for social CRM efforts.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Customer Relationship Management and Social Media

With the advances in information technology, CRM has received increased attention from researchers in both the information systems and marketing literature. Researchers develop CRM frameworks beyond the pure technical de<sup>fi</sup>nition of CRM, placing it at a strategic level that creates dual value for brands and their customers (Payne and Frow 2005). The rise of social media platforms challenges the traditional view of CRM activities and creates an environment in which these activities are less structured, caused by increased customer engagement behavior (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2013). For example, information about <sup>fi</sup>rms is easily accessible, and customers can share their brand stories with other customers publicly, reaching a larger audience with little cost (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Malthouse et al. 2013, Qiu et al. 2015). This causes traditional <sup>fi</sup>rm–customer interactions to switch from private to public (Ward and

Ostrom 2006, Kumar et al. 2022), which may affect brand performance (Gensler et al. 2013). Therefore, a growing body of literature in information systems and marketing has emerged that examines CRM on social media. For example, Rishika et al. (2013) examine the effect of customers’ participation in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media page on the intensity of the brand–customer relationship. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that participation efforts led to an increase in customers’ visit frequency. Malthouse et al. (2013) propose the “social CRM” framework that captures the impact of different customer engagement levels on main CRM activities, such as acquisition, maintenance, and retention.

Recently, a few studies have empirically investigated social CRM activities and complaint management. Examples include Maecker et al. (2016), who investigate the effect of brand–customer interactions on social media on upselling behavior, customer churn, and service request. Gunarathne et al. (2018) investigate the effect of social media in<sup>fl</sup>uence and bystander effects on brand responses to customer complaints. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that brands are more likely to respond to complainants with stronger social media in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and these customers are more likely to receive faster responses. They also <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of a bystander effect on social media. In another study, Gunarathne et al. (2022) <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of racial bias in brand responses to customers on Twitte in the airline industry. Proserpio and Zervas (2017) examine the effect of managerial responses on online reviews in the hotel industry and <sup>fi</sup>nd these responses attract positive reviews when increasing the cost of leaving a negative review, in return, improving <sup>fi</sup>rms online reputation. Gans et al. (2021) examine the relationship between voice, measured by tweet volume, and quality deterioration, measured by on-time per formance, in the U.S. airline industry context. The authors <sup>fi</sup>nd a correlation between the two constructs; that is, as quality deteriorates, tweet volume increases. Also, the authors <sup>fi</sup>nd that, when <sup>fi</sup>rms have a domi nant position in the market, this correlation is more likely to be observed.

It is worth noting that our study in the context of online response management is different from prior literature. For example, our study focuses on the impact of a brand crisis on managerial responses on social media, whereas Proserpio and Zervas (2017) study the impact of managerial responses on online ratings and reviews in the hotel industry. Moreover, Proserpio and Zervas (2017) examine the changes in ratings after hotel responses, relative to before responding, whereas in our study, we examine different response efforts (informativeness, timeliness, and attentiveness) and how these efforts change in response to a brand crisis. In addition, this study examines CRM efforts on brands’ of<sup>fi</sup>cial social media pages in contrast to managerial responses to customer reviews on third-party platforms. Also, the brand crisis in our study is an exogenous event to United Airlines’ social media team; that is, there is no indication that the airline’s social media management team could have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced any decision to remove the passenger from the plane. Gans et al. (2021) examine the impact of quality, measured as on-time performance of <sup>fl</sup>ights, on “voice,” which is measured as customers tweet volume. However, their paper does not examine managerial responses to customer tweets. In addition, on-time performance is different from the brand crisis that is the focus of our study.

## 2.2. Customer Relationship Management and Brand Crisis

The occurrence of a brand crisis can bring devastating impacts to brands. Prior research shows that the negative impact of a brand crisis is not limited to a brand’s market share and sales (Rhee and Haunschild 2006, Van Heerde et al. 2007), but it can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect other noneconomic outcomes, such as brand reputation (Siomkos and Kurzbard 1994, Coombs 2007) and marketing activities (Van Heerde et al. 2007, Cleeren et al. 2013, Liu and Shankar 2015). How a brand overcomes and minimizes the negative consequences of a brand crisis depends on its response strategies, consumer prior perception of a brand, and other external factors, such as media coverage (Siomkos and Kurzbard 1994). Prior studies on brand and product-harm crises investigating different crisis communication strategies and their impact on brands are based on empirical studies (Cleeren 2015), case studies (Laufer and Coombs 2006), and laboratory experiments (Dawar and Pillutla 2000). For example, Coombs (2007) proposes the situational crisis communication theory, a framework of crisis communication strategies that accounts for users’ and stakeholders’ responses to a crisis to protect brand reputation. In the social media era, a crisis can have a different impact on brand reputation and customer reaction compared with traditional media (Sweetser and Metzgar 2007, Schultz et al. 2011, Utz et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2015). Early studies on social media and product-harm crises are mostly case studies. For example, Utz et al. (2013) compare traditional media to social media and examine their effect on reputation, crisis reaction, and crisis communication using an online experiment. However, there is still a limited focus in the information systems literature on how social media can be used strategically to manage brand crisis impact. Few studies investigate the impact of social media on brand performance (Wang et al. 2016) and competitors marketing strategies following a product-harm crisis (He et al. 2018). Yet little is known about how a brand crisis can affect different CRM efforts of a focal brand on social media. We <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap in the literature and contribute to the stream of literature on CRM in social media during a brand crisis.

## 2.3. Organizational Response to Customer Complaints

A service failure is perceived by customers as a breakdown in a brand’s product or service (Maxham 2001). Customer dissatisfaction is not only caused by service failures themselves, but also by how brands respond to these failures (Smith et al. 1999, del R´ıo-Lanza et al. 2009). Prior research shows that effective brand responses to customers can have an impact on brand trust, customer satisfaction, and commitment through their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customers’ evaluation of perceived justice (Tax et al. 1998, Smith et al. 1999, Maxham and Netemeyer 2002). Customer satisfaction with com plaint resolution ultimately reinforces customer loy alty (Andreassen 1999, Homburg and Furst¨ 2005). In addition, research in marketing and service literature <sup>fi</sup>nds that brand response strategies to customer complaints are strongly linked to brands’ efforts in building customer relationships (e.g., Fornell and Wernerfelt 1987, Smith et al. 1999, Davidow 2003).

Past research on organizational service recovery examines various organizational response dimensions that in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ perception of justice, which, in turn, in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer satisfaction (e.g., Blodgett et al. 1997, Tax et al. 1998, Smith et al. 1999, Davidow 2000). In particular, different brand response strategies can affect different dimensions of customers perceived justice, which are the perceived outcome of complaint handling (i.e., distributive justice), the procedures a brand follows to handle a complaint and accomplish outcomes (i.e., procedural justice), and the treatment received during procedures of delivering the outcomes and the interaction with brand customer service (i.e., interactional justice) (Tax et al. 1998, Smith et al. 1999, Davidow 2003). For example, Smith et al. (1999) <sup>fi</sup>nd that response speed has the greatest effect on customer perception of procedural justice, and compensation has the greatest effect on customer perception of distributive justice.

During a service failure, customers are emotionally involved, observing how a brand responds and reacts to their complaints (Smith et al. 1999). In times of a brand crisis, customers’ emotions are likely to be highly intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed, experiencing deepened feelings of anger as customers attribute the responsibility of the crisis to the brand, increasing customers’ propensity to complain and putting the brand under great pressure (Coombs 2007). In addition, a brand crisis can negatively affect brand equity and customer loyalty (Dawar and Pillutla 2000). Therefore, after a brand crisis, a brand has more incentives to in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ perception of justice through their response efforts, restoring customer satisfaction, and reinforcing customer loyalty.

Building on this theorizing, this is also applicable to social CRM. Social media platforms allow customers to complain to brands at a low cost, reaching a wide range of other customers. Consequently, these complaints can cause harm to brands. In turn, brand responses to their complainants become important yet challenging (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Bolton and Saxena-Iyer 2009). The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social media response efforts on customers and their evaluation of the perceived justice with complaint handling are visible to other customers on these platforms. Therefore, we argue that a brand crisis impacts social CRM efforts and posit that, after a brand crisis, social CRM efforts are adjusted to in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ perceived justice evaluations, which can restore customer satisfaction and, eventually, customer loyalty. However, there is little understanding of what the proper response efforts are that can be used to address customers on social media. In this research, we focus on the three dimensions of CRM efforts that can be easily acted on by managers and customer service agents, which are informativeness, timeliness, and attentiveness.

## 3. Hypothesis Development 3.1. Impact of Brand Crisis on Social CRM Informativeness Effort

Informativeness refers to a brand’s effort in providing an explanation to the consumer. Prior research on customer service in off-line settings <sup>fi</sup>nds that providing an explanation to a customer’s complaint to be more effective on a customer’s postcomplaint behavior, such as satisfaction (e.g., Conlon and Murray 1996, Davidow 2000). An explanation communicates a brand’s willingness to address customers’ complaints. This is critical to customers in evaluating the outcome of brand responses, that is, positively in<sup>fl</sup>uencing distributive justice. A positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on distributive justice leads to increased customer satisfaction with complaint resolution and brand trust (Tax et al. 1998, Smith et al. 1999).

There are multiple reasons that customers turn to social media platforms, which include complaining to brands, expressing their concerns, and spreading negative word of mouth (Sreenivasan et al. 2012). During a brand crisis, customers can be even more inclined to take their complaints to social media platforms as their negative emotions are heightened, and this puts pressure on the brand. As a result, brands need to consider not only how their responses affect the complaining customers, but also how they affect other customers observing these responses. Subsequently, a brand may have higher incentives to increase its informativeness efforts by providing more explanation in its responses to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ evaluation of the brand and reinforce their loyalty, including those of observing customers. As such, we expect that brands increase their informativeness efforts after a brand crisis.

We believe that social media platforms, such as Twitter, offer observed factors that can be used as signals of informativeness efforts. In this paper, we operationalize informativeness using tweet length (Chevalier and May zlin 2006, Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011), which is a message characteristic. Prior research in information systems literature <sup>fi</sup>nds that longer reviews, for example, have a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the helpfulness of the reviews (Mudambi and Schuff 2010). Longer messages or reviews tend to be more informative and increase customers’ con<sup>fi</sup>dence (Mudambi and Schuff 2010, Yang et al. 2019). Therefore, a longer tweet can represent an increased effort to provide more explanation (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). In addition, it is reasonable to assume that a longer tweet is more helpful and useful. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. A brand crisis leads to an increase in the social CRM informativeness effort measured by the average length of brand response tweets in a thread on social media

## 3.2. Impact of Brand Crisis on Social CRM Timeliness Effort

Timeliness refers to a brand’s response speed to a customer complaint (Davidow 2000). Prior research shows that timeliness has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customer satisfaction and postcomplaint behavior (Davidow 2000, 2003). Conlon and Murray (1996) report a positive rela tionship between response speed and customer satisfaction and willingness to repurchase. Smith et al. (1999) <sup>fi</sup>nd that speedy responses have a positive effect on customers’ perception of procedural justice. On social media, response speed is an important dimension of customer service management (Gunarathne et al. 2018). A fast brand response to customer messages on social media is an essential factor in mitigating the virality of these messages (Herhausen et al. 2019). Therefore, a brand may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced to provide faster responses to customer complaints because any delays can be perceived as an unwillingness to address these complai nants. In addition, slow responses on social media following a brand crisis can create a negative perception of the brand and increase complainants’ dissatisfaction, which, in turn, can spread to a wider range of customers.

However, studies in organizational service response report mixed results on timeliness efforts. For example, Blodgett et al. (1997) <sup>fi</sup>nd that timeliness does not affect customers’ repurchase intentions or negative word of mouth. In the context of social media, a poor response to customer complaints can create a greater risk to the brand than a delayed response, damaging its image (Thomas et al. 2012). A poor brand response can reduce customer satisfaction and increase negative word of mouth. Additionally, a <sup>fi</sup>rm needs not only provide a fast response, but also needs to be careful in addressing customers (Herhausen et al. 2019). Therefore, a brand may want to delay its responses to customers on social media to appropriately address their complaints without increasing the negative effect of the brand crisis. Thus, we offer the following competing hypotheses.

Hypothesis 2a. A brand crisis leads to an increase in the CRM timeliness effort measured by the average amount of time a brand takes to respond to tweets in a thread on social media.

Hypothesis 2b. A brand crisis leads to a decrease in the CRM timeliness effort measured by the average amount of time a brand takes to respond to tweets in a thread on social media.

## 3.3. Impact of Brand Crisis on Social CRM Attentiveness Effort

Attentiveness refers to the degree of brand interactions with customers to show care and interest in listening to them (Davidow 2000, 2003). A brand effort in showing interest can improve customers’ perception of interactional justice, leading to positive customer satisfaction and brand trust (Tax et al. 1998). For example, Blodgett et al. (1997) <sup>fi</sup>nd that attentiveness has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on postcomplaint behavior. Brand responses on social media are the typical organizational response efforts to provide economic and social compensation to customers (Gu and Ye 2014). Attentiveness on social media can be measured as the number of brand responses to a customer during their interaction. The degree of brand interactions is shown to positively affect customers’ evaluation of brands (de Vries et al. 2012). Therefore, a brand may increase its attentiveness efforts by increasing its number of responses to a customer on social media after a brand crisis.

However, social media is a double-edged sword. A study by Gu and Ye (2014) shows that, although online brand responses positively affect customers with low satisfaction levels, they can have a limited impact on other customers. This can be attributed to the unique public nature of social media in creating a unique characteristic of online brand responses (Gu and Ye 2014). Although brand responses increase the future satisfaction of customers who receive responses, these responses reduce the ultimate satisfaction of customers observing these responses but not receiving the same themselves. Therefore, after a crisis, a brand may want to reduce its attentiveness efforts by lowering its engagement and responses to a complainant on social media. We, therefore, offer the following two competing hypotheses.

Hypothesis 3a. A brand crisis leads to an increase in the attentiveness CRM effort measured by the number of brand tweets in a thread on social media.

Hypothesis 3b. A brand crisis leads to a reduction in the attentiveness CRM effort measured by the number of brand tweets in a thread on social media.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Context and Data

Our research setting is the United Airlines crisis that occurred on April 9, 2017. A passenger on a United Airlines <sup>fl</sup>ight departing from Chicago O’Hare Interna tional Airport was forcefully removed from the <sup>fl</sup>ight, and a video of the incident went viral on social media, causing negative online reactions among online users. The video showed the airport police physically removing the passenger from the plane, resulting in physical injuries. Following an initial response from the airline’s CEO, which heightened the negative emotions among social media users, creating a vast amount of press on the brand, an of<sup>fi</sup>cial apology statement to the passenger, promising that such unfortunate events would not happen in the future was issued on April 11, 2017. This statement was posted on the airline’s of<sup>fi</sup>cial Twitter account and website.

We use a panel data set of Twitter threads between United Airlines and its customers. A customer initiates a thread or conversation by mentioning the of<sup>fi</sup>- cial Twitter account or customer service–speci<sup>fi</sup>c account of the airline using the “@” symbol. However, not all initiated threads receive a response from an airline’s customer service agents. Because we are interested in examining how social CRM efforts of brands change before and after a brand crisis, we only consider threads to which the airline’s social media customer service agents responded in the empirical analysis, and thus, we believe there is no concern for any selection bias. However, we control for the total number of daily tweets to validate our results, which we discuss in the robustness check section. We also consider threads that represent a single interaction between a customer and the airline and exclude threads that include multiuser participation from our data set. All threads in our data set do not span longer than seven days since they were initiated. Also, it is possible to determine the threads that were initiated with tweets that are “retweets.” We exclude these threads from our data set. The <sup>fi</sup>nal data set includes 48,946 United Airlines Twitter threads spanning eight months (i.e., November 2016 to July 2017).

We leverage the brand crisis on United Airlines as a natural experiment and employ a DID estimation to identify and estimate the impact of this event on United Airlines’ CRM efforts on social media. Conceptually, a DID estimation compares the differences in the efforts of United Airlines’ social media customer service agents (i.e., treated group) before and after the crisis to the differences in the efforts of another set of brands’ social media customer service agents $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ control group). Prior research shows that competing brands within the same industry (i.e., airline) as the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm in the crisis (i.e., United Airlines) could experience a negative spillover effect from a crisis (Roehm and Tybout 2006, Borah and Tellis 2016). On social media, competing brands that offer similar products or services can change their marketing activities and efforts in response to a brand crisis (He et al. 2018). Because of this spillover effect, using other nontargeted clinical conditions as controls do not satisfy the stable unit treatment value assumption in causal inference: a subject’s potential outcome is not affected by other subjects’ exposure to the treatment (Imbens and Rubin 2015, Pu et al. 2020, Qiu et al. 2022a). Therefore, using brands from the same airline industry is not a good representative of the counterfactual of the treated brand (i.e., United Airlines). To that end, we use another panel data set of Twitter threads as a control group from randomly selected brands from the Fortune 500 list of companies<sup>5</sup> (Amazon, Comcast, and UPS) and estimate the differences in United Airlines’ social CRM efforts. The total number of Twitter threads for the control group is 169,726. Similar to United Airlines, every thread in the control group is a customer-initiated conversation that mentions one of the control group brands by using its dedicated customer service Twitter handle. Also, every thread in the control group received a response from the mentioned brand.

Using both data sets, we constructed a unique panel data set. For every Twitter thread, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the social media customer service agent who responded to the thread. When social media agents respond to customer complaints, they end their response tweets with their initials or <sup>fi</sup>rst names, preceded by a special character, such as a caret (ˆ) or an asterisk (\*). In the end, we obtained an unbalanced panel data set of 32,959 observations (service agent–day pair).

We operationalize our dependent variables, informativeness, timeliness, and attentiveness, as follows. For informativeness, we measure AverageTweetLength , which is the average number of words in response tweets of customer service agent i to customer threads during time t. Attentiveness is measured as AverageTweetCount , which is the average number of tweets a customer service agent i used to respond to customer threads during time t.

## 4.3. Econometric Identification

As mentioned earlier, we employ a DID estimation to establish the effect of a brand crisis on the brand’s social CRM efforts. DID estimation is popular in estimating causal relationships (Bertrand et al. 2004) and has been frequently used in the information systems literature in recent years (e.g., Goh et al. 2013, Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Wang et al. 2022). This estimation allows us to compare how the social CRM efforts of United Airlines’ social media agents change after the crisis compared with other social media agents in other brands that do not experience the crisis. To control for ex ante differences between the customer service agents of different brands, we use customer service agent <sup>fi</sup>xed effects in the model. Our model to estimate the causal effect is

$$
Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} B r a n d C r i s i s _ {i t} + \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { i t }$ represents AverageTweetLength , Average ResponseTime , or AverageTweetCount , our dependent variables for service agent i during time t. Brand Crisis , a dichotomous variable, is our independent variable of interest. $\alpha _ { i }$ and $\gamma _ { t }$ are customer service agents and time <sup>fi</sup>xed effects, respectively. $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ indicates the error term. We estimate the time of the brand crisis to be the day the of<sup>fi</sup>cial apology statement was issued (i.e., April 11, 2017). We estimate $\beta _ { 1 }$ to test our hypotheses.

Before we discuss the results of our model, we discuss a few concerns regarding the DID estimation. The main assumption of the DID estimation is the parallel trends assumption between treated and control groups in the pretreatment period (Angrist and Pischke 2008). In addition, serial correlation within standard error is another concern with DID estimation (Bertrand et al. 2004). We alleviate these concerns by conducting several robustness checks, which include counterfactual estimators (CEs) that are discussed in detail in the next section. Another concern is that the untreated brands (i.e., control group) might not be a good representative for the counterfactual of the treated brand (i.e., treated group). To remedy this issue, we use a simple form of the interrupted time series design to estimate the effect of the brand crisis on each of the three main outcomes of interest. The single interrupted time series design is a strong and useful quasi-experimental design that evaluates a longitudinal effect of interventions (treatments) and eliminates the need for a separate control group. The interrupted time series design allows us to examine both the slope and intercept separately; that is, it examines whether the change in the outcome is immediate or lagged and whether this change is continuous or not (Penfold and Zhang 2013, Bernal et al. 2017). We include additional robustness tests to validate our results.

## 5.2. Counterfactual Estimators

To further examine and validate the parallel trends assumptions or no unobserved time-varying confounders of the DID model (Angrist and Pischke 2008), we introduce and employ a set of novel CEs. A recently developed framework based on work in the econometrics (Liu et al. 2021), computer science, and statistics literature (Athey et al. 2021), CEs have two advantages that allow us to (i) address unobserved time-varying confounders (e.g., see Pan and Qiu 2022) and (ii) visually examine the dynamic treatment effect estimates (and evaluate the parallel trends assumption) to assess when the treatment effects started emerging and how they evolved.

The central idea of CEs is that they convert a causal identi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem into a prediction problem and help address unobserved time-varying confounders. The estimation strategy using CEs consists of three steps: (1) build the predictive model using the untreated observations (treat the counterfactual outcomes of the treated observations as missing values), (2) predict counterfactual outcomes for the treated observations from the predictive model, and (3) estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) by taking the average difference between observed outcomes and predicted (counterfactual) outcomes of the treated observations. We apply three estimators that are used in this framework to infer causality between the treatment (BrandCrisis) and outcomes of interest (AverageTweetLength, AverageResponseTime, and AverageTweetCount). The three CEs differ in the underlying assumptions on how the treated outcomes in the counterfactual conditions are predicted and in building the predictive models. The estimators, in order of computational dif<sup>fi</sup>culty, are (i) <sup>fi</sup>xed effect counterfactual estimator (FEct), (ii) interactive <sup>fi</sup>xed effect counterfactual estimator (IFEct), and (iii) matrix completion estimator (MC).

The FEct uses the procedure of conventional twoway <sup>fi</sup>xed effects models, sharing the same assumptions. However, FEct is robust when the treatment effect is heterogeneous as opposed to the conventional two-way <sup>fi</sup>xed effects models (de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfoeuille 2020, Liu et al. 2021). FEct is biased when unobserved time-varying confounders exist.

Using factor-augmented models, IFEct models unobserved time-varying confounders when the confounders can be decomposed into time-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors interacted with unit-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor loading (Liu et al. 2021). The IFEct estimator estimates a factor-augmented model instead of a two-way <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model using observation under the control condition. MC was introduced by Athey et al. (2021) from the computer science literature. MC uses the observed elements of the matrix of control outcomes corresponding to untreated observations to predict the missing elements of the matrix, corresponding to treated observations. The main difference between IFEct and MC estimators is that the IFEc estimator decomposes the factors into time-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors and unit-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor loadings, whereas the MC estimator directly estimates observations in the nontreated condition as an optimization problem (Liu et al. 2021). Results of the three CEs for the three outcomes of interest are in Tables 3–5. We observe that the coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients of BrandCrisis are consistent with our main model results, which validates our main empirical results.

## 6.2. Interrupted Time Series

As mentioned earlier, the advantage of the interrupted time series design is that it can be used without including both treated and control groups, which means that we can examine the impact of an intervention (treatment) on a single group without the need for a separate control group in which the intervention did not occur. In this sense, we can focus only on a treatment group and do not need to evaluate the pretreatment differences between the treatment and control groups. This helps in evaluating the robustness of our results and alleviates any concern in the DID estimate speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Interrupted time series is a quasi-experimental design that examines the impact of an intervention on an outcome in the posttreatment period by using the outcome of observations in the pretreatment period as a baseline (Bernal et al. 2017), which further evaluates and validates the robustness of our results. This design is used in studies that include public health policy interventions, such as Penfold and Zhang (2013). ## 7. Empirical Extension

A question that follows the empirical analysis presented so far is whether the adjustments in social CRM efforts that follow a brand crisis are effective and justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. As discussed in the hypothesis development section, social CRM efforts are adjusted after a brand crisis to in-<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ perceived justice evaluations, which can restore customer satisfaction. Also, brands use social media platforms not only to resolve customer complaints, but also to increase brand awareness, customer satisfaction, and boost positive sentiment broadcast about the brand and reduce negative sentiment broadcast (Mithas et al. 2005). Therefore, brands’ decisions to respond to customers on social media are based on whether customer satisfaction is more likely to increase following a brand response. Therefore, it is important to dig deeper into the possible underlying mechanism of the observed effects. This is important as it can help brands to better understand the effectiveness of their social CRM efforts and the opportunities offered by social media to mitigate the negative effects of the brand crisis.

Because we are studying CRM efforts on social media, we consider an outcome variable on social media, that is, CustomerSatisfaction. We conduct a mediation analysis (e.g., Imai et al. 2010, 2014; Tingley et al. 2014; Ding et al. 2021; Qiu et al. 2022b, Yan et al. 2022) to understand the relationship between the treatment, BrandCrisis, and an outcome of interest, CustomerSatis faction, when accounting for the intermediate variables, AverageTweetLength, AverageResponseTime, and AverageTweetCount. In other words, it helps to understand how the intermediate variables that could lie on the causal path affect the outcome of interest and to esti mate the direct and indirect effects of the treatment. The central idea of the mediation analysis is that the total effect of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction is composed of direct and indirect (mediating) effect channels. By breaking down the total effect of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction into direct and indirect effects, we can uncover the possible underlying mechanism that drives the overall treatment effect. Figure 4 illustrates this relationship. The direct effect of the BrandCrisis on CustomerSatisfaction is represented by the solid arrow, whereas the indirect (mediating) effect is represented by the dashed arrows, which suggest that this indirect effect is mediated by each of the three social CRM efforts, which are AverageTweetLength, AverageResponseTime, and AverageTweetCount. It is worth noting that we assume the three social CRM efforts are independent of each other as the robustness check results in Online Appendix E suggest.

## 8. Discussion, Conclusion, and Directions for Future Research

The advent of social media offers brands new means to manage customer relationships and brings changes to the brand–customer relationship dynamics. Compared with traditional CRM channels, such as call centers, social media is an open and transparent channel that allows users to view customers’ complaints and brand responses. Because of the public nature of social media platforms, customers can publicly voice their complaints to brands, which can easily disseminate and reach a wide range of customers. Social media has become the perfect channel for open communication in which customers easily express their opinions publicly, switching from their passive roles on traditional CRM channels to active roles (Khurana et al. 2019). This speci<sup>fi</sup>c public nature of social media allows brands to engage with their customers and subsequently in<sup>fl</sup>uence their satisfaction (Malthouse et al. 2013). As a customer service channel, social media offers brands opportunities to improve brand image and customer loyalty not only by engaging with their customers, but also by addressing customers’ complaints. However, this does not come without challenges. A brand’s reputation can suffer if a message from a dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customer goes viral on social media. This risk can increase if the dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customer has a strong social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, such as having a large number of followers on Twitter (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Trusov et al. 2009). Therefore, this form of customer relationship and complaint management can either help or hurt brands, leaving brands with the challenge of identifying the opportunities that social media offers, yet avoiding its risks, especially following a brand crisis.

During a brand crisis, social media can amplify the damages caused by the crisis unless brands take advantage of social media to handle the brand crisis properly and manage their customer relationships publicly and effectively. Despite recent research on social media and customer management (e.g., Maecker et al. 2016; Gunarathne et al. 2018, 2022; He et al. 2018), it is yet unclear how a brand crisis can affect a brand’s CRM efforts on social media. On one hand, a brand crisis can push a brand to increase its overall CRM efforts on social media and leverage its public nature as a customer service channel to reduce the negative impact caused by the brand crisis. On the other hand, a brand crisis might move a brand toward lowering its social CRM efforts through a tendency to decrease its overall responses to customers and be reactive in its social CRM efforts to avoid increasing customer frustration levels and negative sentiments. Literature on organizational responses and service recovery provides considerable insights on how brand responses to customers can positively impact and reinforce customers’ evaluation of perceived justice through their positive impact on customer satisfaction and brand trust (e.g., Tax et al. 1998, Smith et al. 1999, Maxham and Netemeyer 2002). For example, prior studies show that faster responses have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customer satisfaction and postcomplaint behavior (Davidow 2000, 2003). However, we do not know if these efforts are effective on social media following a brand crisis.

In this paper, we study the effect of a brand crisis on the brand’s social CRM efforts. Drawing on CRM, social media, and organizational response literature, we formulate our hypotheses. Our basic proposition is that, after a brand crisis, a brand adjusts its social CRM efforts to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer evaluation of the brand, which, in turn, can have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customer satisfaction with customer complaint handling, therefore restoring loyalty. To test the hypotheses, we exploit a natural experiment setting and use DID estimation to establish the effect of the United Airlines crisis on its social CRM efforts. The CRM efforts examined in this paper are informativeness, timeliness, and attentiveness.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the propositions. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that customer service agents increase their informativeness efforts following a brand crisis, implying the brand’s willingness to provide more explanation to customer complaints and showing its interest in addressing their concerns. This can be attributed to the public nature of social media in making brand–customer interaction visible to other customers. Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that customer service agents on social media decrease their timeliness efforts by taking a longer time to respond to customers after a brand crisis. This could be a result of customer service agents’ tendency to be more careful in their responses without damaging the brand image if customers are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their responses. A dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customer can easily and quickly share a poor response publicly, therefore damaging the brand image. Finally, despite the opportunities to increase brand–customer interactions on social media, following a brand crisis, we observe that customer service agents decrease their attentiveness efforts. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that a brand might be more alert that, although increased engagement with customers can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence their evaluation of interactional justice, there is a risk that this behavior could increase dissatisfaction levels among other unsatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers who are not getting the same level of attention after a crisis. Also, this could be psychologically attributed to brands’ fear of increasing customer dissatisfaction or negative sentiment toward the brand because of poor response or unfair attention.

We extended our study to further examine how changes in the social CRM efforts mediate the effect of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction. From the mediation analysis, for example, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the slight increase in informativeness effort has a marginal mediating role in reducing the negative impact of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction. This suggests that, although customer service agents on social media slightly increased their informativeness efforts, they were still careful in their efforts. In summary, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that changes in social CRM efforts were not effective in mediating the negative impact of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction.

## 8.1. Theoretical Implications

This work makes several contributions to the growing stream of literature on CRM and social media in information systems. First, this paper contributes to our understanding of the unique nature of social media as an emerging customer service channel and how it challenges traditional CRM efforts. Despite the importance and popularity of social media among customers and brands, social CRM has not been fully explored.

Second, although prior studies attempt to examine postcrisis marketing efforts on social media, they only consider how nonfocal <sup>fi</sup>rms adjust their social media strategies (offensive and defensive marketing) following a major incident (He et al. 2018). Our study adds to this literature by analyzing how a brand crisis affects the social CRM efforts of a focal brand (i.e., the brand in crisis). To the best of our knowledge, we <sup>fi</sup>ll this important gap in information systems and customer relationship management by providing an understanding of how different dimensions of social CRM efforts change and adjust following a brand crisis. This study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst in the literature to study customer service on social media following a brand crisis. A notable exception is a paper by Gans et al. (2021); however, their work examines the relationship between <sup>fi</sup>rms’ competitive structure and customer voice using tweets to U.S. airlines, whereas our work examines different brands’ customer service efforts in response to customer voices on social media. Moreover, this study examines different social CRM efforts other than a dichotomous approach, such as whether a customer received a brand response or not. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide a more nuanced approach to further understand a brand’s efforts in responding to customers on social media.

Third, this work contributes to the brand crisis management literature. Whereas prior studies mostly examine crisis communication strategies and marketing efforts, such as advertising (e.g., Van Heerde et al. 2007, Cleeren et al. 2013, Liu and Shankar 2015), we add to this literature and focus on CRM efforts on social media following a brand crisis. Multiple persuasive reasons can lead to predicting that a brand either increases or decreases its overall social CRM efforts after a brand crisis. We believe our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to provide empirical evidence and insights on social CRM efforts following a brand crisis.

Finally, we view social CRM efforts as an extension of organizational efforts to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ perceptions and satisfaction. Prior studies in organizational responses to customer complaints examine how brand responses affect customer perceived justice, satisfaction with complaint handling, and ultimately customer loyalty (e.g., Tax et al. 1998, Smith et al. 1999, Davidow 2000, Fan et al. 2022). This stream of literature only examines organizational responses in the context of traditional customer service (e.g., Smith et al. 1999, Davidow 2003). We extend this framework in understanding how a brand crisis affects organizational responses as determinants to customers’ evaluation of the brand on social media. We provide new evidence of how brands adjust their response efforts to customers on the public platform Twitter. Moreover, despite the opportunities of the open and public nature of social media, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the mediation analysis model show that these adjusted social CRM efforts were not successful in mitigating the negative impact of the brand crisis on customer satisfaction on social media.

## 8.2. Managerial Implications

This study carries important implications for practitioners and social media management strategists in <sup>fi</sup>nding the best combination of social CRM efforts, especially after witnessing several brands facing brand crises. Although brands switch to social media platforms to connect with customers and get better insights into their needs, our empirical results show that brands lean toward more conservative efforts on social media. Given our proposition, social media CRM teams should carefully examine and consider the consequences of adjusting the different dimensions of social CRM efforts when designing speci<sup>fi</sup>c response efforts to customer complaints. In <sup>fi</sup>nding the optimal combination of social CRM efforts, brands should consider customer evaluations of these efforts that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ postcomplaint satisfaction and loyalty.

The results from the extended empirical study suggest that brands are still not utilizing the power of social media platforms to mitigate the negative effect of a brand crisis on customer satisfaction. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the mediation analysis provide deeper guidance to brands and social media managers in times of crisis. Adjustments to CRM efforts on social media can be implemented in real time, which is a unique feature of social media platforms that provides social media managers the opportunity to react quickly to crises. Public communication and transparency on social media require brands to respond and adjust strategically to manage their customer relationships and increase customer satisfaction. This has important implications for brands in ensuring that their social media teams are well trained and prepared to avoid shortcomings. This echoes various prior media reports that <sup>fi</sup>nd social media marketing managers lack the proper guidelines to respond to and mitigate online <sup>fi</sup>restorms (Herhausen et al. 2019).

Despite the crisis in this study being rare, this type of values-based crisis receives great media and public attention. In recent years, we have witnessed several brands undergoing similar values-based crises that could have negative consequences on their customers derived symbolic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which subsequently, could negatively hurt their brand image. Given that United Airlines is similar to the other major U.S. airlines on dif ferent metrics, such as passenger volume and number of destinations,<sup>7</sup> United Airlines’ social CRM approach could be representative of the other major airlines. Additionally, He et al. (2018) <sup>fi</sup>nd that nonfocal <sup>fi</sup>rms within the airline industry have similar adjustments to their social media marketing strategies after a major cri sis of a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. This could be an indicator that <sup>fi</sup>rms within the same industry can implement similar efforts and strategies. Therefore, our results provide practical guidance to similar brands to prepare and implement proper changes to their own social CRM efforts in the wake of such a crisis. In addition, our results may have important implications for smaller brands seeking to harness the power of social media, while avoiding its risks. Social media is an important channel in which <sup>fi</sup>rms are allocating resources and marketing budgets. However, smaller brands could have a limited budget and fewer resources to design proper response efforts. Therefore, they can learn from efforts employed by leading brands, such as United Airlines, and design an appropriate mix of social CRM efforts.

## 8.3. Conclusion and Future Research Directions

Whereas social CRM efforts can be determinants of customer satisfaction and their evaluation of brands, little is known about how they change in response to a brand crisis. Our study is not without limitations. First, this study was conducted in the airline industry and with relatively large brands. Therefore, we should be careful in generalizing the results to other industries and smaller brands. A natural extension is to consider how a brand crisis affects the social CRM efforts of brands in other industries. Second, we should be careful in generalizing the results in this study to other social media platforms, which can be an interesting addition to this research to examine the different effects across different social media platforms. Third, we only consider three dimensions of CRM efforts in this work. Extending this work to other dimensions, such as redress, would be valuable and provide rich insights to brands in helping them with their social media efforts and strategies. Future research can also perform a more sophisticated textual analysis of brand responses to investigate potential changes in the content of those responses. Fourth, this study does not consider different types of brand crises and their effect on social CRM efforts. This is an important future research question to examine whether different types of brand crises elicit different impacts on social CRM efforts. Finally, although we provide empirical evidence of the effectiveness of social CRM effort changes, future studies can further improve the causal mechanism of a brand crisis on customer satisfaction and the possible underlying drivers of the observed effects of a brand crisis on social CRM efforts.
