# Platform Refund Insurance or Being Cast Out: Quantifying the Signaling Effect of Refund Options in the Online Service Marketplace

Abstract. This study examines whether and how an online service marketplace can leverage refund options endorsed by different parties (i.e., the platform or sellers) to address the “lemons” problem that is caused by the intangibility, variability, and unreturnable nature of the services sought. Through developing a signaling mechanism and a corresponding demand estimation model, we show that both platform refund insurance and a seller-guaranteed refund increase service demand, with platform refund insurance as the more effective option and hence having a more effective signaling mechanism. A reduced-form analysis suggests that sellers with a better reputation or less popularity might benefit less from refund options. An investigation on further use of the more effective refund option, a “having platform refund insurance or being cast out” policy (i.e., retaining platform refund-insured sellers but expelling uninsured ones), using counterfactual simulations and supply-side single interrupted time series designs, reveals the effectiveness of this policy in filtering out low-quality sellers, shown as an improved quality of sellers on the platform due to the change in sellers (i.e., new sellers’ replacing those who were expelled) both immediately after the policy and in the (near) equilibrium, yet a cost (i.e., a loss in demand and consumer welfare) for the platform in the (near) equilibrium due to the changes in characteristics (e.g., price) of sellers. This cost, however, is lower than the benefit from the improved quality of the sellers, so that the platform’s overall performance improves. The study also quantifies the consumer welfare of the online service marketplace and provides practical insight for consumers, sellers, and online service marketplace operators.

## 1. Introduction

The trend toward digitalization has expanded the product line of the online marketplace from traditional physical products to services. A service marketplace connects businesses with consumers who need to do such things as purchase or recharge a prepaid phone card or find a babysitter, expanding greatly the breadth of and demand for products and services in the online marketplace. This expansion makes the marketplace more transparent, easier to use, and more popular with online consumers. For example, in the case of purchasing prepaid phone cards, without an online tool, consumers must find and contact retailers individually, compare prices, and then make a decision. In the online marketplace, these steps are completed in several minutes by using keywords and filters. Services vary from simple tasks, such as those for home improvement, to more sophisticated ones, such as coaching sessions, professional editing, and even system development. Well-funded marketplaces include home-improvement platforms, such as Thumbtack, Amazon services, Handy, and Zaarly; freelance platforms, such as Elance, Freelancer, and ODesk; and more specialized platforms, such as Skillbridge for business consulting, Vouched for financial services, and UpCounsel for legal services. Industry observers estimate a U.S. market size of at least \$250 billion for the home repair and improvement market alone (Seetharaman 2014). Other reports indicate a profit margin of about 20% for the service marketplace by Amazon, suggesting the potential for huge profitability (Perez and Etherington 2015).

Compared with physical items sold in the online marketplace, service offerings are more vulnerable to information asymmetry. A better service provider is more efficient and trustworthy, whereas a low-quality one might be slow in job delivery or even abscond with advance payments. Our descriptive statistics show that a good service provider (we also use “seller” interchangeably) completes a task in less than 10 minutes consistently, whereas a bad one takes longer than 27 days. Unlike with a physical item, however, the differences in service quality are intangible and insubstantial. In addition, each service is generated one time and is unique, which results in variability and inconsistency, even by the same provider. As a result, consumers find it difficult to gauge with accuracy the quality/utility of services by comparing measurable characteristics across alternatives, a process that is typically applied when evaluating physical products. Moreover, unlike a physical product, a service is unreturnable, which intensifies a consumer’s propensity to abandon the purchase under uncertainty, thus leading to a “lemons” market in which adverse selection drives good-quality sellers away (Akerlof 1970).

An effective approach to combat information asymmetry is to provide quality indicators (signals). Online platform owners have used various quality indicators, including feedback-based reputation systems (Ba and Pavlou 2002, Pavlou and Dimoka 2006, Ghose 2009, Bockstedt and Goh 2011, Yoganarasimhan 2013), e-images (Gregg and Walczak 2008, Zhang et al. 2022), and website quality (Schlosser et al. 2006, Wells et al. 2011), which have been shown to have varying degrees of effectiveness in mitigating information asymmetry. We aim to extend the scope of understanding quality indicators to refund options, which include a classic seller-guaranteed refund and a platform-endorsed refund option, termed platform refund insurance. Although both tools are widely adopted by the online service marketplace and e-commerce platforms, there is no consensus on their effectiveness, especially regarding the latter. Unlike the classic refund guaranteed by sellers, platform refund insurance is a program that provides coverage for the seller's refund cost, whereby the seller pays the platform an insurance premium. Consumers who transact with sellers who are covered by platform refund insurance have an option to process their refund requests with the platform's specialists rather than with individual sellers. The involvement of the platform as a third party to the two parties of consumers-sellers substantially reshapes the signaling mechanisms of platform refund insurance as compared with that of the classic refund guaranteed by the seller, as discussed in the literature on refund options for physical product retailing (Li et al. 2009, Shang et al. 2017) and refund policies that vary in terms of leniency (Wood 2001, Bonifield et al. 2010). Thus, the findings of these studies might have limited generalizability, particularly regarding platform refund insurance or refund options in the context of the online service marketplace.

This paper aims to examine the effectiveness of refund options on demand in the online service marketplace. We draw from signaling theory (Akerlof 1970) to develop the signaling mechanism of the classic seller-guaranteed refund and platform refund insurance and expect that platform refund insurance will address more effectively individuals' quality uncertainty due to its higher credibility in the commitment compared with a seller-guaranteed refund. That is, because the platform is an objective third party to the two parties of consumers and sellers and has an information advantage, and platform refund insurance requires the seller to pay the signaling cost in advance, platform refund insurance is more likely to ensure a higher signaling cost for "low-type" sellers.

To examine our expectation, we collect aggregate-level demand data and seller characteristics from a large online service marketplace and develop a random coefficient logit model from the classic Berry, Levinson, and Pakes framework (BLP; Berry et al. 1995), as this model best suits the aggregate demand data. Moreover, it is worth noticing that, first, consumers are commonly assumed to have varied risk preference/tolerance, such that refund options have varied effects across different consumers. Ignoring this by assuming homogeneous taste results in the independence of irrelevant alternative (IIA) problem that can bias the marginal effects' estimates. Second, sellers have the freedom to strategically adjust refund options based on the unobserved shock of demand. Therefore, the correlation between unobserved shock and refund options constitutes a classic endogeneity problem similar to that of prices. The BLP random coefficient logit model (hereafter "the BLP model") addresses both issues, as it incorporates random coefficients to allow for heterogeneous taste coefficients across consumers and instrumental variables to provide unbiased estimates with endogeneity taken into consideration. In addition, the structural framework allows counterfactual simulations and enables closed-form consumer surplus, both of which can help to reveal the economic value caused by market structure changes.

We apply the model to the data for the specialized category of service that sells prepaid phone cards and recharges them remotely. One important reason for choosing this data set is its uniqueness in covering a “having platform refund insurance or being cast out” policy (hereafter “the policy”) that is related to one of our focal variables, platform refund insurance. The policy requires all sellers in the marketplace to enroll in platform refund insurance, which is our focal category, to improve platform-wide competency and service quality. Sellers who refuse to enroll will be expelled by having their business discontinued on the platform. This policy provides us with a prime opportunity to investigate the effectiveness of a platform's use of quality indicators to filter out unqualified sellers with the purpose of improving the platform's performance. To understand the underlying mechanism of the policy change and isolate two impacts of the policy, we leverage the counterfactual analysis capability based on the estimated BLP model. First, we examine the policy impact caused by the changes of sellers, which is a combination of unqualified sellers who leave and newcomer sellers who enter, to reveal whether the policy effectively filters out low-type sellers. Second, we consider the impact caused by characteristic changes among the rest of the sellers who stay on the platform throughout the policy change. Such sellers might react to the demand changes caused by the externality of the changes of sellers, whereas some who are newly insured also face changed costs from paying the insurance premium. Their strategic reactions, for example, marking up prices, might cause an unintended impact on platform-wide performance. We disentangle the two-sided impacts of the policy in (near) equilibrium to generate more insightful managerial implications for practitioners. We further enrich the policy analysis with single interrupted time series (SITS) designs to gauge the immediate impact of the policy on the changes in characteristics of the remaining sellers and the average seller characteristics on the marketplace. This supply-side analysis focuses on the immediate impacts and complements the demand-side-based counterfactual simulation on (near) equilibrium.

Our estimation results consistently show that both a seller-guaranteed refund and platform refund insurance increase the demand of sellers, indicating the quality-signaling effects of both refund options. The BLP estimation results further reveal diversified preferences among consumers. Moreover, the marginal effect of platform refund insurance on seller demand is stronger than that of the seller-guaranteed refund. This validates our hypothetical expectation that the signaling effect of platform refund insurance is stronger and justifies the platform's use of platform refund insurance as a quality indicator to filter out low-type sellers. Our reduced-form analysis further shows interactive effects between the two refund options and feedback-based reputation systems, suggesting that sellers with a better reputation or lower popularity might benefit less from refund options. The consumer surplus derived from each market reveals the economic value of the online service marketplace and indicates not only welfare loss during the policy change when the market experiences a salient seller loss but also welfare gain over the long term after the market moves to a new equilibrium.

Our simulation analysis, contingent on a few mild assumptions, shows that the changes of sellers (the combination of new sellers' entering and expelled sellers' leaving) after the policy largely increased platform-wide demand and consumer welfare. The number of newcomer sellers is even less than that of expelled sellers. The positive gain of consumer welfare and demand suggests that both aspects contributed by the newcomer sellers are, on average, higher than those by the expelled sellers, indicating a higher quality of newcomer sellers than expelled ones, on average. To a limited degree, however, the decrease in both demand and consumer welfare can be attributed to the changes in characteristics of the remaining sellers, for example, rearrangement of their adjustable characteristics and price markup after the same policy, which indicates an indirect side-cost for the platform caused by the policy change. Despite there being no analytical solution for the formation of the new equilibrium, our partial equilibrium finding is consistent with the theoretical reasoning that sellers would restructure their price and cost function on other characteristics to compensate for the additional cost of platform refund insurance and would react to the demand changes. Finally, the supply-side analysis supplements results that conform to our expectations: the policy causes immediate improvements in the average quality of sellers, captured by the average characteristics, but few immediate changes in characteristics other than platform refund insurance of the remaining sellers. From the perspective of the platform managers, both analyses suggest the effectiveness of the policy in filtering out low-type sellers, which also indicates the effectiveness of platform refund insurance as a quality indicator.

Our work narrows the gap in the literature on the quality signaling of refund options by adding empirical evidence, extending the scope to services, and considering a new form of refund option, a platform-endorsed one, that differs from traditional refund option designs from a credibility perspective. In addition, we contribute to the research stream that addresses quality uncertainties in the online marketplace by adding a new platform signal indicator (i.e., the platform refund insurance), examining the effectiveness of refund options in an online service marketplace, which is more vulnerable to the “lemons” problem, and exploring the feasibility of using a quality signal to filter “lemons” sellers and, thus, improve platform-wide performance. The demand estimation of an online service marketplace, including the estimation of demand elasticity of refund options, and the quantification of consumer welfare derived from such a marketplace, as well as the variety and quality changes due to the policy, further extend the scope of application for the BLP model and add to the literature stream on consumer welfare measurement. In practical terms, we provide valuable managerial insights regarding the adoption and the choice of refund options from the standpoint of platform managers and individual sellers. The understanding of the platform refund insurance mechanism has implications for the refund policy design of online marketplaces/platforms. An analysis of the policy provides a retrospective and economic evaluation for practitioners in our focal platform and generates actionable insights regarding seller screening policies for practitioners in broader platforms.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the related literature and our contribution. Section 3 provides the research context and our data set. We develop a demand estimation model, following Berry et al. (1995), in Section 4 and then discuss our estimation strategy and explain how the endogeneity issue is rigorously controlled in Section 5. Section 6 presents the reduced-form results, the BLP estimation results, robustness checks, and the consumer surplus recovered. Section 7 provides an analysis of the platform's (near-) equilibrium and immediate benefits and the costs of launching the policy with counterfactual simulations and a supply-side analysis. Section 8 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

We next present four streams of literature that our study relates to and adds to. Table 1 summarizes the related literature and their comparison with our study.

## 2.1. Signaling Effect of Refund Options in Direct Selling

We focus on how different designs of refund options affect demand through signaling service quality in direct selling. This is different from the general retailing context in which the retailer and manufacturer are not the same entity; thus, retailers' return policy cannot signal product quality but can only mitigate the misfit uncertainty (i.e., product risk) of consumers (Che 1996, Davis et al. 1998, Anderson et al. 2009). The direct selling also is distinct from advance selling, whereby refund policies offered by sellers (e.g., free cancellation) serve to hedge risks related to demand uncertainty (Courty and Li 2000, Guo 2009, Huang and Zhang 2020). Other streams in the refund option literature in retailing and e-tailing include the antecedents of refund and return (Nasr-Bechwati and Siegal 2005), the impact on postconsumption evaluation and future (re)purchases (Petersen and Kumar 2009, Bower and Maxham 2012, Li et al. 2019), and countering consumers' opportunistic refunds (Hess et al. 1996, Chu et al. 1998, Davis et al. 1998). The broader literature extends to the impact on the supply chain (Su 2009) and retailer-manufacturer coordination due to the manufacturer's return policy (Padmanabhan and Png 1997, Emmons and Gilbert 1998, Tran et al. 2018). Such areas, however, are beyond the scope of this study.

The literature on the quality signaling of varied refund options remains inconclusive regarding its effectiveness. Earlier theoretical works suggest that a lenient refund policy will be used by high-quality sellers when consumers are uninformed (Moorthy and Srinivasan 1995, Shieh 1996). In contrast, McWilliams (2012) shows the equilibrium of ubiquity of refund options offered by all (high- and low-quality) sellers when the salvage value for returned products is high. Wood (2001), Lee et al. (2005), and Suwelack et al. (2011) find empirical evidence that supports the signaling roles of refund for high-quality (web) retailers, showing that sellers' offering (lenient) refunds can reduce consumers' search time while increasing pre-purchase quality perception, purchase intentions, willingness to pay, and sales. Pei et al. (2014) and Zhang et al. (2017) find similar results on purchase intentions from survey data. In contrast, Bonifield et al. (2010) find an insignificant correlation between the e-tailer quality and offering a refund for consumable products on BizRate.com. Finally, contrasting empirical results are drawn from the observational data of eBay auction, for which Li et al. (2009) show that a seller-guaranteed refund can signal high quality, but Shang et al. (2017) find evidence of little to no value for such a return and refund option (as reflected in the final auction price).

Our study contributes to this literature stream in two main ways. First, whereas the existing literature focuses on refund options for physical product retailing, our study, to the best of our knowledge, is the first for services that are inherently more vulnerable to quality uncertainty than are tangible products (Yoganarasimhan 2013). Second, in addition to examining the classic seller-endorsed refund options in a new context of services, we extend the scope of refund options to further include a platform-endorsed one (i.e., platform refund insurance) enabled by an online platform context and compare this new refund option to the classic seller-endorsed refund. From the signaling theory perspective, these two refund options mainly differ in the credibility of the endorser. Although the literature on manufacturer warranty policies documents enhanced signaling of product quality when the credibility of the commitment is high (Boulding and Kirmani 1993, Purohit and Srivastava 2001), research on refund options, however, has not explored or examined designs from the perspective of credibility. Instead, the literature focuses mainly on the effectiveness of different refund policies that vary in terms of leniency (Wood 2001, Bonifield et al. 2010), which is more specifically determined by time limits (Zhang et al. 2017), refund depth (e.g., partial- or attribute-specific versus full versus doubled refund) (Desmet 2014, Pei et al. 2014, Zhang et al. 2017), and other restrictive conditions (Bonifield et al. 2010). Our investigation of different refund policies that vary in credibility hence expands the design variation of this literature.

## 2.2. Quality Signaling in Online Marketplaces

The extant research largely considers reputation systems whose components include, for example, ratings (Ba and Pavlou 2002), textual feedback comments (Pavlou and Dimoka 2006), and duration of time to sell products (Ghose 2009). Broader quality indicators whose effectiveness has been documented include, for example, Internet auction quality and credibility indicators (Li et al. 2009), visibility-enhancing and quality-signaling discretionary auction attributes (Bockstedt and Goh 2011), diagnostic product descriptions and third-party product assurances (Dimoka et al. 2012), quality of e-images (Gregg and Walczak 2008), leniency of return policy (Zhang et al. 2017), and quality of the website (Schlosser et al. 2006, Wells et al. 2011). Very few of these, however, are situated in the context of the online service marketplace, where the information asymmetry problem is more pronounced. An exception is found in Yoganarasimhan (2013), who considers a typical service marketplace that is understood as a freelance marketplace and measures the effectiveness of a feedback-based reputation system. Moreover, the effectiveness of refund options to signal quality is rarely examined in the online marketplace, except by Li et al. (2009) and Shang et al. (2017), whose research is situated in the context of eBay auction for physical products. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the effectiveness of refund options in the context of the online service marketplace, which narrows the research gap in both ways; It also introduces a new signaling indicator, platform refund insurance, for the first time in this research stream. Moreover, this is the first study that investigates platform managers' strategy of using a valid quality indicator (platform refund insurance) to filter out low-type sellers and thus improve the platform-wide performance.

## 2.3. Consumer Welfare of Product/Service Variety and Quality Change

Petrin (2002) estimates consumer welfare and the increase in demand generated by a new product, a minivan. Nevo (2003) estimates the impact of quality change and new products on brand-level demand and consumer welfare in the ready-to-eat cereal market. Brynjolfsson et al. (2003) estimate the compensating variation from increased product variety in the Amazon bookstore to be between 7 and 10 times that from increased competition and lower prices in the market. Ghose and Han (2014) estimate that the enhanced consumer surplus gained from the availability of mobile apps is approximately \$33.6 billion annually in the United States. This study extends this literature stream by measuring consumer welfare derived from the availability of the online service marketplace. In addition, we measure welfare changes related to the policy that attempts to enforce quality improvement at the primary cost of losing (the variety of) sellers. By determining the welfare changes due to seller changes compared with that due to quality changes, we can understand the cost effectiveness of this actionable policy.

## 2.4. BLP-Style Demand Estimation

The BLP-style model shows its superiority in demand estimation by allowing heterogeneity in consumer taste and endogeneity of product characteristics with only aggregate-level data (Berry et al. 1995). It also allows for the estimation of consumer welfare, given its structure of individual-level decision making that maximizes latent utility. Researchers continue to develop the BLP model to enhance its applicability and performance by, for example, using consumer demographic information to improve precision (Petrin 2002) and applying a Mathematical Program with Equilibrium Constraints algorithm to accelerate the computation speed (Dubé et al. 2012). The model allows for wide application across various areas, such as industrial organizations, marketing, and information systems in a variety of industries, including the automobile industry (Berry et al. 1995; 1999; Petrin 2002), ready-to-eat cereal (Nevo 2000b; 2001; 2003), movies (Davis 2006), online hotel booking (Ghose et al. 2012), and mobile app use (Ghose and Han 2014). $^{1}$ Our paper extends the applicability of the BLP model to the online service marketplace, which is characterized by high levels of information asymmetry and competitiveness. We are also, to the best of our knowledge, the first to use BLP to quantify the demand elasticity of refund options, considering a salient heterogeneity of such elasticity across consumers (Anderson et al. 2009) and the near-equilibrium impact of a quality-indicator-based seller screening policy.

## 3. Context, Data, and Descriptive Findings

## 3.1. Research Context

Our data are from one of the world's largest online marketplaces based in China. The marketplace is famous for traditional physical products but has been expanding business into services recently, with the addition of sites for travel agents, electronic device repairs, and prepaid phone recharging. We focus on online prepaid phone recharging because this service involves a fixed amount of recharging per transaction (e.g., 20, 50, 100 RMB) and is known for varied service quality. We further focus on the submarket of a 50-RMB recharging value. With the fixed value of prepaid cards, horizontal differentiation among sellers is minimized, and vertical service quality becomes the major determinant of the differentiated price and demand. This avoids the inflation of the error term that captures unobserved horizontal differentiation, which greatly simplifies the model's challenges and provides us with a good opportunity to investigate the impact of the refund option on demand through the mechanism of quality signaling rather than “taste and fit” (Anderson et al. 2009). Given that one seller is equivalent to the option of services in the consumer's choice set, we use seller and service interchangeably.

The focal submarketplace (which we later refer to as the marketplace) has a sufficient size and variety of seller characteristics for us to identify empirically the effectiveness of the refund options. Media reports find that prepaid phone cards occupy more than 50% of the market share of the telecommunications market in China and constitute an enormous demand from consumers who seek to recharge their prepaid phones (Sohu IT 2014). Overall, the marketplace studied herein accounts for the largest market share of the online recharging prepaid phone business. As seen from the supply side, the marketplace is fragmented. On any given day, there are approximately 18,343 active sellers available on the platform, with each mega-seller's occupying as large as 5% of the total marketplace, which is approximately 70,000 times that of a 25% quantile small seller. Providing effective quality signals would alleviate information asymmetry in the online market, which is due to the intangibility, variability, and unreturnable nature of services. The existence of heterogeneous seller size indicates the effectiveness of quality signals, which helps consumers avoid purchasing a “lemon” and leaves low-quality sellers behind. We expect the huge variance in demand to be partially explained by a seller's state of refund options, as they might serve as one of the quality signals.

The marketplace offers two refund options, that is, platform refund insurance and a seller-guaranteed refund, as part of an integrated platform certifications system in which sellers can enroll. There are 11 certifications in our focal marketplace, and each corresponds to one particular feature certified by the platform. Unlike other seller features, platform certifications endow the seller with corresponding standardized badges to display on the platform's search results page, highlighting the information that sellers would like to present to consumers. They are also part of the filtered search, whereby consumers can verify one or many certifications to narrow the scope of their search, and the ranking index, which ranks sellers with certain certifications (and with higher popularity and better reputation) higher. Therefore, those certifications and both refund options should be easily perceived by consumers. Sellers can enroll in or unenroll from any of the 11 certifications on a monthly, semiannual, or annual basis with a fee, depending on the choice of certifications, the length of time, the size, and historical performance of the seller. On enrollment, sellers will be monitored by the platform to ensure that they do not violate the requirements of their certifications.

In terms of certifications, sellers can enroll in none, one, or both types of refund options; that is, platform refund insurance and/or the seller-guaranteed refund. $^{2}$ With either refund option, sellers are required to issue a refund upon a consumer's request under the condition of nondelivered or unsatisfactorily delivered. The refund processes vary, however, between these two options. Requests under the platform's refund insurance are evaluated by the platform's specialists, and, if legitimate, they will issue a reimbursement from the refund insurance that is managed by the platform. This process is made possible because sellers prepay the insurance premium to the platform, which typically varies between 5 and 430, depending on the coverage amount and the policy length. A seller who has more refund requests in the past is mandated to have a higher coverage amount. In contrast, requests for a seller-guaranteed refund are evaluated by sellers, following their self-defined policies. In these cases, the refund is issued directly by the sellers if they agree and the cost of enrolling in the certification is much lower. Although the refund option is monitored by the platform to ensure compliance with general platform-issued guidelines, sellers can set more restrictive return policies by adding additional constraints. When a violation of the guidelines is detected, the platform punishes the sellers by canceling their certifications or deactivating their accounts temporarily or permanently. The platform, however, cannot force the sellers to issue a refund directly, nor can the platform do so on behalf of the sellers. Therefore, the refund cost of offering a seller-guaranteed refund could be lower than offering platform refund insurance. It is worth noting that an even more risky transaction could occur if the seller chooses not to enroll in either refund option. Any refund disputes, under such circumstances, would be subject to the seller, and the platform would not intervene.

## 3.2. Having Platform Refund Insurance or Being Cast Out Policy

Our focal platform is determined to enhance purchase protection for consumers and reduce the hassle of seeking a refund. The platform attempts to unify the refund policies and centrally manage all refunds, following a practice that resembles that of Amazon Prime. In doing so, the platform aims to establish a platform-wide platform refund insurance through the policy, which initiates at the beginning of Week 10 in our observation. Platform refund insurance incurs a mandatory insurance premium for all sellers. The overall percentage of insured sellers before the policy change was low ( $\sim$ 13%). The platform negotiated with all the sellers in our category on a case-by-case basis. Specifically, due to limited capacity, the platform can negotiate with approximately 5,000 sellers ( $\sim$ 25% of all sellers) per week and thus finish all negotiations in four consecutive weeks (i.e., Weeks 10 to 13 in our observation). Sellers who originally had agreed to switch to platform refund insurance could continue their business, whereas those who refused to join would be “cast out” by the end of the week of the negotiation. In the latter case, their account would be permanently deactivated, or their services would be unlisted from the search results. According to the plan, nearly all sellers were expected to be insured with a platform refund policy by the end of Week 13. Notably, because the policy was launched in the form of a case-by-case negotiation, no public announcement was made regarding the policy. Hence, neither the sellers nor consumers were informed or could anticipate the policy before the starting point (i.e., Week 10). In addition, the policy change was not reversed since being launched: a “cast out” seller can only “return” with a new account and is thus considered a new seller by the platform, consumers, and us.

The descriptive findings provide us with insight into the demand system and the impact of the policy, but they do not adequately quantify how and to what extent platform refund insurance and seller-guaranteed refunds affect demand. The findings also fail to provide accurate estimates through which to understand consumer welfare and the effect of the policy. As there is a significant number of sellers, and sellers have a very large action space, the changes in demand might not be due solely to the change in one action, but rather, to a combination of many actions, including those by other sellers. Without controlling other actions and state of certification variables, we are unable to determine the exact effects of even one possible action if we observe a correlation. In addition, we note some demand changes (e.g., the sharp increase starting at Week 29) that neither align with the number of sellers nor conform to seller characteristics (i.e., platform refund insurance and price) in Figures 1 and 3. In fact, we cannot find any known event(s) or visible characteristics change(s) to explain these changes, which suggest the existence of unobservable event(s) or demand shock(s) that can confound and, thus, mislead the causal impact based on the descriptive findings. $^{4}$ To this end, we build demand estimation models that are compatible with nonstationary demand and can isolate the time-varying unobserved demand shocks to recover the data-generation process, which thereby provide accurate estimates that help us to understand the policy change by counterfactual analysis.

## 4. Model

We present a model for the data generation process for the market share of each seller. Specifically, we follow McFadden (1973) and, more specifically, Berry et al. (1995) to account for unobserved consumer heterogeneity and potential endogeneity with a multinomial logistic model. We refer readers to Nevo (2000a) for a verified discussion of the methodological advantages of this model.

A market is defined as the entire 50-RMB recharging service market at a week (m), which consists of not only all sellers in the focal marketplace but also those outside (i.e., outside goods). We first consider each seller $j = 1, 2, \cdots, J^{m}$ in the marketplace in market $m = 1, 2, \cdots, M$ . A transaction is defined as a one-time service of recharging a prepaid phone through a seller. Active consumers $i = 1, 2, \cdots, I$ of the market cannot have perfect information about the utility value of the service; therefore, they rely on information provided by the market to infer expected utility and choose a seller. The conditional indirect expected utility of consumers at market m from service provided by j (in the marketplace) is specified as

![](/api/attachments/NNT3JZGY/fulltext/images/d60b997661e288c8a4e539291bddb995d886ac253b95433d13e9a6f10553b03c.jpg)

$$
u _ {i j m} = \beta_ {i} X _ {j m} + \xi_ {j m} + \varepsilon_ {i j},\tag{1}
$$

where $X_{jm}$ is a vector that captures the multidimensional, consumer-observable characteristics of seller j. We do not model one particular individual consumer i. The variable $\beta_{i}$ is drawn from the distribution of the taste of the entire consumer population, which, following BLP, is modeled as a multivariate normal distribution. Specifically,

$$
\beta_ {i} \sim \beta + \sum v _ {i},\tag{2}
$$

$$
v _ {i} \sim M V N (0, I),\tag{3}
$$

where $\beta$ is a $20 \times 1$ vector of mean parameters that captures the mean taste of the consumer population to different characteristics, whereas $\Sigma v_{i}$ captures the taste heterogeneity within the consumer population. Like BLP, $v_{i}$ has a mean of zero and standard diagonal variance; $\Sigma$ rescales the variance of taste coefficients and thus captures the levels of taste heterogeneity toward characteristics. We model $\Sigma$ as a diagonal matrix for tractability.

The focal variables in $X_{jm}$ are platform refund insurance and seller-guaranteed refund. We expect both to increase the expected utility because they signal the quality of service. Specifically, the use of a seller-guaranteed refund might incur the signaling cost as the future potential refund, which is higher for low-quality sellers due to a higher refund rate (Moorthy and Srinivasan 1995, Shieh 1996). Similarly, the signaling cost of platform refund insurance for low-quality sellers is higher, as the prepaid insurance premium directly reflects the seller's past refund rates and other factors that affect credit scores. Therefore, high-quality sellers are more likely to use these two refund options due to their low cost. Consumers should expect higher utility of sellers with one or both certifications. In addition, due to the information advantage of the platform and the nature of third-party product guarantees (Dean and Biswas 2001, Dimoka et al. 2012), platform refund insurance should have higher credibility in terms of commitments and higher signaling cost for low-quality sellers than does a seller-guaranteed refund. Specifically, with platform refund insurance, the relationship between the seller and the refund endorser is more distant, and the signaling cost (shown in insurance premiums) is determined by the platform's information about the seller, which might be more comprehensive than that of the individual consumers and is paid in advance. The credibility of the refund commitment is unlikely to be contaminated by self-interested sellers with low cost (Dellarocas 2003). Conversely, sellers that offer a seller-guaranteed refund could more easily lower their signaling cost and thus destroy their credibility by, for example, setting more restrictive refund policies or absconding with the promised future refund. Analogous to the positive correlation between the credibility of the commitment and quality signal strength, seen in the manufacturer warranty literature (Boulding and Kirmani 1993, Purohit and Srivastava 2001) and drawn from the signaling cost explanation (Moorthy and Srinivasan 1995, Shieh 1996), we expect a greater average increase in the expected utility of a platform refund than that of a seller-guaranteed refund. Finally, we expect both refund options to have moderate taste heterogeneity because consumers have varied risk preferences/tolerances, and most consumers would consider refund options to be beneficial.

We also include as much consumer-observable and market-varying information in $X_{jm}$ as possible to avoid the potential issue of omitted variables, making the model a better reflection of the data generation process. Specifically, we include all remaining platform certifications as follows: (a) certification for accepting coupons, (b) certification for platform recommended limited-time offer, (c) certification for a threshold discount, (d) certification for giving a sample gift, (e) certification for a limited-time offer, (f) certification for a platform's choice, (g) certification for accepting credit cards, and (h) certification for verified pictures by customer. The listed variables are important as controls because sellers enroll in them, together with platform refund insurance or seller-guaranteed refunds, as bundles. Sellers who decide to enroll in platform refund insurance might simultaneously modify the enrollment for other certifications. Therefore, correlations among different certifications exist. Moreover, because the policy change that requires all sellers to have platform refund insurance might simultaneously trigger the alteration of their other certifications in the bundles, eventually including all certifications, later on, this could enable us to isolate the impact of the changes in other certifications on demand.

The other consumer-observable and market-varying variables to control for include all numerically measurable variables that are relevant to a seller's feedback-based reputation, as follows: (i) reported delivery time, (j) reported disputes, (k) average rating, (l) volume of ratings, (m) reported refund claims, (n) number of positive evaluations, and (o) number of negative evaluations. In addition, we include market-varying seller characteristics, including (p) within-seller variety, (q) duration of membership on the platform, and (r) average price of the service.

Consumers might capture additional seller information that is unobserved by econometricians, for example, offline reputation. We, therefore, denote $\xi_{jm}$ as information about seller j in market m observed by consumers but not by us. This value is market- and seller-specific and is common to the consumer population. Consumers prefer a service with higher $\xi_{jm}$ because it provides more expected utility. Leveraging the panel structure of our data, we further add the seller fixed effect, denoted as $\xi_{j}$ , to control for the potential unobserved persistent seller characteristics, following Nevo (2000a and 2001), $^{5}$ as

$$
\xi_ {j m} = \xi_ {j} + \Delta \xi_ {j m},\tag{4}
$$

where $\Delta\xi_{jm}$ is a serially uncorrelated component of the unobserved characteristic of seller j in market m.

Finally, consumers also have persistent idiosyncratic tastes for the service. We represent them as $\varepsilon_{ij}$ and assume them to be traditional independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) type I extreme value draws across sellers and consumers. After rearranging the utility function in a more hierarchical manner and defining seller level mean utility as $\delta_{jm} = \beta X_{jm} + \xi_j + \Delta \xi_{jm}$ , we have a suppressed format of utility:

$$
u _ {i j m} = \delta_ {j m} + \sum v _ {i} X _ {j m} + \varepsilon_ {i j}.\tag{5}
$$

To accomplish the specification of the market-wide demand, we next specify the utility of outside goods (i.e., sellers outside the focal marketplace). Specifically, consumers might decide not to purchase the service from the focal online marketplace; instead, they might purchase a similar service from another marketplace. We model the conditional indirect utility from the outside goods as

$$
u _ {i 0 m} = c _ {0 m} + \varepsilon_ {i 0},\tag{6}
$$

where $c_{0m}$ captures market(week)-specific mean utility of outside goods, and $\varepsilon_{i0}$ is the idiosyncratic tastes that reflect heterogeneity in the preference to choose outside goods that follows a type I extreme value distribution. We note three points regarding the importance of modeling the outside goods. First, it allows postpolicy-change separating equilibrium; even when all sellers on the marketplace have platform refund insurance, the certification can still serve as a valid quality signal sent by sellers because outside sellers may not all have it. $^{6}$ Second, it relaxes the assumption of constant marketplace-wide demand across time. The marketplace can cannibalize or lose to the outside demand, which allows not only the more flexible fitting of the model to the observed demand, but also more realistic counterfactual simulations. Third, $c_{0m}$ essentially serves as time fixed effects that isolate time-specific unexplained demand shocks (e.g., seasonality, and time trend), which empowers the model to fit in nonstationary demand.

Considering the property of type I extreme value distribution, the market share of seller j in market m conditional on one draw of $v_{i}$ from its distribution is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname * {P r} _ {m} (j | v _ {i}) \\ = \frac {\exp (\delta_ {j m} + \sum v _ {i} X _ {j m})}{\exp (c _ {0} + v _ {i 0} \sigma_ {0}) + \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {J ^ {m}} \exp (\delta_ {s m} + \sum v _ {i} X _ {s m})}. \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

The expected market share for product j is computed by further unconditioning on $v_{i}$ . We use $P(v_{i})$ to denote the distribution function of $MVN(0,I)$ . By taking integration over $v_{i}$ , the expected market share of seller j in market m is

$$
s _ {j m} (X. _ {m}, \delta . _ {m}; \beta , \Sigma) = \int \operatorname * {P r} _ {m} (j | v _ {i}) d (P (v _ {i})).\tag{8}
$$

## 5. Estimation

## 5.1. Identification and Estimation

Our identification strategy generally follows Berry et al. (1995) and is comprised of two moment conditions. The first one equates the expected market share to the observed market share $S_{jm}$ from our data, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} s _ {j m} \big (X. _ {m}, \delta . _ {m}; \beta , \Sigma \big) - S _ {j m} = 0 \text {for} j = 1, 2, \dots , J \text {and} \\ m = 1, 2, \dots , M. \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

This condition guarantees a mapping between $\Sigma$ and $\delta_{jm}$ . Berry shows the existence and uniqueness of $\Sigma$ and $\delta_{jm}$ that guarantee this moment under mild regularity conditions on the distribution of consumer tastes. The second-moment condition concerns market-level disturbance. It requires an unobserved and serially uncorrelated market-level disturbance $\Delta\xi_{jm}$ to be uncorrelated with observed exogenous variables and instrumental variables (IVs) denoted as Z and shown as

$$
E (\Delta \xi_ {j m} | Z) = 0 \text { for } j = 1, 2, \dots , J \text { and } m = 1, 2, \dots , M.\tag{10}
$$

Given $\delta_{jm}$ from the first-moment condition, the second-moment condition identifies $\beta$ as coefficients in regressing $\delta_{jm}$ on $X_{jm}$ instrumented by Z. With both conditions combined, we would have a unique optimal extremum point that identifies both $\Sigma$ and $\beta$ . $^{8}$

Similar to many empirical works on the online marketplace, our model faces the critical challenge of endogeneity. Endogeneity biases arise when sellers are allowed to choose or adjust product characteristics, which are correlated with omitted variables (i.e., sellers' unobserved characteristics). The traditional demand estimation literature (Berry et al. 1995, Petrin 2002) assumes price to be the only adjustable strategic action by sellers in the short run, whereas it considers other characteristics as exogenous. Nevertheless, in the case of the online service marketplace, a much larger action space is adjusted on a high-frequency basis, which is unable to instrument itself. In fact, only past consumer-generated reputation and sellers' duration of membership are exempt from the simultaneity issue, as new consumers would have access to only the reputation system updated to time m-1, which is earlier than the arrival of unobserved demand shock at m. Sellers can adjust their platform certification characteristics simply by enrolling in or unenrolling from a corresponding service from the platform manager, implying that platform certification characteristics could be the outcomes of strategic actions, given market and product demand shock $\Delta \xi_{jm}$ . A similar argument could be applied to the price and variety of services, which are classic assumptions for a supply-and-demand system.

To address the endogeneity issue, we follow Nevo (2001), who builds on the IV approach in Berry et al. (1995) with a further inclusion of time and seller fixed effects (i.e., $c_{0m}$ and $\xi_{j}$ , respectively). As such, the identifying variations for the effects of refund options are essentially the seller-by-time variation in demand and IVs. $^{9}$ We note that the IVs for platform refund insurance contain seller-by-time variation even after the policy change because newcomer sellers may initially not be insured with the platform refund and opt in later. Table A1 in the online supplement provides additional details.

In the estimation, we recover the point estimates with the contraction mapping and generalized method of moments approach proposed by Berry et al. (1995), as they better suit our data structure. For the standard errors, we use the bootstrap method, following Efron and Tibshirani (1986), instead of the standard GMMstyle standard errors because the computation of bootstrap standard errors can avoid computing the Jacobian of the moment conditions, which is extremely burdensome when the number of sellers is large like ours. The market share $S_{jm}$ is calculated as the ratio of j's sales to the total sales at market wide. $^{10}$ To avoid zero demand, we adopt the Laplace transformation-like demand correction proposed by Gandhi et al. (2022). Additional technical details and discussion of the choice of estimation approach are presented in Table A2 in the online supplement.

## 5.2. Instrumental Variables

We include two sets of IVs in Z. First, we follow BLP and Berry (1994) to apply measures of how a product characteristic differs from the market average characteristics (i.e., BLP-style IVs), shown in the following:

$$
z _ {. j m} = \frac {1}{J - 1} \sum_ {j ^ {\prime} \neq j} x _ {. j ^ {\prime} m}.\tag{11}
$$

The idea is to measure the isolation in product space, which reflects the competition faced by each seller and is hence correlated with its own endogenous characteristics. Our model focuses on a highly fragmented market. This focus implies that each seller's unobserved characteristics have little potential to affect or reshape market average characteristics. In other words, even though seller-specific unobserved characteristics are likely to be correlated with seller-specific observed characteristics or those of a competing seller, they are unlikely to be correlated with market average characteristics. This justifies the validity of using a BLP-style instrument in our model (Berry and Haile 2014; 2016; Berry et al. 1995; Nevo 2000a). Notably, one common critic of the BLP-style IV is its limited variation across markets. Our context, however, may largely avoid this issue because the policy change may trigger large enough variation in market average characteristics across markets. Table A1 in the online supplement contains further discussion.

Second, we follow the suggestions in Berry et al. (1995) and Berry and Haile (2014; 2016) on further including Villas-Boas-Winer-style IVs (Villas-Boas and Winer 1999), which uses lagged characteristics, to address the weak instrument problem. Recall that we define each market as a market at a certain time. The Villas-Boas-Winer-style IVs in our example are, indeed, a special form of Hausman-style IVs (Hausman 1996). This IV leverages the serial correlation of characteristics due to the continuity of strategic behavior and similar cost structure. A demand shock in time $t$ might result in a strategic adjustment of characteristics in time $t$ but not in time $t - 1$ due to the reversed order. The validity of this IV is documented in earlier dynamic panel data literature (Arellano and Bond 1991, Blundell and Bond

1998) and is analogous to Sweeting (2013). Specifically, it relies on the condition of no persistence (i.e., serial correlation) in the error terms. Our model should satisfy this condition because the inclusion of seller fixed effects leaves $\Delta\xi_{jm}$ serially uncorrelated; $\Delta\xi_{jm}$ should thus be uncorrelated with lagged characteristics. Notably, following Arellano and Bond (1991), we include lagged cumulative sales in this set of IVs rather than adding this as a control variable. $^{11}$

We further follow Arellano and Bond (1991) to test the validity condition for Villas-Boas-Winer-style IVs by conducting an AR(1) test on the residuals from the estimated model. The autoregressive coefficient is estimated to be insignificantly different from zero (estimated to 0.12 with p = 0.51), by which we cannot reject the null hypothesis of serial uncorrelated residuals, suggesting that the validity condition is satisfied. Finally, we report that the F statistic of a weak identification test on the final instruments is 67.91, which is much greater than the recommended threshold of 10 and suggests that our instruments are not weak for the endogenous variables.

## 6.1. Results of Logit Aggregate Demand Model (Without Random Coefficients)

We first estimate a logit (aggregate) demand model that essentially suppresses the random coefficients from the BLP to serve as reduced-form evidence and for robustness check purposes. For coherence, we keep the remaining specifications (i.e., seller and time fixed effects, control variables, and IVs) identical to the BLP specification. Berry (1994) shows that the logit demand model reduces to a log-normal model that regresses the logarithm-transformed demand on the considered independent variables. Considering the IVs, a two-stage least-squares (2SLS) estimator is then used. The resulting Hansen-J statistics is 37.09, which cannot reject the null hypothesis of uncorrelation between the IVs and the regression residuals ( $p = 0.1745$ ) and supports the validity of our IVs. We further note that this model has an advantage, as it allows interpretable interaction effects by adding interaction terms, whereas nonlinear models, such as the BLP, do not. $^{12}$ In our context, sellers with different feedback-based reputations may have varied preferences for refund options, which might lead to interaction effects between the feedback-based reputation variables and refund options. Therefore, in addition to Model 1 with main effects only, we estimate Model 2 that adds interaction effects between our focal variables and the variables in the feedback-based reputation. The estimation results are shown in Table 3.

The positive and significant coefficients for both platform refund insurance and seller-guaranteed refunds in both Models 1 and 2 suggest that having these two refund certifications increases the demand by, on average, 26% (p < 0.001) and 2% (p < 0.001), respectively. $^{13}$ This result validates our hypothetical expectation that both refund options serve as viable quality signals to address information asymmetry and that platform refund insurance has a stronger signaling effect between the two. The coefficients of interaction terms further show that the reputation of a seller moderates these two signaling effects. Both refund certifications are more effective for sellers with a higher number of accumulated disputes, whereas platform refund insurance is more effective for sellers with fewer reviews. At the same time, seller-guaranteed refunds are more effective for sellers with greater refund requests and negative postpurchase evaluations. These findings suggest that sellers with worse feedback-based reputations benefit more from these two refund certifications. We also find that platform refund insurance is more effective for sellers who have more positive postpurchase evaluations. This may be because the number of positive postpurchase evaluations is highly correlated with the total number of transactions and, according to the ranking algorithm of the platform, sellers with more transactions rank higher. Because the higher-ranked seller's platform refund insurance may be perceived by more consumers, it may increase the seller's demand more effectively. This may dominate the expected negative interaction effect, which is observed in other feedback-based reputation variables and explains why early adopters of platform refund insurance sell more. ## 6.2. BLP Parameter Estimates

The estimation results for the BLP model are reported in Table 3 (Model 3). In addition, we estimate a BLP model with interaction terms, following the specification of Model 2, referred to as Model 4, for a robustness check and clarify that this model is difficult to interpret because it has interaction terms with a logit transformation. Therefore, we interpret mainly the estimation results of Model 3. The good properties of a random coefficient logit model that allows distinct sellers to have different marginal effects for a specific variable. Confusion results, however, when the sign of the mean parameters is inconsistent with the sign of some sellers' marginal effect. $^{14}$ To avoid any potentially misleading results, we numerically calculate the marginal effect of our focal variable in addition to the estimates of the mean and variability parameters. Because the marginal effect of each characteristic is seller and market specific, we calculate the marginal effect of several representative sellers, including a mega-seller who has a market share of more than 5%, a median seller, and a 25% quantile small seller in market m = 1 (we refer to all as representative sellers). Because our focal variables are dummy variables, the marginal effects are defined as doing a what-if analysis to show the differences between the effect of a data-observed choice and that of an alternative choice.

The results reconsolidate the effectiveness of platform refund insurance and seller-guaranteed refunds as quality signals. The mean coefficient, representing the average added expected utility of platform refund insurance, is estimated as 0.71, and the variability coefficient, representing the variance of consumers' risk preference, is estimated as 0.11. This suggests a homogeneous and positive effect of platform refund insurance on the demand and further indicates that platform refund insurance increases the expected utility of the majority of consumers. Quantitatively, having platform refund insurance results in 7.71%, 29.45%, and 31.47% (p < 0.001) additional demand for representative sellers, respectively. The mean coefficient of the seller-guaranteed refund also is estimated positively but to a lesser extent compared with that of platform refund insurance. The variability coefficient of a seller-guaranteed refund is close to that of platform refund insurance. This certification shows the positive effect on demand by 0.17%, 2.01%, and 4.65% (p < 0.001) for representative sellers, respectively. This finding is consistent with our expectation, as the signaling effects of this certification are shown to be weaker than that of platform refund insurance due to its potential lower credibility in the commitment. We further note that these marginal effects differ largely from their counterparts that are estimated from the logit demand model without random coefficients (i.e., 26% (p < 0.001) of platform refund insurance and 2% (p < 0.001) of seller-guaranteed refund for all three representative sellers) and are not restricted to be the same across sellers by the IIA property, which suggests a better fit of the random coefficient BLP in our analysis.

The signaling effects of both refund options, according to Moorthy and Srinivasan (1995) and Shieh (1996), means that sellers with both refund options should have lower signaling cost that refers to the cost of a future refund and can be measured by refund rates (i.e., the ratio of number of refunds to sales). Furthermore, the higher signaling effect of platform refund insurance suggests even lower refund rates for sellers with platform refund insurance. To support this claim, we further separately regress the refund rates, as well as dispute rates for stronger robustness, on each refund option. The results show that sellers with platform refund insurance, on average, have 12% (p < 0.001) lower refund rates and 9% (p < 0.001) lower disputes rates than do those without (i.e., low-type sellers), whereas sellers with seller-guaranteed refunds, on average, have only 4.2% fewer refund rates (p < 0.01) and 3.7% (p < 0.001) lower dispute rates than do those without. These results fully support the signal theory explanation and further suggest the effectiveness of the platform's use of the policy to enforce every seller's adoption of platform refund insurance—The associated saliently high costs may eliminate low-type sellers, which improves the average quality of the platform.

Although it is beyond the focus of this work, we also find that most coefficients for the control variables are significant and coincide with the findings in the literature. The marginal effects of feedback-based reputation variables are consistent with the sign of the mean parameters, given that the variabilities are mostly moderate to small, compared with the mean parameter, and are similar to the reduced-form results. If all else is equal, consumers are more inclined to buy from sellers with faster service, higher-than-average ratings, a larger rating volume, fewer refund claims, and more positive postpurchase evaluations. This suggests the effectiveness of using feedback-based reputation to signal service quality, which is consistent with the findings of Yoganarasimhan (2013). The other platform certifications, in contrast, do not show a uniform effect on demand. Each certification's variability coefficient estimate is larger than its mean coefficient estimate, implying a heterogeneous effect on demand and varied quality signaling effects across individuals. One possible explanation is that a large enough untargeted group (e.g., consumers without a coupon, those who are indifferent to sample gifts, those who do not use a credit card) might hold an opposite preference than the targeted group because they do not benefit from the certifications and would absorb the seller's enrollment cost. The sign of the mean parameters might be inconsistent with those of marginal effects when variability is large. We calculate marginal effects that show that limited-time offers, platform's choice, platform-recommended limited-time offers, accepting credit cards, and providing customer-verified pictures increase seller demand, whereas accepting coupons and threshold discounts decreases it. Finally, we find the expected effects of other seller-specific characteristics. The marginal effects and the reduced form analysis imply consumers' preferences for sellers with a greater variety, a longer transactional history, and lower prices. The variability parameter of within-seller variety is large, indicating heterogeneous tastes for a more specialized seller or wholesaler.

## 6.3. Robustness Checks

We conducted a few robustness checks for the BLP estimates. First, our model might be sensitive to the market unit due to our use of market-level aggregate data to approximate what individuals have observed. To test whether the market unit will affect our estimation, we estimate our model by setting each market to a biweekly demand and a four-weekly one, separately. $^{15}$ Second, we note the sharp increase in demand around Week 29 that cannot be explained by the presence and characteristic changes of sellers or any other known events. Despite time-fixed effects as able to sufficiently control for unobserved time-specific demand shocks in theory, we further exclude the data of the concerning time periods (i.e., Weeks 29 to 35) for estimation as a reexamination. Third, despite our focus on one price category as a means to let the vertical differentiation fully reflect the variation in demand, the exclusion of the competition from other price categories may trigger market(time)-specific confounding bias. Hence, we include other price categories (i.e., 20 and 100 RMB) as individual services for estimation. Notably, such omitted competition is time-specific and should theoretically be isolated by the time-fixed effects, as in the second concern. Fourth, we additionally estimate the main specification with an adjustment for the effect of the view of sellers that also approximates the rank of sellers in the menu page display. $^{16}$ Their estimation results, reported in Tables A1 to A4 in the online supplement, are all qualitatively consistent with our main results in regard to the signs and magnitudes of the two refund options' effects. We also note some inconsistency with our main results for control variables. These point estimates, however, are associated with inflated standard errors due to the decreased data size, potential collinearity, and measurement errors.

## 6.4. Welfare Analysis

Our model provides a closed-form solution for consumer surplus. Therefore, we calculate the consumer surplus across different time periods to examine how a compound market structure change affects consumers. Following McFadden (1981), we calculate the average individual-level consumer surplus in a random coefficient logit model:

$$
C W _ {m} = \int \frac {\ln \left(\sum_ {j = 0} ^ {J _ {m}} \exp (u _ {i j m})\right)}{- \beta_ {2 0 i}} d (P (v _ {i})),\tag{12}
$$

where $u_{ijm}$ is the individual-specific indirect utility function shown in Equations (5) and (6), $J_{m}$ is the number of sellers available in market m, and $\beta_{20i}$ is the coefficient for price, which is equal to mean parameter $\beta_{20}$ plus the deviations from the mean parameter $\Sigma_{20}\upsilon_{i}$ . By dividing indirect utility by marginal effects of income $\beta_{20i}$ , $CW_{m}$ estimates the consumer welfare in a monetary unit. Given an initial market 0 and any given market m that reflects the event change, $CW_{0}-CW_{m}$ can be interpreted as compensating variation, and $CW_{m}-CW_{0}$ can be interpreted as equivalent variation (Nevo 2000b, Petrin 2002). $^{17}$ Therefore, comparing $CW_{m}$ allows us to understand whether consumers are better off, given a specific market structure.

The initially decreasing pattern of consumer surplus when sellers are expelled after Week 9 is consistent with the literature that shows increased consumer welfare with higher levels of product variety (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Ghose et al. 2006). It is worth noting, however, that the decrease in consumer welfare (\~1/3) is much smaller than that observed in the number of sellers (\~2/3), which indicates that most expelled sellers deliver lower-than-average consumer welfare and, thus, are likely to be low-quality ones. Nevertheless, we further observe that, as the market structure moves to a new equilibrium after the exogenous shock and the seller number rebounds, consumer surplus recovers to a much greater degree than does the number of sellers, which suggests that most newcomer sellers who arrive after the policy shock are high-quality ones.

It is worth noting that the welfare calculation involves multiple market structure factors, such as seasonality, that are intertwined, so the inferences about the policy may not be rigorous. For example, the increasing welfare after the policy shock might be driven by seasonality rather than the changes of sellers, which is a combination of low-type seller exits and high-type seller entries. The welfare change also might be due to the strategically adjusted characteristics (other than platform refund insurance) of the sellers who had been there throughout (e.g., increased price, as we have shown in the preliminary analysis). We use a simulation approach to isolate the impact of several market structure factors to better understand the underlying mechanism and, thus, evaluate the effectiveness of the policy in the next section.

## 7. Policy Analysis

## 7.1. Counterfactual Simulation

To evaluate more comprehensively the design of the policy and address the issue in the welfare analysis, we conduct a finer-grained decomposition of the compound demand and welfare change to control for irrelevant factors as well as to isolate two perspectives regarding the policy's impact. Doing so will provide a meaningful understanding of the policy's underlying mechanism. First, we want to isolate the impact of the changes of sellers after the policy, which is a combination of the expelled sellers' exits and the entry of new sellers (i.e., newcomer sellers) after the policy shock. This is important to understand because it directly reflects the effectiveness of using the policy to filter out low-type sellers. Although one should expect low-type sellers to leave and high-type sellers to remain or enter, particularly because platform refund insurance is a valid quality signal, it is worth noting that the platform refund insurance premium also positively relates to seller scale, and thus, its signaling cost for high-type sellers is not necessarily low. This indicates that the policy could potentially cause the platform to lose some high-type sellers. We also observe that the number of sellers after the policy shock never returns to its original state. If the average quality of newcomer sellers is not significantly better than that of the expelled sellers, the net loss of sellers might indicate a net loss of demand and consumer welfare.

Second, we want to isolate the impact of the secondary characteristic changes of the sellers who remain on the platform before and after the policy change (i.e., remaining sellers). It is difficult to extrapolate these two isolations through analytical methods that demonstrate the theoretically optimal actions, assuming that all sellers are profit-maximizers, as this contradicts our observation: Sellers are not rational enough to behave optimally immediately after the policy change. Furthermore, it is theoretically impossible to use this approach to solve the case with a high dimension of action space (e.g., exiting, enrolling in certifications, pricing) and a larger number of players. Hence, we take a partial-equilibrium approach, based on counterfactual simulation, shown in Table 4. We acknowledge that this approach is subject to certain assumptions. First, the pre- and postpolicy equilibrium markets are assumed to be near equilibrium before and after the policy change. To rationalize this assumption, the two tails of the observed market (Markets 1 and 48) are used, respectively, as, unlike with most markets that are likely to lose equilibrium due to their proximity to the exogenous shock, they are closer to equilibrium before and after the policy change. Second, all the changes of sellers and their characteristics before and after the policy shock are assumed to be due to the policy change, with the exception of time-specific demand shocks. This assumption is mild because, to the best of our knowledge, no other significant policy change or demand shock related to sellers' presence or characteristics was observed during our data window. Third, the assumptions in the BLP model are embedded because the simulations are based on the BLP model and its properties.

The difference between Markets (b) and (a) is 3.96% additional demand for the platform and a 2.48% gain (\$0.02) of consumer surplus, suggesting a positive impact of the changes of sellers due to the policy. The positive demand and welfare gains, along with the reduced number of sellers after the policy change, indicate that the average quality of newcomer sellers is significantly better than that of the expelled sellers. $^{19}$ This suggests that the policy is effective in filtering out low-quality sellers. This effectiveness, according to Akerlof (1970), also provides a coherent validation for leveraging platform refund insurance as a quality signal.

The difference between Markets (c) and (b), however, is a 0.49% loss of market share and a 0.33% loss of consumer welfare (-\$0.002 if Market (b) is set as the initial policy), suggesting that the utility derived from the remaining sellers deteriorates slightly after the policy change. Such a deterioration might be caused by the remaining sellers who mark up prices or change other certifications or characteristics, which reveals the side cost of the policy design from the perspective of platform managers. The magnitude of the cost, however, is largely dominated by the gain from the changes of sellers, suggesting that the policy design eventually adds value for the platform. More generally, our policy analysis indicates the feasibility and effectiveness of retaining or expelling sellers based on their states of valid quality signals for the purpose of platform-wide quality escalation.

## 7.2. Supply-Side Analysis

The previous simulation analysis is based mainly on the demand model and focuses on pre- and postpolicy (near) equilibrium. We complement it by drawing direct empirical evidence on the supply-(seller-)side strategic response to the policy change and by focusing on the immediate impacts at or near the policy shock. Our focal policy shock was earlier planned and exogenously imposed by the platform. During that time, it was the only market-wide policy that may impose an immediate interruption on sellers' responses, whereas other covariates tend to change mildly and continuously, and so do their effects on sellers' responses. These resemble a quasi-experimental SITS design that allows attributing the discrete changes of sellers and characteristic changes of the remaining sellers at the policy time as the causal responses of sellers to the policy. $^{20}$ Specifically, we specify two parametric equations, one at the market level and the other at the individual seller level, shown as

$$
X _ {m} = a _ {0} + a _ {1} P _ {m} + a _ {2} f (T _ {m}) + a _ {3} P _ {m} f (T _ {m}) + e _ {m},\tag{13}
$$

$$
X _ {j m} = \gamma_ {j 0} + \gamma_ {1} P _ {m} + \gamma_ {2} f (T _ {m}) + \gamma_ {3} P _ {m} f (T _ {m}) + e _ {j m},\tag{14}
$$

where $X_{m}$ refers to a market-level aggregate characteristic (e.g., the total number of sellers or the average of one characteristic across sellers) in market m, and $X_{jm}$ refers to one characteristic of seller j in market m. $P_{m}$ is an indicator variable equal to one for market m after the policy shock (i.e., interruption). The expression $f(T_{m})$ is a polynomial in time difference to the interruption that fits continuous time trends, and $P_{m}f(T_{m})$ is included to allow for a continuous kink at the interruption. The terms $a_{0}$ and $\gamma_{j0}$ are, respectively, the intercept and seller-specific intercepts, and $e_{m}$ and $e_{jm}$ are the residuals. As the policy lasts for four weeks, Weeks 10 to 13 are collapsed to let $a_{1}$ and $\gamma_{1}$ capture the total change in the four weeks. $^{21}$ Notably, the interpretation vastly differs between the two models. Whereas the market-level model (i.e., Equation (13)) captures the discrete changes in the market average that can be the result of not only systematic characteristic changes of remaining sellers but also the changes of sellers (presence), $^{22}$ the seller-level model (i.e., Equation (14)) identifies only sharp characteristic changes of the remaining sellers. From the perspective of identification, the remaining sellers whose time series contain the interruption are used for identifying the interruption in Equation (14). Both models are applied to each observable seller characteristic, and the market-level model is additionally applied to the number of sellers and the market concentration measured by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI).

Table 5 presents the estimation results with linear continuity fitting, which are interpreted mainly as being generally consistent with the results for quadratic and cubic fitting (see Table A2 in the online supplement). We find sharp market-level changes at the policy change. Quantitively, 58% (p < 0.001) of sellers decide to exit the marketplace, and the marketplace-wide registration rates of platform refund insurance rate and seller-guaranteed refunds increased by 84% (p < 0.001) and 5% (p < 0.001), respectively. Regarding other seller characteristics, we find a sharp increase in registration rates for other certifications and generally a better marketplace-average feedback-based reputation, shown as faster reported delivery speed, higher rating valence and volume, more positive evaluations, and fewer negative evaluations. Sellers' service variety and duration also increase sharply. Consistent with the changes in market average price shown in Figure 3, we find that changes in average price are statistically insignificant, and so do the changes in the HHI, $^{23}$ as well as the average number of disputes and refunds. Taken together and following the model interpretation, those changes suggest one or the combination of two immediate impacts of the policy: (1) the remaining sellers increased sharply their registration in the certifications and service variety and duration and boosted their reputation, or (2) many small and low-type sellers (i.e., sellers with less certifications registration, worse reputation, less variety, and shorter duration, and add little to the HHI) exited after considering the dramatic drop in sales.

The seller-level results further show that 14% (p < 0.001) of remaining sellers switch to platform refund insurance during the policy change, but there is no consistent or salient change in other characteristics of remaining sellers, except their duration. $^{24}$ The change in duration at the seller level is not due to the sellers' response but simply a four-week gain during the policy change and that even the 14% increase in platform refund insurance registration cannot fully explain the 84% increase at the market level without many sellers' exits. The results thus can rule out the characteristic changes of the remaining sellers at the policy change, except for platform refund insurance, and suggest the exits of many low-type sellers as the main response to the policy of sellers and as the sole explanation for the market-level changes. This finding suggests the effectiveness of the policy in filtering out low-type sellers during the policy change, which aligns with and complements the simulation-based near-equilibrium results. The result of no significant characteristic changes here does not conflict with the “deteriorated” secondary characteristics of the remaining sellers shown, as discussed in Section 7.1, due to the difference between the near-equilibrium and immediate impact. In fact, we also show in Table 5 that, compared with Market 1, Market 48 indeed has a higher price, longer reported delivery time, more negative evaluations, lower rating, rating volume, and registration rates in five of eight other certifications, which all support the near-equilibrium result.

## 8. Conclusion and Implications

Unlike most physical goods, services that are sold via an online marketplace come with a higher degree of consumer uncertainty regarding utility, such that consumers are especially dependent on quality signals to choose sellers. This study first systematically evaluates the effectiveness of using platform refund insurance and seller-guaranteed refunds as quality signals for sellers in the online service marketplace. For the inference, a BLP-style demand estimation with aggregate data are built for an online service marketplace, accounting for the heterogeneous risk preference/tolerance among consumers, the potential endogeneity of sellers' adopting refund options, and the competitive nature among sellers. The main takeaways from the findings are as follows: (1) both platform refund insurance and seller-guaranteed refunds increase the demand for sellers, suggesting their effectiveness in signaling quality; and (2) platform refund insurance is stronger than seller-guaranteed refunds on the effects, which is consistent with the theoretical expectations that platform refund insurance is more credible in the commitment and can incur a higher signaling cost for low-type sellers because the cost is endorsed by the platform, which is a third party with an information advantage for consumers and sellers and is prepaid. The reduced-form analysis further suggests that sellers with a better reputation or lower popularity might benefit less from the refund options.

The study next evaluates a policy that retains platform refund-insured sellers but expels uninsured ones regarding (1) whether the policy filters out low-type sellers and attracts high-type newcomer sellers and (2) how the policy affects the sellers who remain before and after the policy change. In the analyses, we leverage counterfactual simulations based on BLP estimates of policy-invariant parameters to examine the (near) equilibrium impacts of the policy change on the demand side, togethering with SITS designs for seller characteristics and presence to complement the immediate impacts on the supply side. The results of both sides of the analyses suggest the effectiveness, from the perspective of the service marketplace managers, of the policy regarding filtering out low-type sellers and maintaining high-type ones, which encores the effectiveness of platform refund insurance as a quality signal. The demand-side results further show a limited consumer welfare (and demand) loss from the changes in characteristics of remaining sellers at the (near) equilibrium after the policy.

This study contributes to four research streams. First, it largely complements the literature on the quality signaling of refund options by (1) adding empirical evidence of their quality-signaling effects, (2) expanding to the refund options for services, and (3) extending the refund option scope to include a platform-endorsed refund. Notably, the comparison between the platform-endorsed refund and the classic seller-guaranteed refund explores and examines refund option designs from a credibility perspective. This differs from and adds to the design variation of this literature, which is concentrated on policy leniency. Second, to the literature on quality signals in online marketplaces, this study adds among the first piece of empirical evidence on the effectiveness of refund options in online service marketplaces and adds a new signaling indicator, platform refund insurance. To our knowledge, this is also the first empirical study on quality signals that adopts the standpoint of platform/marketplace managers/owners and considers the use of a quality signal to filter undesired sellers and, thus, improve platform-wide performance. Third, this study contributes to the literature on measuring consumer welfare of product/service variety and quality change by quantifying the welfare gained from the availability of the online service marketplace, as well as that due to the quality changes and the variety changes triggered by the policy. Last, this study extends the applicability of the BLP model to online service marketplaces and to quantify the demand elasticity of refund options, as well as the near-equilibrium impact of a quality-indicator-based seller screening policy.

Our findings generate managerial implications for practitioners, including sellers and platforms. From the perspective of sellers, especially those that have not yet accumulated enough of a feedback-based reputation or do not have a good one, our findings imply that offering a refund option is a consistently effective way to signal quality and attract consumers. A seller should be cautious, however, when designing specific refund policies or choosing from various refund options. A seller is encouraged to adopt refund options/policies that exhibit credibility and are difficult for low-type competitors to adopt, such as building the reputation of being fair, objective, or even liberal when refund disputes arise or providing refund options that are endorsed or protected by the platform or a reputable third party (e.g., consumption protection insurances).

From the perspective of the platform, our findings imply that compared with the conventional seller-guaranteed refunds, offering the option of platform-endorsed refund more effectively addresses the “lemons” problem and improves the platform-wide demand, and accordingly, platforms can leverage a screening policy that is contingent on platform-endorsed refund programs, such as making the program compulsory, to cast out low-type sellers, and improve the platform-wide quality and competency. Notably, both practices are high in executability across online marketplace contexts. In addition, recall that the platform-endorsed refund is distinct from the conventional refund activity between sellers and buyers by that it can strictly impose a higher cost for low-type sellers, empowered by the platform’s information advantage about sellers over buyers. The platform managers, thus, are recommended to ensure the strict enforcement of the program policy, for example, require sellers to make a security deposit, penalize sellers high in refunds with increased premium, and be lenient with buyers’ refund requests. To further improve the refund program designs, platforms can collaborate with third information-advantaged parties, such as financial institutes (credit card issuers, insurance companies, etc.), to incur even more discriminatory signaling costs for sellers. To further escalate the overall performance, platforms can leverage their information advantage to provide more valid quality signals and could leverage them as the premise for selecting high-type sellers, casting out low-type sellers, and improving the platform’s competency.

There are limitations of this study, in addition to the noted restrictive assumptions of the policy analysis, due to the constraints of our current data and modeling and the inherent limitations of the BLP model. First, our findings may generalize to most decentralized-matching platforms wherein buyers take the majority of the responsibility in choosing sellers, and the signaling effect can drive such choices. This type of platform encompasses most online marketplaces and overlaps with some forms of sharing-economy platforms (e.g., freelance marketplace, Airbnb). Nevertheless, we cautiously note that the generalizability to online platforms with centralized matchings, (e.g., Uber) or homogenous products/sellers may be limited due to the absence of signaling effects. Second, because our data are limited to the specific category that is affected by the policy, a valid control group is not available for a randomized control trial, which may more clearly identify the effects of refund options and the policy shock. Third, a more accurate and efficient model could be developed if a cost function were incorporated. Although it may be unfeasible to model a fully two-sided market, a cost function would allow us to endogenize other certification variables and the entry/exit decisions of sellers as well as allow us to understand the strategic action of sellers and the formation of equilibrium. To do so, however, poses a methodological challenge in our context, given the very large space for the decisions of sellers and number of sellers. The nonstationary evolution of the seller characteristics also indicates potential off-equilibrium decisions of sellers, which cast doubt on the validity of the profit maximizer assumption. We leave this issue to be addressed in future studies with an observed new equilibrium. Fourth, future research could leverage individual-level data and demographic information to reflect heterogeneous tastes for characteristics driven by demographics and to investigate other important impacts, such as the search pattern of consumers. Doing so would generate more accurate results, as it would free the model from a heavy dependence on idiosyncratic logit error (Petrin 2002). We believe that the limitations of our paper open avenues for more research in this area.
