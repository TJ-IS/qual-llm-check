# A Theory of Information Compression: When Judgments Are Costly

Abstract. A theory of information compression (TIC) conceptualizes how anticipated judgment costs can affect decision quality. We theorize—inductively from decision making in medicine, energy pricing, auditing, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial analytics—how judgment networks can exacerbate <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non<sup>fi</sup>nancial judgment costs that compress information. Information compression occurs when a process intended to inform decision making generates information that has little variation. This can reduce decision quality and market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. We offer potential remedies to mitigate its adverse societal consequences. We use complementary theoretical perspectives to nomologically contextualize how information compression arises. We introduce an information compression measure based on information entropy. TIC’s theoretical crux is that the expansion of a judgment network’s publicness exacerbates information compression by increasing judgment costs for some entities in a judgment network. We close with future research ideas on TIC’s core propositions and its broader theoretical implications for information systems research.

## 1. Information Compression

Decision making is the central activity of an organization (Huber 1984), and information systems are intended to improve the timeliness and relevance of information to enhance collective and individual judgments. Because they are sociotechnical interventions, information systems (IS) can induce state changes when they modify behaviors, create new patterns of interaction, or change power structures. These perturbations can produce abrupt unintended changes, triggering adverse societal and organizational effects. We theorize—counter to the roots of our <sup>fi</sup>eld—that an increase in data, such as a stream of ratings, under some situations can paradoxically reduce information. When there is complete certainty, such as when recent ratings are identical, there is no information (Platt 1974), thus contradicting the longreceived wisdom that information systems improve the quality of decision making (e.g., Davis and Olson 1985). This article investigates situations in which information systems interventions cause signi<sup>fi</sup>cant state changes in decision makers’ judgment costs, causing the value of information to decline toward zero. In the extreme, there is substantial information compression, and counterintuitively, an information system provides little information.

Information compression occurs when the information generated by a process intended to inform decision making has little variation. Consequently, this information minimally discriminates among alternatives and considerably reduces its usefulness in informing judg ments. For example, rating systems are typical for a wide variety of situations (e.g., surgical procedures and restaurant quality). IS scholars have implicitly assumed that the publicness of judgment codifying systems assists decision makers in discerning among them. However, this presupposition erroneously confounds the volume of data with information. Our focal research question is about how the unrecognized phenomenon of information compression arises. The “how” refers to exposing an explanatory mechanism linking judgment costs to information compression. Introducing a theory of information compression (TIC) enables scholars to understand the causes and consequences of information compression so they can propose rectifying interventions to mitigate its deleterious social effects (Figure 1).

Formal and informal systems tend to evolve and oscillate between relatively long periods of incremental modi<sup>fi</sup>cation and abrupt radical change. Periods of relative equilibrium are punctuated by abrupt change.

The state changes associated with the evolutionary dynamics of judgment networks give us insights into the causes and consequences of information compression and possible rectifying interventions. State changes can be planned, such as introducing online retailing, or unplanned, such as a pandemic, and their consequences can exceed expectations (Berthon et al. 2008). We rely on a punctuated equilibrium perspective to understand the overall effects of information compression. It is a lens for investigating phenomena whose effects can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly alter with a state change.

Phenomenon-driven theorizing (Gregory and Henfridsson 2021) does not follow traditional templates. The need to meet the how, why, when, and utility of a theoretical contribution (Bacharach 1989, Whetten 1989) led us to structure the paper as follows: (1) introduce punctuated equilibrium as a lens for state changes that can induce or reduce information compression; (2) review the literature on rating systems, in which information compression is often visible; (3) present an example of two state changes in medical judgments, one creating and one mitigating information compression (when); (4) apply Shannon’s information theory to measure information compression and delineate the communication process in a judgment network (how); (5) describe further three diverse settings in which information compression has recti<sup>fi</sup>able social impacts (utility); and (6) synthesize multiple theories to explain the process by which information compression is created (why).

This article offers several key contributions to the literature. Primarily, by explicating how information compression arises across several diverse judgment contexts, we introduce a TIC (see Table 1 for the key concepts). We show how related theories—that is, communication, social brain, and agency—complement TIC. We describe how information compression results from judgments in a judgment network with judgment costs borne by critical agents within that network.<sup>1</sup> In so doing, we highlight when information systems fail to deliver information. An additional contribution is to demonstrate the use of punctuated equilibrium to examine a dynamic phenomenon over multiple state changes.

## 2. A Punctuated Equilibrium Perspective

Our interest in information compression was stimulated by three modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in a physician’s prescribing structure and processes. In seeking to understand these changes, we focus on the state changes (Figure 2). How one seeks to explain is based on the settings of one’s mental camera: a static snapshot for an explanatory variance model (Markus and Robey 1988) of the current steady state, a recording of a scene to capture the process dynamics (Pentland 1995) to generate eventoriented theory (Morgeson et al. 2015) and, in our case, a movie of multiple scenes to discern the effect of significant state changes.<sup>2</sup> A new information system, for instance, can so disrupt existing process dynamics as to create a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant state change whose properties or behavior can be markedly different from the prior state. Whereas the intention of a state change is typically positive, there can be a range of unintended, potentially harmful consequences (Berthon et al. 2008).

Punctuated equilibrium, a biological theory (Gould and Eldredge 1977) applied to organizational change (Romanelli and Tushman 1994), group development (Gersick 1991), and information systems transformation (Boudreau 2006, Silva and Hirschheim 2007, Gregory et al. 2018) is a different way of seeing (Gould 1982) a phenomenon based on gradual versus abrupt change, such as organizational growth (Greiner 1972). Changes of state, such as new actors (people or machines) joining a judgment network, can alter processes and create different explanatory factors. A punctuated equilibrium perspective identi<sup>fi</sup>es the critical process changes (e.g., online reviewing of physicians) that can each have one or more associated variance models to explain causal relationships (e.g., how age or locality in<sup>fl</sup>uences a doctor’s prescribing).

Using the narrative properties of process theory– building guidelines (Pentland 1999), we develop a framework for analyzing punctuated equilibria (Table 2). Our examination of information compression focuses on state changes in the network within which the focal actors, agents, and principals (Jensen and Meckling 1976) interact, and the effect of abrupt interventions, such as new information systems, on their judgments. We want to understand the central role of information systems in the formation and evolution of judgment networks and judgment. As we investigated the phenomenon, we became sensitized to the voice of social agents. Thus, the evaluative context is the ethical expectations of agents in the spirit of a utilitarian ethical perspective (Fritzsche and Becker 1984).

For example, American hip replacement patients are willing to pay over \$2,600 extra, on average, for each additional rating “star” earned by a surgeon from previous patients (Schwartz et al. 2021). Thus, not surprisingly, physicians have nonsurgical advice on how to raise their patients’ public ratings (Kennedy et al., 2014). Individuals have long sought preconsumption advice (Dost et al. 2019), but the traditional word-of-mouth information transfer between friends, family, and neighbors has been primarily replaced by large-scale anonymous public rating systems (Qahri-Saremi and Montazemi 2019). These systems cover nearly every service, and they are often actively managed to generate more stars, leading to ratings clustering at the high extreme of the scale. This results in information compression when the variance in ratings is minimal, contributing to market imperfections and underappreciated dysfunction in the principal–agent relationship. Information compression results in less information, yet the IS literature lacks a theory that can both account for and remedy the consequences.

## 4. Exploring the Process of Information Compression in Medical Judgments

An intervention that in<sup>fl</sup>uences the nature of medica decisions can have an important social consequence, such as the overprescribing of antibiotics and opiates. These two problems have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant public health impacts (Murray et al. 2022) and socially destructive outcomes (Quinones 2015). Surprisingly, information systems have had a key role in aggravating these circumstances. This insight was the galvanizing event for this research. We analyze two state changes in the behavior of prescribing physicians. As the narrative presentation intertwines the framework’s various properties, the critical theoretical observations are summarized in Table 2.

Many countries have opiate addiction problems, exacerbated by the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty medical practitioners (agents) have in assessing a patient’s (principal’s) pain intensity. A physician relies on the patient’s assessment of their pain level to judge the prescribed opioid dosage, and according to medical ethics, this should be the dominant consideration (evaluative context) (Figure 2, panel a). However, patients can now publicly rate physicians on internet websites (a state change), especially in marketized healthcare systems such as the United States. Some researchers<sup>3</sup> speculate that physicians anticipate the possible score they will receive when making prescription decisions. Patients have always had opinions about their physician’s decisions, but they had no means of socially amplifying them until public<sup>4</sup> rating systems, such as Yelp and RateMDs.com, appeared. Such IS interventions have changed some physicians decision making.

In the case of antibiotics, the Pew Charitable Trusts investigated why physicians might overprescribe or misprescribe, such as prescribing an antibiotic when the patient has a viral infection.<sup>5</sup> The physician (a social voice) who directed the study observed (Rama prasad 1983, p. 4; Jasny et al. 2015, p. 8), “There are a lot of social and economic and behavioral factors that play out during the interaction between patient and provider.” Because of online reviews, physicians are now enmeshed in a judgment network. Concerned about the effect of online review sites, Physicians Working Together<sup>6</sup> started a petition<sup>7</sup> asking such sites to cease reviewing physicians because they are not allowed to respond because of HIPAA and medical privacy laws. As one physician commented on the petition site, “Physician reviews are often used as a backlash against not obtaining narcotics or other potentially harmful or unnecessary treatment that the patient decides that they want.” A judgment network is formed because of a series of connected processes (e.g., a patient posting a rating to a review site) (Figure 2, panel b). When physicians prescribe medications, their decisions could be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the anticipated public rating because a poorly rated physician can suffer judgment costs, such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial and psychological harm.

In the case of opiate prescriptions, another party has joined the judgment network (state change) to exacerbate physicians’ judgment costs. The San Diego County Medical Examiner’s Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (agent) now noti<sup>fi</sup>es physicians who prescribed opioids to patients who later died. Its letter states (social agent):<sup>8</sup> “This is a courtesy communication to inform you that your patient (name, date of birth) died on (date). Prescription drug overdose was either the primary cause or key contribution to death. We hope that you will take this as an opportunity to join us in preventing future deaths from a drug overdose.”

Over the next three months, physicians who received such a letter were 7% less likely to start new patients on opioids and issued fewer high-dose prescriptions than those not getting a letter (Doctor et al. 2018). Furthermore, those who received a letter had a 9.7% reduction (p < 0.0001) in the total amount of opioids they prescribed. The coroner’s actions add another process, the letter to the physician, and the judgment network is altered (Figure 2, panel c). The prescribing physician now needs to consider both the patient’s potential rating and a possible letter from the coroner. Information systems have created both these reactions. One enables patients to rate their physician publicly. Another allows a coroner (or other government agency) to link data across multiple databases to identify physicians who have prescribed opiates to a patient and the subsequent drug-related death of that person.

In this illustrative example, we <sup>fi</sup>rst observe that a state change, an IS rating system, alters decision makers judgments, sometimes lowering their quality, because of the anticipated <sup>fi</sup>nancial loss of not prescribing what patients want. Public rating systems can raise judgment costs (see Table 1 for a de<sup>fi</sup>nition) as a poorly rated physician might attract fewer patients (Emmert et al. 2013), earn less income (Gao et al. 2012), and suffer reputational and psychological damage (Kendall and Botello 2016) despite a medical decision’s validity. We then see another IS-induced state change, the coroner’s report, that further alters the judgment network by adding an anticipated psychological cost, the death of a patient, to the physician’s judgment calculus.

Once physicians recognize that public rating systems can impact them, some change their prescribing behavior to raise their ratings. Concern for their publicly visible rating can change a physician’s medical decision making. This change can also lead to information compression (see Table 1), whereby most physicians are rated about the same because they strive to meet patients’ expectations even though these could be medically inappropriate. For instance, the average rating for 614 American surgeons was 4.4 out of <sup>fi</sup>ve, but there was no correlation between the online score and the risk-adjusted mortality rating (Peter-Bibb et al. 2016).

Information compression introduces market imperfection (evaluative context). When there is little variation in ratings, prospective consumers (e.g., patients) cannot differentiate between offerings based on ratings alone. Furthermore, the knowledge markets, in which professionals (e.g., physicians) operate and are expected to apply their knowledge without fear or favor, are also perturbed. Knowledge-driven decision making is warped to meet consumers’ expectations. Whereas judgment networks (e.g., a tribe) and judgment costs (e.g., loss of face) have long existed, their ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation by social media may create large-scale undesirable social effects.

Our examination of the impact of public rating systems on medical decision making identi<sup>fi</sup>es three temporally linked concepts: judgment networks, judgment costs, and information compression (Figure 3). The medical example provides the genesis of theory development by surfacing questions of how, why, and when (Bacharach 1989) and revealing the sequence of events that result in information compression. It shows a state change (when) can in<sup>fl</sup>uence physicians’ (agents) expected judgment costs (why) and change their behavior (how) to avoid these costs. Thus, we propose and support with further examples the following.

Proposition 1. Higher expected judgment costs increase information compression.

Some theories introduce key concepts and their relationships and leave the operationalization of concepts as variables to empiricists. However, by introducing a measure for information compression, we address this issue and extend the theoretical connections of this work.

## 5. Shannon’s Information Theory

Some of the critical foundations of digital computing are attributed to Claude Shannon (Verdu 1998) in his treatise on communication theory (Shannon 1948). His work’s two key features are the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of information entropy and the Shannon–Weaver model of communication (Shannon and Weaver 1949).

## 5.1. Information Entropy and Measuring Information Compression

Shannon was concerned with error-free communication because noise was a major issue for the analog communication systems of his time. There is no uncertainty in a message’s information if noise is eliminated. Shannon recognized that the concept of entropy, which <sup>fi</sup>rst appeared in physics (Cropper 1986), could be applied to information (Shannon and Weaver 1949). Entropy is the number of indistinguishable con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of a physical entity (Rovelli 2019) or a message. For example, repeatedly sending the same error-free message produces no new information after the <sup>fi</sup>rst message. A stream of identical messages has zero information entropy.

Information entropy measures the average rate at which a stochastic source, such as a communication network, generates information (Shannon and Weaver 1949). It quantitatively measures the uncertainty of outcomes (Jaynes 1957, Harte 2011). Formally,

$$
S = - \sum_ {i} P _ {i}. \log_ {2} P _ {i} \text {   where   } P _ {i} \text {   is   the   probability   of   rating   } i.\tag{1}
$$

The more uniform a distribution, the higher its information entropy because all possible events have a near-equal chance of occurrence. In contrast, a skewed distribution has lower information entropy because outcomes close to the peak are more likely to occur (Jaynes 1957, Harte 2011). In the extreme, when all messages are identical, then $S = 0$ . Complete certainty carries no information (Platt 1974).

Information entropy is appropriate for assessing discrete scores, such as ratings, because they have distinct values within a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed range. As a result, it is possible to calculate the maximum entropy for any rating scale. In the case of an n-point rating system, the upper limit, $m a x _ { n } ,$ for $\begin{array} { r } { S = - \sum _ { n } \frac { 1 } { n } . l o g _ { 2 } \frac { 1 } { n } = - l o g _ { 2 } \frac { 1 } { n } } \end{array}$ (Jaynes 1957). Consequently, a compression index (CI) in the range $^ { [ 0 , 1 ] }$ can be created with the higher the value, the more compressed the information. That is,

$$
C I = \left| \frac {S}{\max _ {n}} - 1 \right|, \text {   where   n   is   the   range   of   a   discrete   scale. }\tag{2}
$$

When $C I = 1$ for a product evaluation, consumers see all providers as equally good or poor.

For potential consumers, recent ratings are more likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decisions. Also, it might be the case that a rating series converges over time to a more peaked distribution as reported by some of the studies we cite earlier. Thus, it seems reasonable to adopt a sliding-window approach when computing CI for a contiguous set of observations and to report it as a time ser ies rather than a single value.

## 5.2. Communication Theory and Evaluation Systems

We gain further insights into the nature of messages in a judgment network by using the lens of Shannon and Weaver’s (1949) communication model (Figure 4). In rating systems, communication is galvanized by an instrument that assesses the consumer’s perception of a product experience. Then, that rating is included with other consumers’ ratings shown on a website. These ratings are usually displayed with summary information about the ratings, such as the overall average. The intended receiver, someone thinking of buying the product, accesses the information as can the product originator. When a judgment network is public, ratings might be collective, such as the rating of a clinic with multiple physicians.

Noise affects the quality of a received message (Shannon and Weaver 1949), and in the original model, only the communication channel is assumed to create noise. However, in a rating system, three sources can introduce noise. First, there can be measurement errors (Bound et al. 2001). Second, the app or website might not report all ratings for legal or commercial reasons (Mukherjee et al. 2013), which is also a form of noise. Finally, summarization typically reduces information content, such as reporting only a mean rating and not its variance or skewness. We augment the Shannon–Weaver model to show that the product source receives indirect feedback because a rating system is usually designed to inform another consumer before consumption, not the provider. Further, a provider might be part of a collective evaluation, so individual performance is not separable. Thus, any feedback received by a product source is indirect and might not be individualized.

Social media rating systems differ from the conventional management notion of feedback, which is “information about the gap between the actual level and the reference level of a system parameter which is used to alter the gap in some way” (Ramaprasad 1983). This is the same sense in which 360◦ assessments (Antonioni 1996) approach feedback. Social media rating systems provide direct information to prospective consumers and can indirectly modify the behavior of rated product providers, which is outside the ambit of traditional managerial feedback research.

Shannon’s work provides a conceptual foundation for measuring information compression and understanding the dynamics of the communication of a message between a consumer, potential consumer, and the product provider. Information compression is an extension of information theory beyond Shannon’s focus on sending a single error-free message to consideration of the value of the information delivered as a stream of judgments. We need an elaborated theory of information that moves beyond technical noise (channel features) to embrace social noise (encoder and decoder behavior). TIC is a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in building a sociotechnical perspective extending Shannon’s theory of information. Next, we elaborate on the nature and importance of information compression beyond the initial medical case (Bacharach 1989).

## 6. Exploring Information Compression in Other Contexts

A theory has value when applicable across a variety of phenomena, so we identify additional information systems in which our punctuated equilibrium model (Figure 3) explains the occurrence of information compression. We structure a series of instances (Barley 1986) to explain the relationship between events and decision makers in different judgment networks to illustrate the occurrence of information compression in diverse settings with important societal and business consequences. We adopt a broad perspective of information systems and consider instances such as electricity pricing and auditing.

## 6.1. Electricity Pricing

Public utility commissions that regulate the rates and services of utilities are common in many countries and states.<sup>10</sup> In the early years of the 20th century, many U.S. electricity businesses gained a territorial monopoly by acceding to rate-of-return regulation by the jurisdictions in which they operated (Hausman and Neufeld 2002). Whereas there has been some deregulation, this state change has remained essentially intact. As a result, most U.S. households currently pay electricity prices that re<sup>fl</sup>ect little, if any, information about the current wholesale price of electricity (Perez-Arriaga et al.´ 2017). They typically pay a <sup>fl</sup>at rate (CI \~1) that might vary slightly by season but not by current demand. Hence, many utilities operate expensive and inef<sup>fi</sup>cient demand response systems to handle peak wholesale prices (Sioshansi 2006). In many jurisdictions, public utility commissioners (agents) appointed by an elected politician (principal) set electricity prices. When commissioners set rates, they might consider the potential judgment costs imposed by their appointers or voters rather than their decisions’ impact on economic ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. These judgment networks fail to deliver a core premise of economics, namely, an effective pricing information system that ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently allocates scarce resources (evalu ate context) (Milgrom and Roberts 1992). Flat prices, despite <sup>fl</sup>uctuating supply and demand, are a form of information compression. Consumers ultimately bear the cost of excess generating capacity to handle demand response incidents.

Because the transition between electricity sources, such as coal to renewables, is about 50 years (Smil 2014), long periods of equilibrium in the governance of electricity supply are not surprising. However, we are in a transition period (Gielen et al. 2019). We can anticipate state changes to accommodate the intermittency of renewable energy sources, which will likely require eliminating the information compression of electricity pricing (Watson et al. 2022). As a forerunner, the UK national grid is experimenting with <sup>fl</sup>exibility, including household electricity prices of zero for nonpeak periods, to adjust consumption patterns to <sup>fi</sup>t with intermittency (Thomas 2022).

## 6.2. Financial Analysts and Investment Banks

A well-functioning capital market (evaluative context) relies on a variety of conduits to inform investors. One key source of information is <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts (agents), who have long conveyed information between <sup>fi</sup>rms and investors (Bradshaw et al. 2017). They typically have <sup>fi</sup>nance degrees and Chartered Financial Analyst certi<sup>fi</sup>cations. Analysts apply reproducible computational methods (e.g., capital asset pricing model) to evaluate the <sup>fi</sup>nancial well-being of <sup>fi</sup>rms (Graham et al. 2002). A study of <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts’ published recommendations reports that 74% were “buy” and 2% “sell” (Barber et al. 2006). A buy-to-sell recommendations ratio of 37:1 suggests information compression (CI 0.82) and supplies little information to investors (principals) because most recommendations are buy.

Not surprisingly, there were allegations that the preponderance of buy recommendations was intended to promote investment banking business with the rated <sup>fi</sup>rms (Barber et al. 2006), especially if there are prospects of lucrative underwriting fees (The Economist 2020). We conjecture that, when analysts consider a sell rating, (1) they factor in the costs of dealing with an investment banker who is doing, or expects to do, business with the <sup>fi</sup>rm rated. If an analyst advises sell, (2) the <sup>fi</sup>rm rated will likely contact its account executive at the bank to complain and threaten a loss of business, and (3) the account executive will typically pass on the message, with some forcefulness, to the analyst. Such a cycle likely in<sup>fl</sup>uences future behavior. Alternatively, if analysts’ bonuses depend on the bank’s performance, there can be an anticipated <sup>fi</sup>nancial cost of a sell recommendation leading to a loss of revenue by the bank. In line with this conjecture, analysts who are less likely to recommend buys outperform those who issue more sells (Barber et al. 2006). Presumably, they are less subject to judgment costs when they recommend buy. Though an analyst who always recommends buy might bear reputational costs.

A vital component of an investor’s information system fails when analysts, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by judgment costs, overwhelmingly recommend buy. To rectify this situation, in 2018, the European Union (social agent) implemented the Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (state change) to increase the level of independence of investment advice. One important positive consequence is a decrease in forecast error (Guo and Mota 2021).

## 6.3. Auditors and Nonaudit Services

Accounting is an information system for investors, creditors, and others concerned with the <sup>fi</sup>nancial status and future of a company. Auditors are crucial in this information system because they validate company reports. Auditors, who generally have accounting degrees and certi<sup>fi</sup>ed public accountant licenses, are typically governed by national boards that dictate auditing practice standards. Many major audit <sup>fi</sup>rms also offer nonaudit services, such as consulting. These additional professional services emerged as accounting <sup>fi</sup>rms deepened their relationship with their audit clients. We can <sup>fi</sup>nd no discernable state change in the history of auditing (e.g., Lee and Ali 2008). For over 40 years, there has been concern about the consequences of one entity providing both audit and nonaudit services to the same client (Paterson and Valencia 2011). There is a general perception that the provision of nonaccounting services compromises audit independence, forti<sup>fi</sup>ed by major failures, such as Enron (Beattie and Fearnley 2002). When nonauditing services are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant revenue stream, auditors face a similar situation to <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts and utility commissioners. If they publish an audit that negatively affects potential client consulting opportunities, then auditors can anticipate judgment repercussions.

The UK Financial Reporting Council (FRC) (social agent) <sup>fi</sup>ned PwC, an audit and professional services <sup>fi</sup>rm, £6.5 million following its investigation of its audit of the collapsed retailer BHS, a subsidiary of Taveta Investments Limited. The lead partner of the audit (agent) was <sup>fi</sup>ned £325,000. The FRC report<sup>11</sup> notes that, in 2014, the Taveta Group (principal), a subsidiary of Taveta Investments, paid PwC £355,000 in audit fees and £2,859,778 in nonaudit fees, a differential factor of eight. It concluded that fees generated from nonaudit work inappropriately in<sup>fl</sup>uenced PwC’s behavior. They note that the lead partner of the audit spent only two hours on the audit of the Taveta Group’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial statement, including BHS. Thus, it seems appropriate that The Financial Times (Ford and Marriage 2018) suggests that the major auditing <sup>fi</sup>rms, “pro<sup>fi</sup>t-hungry professional services conglomerates, are not that worried about audit quality.” The ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market hypothesis (Fama 1970) relies on auditing to inform stock markets of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial value (evaluative context). When the potential loss of nonaudit revenue in<sup>fl</sup>uences audit decisions, it reduces the value of the public accounting information system. Information compression increases because audits no longer differentiate <sup>fi</sup>rms based on their <sup>fi</sup>nancial viability, and the <sup>fi</sup>nancial market loses ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The FRC initiated a state change to raise audit independence to make UK audit practices separate legal entities (Kinder 2020).

## 6.4. A Societally Significant Phenomenon

When we examine different judgment networks and go beyond those presented in this article, perceived judgment costs are prevalent in other everyday situations. As the prior examples illustrate, the impact of social forces changes the nature of judgment. Human decisions can be categorized as independent or socially in<sup>fl</sup>uenced under varying levels of transparency (Bentley et al. 2014). In this work, we focus on the ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence by information systems, such as social media, in a professional context, in which codes of ethics and customs direct agents to advance the interests of the client (e.g., patient, investor) above their self-interest. As a result, we propose a four-quadrant model (Figure 5) based on the orientation of the decision maker (client or self) and the level of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. The ethical model of most professions is client orientation following accepted norms and intended to be strongly inoculated against social forces. Thus, for many years, certain professions were prohibited from advertising. For instance, until 1997, American lawyers could not advertise (Ballard 2002). In current times, social media offer an inexpensive vehicle for lawyers and other professionals to build a reputation through networking (Elefant and Black 2010). Consequently, some might well seek to enhance their reputation via public ratings when making a judgment yet remaining within the ethical bounds of their profession. The shift might not be permanent or for all situations because behaviors are contextualized (Su et al. 2022). Thus, removing the cause of information compression might prompt a reversion to ethical judgment.

People blend moral codes and contextual factors when making judgments, but what happens when other factors, such as judgment costs, create an ethical con<sup>fl</sup>ict and contradictory action (Cowan et al. 2022)? Most professions view self-oriented judgments, especially if they harm the client, as contradicting the profession’s ethical standards. Herdlike judgment is proliferating (Bentley et al. 2014) in areas such as voting (Arawatari 2009), climate change (Ingram and Stern 2007), and <sup>fi</sup>nance (Allen and Wilson 2003). This is worrisome for public health (Benotsch et al. 2004, Bates et al. 2006) and particularly disconcerting when there was a need for mass vaccination (Dovere 2021). We concede that some judgment networks that generate information have been in place for years (e.g., auditing by professional service <sup>fi</sup>rms), but they have not been examined through the lens of judgment costs and information compression. Our perspective, therefore, provides a new lens for the deleterious effects of the interplay between judgment networks for a variety of information systems.

## 6.5. An IS Significant Phenomenon

We gain some additional insights from studying the preceding examples. First, one common feature of judgment networks is a multiplicity of principal–agent relationships and their sometimes chameleon-like nature. For example, in the original physician-oriented example, we see (1) a physician is an agent of the patient when prescribing, (2) a patient can be an agent of a physician rating company, (3) a rating company can be an agent of a prospective patient, (4) a coroner can be an agent of a deceased patient, and (5) the coroner when sending a death letter treats the physician as society’s agent. A physician has multiple agent roles, and a patient and coroner can be both agent and princi pal. We see similar role patterns in other judgment networks.

Existing IS research is deeply entrenched in a presumption of unambiguous principal–agent relationships (Tiwana et al. 2010, Wiener et al. 2019). However, ISs can dynamically alter such existing relationships by adding a larger network of linkages in which none previously existed. These systems can spawn multiprincipal and multiagent networks of stakeholders with only partially convergent interests. Theoretically perplexing linkages can overlap when one agent’s principal might be another principal’s agent. This matters immensely for IS research because of the anticipated repercussions of a decision (i.e., judgment costs), and their visibility across a multiprincipal–multiagent arrangement (i.e., judgment network) often creates a self-perpetuating information compression dynamic, in which a growing volume of data has an inverse relationship with insight or meaning derivable from it. Counterintuitively, greater transparency fostered by IS can lead to dysfunctional outcomes. Simon (1996) ominously anticipated this challenge in characterizing search termination heuristics, which arise because of violation of the assumptions of unambiguous information and a singular principal–agent relationship.

Second, these networks are strongly associated with creating public information services. For example, an audit report is a service for a company’s board, investors, and creditors. Similarly, online physician ratings and the coroner’s letter are designed as services for healthcare consumers. The public availability of an information service’s reports can amplify judgment costs for both excellent and poor judgments. An accurate <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis (i.e., good decision) whose general release is linked to a share price decline can be costly to an analyst. Alternatively, frequent overprescribing of drugs (i.e., poor judgment) can raise a physician’s reputation when many of their patients participate in rating schemes.

IS has implicitly assumed that more data are better and that the shift from data scarcity to overabundance is a data analytics gold mine. Therefore, rating systems have become a readily available basis for IS research and consumer decision making. Our perspective shows that more is not necessarily better: when judgment costs become more visible, there is a danger that the accuracy of the underlying observations decreases further.

## 7. Types of Judgment Costs

As discussed previously, judgments have inherent costs for the recipient and the person making the assessment. For instance, many providers commonly seek to monitor their performance electronically by requesting calls for judgment from their customers. Customers are asked to rate service experiences, such as a ride-sharing service, within minutes of leaving the car, a recent <sup>fl</sup>ight a day after its completion, or a downloaded app when using it. Such judgments take time, add to humans daily cognitive load, and may impose a variety of costs on the rater.

Decision makers need to consider the possible outcomes of their judgments, especially when the associated costs could be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. We sought examples that identi<sup>fi</sup>ed judgment costs to understand the problem's dimensionality. Twenty-seven cases of online reviewers being sued were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by a Google News search using “review” and “lawsuit.” A qualitative analysis (see appendix for the data) reveals <sup>fi</sup>ve types of judgment costs: psychological, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, opportunity, reputational, and privacy. We now brie<sup>fl</sup>y examine and de<sup>fi</sup>ne these costs.

Financial costs involve monetary losses directly or indirectly because of judgments. As several of the preceding judgment network examples illustrate, an agent can anticipate that some judgments incur monetary losses that may include, for example, lower bonuses, <sup>fi</sup>nes from regulators, or even loss of employment or certi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

An opportunity cost is “the loss of other alternatives when one alternative is chosen” (OED). For example, when consumers who rate providers must deal with lawsuits, they spend time and money that could be applied to other pursuits.

There are reputational costs when professionals are chastised for failing to meet their obligations, such as a systematic and independent audit, which can harm both these individuals and their employers.

Psychological costs invoke negative emotional or cog nitive reactions in individuals. For example, when a cor oner’s letter reminds physicians of the harm they may have done by overprescribing opiates, this con<sup>fl</sup>icts with the physicians’ oath that includes “<sup>fi</sup>rst, do no harm,” resulting in psychological costs (Doctor et al. 2018).

Privacy costs involve a loss of control over personal information, generating psychological and reputational costs. We believe that these costs should be recognized as an independent category because their effects and prevalence are ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed by social media, which erase some of the boundaries of what constitutes personal information. Thus, individuals face new privacy consequences for behaviors that once might have drawn some comments of disgust from acquaintances but can now attract trolling and public opprobrium from distant strangers.

## 8. Nomological Linkages to the Theory of Information Compression

Though we investigate a phenomenon lacking prior theoretical consideration, we are not building a theory from scratch but, as in nearly all scholarship, building on and contributing to what is already known (Whetten 1989). As a value add, we situate the proposed TIC within complementary theoretical perspectives to show its intellectual heritage in terms of theories that in<sup>fl</sup>uenced our thinking. We have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed multiple concepts that provide a conceptual scaffolding for the theoretical location of TIC within existing, established theories (Figure 6). This aid also helps identify the order in which we should de<sup>fi</sup>ne the connections: the social brain, actor network theory (ANT), agency theory, and type of good. We now brie<sup>fl</sup>y explain the relationship of these theories with the phenomenon of information compression.

## 8.1. The Social Brain and Social Agency

Humans are a malleable social species. Our behavior is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by those with whom we expect to interact, and we develop structures to in<sup>fl</sup>uence social behavior (e.g., a code of ethics). We have evolved large brains (over 85 billion neurons) plausibly because of the complexity of processing social cues and inferences when living in a group with other humans (Dunbar 1998, Brune and Br ¨ une-Cohrs ¨ 2006). Success in a social system often requires facilities such as tactical deception (Harcourt 1988), coalition formation (Harcourt 1989), and cheater detection (Trivers 1971). We have honed skills for sharing and assessing intentionality (Tomasello et al. 2005). The social intelligence (Whiten 2000) gene was favored during the emergence of early humans in tribal societies. We are accustomed to being immersed in social networks in which we must process reactions and intentions. The topology of a network, its structure, and the members’ properties (e.g., propensity to impose a cost on an agent) shape judgments. Thus, a state change in the structure of a network, such as a new principal, can change role relationships and alter the judgment cost calculus.

Information systems increase the complexity of living in a social world because some professionals’ judgments can be disseminated widely and dissected precisely. The transitivity of judgments is enlarged by information systems. A decision that once might have been con<sup>fi</sup>ned to a few people can now be communicated to many. These days, the social brain, congested by potential reactions from many sources, needs to evaluate the consequences of judgments broadly. Our biologically evolved social analytics module needs a major augmentation to handle the complexity of this dynamically interconnected world. This is an opportunity to design appropriate IS interventions, just as we have done for computational tasks.

Information systems can support social agencies to redress adverse societal outcomes. Because a judgment network can have adverse societal effects, it can galvanize social entities to redress them. In the case of physicians overprescribing opiates, the San Diego coroner assumed the voice of a social agent and developed an information-based intervention, a state change, to successfully modify physicians’ prescribing behavior (Doctor et al. 2018). ## 8.2. Judgment Networks and Agency Theory

Judgment networks are a subclass of the general class of actor networks, for which there is an extensive theoretical exploration in IS because ANT investigates the coexistence of inseparable social and technical systems (Walsham 1997, Hanseth et al. 2004). ANT’s overall concept that actors and relationships are embedded in a sociotechnical network is a general theory. We must recognize that, under this theoretical aegis, there can be many types of networks, including social and judgment networks, that can inherit some aspects of ANT but need distinct conceptualization to have predictive and prescriptive potential. In our case, agency theory gives us a theoretical perspective of examining two key actors, agents and principals, in a judgment network.

Agency theory is founded on recognizing that partners in some joint actions can have different objectives and resources (Jensen and Meckling 1976). This initial formulation was focused on explaining managerial behavior in <sup>fi</sup>rms, in which investors (i.e., principals) dele gate oversight to a board who, as their agents, assign decision making to professional managers (agents). Agency theory purports to address two problems (Eisenhardt 1989): <sup>fi</sup>rst, how to resolve goal con<sup>fl</sup>ict between agents and principals and, second, how a principal can monitor an agent’s performance. Because of the <sup>fi</sup>rm focus, the contract between agent and principal became agency theory’s unit of analysis. Agency theory often presupposes that a principal has the ability and knowledge to create and enforce a contract to limit the agent’s actions or align its behavior with the principal. Furthermore, most agency-theoretic research assumes that there is often a persisting, clearly identi<sup>fi</sup>able relationship between agents and principals.

In TIC’s vocabulary, agents and principals operate within judgment networks (e.g., analysts, investors, boards, and professional managers). The judgment and decisions of each party can have costs for both the agent and principal. Traditional agency theory focuses on the costs borne by the principal because of the agent’s judgment. Nevertheless, when making a judgment, an agent must bear in mind the possible reaction of the principal because professional managers can get <sup>fi</sup>red or lose bonuses.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm orientation of agency theory is unnecessarily myopic today, and one can <sup>fi</sup>nd examples of agency everywhere (Perrow 1986). Many are episodic rather than persistent, and there can be bilateral costs for both agents and principals. Information systems have created novel forms of judgment networks with multiple, intertemporally changing, interacting agents and principals, episodic relationships, and joint judgment costs. For example, physicians and patients can have episodic relationships, even as low as one consultation. A physician’s treatment can impose a cost on the patient as misdiagnoses and mistreatment are not rare, and now through social media, the patient can in<sup>fl</sup>ict a cost on the physician. We also saw that a coroner can intervene, as society’s agent, and impose a judgment cost on physicians.

Furthermore, the auditing and <sup>fi</sup>nancial analyst examples illustrate that, when a network of principals and agents is analyzed, we can explain how judgment costs might impact the quality of information products, such as audit reports. Agency theory is silent about such behavioral dynamics.

In a world of electronic networks and highly accessible information, the issue of agency requires a multidimensional reformulation and a new perspective. Judgment networks are one way to embellish agency thinking. They take the original dyadic model into a multiplayer setting with multiple agents and principals who can also be both agents and principals within the same network. Our formulation explicitly recognizes the existence of bilateral judgment costs on both agents and principals, and we are not limited to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s scope.

Information compression introduces a new concept for understanding agency. Agents vary in the information they hold and are willing to provide to principals (Tirole 2017). Consequently, principals might receive compressed information that favors the agent as seen in some notorious bankruptcies, such as Enron. Information asymmetry between agent and principal is abetted by information compression when the agent deliberately <sup>fi</sup>lters the <sup>fl</sup>ow to create a speci<sup>fi</sup>c perception. Our theory, therefore, complements the body of work on information distortion in agency theory.

Agency theory is concerned with the consequences of judgments made within in<sup>fl</sup>uencing networks, at a minimum a dyad and often more complicated in a world of electronic connections. By spotlighting judgment networks and anticipated judgments costs, we can extend the usefulness of agency theory for human behavior and search for new interventions that constrain deleterious actions. We propose the following.

Proposition 4. In a judgment network, how agents expect principals to react to their judgments determines judgment costs.

In the opening illustrative judgment network of a prescribing physician, there was low concordance or agreement between the two judges, patients, and the coroner. Patients’ ratings were positively associated with overprescribing, and the coroner’s letter aimed to discourage opiate prescriptions. We expect that, when concordance is high, such as when multiple principals goals are aligned, there would be higher levels of information compression because the agent faces reinforcing judgment costs.

Proposition 5. In a multiprincipal judgment network, judg ment concordance amplifies information compression.

8.3. Type of Good and Information Compression Consumer goods can be categorized as search, experience (Nelson 1970), and credence (Darby and Karni 1973). Search goods are those whose quality consumers can assess during the discovery process and after consumption (e.g., clothing and technology). Features of this class of products are often extensively reviewed by consumers and reported in online marketplaces. Experience goods are those whose quality the customer can only assess following personal consumption (e.g., a movie). Credence offerings are “expensive to judge even after purchase” (Darby and Karni 1973, p. 69). For instance, most people cannot judge medical treatments (e.g., a knee replacement) even after surgery. They are incapable of objectively assessing whether the success of a procedure was due to the surgeon’s skills, their health and <sup>fi</sup>tness before the operation, or postsurgery rehabilitation diligence.

These three types of goods vary in their assessment potential preconsumption and postconsumption (Table 3), which also determines the measurement error consumers face when evaluating each type of good. Measurement error occurs when a signal is confusing because of a lack of clarity and con<sup>fl</sup>icting interpretations of its meaning. It introduces noise into the encoding phase of a rating system (Figure 4). Credence goods have the highest level of measurement error because those rating them might be inexpert, underinformed, and often easily swayed by the numerical evidence provided by others just like them. Thus, we expect that the information compression associated with ratings of a type of good is determined by the inherent measurement error related to signals about that type of good.

Proposition 6. The level of information compression is positively correlated with a good’s intrinsic measurement error.

Thus, we expect information compression to be higher for credence than experience goods and higher for expe rience than search goods.

High information compression provides sparse infor mation to potential buyers, and they likely need to seek alternative, surrogate signals to discriminate among options. This search resumption is the opposite of search termination, which occurs when a consumer achieves information satis<sup>fi</sup>cing (Simon 1976). Dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with a search, the consumer must continue searching using another source of information to <sup>fi</sup>nd a satis<sup>fi</sup>cing outcome. We, therefore, expect search costs to correlate with information compression. As a result, we propose the following.

## 9. Conclusion

## 9.1. Boundary Conditions of a Theory of Information Compression

TIC’s utility lies in understanding how state changes can cause and rectify information compression. Hence, its application is bounded by state changes, those creating information compression and those eliminating it. Further, we constrain our consideration to judgment networks in which the decision maker is primarily concerned with the costs they might incur. Within these critical con<sup>fi</sup>nes, a decision maker needs to perceive judgment costs arising from a judgment network exposing them to social forces that shift their judgment calculus from professionally ethical to reputation preserving. If these conditions do not exist, then TIC is not applicable. It is limited to those circumstances that create and eliminate information compression. Given the persistent failure of religious, legal, and ethical codes to eradicate opportunism, it is unlikely that forethought and reform will ever quash the need for TIC.

## 9.3.2. Algorithmic Filtering by Social Media Platforms.

TIC has two theoretical implications for IS research on social media platforms. First, such platforms increasingly rely on algorithmic <sup>fi</sup>ltering, which likely creates information compression when it selects feeds based on prior consumption and “likes.” This form of information compression reinforces existing views and creates “echo chambers” (Jasny et al. 2015). It is another source of noise in a judgment network (Figure 4). Second, humans rely on heuristics that lead them to terminate search for information after they reach a threshold, characterized as satis<sup>fi</sup>cing (Simon 1976). Information compression can simultaneously lower perceived search costs and degrade search quality. As a result, search costs are reduced, but search quality degrades. Information compression in algorithmic <sup>fi</sup>ltering erroneously lowers the threshold for satis<sup>fi</sup>cing because a consumer is exposed to far fewer countervailing views and is more likely to terminate the search too soon.

TIC has two theoretical implications for future IS research on arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence based on machine learning. First, machine learning is dependent on <sup>fi</sup>nding patterns in large data sets, but information compression <sup>fl</sup>attens these patterns. Future research could develop approaches to screen data for information compression by building on our CI measure. Second, information compression can distort variable-to-variable relationships into looking different from what they are as our preceding TAM discussion illustrates. We speculate that this might be caused by the potential for judgment costs in<sup>fl</sup>uencing data values. Future research should, therefore, examine the judgment network environment within which data are generated—its milieu—that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence data quality. This is essential as machine learning applications evolve from affecting machines to affecting humans (Eshete 2021).

9.3.3. Non-IS Circumstances. Information compression is also present in other organizational settings. From a marketing perspective, information compression creates problems for customers because it reduces their choice. Following the logic of Dickson’s (1992) theory of competitive rationality, the lack of choice alerts entrepreneurs to <sup>fi</sup>ll gaps. When the ratings of luxury hotels are all close to perfect, marketing scholars should recog nize the need to develop new discriminating methods that will enhance customers’ decision-making capacity. Similarly, in the human resource arena (e.g., Iqbal et al. 2015), when most employees receive high ratings in appraisals, the data become less valuable to managers. It also offers no incentive to employees when they expect everyone to achieve similarly high scores.

## 9.4. Concluding Thoughts

Human inventions for processing information, such as speech, writing, and digitization, have changed the way we behave and think within the con<sup>fi</sup>nes of a legacy set of information-processing modules evolved in a tribal setting (Barrett and Kurzban 2006, Kurzban 2012). Our social brain is adept at handling the issues of living in a small network of long-lived relationships in which cooperation was critical for survival. New informationprocessing capabilities have perturbed our traditional judgment networks in two fundamental ways. First, the development of systems of inquiry, such as the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c method, introduced a justi<sup>fi</sup>catory method of reasoning that analyzes data and facts to support a judgment (Mercier and Sperber 2017, Watson 2019). Evidencebased medicine and data-driven decision making are today’s incarnations of justi<sup>fi</sup>catory reasoning. Information systems are usually designed to support justi<sup>fi</sup>- catory reasoning. Second, today’s digital connections in<sup>fl</sup>uence judgment networks as shown earlier. They give network members access to vast troves of data and plausibly informed analyses, enabling members to connect seamlessly within and beyond networks.

Our theory implies that IS-enabled justi<sup>fi</sup>catory reasoning and communications can be uncomfortable bedfellows in today’s digital judgment networks. Justi<sup>fi</sup>catory reasoning might be discarded because of the judgment costs it creates when evaluations are widely disseminated. The intriguing social brain can reduce the value of investments in information systems designed to enable superior decision making. For instance, an audit <sup>fi</sup>rm with a capability in data analytics to improve the quality of its audits has its investment undermined when an audit partner’s professional judgment is compromised by consideration of the loss of professional services revenue. Consequently, the general expectation that information systems improve decision making can be nulli<sup>fi</sup>ed by information compression.

The digital world is more malleable and dynamic than the physical world (Kallinikos et al. 2013) because of the inherent <sup>fl</sup>exibility, extensibility, and continuous refashioning of digital products. At the same time, TIC shows how human behavior is not a constant within such a <sup>fl</sup>ux. Moral codes can break when judgment costs are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high. Information systems are developed for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c intended human behavior, but they can be repurposed in many different ways (Berthon et al. 2008) with concomitant effects on humans. In particular, aspersion occurs when an information system produces undesirable human actions counter to the designer’s intentions. From a societal perspective, the pliability of human behavior (e.g., Milgram’s 1975 experiments) is of greater social concern than the malleability of the technology component. Decision makers might begin their career with socially desirable intentions, such as an auditor diligently inspecting a client’s records or a physician ethically prescribing. However, as we show, judgment costs, especially when magni<sup>fi</sup>ed by social media, can motivate a shift from ethical behavior to reputation preserving (Figure 5). Information compression is a symptom of a profound cause that society should aim to mitigate by redesigning the information system and judgment network nexus to reduce information compression.

We develop TIC inductively from diverse, societally signi<sup>fi</sup>cant instances of judgment networks, in which the perceived costs of a judgment can weaken ethical norms and result in self- rather than client-oriented decisions. As a result, the publicly available information resulting from these decisions is often compressed and less helpful in discriminating among options when there are inherent differences, such as in selecting a physician. We use four cases of the phenomenon and a graphical generalization (Figure 3) to understand the generation of information compression. Several theoretical explanations, seven propositions, and related research questions are offered to go beyond a general model to understand some factors that cause degrees of information compression. To assist future research, we also describe a measure of information compression based on information entropy.

We address the theory’s primary goals expected of a theoretical contribution (Bacharach 1989). We explain how information compression occurs when a judgment network emerges that in<sup>fl</sup>icts judgment costs (the why) on decision makers to alter their behavior in a socially undesirable way and reduce the value of information in an information system.

Information compression, we posit, is a pervasive yet understudied phenomenon with critical business and societal impacts. It is an underappreciated, unintended consequence of information systems and, as such, also amenable to information systems design interventions to limit it. Thus, we encourage IS scholars to embrace a TIC as a theoretical green<sup>fi</sup>eld. It merits intensive scholarly investigation based on generating societally important questions arising from this initial formulation.
