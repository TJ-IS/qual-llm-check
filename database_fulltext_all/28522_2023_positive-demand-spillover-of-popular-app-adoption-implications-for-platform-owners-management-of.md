Abstract. As platform owners interact with end users and complementors, their demandside characteristics and performance affect the overall value creation of ecosystems. This research investigated how the emergence of popular complements on a mobile communication platform impacts the usage of other complementary products by the platform’s end users and how platform owners can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from such demand spillovers. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two different forms of demand spillovers (i.e., backward and forward) and conceptualized how each subsequently affects platform expansion. On the basis of individual user-level app usage data, we empirically demonstrated how the presence of a popular app alters the demand structure of a platform through changes in the usage of other apps operating within it. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that popular app adoption by users increases the number of apps used and the duration of app usage, excluding the usage of popular apps, only within the platform offering a popular app. These results support the existence of positive spillovers from popular complement adoption on a platform. Such positive within-platform spillovers are derived from both backward spillovers onto existing apps adopted before popular app adoption and forward spillovers onto new apps to be adopted after the uptake of favored apps. These patterns suggest that positive spillovers of popular app adoption occur through both the increased retrieval of existing apps and reduced uncertainty about newly released apps. Furthermore, forward spillover is considerably stronger than backward spillover, implying that platform owners can reap bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by coordinating the launch of new complements and the promotion of less-known counterparts to end users with the emergence of a popular app. These results shed light on how platform owners can manage their complements and create value beyond direct contributions from popular complements.

## 1. Introduction

In platform-based ecosystems, platform owners play an integral role as they interact with both complementors and end users. Accordingly, their role in managing this interdependence affects the overall value and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the platform ecosystems (e.g., Adner and Kapoor 2010, McIntyre and Srinivasan 2017); the interdependence formed and fostered rests not only on the interaction between end-users and complementors but also on the interplay among complementors. Complementor interdependence and dynamics in<sup>fl</sup>uence the economic health and longevity of platform ecosystems (e.g., Parker and Van Alstyne 2005). However, despite the importance of platform complements, few studies have uncovered strategic insights into how platform owners should govern and expand their ecosystems by successfully managing complements and their interactions (e.g., Adner and Kapoor 2010, Wareham et al. 2014).

In the current study, we focus on the emergence of a popular complement on a platform,<sup>1</sup> investigate its impact on other complementors, and devise strategic responses that a platform owner can undertake in creat ing value for the ecosystem. The direct economic effect of a popular complement on such a platform is evident; when successful, it increases end-user bases and reve nues, creating a virtuous circle and boosting the platform’s overall value (e.g., Boudreau 2010; 2012). An increased end-user base, however, can back<sup>fi</sup>re on com plementors because of rising “over-entry” competition from strong same-side network externalities, thereby engendering negative outcomes for platform ecosystems (e.g., Boudreau 2012, Boudreau and Jeppesen 2015). In this light, our interest lies in exploring the demand-side characteristics of platforms and revolves around examining the following questions: To what extent does the emergence of a popular complement in<sup>fl</sup>uence the usage and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of other complements<sup>2</sup> and thus create positive spillovers? How do the effects of a popular complement vary across existing and new complements? The answers to these questions are expected to serve as practical guidelines for platform owners in forming strategies related to the emergence of a popular complement.

To investigate such questions, we empirically examine changes in the demand structures of other complements after users adopt a popular complement on a mobile communication platform. With the rapid adoption of smartphones and tablets and the widespread use of mobile apps, the development of mobile communication platforms is rising remarkably; the emergence of these new environments is accompanied by a torrent of complementary resources. Focusing on a two-sided mobile communication platform wherein the platform owner manages the apps (complements) and app users (end users), we estimate demand spillovers after popular app adoption within the platform, that is, spillovers of the adoption onto the usage of other apps on the platform offering the popular app. As underlying mechanisms, two forms of demand spillovers are explored in this study: (1) backward spillovers onto the usage of existing apps (i.e., adopted before the adoption of a popular app) and (2) forward spillovers onto the usage of new apps (i.e., adopted after the adoption of a popular app). We further compare such spillovers in both magnitude as well as direction.

At least two distinctive aspects of this work are noteworthy. First, we focused on a popular app that did not have any systematic or promotional issues predetermined by its platform, which could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the use of other apps; that is, we examined the pure role of the popular app on its platform ecosystem. Few platform owners can cultivate perfect foresight about which app will become prevalent in the future. Nonetheless, studying a popular app as a tool for modifying app usage behavior and contributing to the value of platform ecosystems is worth exploring. Previous studies have delved into popular products under various goods categories, such as music, books, movies, video games, and mobile apps (e.g., Sorensen 2007, Binken and Stremersch 2009, Joshi and Mao 2012, Knapp et al. 2014, Yin et al. 2014, Zheng et al. 2019), but such research was devoted mostly to the sales implications of these commodities (e.g., Sorensen 2007, Binken and Stremersch 2009, Joshi and Mao 2012, Knapp et al. 2014). This study therefore goes beyond the sales contributions of popular goods and examines the role of popular apps as “introductory tools” that trigger the use of other apps within a platform offering a popular app.

Second, we conceptually and empirically explored two types of demand spillover effects from popular app adoption. By leveraging the richness of our data and observing the exact times at which all apps are used by all users on a weekly basis during the sample period, we investigated the individual use of apps within a plat form, which enabled us to measure the individual usage of existing and new apps, thereby clearing the way to quantify both the backward spillover effects on the usage of existing apps and the forward spillover effects on the employment of new apps. Although most previous studies have empirically delved into demand spillovers by concentrating on one type of spillover effect (e.g., Hendricks and Sorensen 2009, Joshi and Mao 2012, Knapp et al. 2014), the current study expands the research on demand spillover by classifying spillover effects into two directions (backward and forward) and measuring their relative magnitudes. We also hypothe sized about these two spillover effects and compared them based on the brand association perspectives in the context of digital platforms. We conceptualized a platform as a brand consisting of complements (i.e., apps) with a shared name or title and the emergence of a popular app within a platform as a brand extension. Accordingly, the established hypotheses argue that positive within-platform demand spillovers can occur through both the increased retrieval of existing apps by reviving the platform and reduced uncertainty about new apps by reinforcing the platform.

For our empirical setting, we chose Anipang as the focal popular app for our quasi-experiment. Anipang is a dominant casual mobile game launched by Kakao, a top-ranked mobile communication platform in South Korea. A notable characteristic of Anipang is its position as a third-party complement, which means that an external game company developed the game, whereas Kakao determined its release. The Kakao platform started as Kakao Talk, a communication app, and has subsequently expanded into a powerful platform by launching not only <sup>fi</sup>rst-party (e.g., Kakao Story, a social networking app) but also third-party apps (e.g., Kakao Games). Similar platforms include Facebook, which began as a social media app and now serves various complements, including Instagram and WhatsApp within its platform ecosystem.

For the empirical investigation, we examined how the adoption of Anipang changes the usage of other apps within and outside the Kakao platform. To this end, we ascertained the number of apps used and the duration of app consumption at the individual level over 27 weeks and employed the Gaussian copula-based difference-in-differences (DID) framework with propensity score matching. The proposed copula approach enables an evaluation of the effects exerted by popular app adoption on app usage behavior in an integrative manner. This capability stems from the simultaneous estimation of the number of apps used (discrete variable) and the duration of app usage (continuous variable) as well as the leeway accorded to a <sup>fl</sup>exible correlation between the two. We also performed relative time model estimation to validate the parallel time trends assumption between popular app adopters and nonadopters, as was done by Angrist and Pischke (2008).

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings showed that Anipang adoption increased both the total number of apps used and the total duration of app usage, excluding the usage of the popular app itself, within the platform. This result supports the existence of positive within-platform spillovers. Anipang adoption, however, does not increase the aforementioned number or duration outside the platform. Such positive within-platform demand spillovers stem from both positive backward spillovers onto existing apps and positive forward spillovers onto new apps. Moreover, the forward spillover effect is found to be stronger than the backward spillover effect, implying that the platform owner can strategically coordinate the launch of new apps and the promotion of lesser-known apps to end users with the emergence of a popular app.

The results collectively suggest that the adoption of a popular complement encourages users to engage extensively with the platform offering it. Platform owners can therefore fully utilize the emergence of a popular complement to manage interactions among complementors on the platform. By illuminating these empirical regularities, our study provides insights into how mobile platform ecosystems are expanded through app demand spillovers and increased app engagement in mobiledriven communication environments.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Ex Post Effects of Popular Products

Previous works have examined the sales effects of popular commodities on other products or categories. Sorensen (2007), for example, showed that appearing on the New York Times bestseller list modestly increases sales, providing indirect evidence of positive spillover from bestsellers to nonbestsellers within the same genre. Joshi and Mao (2012) revealed that bestseller-based movies generate higher revenues at the U.S. box of<sup>fi</sup>ce than do non-book-based movies. Knapp et al. (2014) demonstrated that the success of movies adapted from bestsellers increases the sales of source books.

Limited exploration has been directed toward the ex post effects of popular products on platform ecosystems. Binken and Stremersch (2009) found that the introduction of superstar game software titles positively affects the sales of video game consoles over a short period. Lueker et al. (2018) discovered that complementors superstar status on an Android mobile app platform does not promote their innovation. Zheng et al. (2019) investigated the spillover effects of WeChat on the other top 50 apps in the Chinese mobile app market and found positive spillover effects on only two apps.

Existing works have operationalized the construct of popular products on the basis of availability or introduction in the market (e.g., supply) instead of consumers’ actual usage or adoption (e.g., demand), with the exception of Zheng et al. (2019). While Zheng et al. (2019) used one-week cross-sectional data, including the number of clicks in each app per user, the current study used 27-week panel data, including the number of apps used and the app usage duration, which allows us to capture additional temporal variations in users app usage behaviors, and thus control for both individual- and time-speci<sup>fi</sup>c unknown factors more accurately. In addition, extant research has typically focused on one outcome variable, and most have revolved around sales outcomes (with the exceptions of Lueker, Foerderer, and Heinzl (2018) and Zheng et al. (2019)). We expand the scope of the outcome of popular commodities to app consumption in two dimensions, that is, the number of apps used and the app usage duration, in an integrated model using a copula. Lastly, although the two papers that examined the popular complements in the context of mobile platforms (i.e., Lueker, Foerderer, and Heinzl (2018) and Zheng et al. (2019)) focused on general mobile platforms (e.g., Android, the Chinese mobile app market), we consider the emergence of a popular complement in a proprietary, mobile communication platform. ## 2.2. Platform Owners’ Management of Complementors

One stream of research related to platform owners management of complementors has delved into the entry of these players into complementary markets. Platform owners often penetrate such marketplaces to either stimulate innovation efforts for underperforming complementors $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Gawer and Cusumano 2002, Gawe and Henderson 2007) or imitate successful complements (e.g., Farrell and Katz 2000). Mixed empirical results have been derived regarding such entry. In exploring Amazon’s penetration into its third-party sellers’ product spaces, Zhu and Liu (2018) found both positive and negative complementor-associated effects. Venturing into digital complementary markets, Edelman and Lai (2016) found that Google’s inclusion of <sup>fl</sup>ight search data increases users’ paid searches but decreases their organic searches. Several recent studies have scrutinized platforms’ schemes for entry into complementary mobile markets. Li and Agarwal (2017) found that Face book’s integration of Instagram accounts into the social media site increases the demand for <sup>fi</sup>rst-party apps (e.g., Instagram) and large third-party photo-sharing apps but decreases the demand for their smaller counterparts. Regarding Android mobile app platforms, Wen and Zhu (2019) discovered that Google’s entry into app developers’ spaces reduces the updates and increases the prices of affected apps but shifts their innovation initiatives. A study by Foerderer et al. (2018) found that Google’s entry promotes complementary innovation for third-party photo apps, particularly pronounced for larger and more diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed complementors. Rietveld et al. (2019) theoretically argued that platform owners selectively promote complements as a means of managing the overall value of platform ecosystems and empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed them in the context of the console video game industry in the United Kingdom.

Our research extends the literature on platform owners’ management of complementors by focusing on platform owners’ strategic decision on how to optimize the management of complementors upon the emergence of a popular complement. ## 2.3. Demand Spillover Effects

A series of studies on demand spillover effects have documented spillover between new and existing products, primarily orienting their investigations toward backward spillovers (i.e., spillovers from new products to existing ones). For instance, Hendricks and Sorensen (2009) demonstrated that an artist’s release of a new album increases the sales generated from the artist’s previously launched collections. Garthwaite (2014) showed that celebrity endorsements increase the sales earned from existing nonendorsed book titles written by recommended authors. In the context of mobile app markets, Mayrhofer (2013) found positive spillover effects from the entry of second and third apps on the <sup>fi</sup>rst app launched by the same developer on Facebook. Moreover, Xu et al. (2014) con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the release of a mobile app by a major national media company is positively associated with increased demand for the corresponding mobile news website. A recent study on cross-platform spillovers by Krijestorac et al. (2020) revealed that introducing a viral video onto a new platform paves the way for its growth on the existing platform. To our knowledge, few studies have inquired into forward spillovers (i.e., from existing products to new ones). An exception is the work of Joshi and Mao (2012), who discussed the success of movies adapted from bestsellers.

In the marketing literature, explorations into spillover effects have been steadily concentrated in the advertising and brand domains. In research on the cross-category spillover effects of advertising, Erdem and Sun (2002) discovered that advertising in one product category increases sales in another product category for umbrella brands. Through online <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments, Sahni (2016) found that exposure to online ads on a restaurant-search website elevates the chances of sales for nonadvertised restaurants. Extending investigations to brand spillover, Balachander and Ghose (2003) observed that advertising featuring an extension augments the probability that its parent brand will be chosen, consistent with the notion of positive reciprocal spillover effects from brand extension on brand choice. Another study demonstrating positive reciprocal spillover effects by Knapp et al. (2014) showed that a successful extension from bestsellers to movies increases the sales of parent bestsellers.

Recently, demand spillover effects have been considered in various contexts, giving rise to conceptualizations, such as consumer awareness spillover (Song et al. 2021), intertemporal spillover (Haviv et al.2020), spotlight spillover (Liang et al. 2019), and mega app spillover (Zheng et al. 2019). A noteworthy observation is that extant studies have generally focused on one type of spillover effect. We extend this research stream by delving into the spillover effects of popular app adoption on the usage of other apps through backward and forward spillover effects. Taking advantage of our individuallevel app usage panel data, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed existing apps adopted before the adoption of the popular app and new apps adopted after popular app adoption per user to ascertain backward and forward spillover effects. As indicated earlier, forward spillover has been minimally studied in empirical research on demand spillovers, with the exception of Joshi and Mao (2012). Our study centered on spillover effects within a mobile communication platform, thereby adding to growing research on within-platform or cross-platform demand spillovers, most of which have been featured in recently published articles (e.g., Liang et al. 2019, Haviv et al. 2020, Krijestorac et al. 2020, Song et al. 2021).

Furthermore, extant studies have focused on spill over effects on products that a consumer has yet to purchase but had been released as part of the options from which to choose in the market. This treatment is motivated, in part, by previous research centered mostly on spillover effects at the purchase level (rather than the consumption level), with researchers typically investigating the nondigital products of lengthy life cycles as focal commodities (e.g., Balachander and Ghose 2003, Hendricks and Sorensen 2009, Garthwaite 2014, Knapp et al. 2014). However, the assumption of a spillover onto available but unpurchased products may overlook an important spillover extending to digital goods. The purchase of digital products, such as mobile apps, does not always result in consumption regularity (Han et al. 2016); and digital content is monetized through repeated use (Mayrhofer 2013). A few studies on the backward spillover effects of digital products (e.g., Mayrhofer 2013, Xu et al. 2014) have only theoretically included products that users may have already adopted or used in evaluating spillover effects. In developing theoretical constructs and analyzing empirical data, existing research has not distinguished between products that consumers have purchased and products that they have not purchased with respect to capturing and assessing spillover effects. Hence, the current study calls into question the dominant assumption in the literature by constructing the former as a new type of demand spillover characteristics involving digital products and estimating it as a standalone backward spillover. Further, the latter is incorporated into the forward spillover, which has focused on products to be launched in the previous literature (e.g., Joshi and Mao 2012). ## 3. Hypothesis Development

## 3.1. Characteristics of Digital Platform Apps

Our study explores within-platform spillovers in a unique mobile app platform context, where the value of a given digital commodity (i.e., platform apps) depends heavily on the number of users who buy and consume the product. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the level of utility gained from the use of platform apps is contingent on the number of users patronizing the same product category and, more broadly, on the number of consumers in diverse categories who interact with and complement one another within the con<sup>fi</sup>nes of platform ecosystems. In contrast, such signi<sup>fi</sup>cant network externalities do not affect most nonplatform apps. Many mobile users consume platform apps not only to obtain utility from the products but also to satisfy their own utility and social needs (Ellison et al. 2007, Stein-<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. 2008). In this respect, the values of platform apps are often determined by the expected pool and capacity of social liquidity, or the number of users with whom social relationships can be cultivated (Fang et al. 2013, Kwon et al. 2016). At the outset, enjoyment from using a popular app, for example, hinges primarily on the number of people using the app to maximize social utility beyond the hedonic utility gained from the usage itself. However, users’ interaction and engagement in social exchanges can also extend widely across other apps and venues that constitute the ecosystem of a digital platform. This extension can be regarded as a form of network externality driven within platforms (Katz and Shapiro 1994), and a number of factors contribute to the active operation and reproduction of such network effects in enhancing social relationships within digital platforms.

Digital platforms facilitate the easy discovery of apps through simple search functions. The links and connections between and across platform apps and inapp-crossing advertisements also enable discovery and installation processes at minimal search costs and transactional frictions. The in-app currencies and paywall structures that digital platforms afford us further reduce transaction costs associated with downloading and using products while strengthening their interconnections and social values accordingly. Moreover, same-platform apps share user interface designs and functionalities, making users feel comfortable and familiarizing them with these technologies. Finally, digital platforms reinforce the connectedness of users as well as apps via their own messaging applications, which readily facilitate live communications and social interplays that form and strengthen interactions and reciprocity among users. Consequently, the emergence of a popular app systematically encourages the creation of a variety of demand spillovers among digital platform apps.

## 3.2. Within-Platform Demand Spillovers

Within the realm of digital ecosystems, a platform often becomes a “brand” consisting of numerous complements (i.e., apps), as these same-family members directly and indirectly share the platform’s name or title. A user’s knowledge about a platform and its com plements is interconnected through linkages between corresponding nodes—a process that drives users to associate brands with the platform (Balachander and Ghose 2003). Complements that are released by a single platform can be carried under an umbrella or parent brand so that the release of new complements can be regarded as a brand extension. The introduction of brand extensions may result in positive spillovers onto a parent brand through increased brand awareness (Balachander and Ghose 2003, Knapp et al. 2014) and a favorable perceived quality of other products (Balachander and Ghose 2003).

The emergence of a popular complement encourages users to adopt and consume same-platform complements that constitute a single brand. Given that consumers naturally wish to optimize the value of brands, they look for complements that are already available within a platform. The technical connectedness or viral mechanisms employed by users on a digital platform, evoked by their experiences of the platform’s shared features, substantially reduce transaction costs associated with discovering and installing platform-based apps. For example, same-platform complements are characterized by similar user experiences, the same payment system and in-platform currencies, common social networking features, and hyperlinks to other complements, all of which enhance the interplay and connectedness among users and minimize expenditures related to transactions. This low transactional friction, in turn, strengthens brand association and attachment (Fedorikhin et al. 2008) while enhancing the scope of social liquidity through which users can fortify social relationships and interact with other users (Kwon et al. 2016). Brand values play an important role in eliciting social interactions, as consumers who use products marketed under the same brand also share personal images and thus actively engage in social relationships (Kuksov 2007). The widespread use of a popular complement can also signal its high quality among its adopters. Consequently, a digital platform’s introduction of a popular complement can generate positive spillover onto the platform (i.e., the parent brand). When a user adopts a popular complement on a digital platform, he or she may acquire a family of associated products operating within the platform as a result of brand associations and perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts stemming from high product quality. Accordingly, the adoption of popular complements creates positive spillovers onto other complements released by the same platform, thereby increasing overall platform usage. Consequently, we formulate the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. The adoption of a popular complement increases the usage of other complements within the same platform.

## 3.3. Backward and Forward Spillovers

The monetization of digital content is accomplished through repeated use; but the purchase of digital products, such as mobile apps, rarely stimulates regular consumption (Han et al. 2016). Moreover, many mobile consumers are hesitant to install and consume apps because of the uncertainty associated with product quality in the presence of numerous alternatives (Al-Natour et al. 2020).

The aforementioned within-platform spillovers can be generated broadly through backward and forward demand. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the low transactional friction facilitated by digital platforms brings about backward spillovers, wherein the adoption of a popular complement induces the active consumption of existing complements offered under the same brand through brand revival, which refers to the resurrection of a previously eliminated or forgotten brand (Brown et al. 2003).

Studies revolving around brand associations (Herr et al. 1996), brand attachment (Fedorikhin et al. 2008), and brand retrieval cues (Alba and Chattopadhyay 1985) have postulated that consumers are predisposed to store information related to their attachment and experience with a particular brand, retrieving them when they evaluate products from the same or similar brands. Consumers’ adoption or use of new goods increases their awareness of existing products under an umbrella brand by triggering their recall of such commodities (i.e., brand revival effect) (Hendricks and Sorensen 2009) or their rediscovery of existing goods manufactured by the same producer (Mayrhofer 2013). Thus, adopting a popular complement can elevate the attention devoted to these products by popular complement adopters (versus nonadopters). This effect then drives them to rediscover and use already-adopted complements, as doing so can maximize the “product utility” and “social utility” arising from using the same-brand products. That is, upon the adoption of a popular complement, consumers are encouraged to look for same-platform complements to maximize the brand value and enhance the pool of social liquidity. Such encouragements and motivations are further reinforced through advanced platform functionalities, which thereby increase the usage of preinstalled complements offered under the same brand. In this respect, when a consumer adopts a popular complement, brand associations and retrieval cues trigger product and social needs, generating backward demand spillovers that substantially increase the consumption of installed but underused complements within the same platform. With these effects in mind, we postulate the following:

Hypothesis 2a. The adoption of a popular complement increases the usage of existing complements adopted before the uptake of the former within the same platform.

The adoption of popular complements can also generate forward spillovers within a digital platform, as it attracts new purchases and installations of complements belonging to the same brand. Forward spillovers may involve taking advantage of strong brand associations and networking to enhance the consumption of new complements that are newly adopted after the uptake of a popular complement within a digital platform. However, this process is more effectively achieved through brand reinforcement, which reduces uncertainty about quality assurance. Brands can be viewed as signi<sup>fi</sup>ers that transfer meaning (Mick 1986), acting as information cues of quality and credibility (Zeithaml 1988). Brand reinforcement reduces uncertainty around product quality and value, thereby strengthening con<sup>fi</sup>dence in a prod uct and promising user satisfaction.

Existing products may create forward spillovers onto new ones by providing information about the quality of new goods categorized under the same brand and reducing the uncertainty that surrounds them (Joshi and Mao 2012). After the adoption of a popular comple ment, consumers are willing to try out new equivalents released by the same digital platform on the basis of their evaluation regarding the quality of the popular complement. The uncertainty felt about new comple ments then diminishes after popular complement adop tion. The adoption of a popular complement also confers new complements with the image of high qual ity (given its popularity) and encourages the adoption of new complements within the same platform. Consequently, the adoption of a popular complement brings about forward spillovers within the same platform, as it strengthens users’ product con<sup>fi</sup>dence. In line with these observations, we develop Hypothesis 2b:

Hypothesis 2b. The adoption of a popular complement increases the adoption and usage of new complements after the uptake of the former within the same platform.

## 3.4. A Comparison of Backward and Forward Spillovers

Although both backward and forward demand spillovers occur after the uptake of a popular complement, their magnitude may vary given consumers’ perceptions toward expected values. On the basis of brand association and social liquidity perspectives, we compare these two forms of within-platform demand spillovers, which affect the usage of same-platform apps after the adoption of a popular complement. When all else is equal, the value of most products depreciates; and such patterns are more prominent among digital goods (Sundararajan 2004). Consistent with these phenomena, the value of “old,” previously purchased complements diminishes over time as new alternatives emerge. Accordingly, consumers are inclined to discount their brand association and attachment with the former more so than with the latter. Given that rational platform users wisely form expectations in accordance with changes in their future social liquidity, they are tempted to consume “new” complements more actively than old counterparts (Kwon et al. 2016). This is likely because they perceive that the former can more effectively enhance social liquidity than the latter. This reduced value depreciation and increased capacity for elevating social liquidity suggest that the quantitative magnitude of forward demand spillovers exceeds that of backward spillovers. Correspondingly, we posit the following:

Hypothesis 3. Forward spillovers on the usage of new complements outcompete backward spillovers on the usage of existing complements after the adoption of a popular complement within a platform.

## 4. Data and Measures

## 4.1. Data Description

Large-scale panel data that comprise individual users mobile app time usage histories were obtained from Nielsen KoreanClick, a research <sup>fi</sup>rm that collects and analyzes information on the internet and the mobile usage of Android users. Android is a dominant operating system of mobile devices worldwide, accounting for 91.4% of the Korean market during our study period.<sup>3</sup> Panel participants of all age groups (ranging from teenagers to seniors) were recruited using a strati<sup>fi</sup>ed sampling method to ensure the representativeness of the population. Nielsen employees who were responsible for panel selection randomly called candidates from the target population and invited them to join the panel. After agreeing to be part of the panel, the participants were asked to download and install a tracking app from Nielsen KoreanClick on their mobile devices. Subsequently, they were rewarded with incentive points redeemable for gift cards. The tracking app ran in the background of the panel member’s device and collected information on their use of mobile apps. The tracking app regularly transmitted encrypted log <sup>fi</sup>les to a server via a secure cellular connection or Wi-Fi. The data also contained self-reported user demographic information, such as age, gender, monthly income, and educational level. The tracking app collected information even when the mobile devices were not connected to the internet. Thus, our data provided precise information on mobile use as compared with the information one might have gathered from companies’ servers.

Between April 30 and November 4, 2012 (27 weeks), the tracking app collected data on 1,479 panel members who used one or more mobile apps every week throughout the sample period. On a weekly basis, we observed individual access to different mobile apps and visit duration. It was found that smartphone users devoted an average of 983 minutes (standard deviation: 759 minutes) every week or 2 hours and 20 minutes per day on average to mobile apps. The panel members accessed an average of 30 different mobile apps each week (standard deviation: 12.5 apps).

Nielsen KoreanClick classi<sup>fi</sup>es mobile apps into 13 broad categories: game, communication, multimedia/ entertainment, portal/search, lifestyle, social network, utility, personal <sup>fi</sup>nance, e-commerce, news, job/education, sports/leisure/travel, and unde<sup>fi</sup>ned apps. Our empirical analysis adheres to this categorization. We observed that users allocated the largest amount of time (26.9%) to communication apps (e.g., mobile messengers), followed by multimedia or entertainment apps (e.g., video, music, and photo apps) (21.4%), and game apps (21.1%). The largest number of apps (25.6%) used were lifestyle apps (e.g., map/navigation, weather, food, and health apps), followed by utility apps (e.g., contact, app stores, clock/alarm, and schedule/memo apps) (19.4%), and multimedia or entertainment apps (18.5%) (See Figure B.1 in Appendix B for details).

## 4.2. Empirical Context

The Kakao platform, our platform of interest, started as Kakao Talk, a dominant messenger app in South Korea. Kakao Talk has various advantageous functionalities, such as automatic synchronization of the users contact list in their phones to Kakao Talk’s friends list, accommodated group conversations, and free downloads, all of which fuel its growth. Kakao Talk has ultimately become Kakao’s main platform that offers not only <sup>fi</sup>rst-party apps developed and released by the Kakao platform but also third-party apps developed by external developers and released by the Kakao platform. During our sample period, Kakao Story, a social networking app, was the <sup>fi</sup>rst-party app, and all gaming apps (namely, Kakao Games) were the third-party apps. Any apps offered by the Kakao platform can be downloaded as separate apps with links to Kakao Talk. Users can search and download such apps either inside Kakao Talk or from Android or iOS app stores. However, even if a user accesses an app to download inside Kakao Talk, he or she will be directed to the Android or iOS app stores because the Kakao platform does not operate its own app store. Only users who are registered for Kakao Talk can download other apps released by the Kakao platform using their Kakao Talk accounts.

We now provide more details about how the Kakao platform operated Kakao Games (third-party apps) during our sample period. When the Kakao platform started its gaming services in 2012, many game developers wanted to join the platform due to the success of early gaming apps such as Anipang and Dragon Flight. The Kakao platform meticulously selected partners to offer quality services and expanded steadily from 10 games at the end of July to 35 at the end of November in 2012.<sup>4</sup> Game developers were not charged to offer apps on the Kakao platform or for user downloads and subscriptions.<sup>5</sup> Moreover, the Kakao platform shared revenue from in-app purchases and other features with game developers.<sup>6</sup>

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a focal popular complement as a “popular app” that was ranked the highest based on the total usage time among newly released apps during the sample period. The 15 most popular apps in terms of total usage time (see Table B.1 in Appendix B for details) jointly accounted for 52% of the total app usage. Among these, Kakao Talk, a leading communication app in South Korea (similar to WhatsApp, WeChat, or Line), was the most heavily used app in our sample. However, we did not select Kakao Talk as a focal popular complement because it is a mobile communication platform and not a complement. Instead, we selected Anipang as our focal popular complement, as it was the second most heavily used and was released on July 30, 2012, which fell within the fourteenth week of our sample period.

Anipang is a standalone app: it is a timed puzzle game where players match three or more identical animal icons to obtain a high score. The game is free to download. To play the game, one must pay a virtual game token, which is automatically generated every eight minutes, with a maximum storage of <sup>fi</sup>ve free tokens. Players can also purchase additional game tokens within the app. A distinctive feature of this game is that it was released by the Kakao platform such that users should register for Kakao Talk to play

Anipang, thereby leveraging the viral mechanics of messaging apps. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, an Anipang player can exchange game tokens with friends in his or her Kakao Talk contact list through messages and be motivated by leaderboard-based competition displayed in his or her Kakao Talk contact list. (Figure B.2 in Appendix B includes screenshots of Anipang’s login window and its gameplay.) Although these communicationbased functions may provide some values to users, Anipang is free to download as a separate app accessible outside the Kakao platform; users do not need to open Kakao Talk to play it.<sup>7</sup> This feature allowed us to measure the unbiased spillover effect of Anipang adoption, as it eliminated systematic confounding factors arising from the platform architecture. Furthermore, there were no in-app advertisements within Anipang during our sample period,<sup>8</sup> thereby mitigating the concern over biases that can result from advertisements. Anipang shows a short life cycle, allowing us to study it thoroughly during our sample period. The number of active Anipang users increased in the <sup>fi</sup>rst nine weeks after the release and decreased afterward, whereas the weekly average duration of Anipang increased in the <sup>fi</sup>rst seven weeks and then decreased (See Figure B.3 in Appendix B for details).

## 5. Empirical Approach

## 5.1. Gaussian Copula-Based Differencein-Differences Model

To quantify the spillover effects of popular app adoption on app usage in terms of counts and duration, we used a Gaussian copula-based difference-in-differences approach, which extends the traditional DID model to the multivariate setting. The DID analysis compares a treatment group (TG) to a control group (CG) before and after the adoption of Anipang. The TG consists of Anipang adopters, whereas the CG comprises Anipang nonadopters throughout the sample period such that there are 1,060 users in the TG and 419 users in the CG. Thus, the resulting sample is composed of 1,479 users and 39,933 observations ( 27 weeks 1,479 users).

The proposed Gaussian copula-based DID model may, however, still be susceptible to time-varying selfselection biases, similar to the traditional DID model. For example, the upgrading/downgrading of users data plan at certain weeks (unknown to researchers) can in<sup>fl</sup>uence their adoption timing of Anipang and subsequent use of other apps. To alleviate the concern that the treated and control users may not have followed parallel paths in the absence of Anipang adoption because of unobserved time-varying factors, we estimated relative time models. These models assessed both the anticipatory (preadoption) and ensuing (postadoption) effects of Anipang adoption by splitting an indicator for Anipang adoption into a series of indicators for lags (preadoption) and leads (postadoption) in the conventional DID model (Angrist and Pischke 2008). If the anticipatory (preadoption) effects of Anipang adoption are statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant and economically negligible, then we can assert that endogeneity issues pertaining to the different timing of Anipang adoption among individual users are not present in the sense that users who would expect to change their app usage levels did not necessarily adopt Anipang, thus lending additional support to our casual interpretation of Anipang adoption effects.

We considered the number of apps used and the log-transformed app usage duration from all the apps (except Anipang) as the outcome variables (dependent variables) in the DID model. The DID model is speci-<sup>fi</sup>ed as follows:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c} \operatorname * {P r} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \mathrm{n}) = \frac {\Gamma (\theta + \mathrm{n})}{\Gamma (\mathrm{n} + 1) \Gamma (\theta)} \mathbf {r} _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {\mathrm{n}} (1 - \mathbf {r} _ {\mathrm{it}}) ^ {\theta}, \mathbf {r} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \frac {\mu_ {\mathrm{it}}}{\theta + \mu_ {\mathrm{it}}} \\ \text { and } \mu_ {\mathrm{it}} = \exp \Bigl (\alpha_ {0 \mathrm{i}} ^ {\mathrm{N}} + \beta_ {\mathrm{t}} ^ {\mathrm{N}} + \alpha_ {1 \mathrm{i}} ^ {\mathrm{N}} \cdot \mathbf {t} + \sum_ {\mathrm{j}} \delta_ {\mathrm{j}} \cdot \mathrm{I} _ {\mathrm{itj}} \Bigr) \\ \mathrm{T} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \alpha_ {0 \mathrm{i}} ^ {\mathrm{T}} + \beta_ {\mathrm{t}} ^ {\mathrm{T}} + \alpha_ {1 \mathrm{i}} ^ {\mathrm{T}} \cdot \mathbf {t} + \sum_ {\mathrm{j}} \gamma_ {\mathrm{j}} \cdot \mathrm{I} _ {\mathrm{itj}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}} \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

for user i $( \mathrm { i } = 1 , 2 , \dots , 1 , 4 7 9 )$ at week $\mathsf { t } ( \mathsf { t } = 1 , 2 , \ldots , 2 7 )$ The <sup>fi</sup>rst equation is the negative binomial regression model for the number of apps used, and the second equation is the log-normal regression model for app usage duration. The number of apps used is denoted by $\mathrm { N _ { i t } }$ and the log-transformed duration of app usage $\dot { \mathrm { T _ { i t } } } { = } \ln ( 1 + \mathrm { A p p }$ Usage Duration indicates the logtransformed duration of app usage. We excluded Anipang usage in computing $\mathrm { N _ { i t } }$ and $\mathrm { T _ { i t } }$ to focus on the spillover effects of Anipang adoption. In the negative binomial regression speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, Pr(A) denotes the probability of $\mathrm { A } , \mu _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ is the expectation of $\mathrm { N _ { i t } }$ and $1 / \theta \left( > \bar { 0 } \right)$ is a dispersion or heterogeneity parameter. We note that if $1 / \dot { \theta }$ approaches zero, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the estimated value of $1 / \theta$ is close to zero, whereas its standard error is relatively large, making $1 / \theta$ insigni<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from zero. Then, the negative binomial regression model becomes the Poisson regression model.

In Equation (1), $\alpha _ { 0 \mathrm { i } } ^ { \mathrm { N } }$ and $\alpha _ { 0 \mathrm { i } } ^ { \mathrm { T } }$ are user <sup>fi</sup>xed effects that control for the unobserved heterogeneity across users; $\beta _ { \mathrm { t } } ^ { \mathrm { N } }$ and $\beta _ { \mathrm { t } } ^ { \mathrm { T } }$ are week <sup>fi</sup>xed effects that capture the unobserved temporal effects common to all users; $\alpha _ { \mathrm { 1 i } } ^ { \mathrm { N } }$ and $\alpha _ { \mathrm { 1 i } } ^ { \mathrm { T } }$ estimate the user-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trends by multiplying the linear time trend variable, t, which addresses the unobserved different time trends across users,

$$
I _ {i t j} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} 1, \text {   if   } (u s e r i \in T G) \text {   and   } (t = u s e r i ^ {\prime} s A n i p a n g \\ \quad \text { adoption   week + j   weeks }) \\ 0, \text {   Otherwise, } \end{array} \right.
$$

and $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ are random error terms. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of main interest are $\delta _ { \mathrm { j } }$ and $\gamma _ { \mathrm { j } }$ . When j is nonnegative, $\delta _ { \mathrm { j } }$ and $\gamma _ { \mathrm { j } }$ estimate the postadoption spillover effects of Anipang adoption on the number of apps used and the app usage duration at the $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { j } } ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ week after the adoption, respectively. When j is negative, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients $\overset { \bullet } { \delta } _ { \mathrm { j } }$ and $\gamma _ { \mathrm { j } }$ estimate the preadoption effects at the $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { j } } ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ week prior to Anipang adoption. To estimate all pre- and postadoption effects, we collapse all preadoption periods greater than or equal to six weeks before the adoption into one dummy variable and all postadoption periods greater than or equal to six weeks following the adop tion into another dummy. For identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, we set the preadoption effect at the week just before the Anipang adoption to zero $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \delta _ { - 1 } = \gamma _ { - 1 } \overset { \cdot } { = } 0 )$ . We note that both $\delta _ { \mathrm { j } }$ and $\gamma _ { \mathrm { j } }$ can be interpreted as the percent change in our model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. From the above de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ${ \mathrm { I } } _ { \mathrm { i t j } } ,$ we con<sup>fi</sup>rm that there is no movement between the treatment and control groups throughout the sample period: the treatment group is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as users who adopted Anipang sometime during the sample period, whereas the control group is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as users who never adopted Anipang during the sample period.

We assessed the spillover effects of Anipang adoption from an integrative view by jointly estimating the two equations in Equation (1) because the two depend ent variables of interest, the number of apps used and the app usage duration, can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by common unobserved factors. For example, if a game platform runs a special promotion, users might spend more time playing games and might try several new games. Consequently, there may be a potential correlation between unobserved random shocks in the number of apps used and the app usage duration in Equation (1). Incorporating such a correlation into the model allows us to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of our estimates. To this end, we cannot use a regular bivariate model because the number of apps used follows the discrete negative binomial or Poisson distribution, whereas the app usage duration follows the continuous log-normal distribution.

To address this issue, we used a copula model to construct a joint model of these two distinct marginal distributions. Copular models have been used in various empirical business studies. Prieger (2002) and Smith (2003), for example, used copula models to jointly estimate the effects of insurance coverage and managed care on hospital admittance and the duration of stay. In the marketing context, Hwang and Park (2016) simultaneously estimated the impacts of Walmart supercenter conversion on store visits and expenditures using a copula function. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when these researchers estimated the impact of Walmart supercenter conversion on consumer shopping behavior using difference-in-differences models, they considered a zero-in<sup>fl</sup>ated Poisson process to model weekly visits and a log-linear regression to model weekly spending. To capture the potential correlation between weekly visits and spending, they linked the discrete outcome of visits to continuous unobserved random shocks in spending via the Gaussian copula. Among others, the Gaussian copula is known as being <sup>fl</sup>exible and robust in many applications. This mechanism expresses an explicit correlation between two random variables, thereby enabling us to interpret the correlation easily. Thus, we used the Gaussian copula function to model the correlation between unobserved random shocks in the number of apps used and the app usage duration. (See Danaher and Smith (2011) and Park and Gupta (2012) for details of copula functions.) We <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ned a latent standard normal variable $\omega _ { \mathrm { i t } } ,$ which is related to the number of apps used $\mathrm { N _ { i t } }$ as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \{\mathrm {N_ {it}} = \mathrm{n} \} \text { is   equivalent   to } \{\operatorname * {P r} (\mathrm {N_ {it}} \leq \mathrm{n-1}) \leq \Phi (\omega_ {\mathrm{it}}) \\ <   \operatorname * {P r} (\mathrm {N_ {it}} \leq \mathrm{n}) \}, \end{array}
$$

where Φ is a standard normal cumulative distribution function. We next used the Gaussian copula to model the correlation between unobserved random shocks in the number of apps used and the app usage duration as follows:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} \omega_ {\mathrm{it}} \\ \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}} \end{array} \right] \sim \mathrm{N} \biggl (\left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 0 \end{array} \right], \left[ \begin{array}{c c} 1 & \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} \\ \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} & \sigma_ {\varepsilon} ^ {2} \end{array} \right] \biggr),
$$

where $\omega _ { \mathrm { i t } } \sim \mathrm { N } ( 0 , 1 )$ and $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } } \sim \Nu ( 0 , \sigma _ { \varepsilon } ^ { 2 } )$ . The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient $\rho$ denotes the correlation between unobserved random shocks in the number of apps used and the app usage duration. The proposed model is similar to Heckman’s (1979) and Lee’s (1983) selectivity models, in that it links a discrete variable to a continuous outcome. For the model estimation, we used a two-step estimation procedure, as in Heckman (1979) and Lee (1983) (See Appendix C for details).

## 5.2. Propensity Score Matching

Some factors unobservable (to researchers) may affect both users’ decision to adopt Anipang and the consumption of other apps. For example, a user who is more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with his or her prior experiences on the Kakao platform will be more likely to download Anipang and increase his or her use of other apps available on the platform. Such unobserved factors may create an endogeneity problem and bias our estimates of the Anipang adoption effect. To tackle this, we employed a propensity score matching method, which reduces such biases by making Anipang adopters and nonadopters comparable conditioned on observed variables, in our Gaussian copula-based DID analysis.

Using the TG (i.e., Anipang adopters) and the CG (i.e., Anipang nonadopters) in our sample, we implemented a static one-to-one matching without replacement to pair the adopters and nonadopters of Anipang, in which the latter are the most similar to the former under a caliper size of 0.05 times the standard deviation of the propensity scores (e.g., Rosenbaum and Rubin 1985). These propensity scores were calculated using a logit regression model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the dependent variable indicated the status of Anipang adoption (i.e., one if adopted, zero otherwise); and the covariates were each week’s log-transformed app usage time for each of 17 app categories<sup>9</sup> at weeks 1–13 (before the release of Anipang), each week’s log-transformed average usage time for each of the 17 app categories before Anipang adoption during weeks 14–27 (after the release of Anipang), and four categorical demographic variables (age, gender, income level, and education level). The propensity scores are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the predicted probabilities from the logit regression model. Our matched sample includes 410 users and 11,070 observations ( 27 weeks 410 users). From several formal tests and <sup>fi</sup>gures, we ensured that the matched TG and CG are comparable (See Appendix D for details).

Regarding the economic impact of popular app adoption, we now discuss how a mobile app platform can reap economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the release of popular apps, excluding the direct monetary value from the popular app itself. ## 7. Implications for Research and Practice

This study contributes to the extant literature related to the strategic evolution and economic growth of mobile communication platforms, which create and sustain value for their end users by cultivating an ecosystem of complementary resources. Although an array of theorizations and anecdotes have arisen to explain the interplay between such platforms and complementors, little is known empirically as to how the emergence of popular complements spawns demand spillover effects on other complementary products within a platform and subsequently in<sup>fl</sup>uences its growth and sustainability (e.g., Lueker et al. 2018, Zheng et al. 2019). In particular, limited attention has been devoted to within-platform spillovers, wherein the new involvement of a popular app as a complementary resource on a mobile communication platform affects the usage of the platform and that of other apps operating within it.

To shed light on the nature and mechanisms of such within-platform spillovers, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two forms of demand spillovers $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ backward and forward) and accordingly quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed and compared their differential effects on platform expansion. In this respect, our study advances the literature on demand spillovers (e.g., Joshi and Mao 2012, Mayrhofer 2013) by assessing the effects of “complementary shocks” (i.e., the emergence of a popular product) on immediate as well as future demand for platforms. Moreover, previous works on demand spillovers have focused on discrete measures of sales or downloads to quantify the effects of spillovers (e.g., Garthwaite 2014, Knapp et al. 2014), whereas our study employed actual product consumption, which accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ects continuous spillovers for extended periods. Given that many mobile apps are short-lived and infrequently used, the use of downloads or purchases alone as a measurement scheme may not precisely mirror spillovers in a continuous, dynamic fashion. By leveraging individual-level pane data that capture the frequency and usage of numerous apps, we conducted more accurate and dynamic assessments of the various forms of demand spillovers occurring on mobile communication platforms.

Furthermore, we theorized on the existence of spillover effects from popular app adoption on a corresponding platform by applying brand association (Herr et al. 1996), revival (Brown et al. 2003), and reinforce ment (Mick 1986, Zeithaml 1988) perspectives to the digital platform context as we conceptualized a platform as a brand consisting of complements (i.e., apps) with a shared name or title and the emergence of a popular app within a platform as a brand extension. As best we can tell, the current study is a novel endeavor that looked into brand spillovers in the context of mobile platforms. This research therefore advances the scholarship on demand spillovers in the context of platforms, both conceptually and empirically. The conceptualizations and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings derived can help platform owners make effective strategic decisions that revolve around the acquisition and management of key complementary resources for platform expansion and survival.

In the competitive mobile app market, the release and promotion of apps are critical in increasing downloads and acquiring new users. The adoption of a popular app augments app usage by driving consumers to spend considerable time not only on the apps that they adopt before popular app uptake but also on the apps that they acquire after the adoption within the platform in which the popu lar app is available. This result implies that mobile platform owners governing the release of their complements $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Facebook) can, for instance, effectively launch apps following the release of popular apps and target prospective user groups by placing in-app advertisements within previously adopted apps among users and popular apps. They may also recommend previously released but lesser-known apps or apps with fewer prior downloads to users or promote them by displaying advertisements within popular apps. Within more general mobile platforms, such as Google’s Play Store and Apple’s App Store, wherein platform owners cannot control the release of their third-party complements, complementors can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from releasing their apps and promoting them following the introduction of popular apps, which further creates value for platforms.

Platform owners can gain competitive advantage if they effectively use their own resources, including actual app usage time and the frequency of their customers. By exploring and analyzing adopters’ actual consumption levels of popular apps, platform owners can gain insights into adopter usage behavior beyond their adoption decisions. From the additional analyses (see Appendix G for details), we observed that with the increased usage time of popular apps until the previous week, the usage of new apps in the current week was elevated; and the increased consumption of popular apps stimulated users to spend more time on other apps offered by the same platform. These patterns suggest that platform owners can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the released popular apps by promoting the use of popular apps beyond the adoption.

## 8. Conclusion

This study offers unique managerial insights into the nature of popular complements offered by platforms by suggesting that the complements be considered as tools that platform owners can use to effectively manage their complements, thereby creating value for their ecosystems. Using detailed individual-level app usage data, we empirically validated the potential of platform-based popular apps as the stimuli of app consumption within the platform, stemming from the increased usage of both existing and new apps after the adoption of popular apps but more pronounced for new apps.

This study is encumbered by a few limitations that can be addressed in future research. A more comprehensive investigation of popular app adopters’ behaviors would further enlighten us on issues connected to the mechanisms with which popular apps induce users to consume other apps within a platform. Such detailed explorations would also provide further practical strategic insights in managing complementors by taking advantage of popular apps. For instance, a possible extension would be to examine whether the design of popular apps and/or the structure of platforms prior to downloading popular apps can drive the usage of other apps after the adoption of popular apps. Furthermore, our data have some limitations with respect to general ization. Compared with the overall population in South Korea, our sample included users that were relatively younger, more educated, and had high income levels.<sup>12</sup> Thus, our results may be more applicable to user segments covering individuals in their 20s and 30s who are highly educated and are high-income earners. Our data set was collected in 2012, which renders it slightly out dated; and the studied platform was at an early stage of expanding its services. Our sample is highly homogene ous in terms of the time since users joined the platform, the device type or operation systems they accessed, and their prior spending on the platform because of the data collection process. Therefore, researchers could acquire more extendable and generalizable empirical results by utilizing data derived from more recent settings, such as those including a wider range of user pro<sup>fi</sup>les in terms of age, socioeconomic level, device types, tenure with platforms, and monetary spending on platforms. Additionally, this study used observational data and is subject to an innate limitation in drawing causal relationships, compared with randomized controlled experiments. Future investigations based on different identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies are thus required. Finally, the data generated from the tracking app installed on users devices may have affected app adoption and usage behaviors, potentially raising concerns over internal validity. Nevertheless, using the tracking app is arguably more accurate than administering a survey in collecting and understanding individual users’ app usage. Notwithstanding the shortcomings acknowledged above, ongoing practical and academic research on mobile platform ecosystems initiated in the current work may prompt additional investigations of both mobile platform users’ demand dynamics and mobile platform owners’ management strategies.

## Appendix C. Two-Step Estimation (Heckman 1979, Lee 1983)

To jointly estimate the spillover effects of Anipang adoption on both the number of apps used and the app usage duration by using the Gaussian copula-based DID approach, we used a two-step estimation procedure for the bivariate selectivity model, as in studies by Heckman (1979) and Lee (1983). As the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we estimated the negative binomial or Poisson regression model for the number of apps used using maximum likelihood estimation. Next, we added the correction term that deals with the correlation between unobserved random shocks in the number of apps used and the app usage duration. We then estimated the adjusted lognormal regression model for the app usage duration using ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation. The details of our two-step estimation procedure are as follows.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ne a latent standard normal variable $\omega _ { i t } ,$ , which is related to the number of apps used $N _ { i t }$ such that $\{ N _ { i t } = n \}$ is equivalent to $\{ \mathrm { P r } ( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { i t } } \leq \mathrm { n } { - } 1 ) \leq \Phi ( \omega _ { \mathrm { i t } } ) < \mathrm { P r } ( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { i t } } \leq \mathrm { n } ) \}$ , where Φ is a standard normal cumulative distribution function. We next use the Gaussian copula to model the correlation between unobserved random shocks in the number of apps used and the app usage duration as follows:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} \omega_ {\mathrm{it}} \\ \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}} \end{array} \right] \sim \mathrm{N} \biggl (\left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 0 \end{array} \right], \left[ \begin{array}{c c} 1 & \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} \\ \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} & \sigma_ {\varepsilon} ^ {2} \end{array} \right] \biggr),
$$

where $\omega _ { \mathrm { i t } } \sim \mathrm { N } ( 0 , 1 )$ and $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } } \sim \Nu ( 0 , \sigma _ { \varepsilon } ^ { 2 } )$ represent unobserved random shocks in the number of apps used and the app usage duration, respectively, and $\rho$ is the correlation between them. Then, we can write $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } } { = } \rho \sigma _ { \varepsilon } \omega _ { \mathrm { i t } } { + } \sigma _ { \varepsilon } \sqrt { 1 { - } \rho ^ { 2 } } \epsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ by the Cholesky decomposition, given that $\epsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } } \sim \mathrm { N } ( 0 , 1 )$ is not correlated with $\omega _ { \mathrm { i t } } .$ . Thus, the conditional expectation of $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ given $\mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { i t } } = \mathrm { n }$ is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{E} [ \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}} | \mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \mathrm{n} ] = \mathrm{E} [ \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{it}} | \Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n-1})) \leq \omega_ {\mathrm{it}} <   \Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n})) ] \\ \quad = \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} \mathrm{E} [ \omega_ {\mathrm{it}} | \Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n-1})) \leq \omega_ {\mathrm{it}} \\ \quad <   \Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n})) ] \\ \quad + \sigma_ {\varepsilon} \sqrt {1 - \rho^ {2}} \mathrm{E} [ \epsilon_ {\mathrm{it}} | \Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n-1})) \leq \omega_ {\mathrm{it}} \\ \quad <   \Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n})) ] \\ \quad = - \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} \frac {\phi (\Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n}))) - (\Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n-1})))}{\mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n}) - \mathrm{Pr} (\mathrm{N} _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq \mathrm{n-1})} \\ \equiv - \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon}   \vartheta_ {\mathrm{it}} (\mathrm{n}), \end{array}
$$

where $\vartheta _ { \mathrm { i t } } ( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { n } } ) = \{ \Phi ( \Phi ^ { - 1 } ( \mathrm { P r } ( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { i t } } \leq \boldsymbol { \mathrm { n } } ) ) ) - \Phi ( \Phi ^ { - 1 } ( \mathrm { P r } ( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { i t } } \leq \boldsymbol { \mathrm { n } } - 1 ) ) ) \} /$ $\{ \operatorname* { P r } ( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { i t } } \leq \mathrm { n } ) - \operatorname* { P r } ( \mathrm { N } _ { \mathrm { i t } } \leq \mathrm { n } - 1 ) \}$ and a is the standard normal density function.

At <sup>fi</sup>rst, we estimate the negative binomial or Poisson regression model for the number of apps used (the <sup>fi</sup>rst equation in Equation (1)) using maximum likelihood estimation and compute $\vartheta _ { i t } ( n )$ . At the second step, we estimate the following model, in which $- \rho \sigma _ { \varepsilon } \vartheta _ { \mathrm { i t } } ( \mathbf { n } )$ is entered as a regressor into the second equation in Equation (1) for the app usage duration, using OLS estimation:

$$
\mathrm {T_ {it}} = \alpha_ {\mathrm{0i}} ^ {\mathrm{T}} + \beta_ {\mathrm{t}} ^ {\mathrm{T}} + \alpha_ {\mathrm{1i}} ^ {\mathrm{T}} \cdot \mathbf {t} + \sum_ {\mathrm{j}} \gamma_ {\mathrm{j}} \cdot \mathrm {I_ {itj}} - \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} \vartheta_ {\mathrm{it}} (\mathbf {n}) + \eta_ {\mathrm{it}},\tag{C.1}
$$

where $\eta _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ are random errors with mean zero. Moreover, as did Heckman (1979), we can estimate $\sigma _ { \varepsilon }$ and $\rho$ separately using the following formulas:

$$
\widehat {\sigma} _ {\varepsilon} = \sqrt {\frac {\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{IW}} + \frac {\widehat {\rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon}} ^ {2}}{\mathrm{IW}} \sum_ {\mathrm{i} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{I}} \sum_ {\mathrm{t} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{W} _ {\mathrm{i}}} \{\pi_ {\mathrm{it}} (\mathrm{n}) + \vartheta_ {\mathrm{it}} (\mathrm{n}) ^ {2} \} \text {and} \hat {\rho} = \frac {\widehat {\rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon}}}{\widehat {\sigma_ {\varepsilon}}}},
$$

where SSE is the sum of squares of residuals computed from Equation (C.1), I is the total number of users, $\begin{array} { r } { \boldsymbol { W } = \sum _ { \mathrm { i } = 1 } ^ { \mathrm { I } } \boldsymbol { W } _ { \mathrm { i } } , } \end{array}$ $\mathrm { \Delta W _ { i } }$ is the number of weeks for user i, $\widehat { \rho \sigma _ { \varepsilon } }$ is the estimate obtained from Equation (C.1), and

$$
\begin{array}{c} \Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (N _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq n)) \phi (\Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (N _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq n))) - \Phi^ {- 1} \\ \pi_ {\mathrm{it}} (n) = \frac {(\mathrm{Pr} (N _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq n - 1)) \phi (\Phi^ {- 1} (\mathrm{Pr} (N _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq n - 1)))}{\mathrm{Pr} (N _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq n) - \mathrm{Pr} (N _ {\mathrm{it}} \leq n - 1)}. \end{array}
$$

The standard errors obtained at the second step are underestimated. Thus, we compute the correct standard errors using a nonparametric bootstrap method with 100 replications (Cameron and Trivedi 2005).

To show the comparability between the treatment and control groups, we <sup>fi</sup>rst compared the differences between the means of the Anipang adopters’ and nonadopters’ covariates used for the logit regression (to compute the propensity scores) before and after the matching. As indicated in Table D.1, no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant t-statistics for users’ demographic pro<sup>fi</sup>les (i.e., age, gender, income level, education level) in the matched sample were found, whereas signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences existed in the unmatched original sample. We also did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between Anipang adopters and nonadopters in the matched sample regarding the 225 covariates, including the log-transformed weekly app usage duration for each of 17 app categories, whereas we observed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences for most of these 225 covariates between the two groups in the unmatched sample.<sup>14</sup>

In addition, there is little concern regarding the comparabil ity between the treatment and control groups in terms of the time since users joined the platform, the device type or operating systems on which they accessed the platform, and the amount of money they previously spent on the platform. First, we found no change in the time elapsed since users joined the platform because of the high penetration rate of Kakao Talk, the parent app of the Kakao platform. Only one user in the control group did not use Kakao Talk, and there were no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in Kakao Talk usage between the treatment and control groups prior to Anipang adoption (Figure D.2). Second, all users in our sample used smartphones based on the Android operating system, indicating that there is no operating system heterogeneity across users. Tablets were excluded from the sample because of the panel’ exclusive focus on Android smartphone users. Lastly, users spent little money on the Kakao platform prior to the release of Anipang because Kakao Talk and Kakao Story (two apps available prior to the release of Anipang) are both free to download and register and contained few in-app purchases (e.g., gift sending, emoticons) during the sample period.<sup>15</sup>

## Appendix G. Additional Results

We investigate how the usage time of Anipang in<sup>fl</sup>uences the usage of other apps within the Kakao platform in two ways: (1) how users’ cumulative usage of Anipang affects their exploration of new apps and (2) how the current consumption level of Anipang in<sup>fl</sup>uences the usage of other apps.
