# Human vs. Automated Sales Agents: How and Why Customer Responses Shift Across Sales Stages

Martin Adam,<sup>a,</sup>\* Konstantin Roethke,<sup>a</sup> Alexander Benlian<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. Customers in sales processes increasingly encounter automated sales agents (ASAs) that complement or replace human sales agents (HSAs). Yet, little is known about whether, how, and why customers respond to ASAs in contrast to HSAs across successive decision stages of the same sales process. Even less is known about customer responses to HSA-ASA combinations, where both agents assume distinct roles and focus on complementary tasks that are traditionally performed by only one single sales agent. Against this backdrop, this paper explores the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of increasingly common sales representative (rep) types (i.e., ASA, HSA, and HSA-ASA) on customer decisions across sales stages. Drawing on information processing theory and the literature on hedonic-utilitarian decision making, we investigate customer responses to text-based ASAs from vendor companies in two common early stages of email sales processes (i.e., sales initiation stages) when customers successively decide whether to indicate their initial interest in an offer and then, whether to provide their contact information. Specifically, we conducted two complemen tary multi-decision experiments, namely (1) a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in a highstakes sales initiation setting (n 325) and (2) a subsequent randomized online experiment to complement the real-world insights (n 408). Our core <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal reversing effect patterns of sales rep types across stages: although customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest to HSAs (versus ASAs) because of HSAs’ higher levels of social presence, they are less likely to provide contact information to HSAs because of HSAs’ lower levels of performance expectancy and effort expectancy. We also show that HSA-ASA combinations can be reasonable options for single ASAs, yet contextual features of the sales setting may affect differential customer responses to HSA-ASA combinations (versus ASAs) in each sales stage. Taken together, we uncover shifting effect patterns in customer responses to sales rep types across successive sales stages and shed light on the consecutive underlying mechanisms that explain these shifts. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for vendor companies seeking to allocate HSAs and/or ASAs effectively across vari ous decision stages in sales processes and beyond.

## 1. Introduction

Customers in various sales settings increasingly encounter automated sales agents<sup>1</sup> (ASAs) that emulate humanhuman interactions and complement or replace human sales agents (HSAs). Particularly in the early stages of vendor companies’ email sales processes (hereinafter called email sales initiation stages), customers increasingly encounter text-based ASAs that undertake customer outreach via email. For example, ASAs, such as 7Targets’s Assistant (2022), Conversica (2022), Pega’s Email Bot (2022), and Salesforce’s Einstein Assistant (2022), can initiate email communications with customers for sales offers, elicit interest, and <sup>fi</sup>nally, collect contact information to schedule sales appointment meetings for the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and <sup>fi</sup>nalization of sales offers via more personal communication channels (e.g., live chats or phone communication). The deployment of ASAs in the emai sales initiation stages is particularly consequential for vendor companies because email is one of the most effective business communication channels in technologymediated sales, often outranking websites and chats (e.g., The Relevancy Group 2017, Econsultancy 2019). Moreover, the largest number of customers are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced in sales initiation stages, making it hard to compensate for early mistakes in the later stages of the sales process. As such, it comes as no surprise that more than 50% of enterprises engage in some form of email communication automation and consider ASAs an explicit means through which to communicate with customers (e.g., Gartner 2017, Statista 2021). Given these reports and developments, deploying ASAs in sales processes can be considered one of the most relevant technological trends for vendor companies at the customer interface.

The increasing deployment of ASAs is closely connected to the ongoing transformation of business processes because of the automation of routine processes and the resulting complementation or substitution of HSAs with ASAs (e.g., Daugherty and Wilson 2018, Rai et al. 2019). In contrast to HSAs, ASAs promise unprecedented utilitarian value (i.e., functional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, goal achievement) through unique and scalable performance and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains in communication channels for both customers and vendor companies, such as through cost savings, 24/7 availability, and reduced response times (e.g., Puntoni et al. 2021, Schanke et al. 2021). At the same time, customers may anticipate drawbacks in interactions with automated agents (e.g., Dietvorst et al. 2015, 2018; Srinivasan and Sarial-Abi 2021). For instance, customers may perceive that ASAs provide less hedonic value (i.e., affective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, experiential pleasure) than HSAs, such as by displaying a lack of depth, compassion, and warmth (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022). Given these associations, customers may respond differently to ASAs versus HSAs depending on whether they prioritize hedonic or utilitarian value at different sales stages. As such, it may even be better for customers to encounter a combination of an ASA and an HSA, where both agents assume distinct roles and focus on complementary tasks that are traditionally performed by only one single sales agent (e.g., Exceed.ai 2019, Paschen et al. 2020). In one prevalent combinatorial type that emphasizes relative strengths, the HSA is presented as being in charge of customer communication, while the ASA is presented as being responsible for sales offer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations in the later stages of the sales process (hereafter referred to as HSA-ASA).

Surprisingly, although customers are increasingly dealing with ASAs, research has devoted only limited attention to examining differential customer responses to sales representative (rep) types (i.e., ASA, HSA, and HSA-ASA), particularly across key email sales initiation stages (i.e., customers’ initial interest indication and contact infor mation provision). From our review of the information sys tems literature on recommendation agents and related research, we highlight three salient issues.

First, prior studies on customers’ acceptance of HSAs and ASAs have mainly taken a monolithic view of sales processes, treating them as single-stage phenomena. For example, previous research has mainly investigated the degree to which customers accept advice and purchase products from HSAs versus ASAs (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Luo et al. 2019b). Yet, customers in various sales settings need to make subsequent decisions and thus, pass through multiple decision stages in which customers prioritize and process information differently (e.g., Bettman et al. 1998, Huang et al. 2019). Especially in the early stages of emai sales processes (e.g., Peppers and Rogers 2004, Woodpecker 2022), customers are likely to change priorities across decision stages and related decisions, as they <sup>fi</sup>rst decide (1) whether to indicate their initial interest in a sales offer before deciding (2) whether to provide contact information for further sales interactions. Given these changes in priorities, customers may respond differently to HSAs than ASAs across consecutive decision stages. Thus, it is theoretically intriguing to move beyond customer responses in single-stage sales processes and the implicit assumption of uniform and stable effects of sales rep types. In doing so, we may reveal shifting customer responses to sales rep types across successive decision stages and demonstrate that HSAs and ASAs have distinct strengths in different decision stages.

Second and relatedly, previous studies on customer acceptance of HSAs and ASAs have mainly looked at the direct effects of sales rep types on single-decision outcomes, such as whether customers accept a product recommendation or a sales offer from HSAs versus ASAs (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022). A few papers have also examined isolated effect mechanisms (e.g., trust, eeriness) that explain single-stage decisions (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Mende et al. 2019). Yet, previous research has largely neglected to explicate the distinct mechanisms underlying the potentially changing direct effects of sales rep types across consecutive customer decisions. Indeed, customers may differentially prioritize hedonic and utilitarian attributes of sales reps and thus, make different hedonic-utilitarian attribute trade-offs across different sales stages, which may explain shifting customer responses to HSAs and ASAs. The implications of potentially changing explanatory mechanisms are important: without knowing why ASAs (versus HSAs) may be superior in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing customer responses at a certain stage, vendor companies may assume they are more appreciated and thus, more effective in situations where the exact opposite is perhaps true.

Third and lastly, previous research has mainly investigated customer acceptance of different types of ASAs (e.g., Qiu and Benbasat 2009, Li and Karahanna 2015) or responses to HSAs versus ASAs (e.g., Burton et al. 2019, Castelo et al. 2019). Recent research has started to shed light on the hitherto “missing middle” in which HSAs and ASAs are not presented in isolation but in combination; that is, humans are an integral part of a sales rep con<sup>fi</sup>guration (Daugherty and Wilson 2018). For example, initial studies on HSA-ASA combinations have investigated ASAs performing the same activities as HSAs (e.g., Xu et al. 2020) and ASAs acting as subordinates to HSAs (e.g., McLeay et al. 2021, Longoni and Cian 2022). Yet, we still know little about customer responses to HSA-ASA combinations having distinct roles and performing complementary tasks, especially in situations in which the HSA is in charge of customer communication, while the ASA is responsible for sales offer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. This lack of research is puzzling because both research and practice point toward the synergistic value of employing HSAs and ASAs together, especially in distinct and complementary roles (e.g., Jain et al. 2021, Fugener et al.¨ 2022). As such, it is of scholarly interest to investigate customer responses to HSA-ASA combinations relative to HSAs and ASAs in isolation. Against this backdrop, our paper aims to address the following research question:

How and why do customers differentially respond to sales rep types (i.e., ASA, HSA, HSA-ASA) across critical sales stages (e.g., email sales initiation stages)?

To answer our research question, we conducted a multi-method investigation (e.g., Mingers 2003, Venkatesh et al. 2013) in the form of two complementary multidecision experiments. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigated how and why customers respond to sales rep types (i.e., ASA, HSA, and HSA-ASA) when making two crucial decisions in the email sales initiation stages (i.e., the initial interest indication and contact information provision). First, we partnered with the sales department of a leading European pharmaceutical company to design and implement a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment with real customers (n 325). The study re<sup>fl</sup>ected a high-stakes <sup>fi</sup>eld setting with serious consequences for both the pharmaceutical company and the customers in the form of potentially high-volume sales transactions of up to <sup>e</sup>20,000 per customer. Subsequently, we applied an analogous design in a randomized online experiment (n 408) to complement the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment with an exploratory investigation of the consecutive underlying mechanisms that explain the observed direct effects of sales rep types across the email sales initiation stages.

Based on our insights, we make three main contributions. First, in advancing prior work that has largely focused on the effects of sales rep types in single-stage sales processes (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Luo et al. 2019b), we shed light on their disparate effects across multiple consecutive decision stages. Our results reveal whether and how customer responses to HSAs versus ASAs shift, highlighting that the effects of sales rep types are not uniform across decision stages and are more complex than previously noted. Second, depart ing from studies that mainly looked at the direct effects of sales rep types (e.g., Burton et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022), we shed light on key consecutive explana tory mechanisms (i.e., social presence, performance ex pectancy, and effort expectancy) underlying the direct effects of sales rep types in different stages of email sales initiations. As such, we unpack distinct explanatory mechanisms that explain why customer responses shift across sales stages and reveal that the effects of sales rep types must be considered together with customers hedonic-utilitarian attribute trade-offs during sales proc esses. Third, our paper is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to move beyond treating customer responses to ASAs versus HSAs as two mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive choice options (e.g., Burton et al. 2019, Castelo et al. 2019). Our research shows that although promising HSA-ASA com binations (such as those in which HSAs and ASAs assume distinct yet complementary roles) can be viable options for single ASAs, contextual features of the sales settings are important boundary conditions (the when) that may shape customer responses to HSA-ASA combinations in each sales stage, and ultimately, the response shifts across stages. In doing so, we provide valuable insights into customer responses to increasingly relevant combinatorial sales rep types above and beyond HSAs and ASAs that act in strict isolation from one another. Taken together, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that vendors should carefully con sider the priorities of their clients at various decision stages of the sales process and employ the appropriate sales rep types.

## 2. Related Literature and Theoretical Background

## 2.1. Customer Responses to Automated Sales Agents

Early research on recommendation agents has treated ASAs as a specialized form of recommendation agent— namely, one that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally intends not only to recommend products but also, to initiate and close sales (e.g., Qiu and Benbasat 2009, Li and Karahanna 2015). This early research primarily analyzed customer acceptance of ASAs in isolation and without any reference groups (e.g., in contrast to HSAs), focusing on the design of their interface features, such as recommendation presentation (e.g., set size, sorting cues) (e.g., Tam and Ho 2005, Komiak and Benbasat 2006) and avatar features (e.g., anthropomorphism, explanation facility) (e.g.,

Wang and Benbasat 2007, Qiu and Benbasat 2009). Because of the increasing emergence of ASAs in sales contexts (e.g., Mende et al. 2019, Luo et al. 2021, Schanke et al. 2021), recent research has started to make comparisons between HSAs and ASAs (e.g., Burton et al. 2019, Castelo et al. 2019), investigating customer responses to HSAs versus ASAs and the related effects of these sales rep types on, for instance, perceived decision quality (Xu et al. 2017), consumption (Mende et al. 2019), and sales (Luo et al. 2019b).

More recently, scholars’ interest in the deployment of HSA-ASA combinations has been increasing (e.g., Daugherty and Wilson 2018, Jain et al. 2021). For example, studies on HSA-ASA combinations have investigated HSAs performing the identical activities as ASAs (Xu et al. 2020), HSAs acting as managers of ASAs (e.g., Longoni et al. 2019, McLeay et al. 2021, Longoni and Cian 2022), and the resulting effects of these combinations on, for instance, recommendation uncertainty (Xu et al. 2020) and feedback effectiveness (Luo et al. 2021). Indeed, researchers and practitioners increasingly consider that the most effective con<sup>fi</sup>gurations may not simply be “set-and-forget-it automations” that no longer include humans but rather, those that derive their power from combinations between humans and machines (e.g., Xu et al. 2014, 2020; Fugener et al.¨ 2022). A previously largely under-researched combination is where HSAs act in complementary partnership with ASAs, where an HSA and an ASA assume distinct roles that are traditionally performed by only one single sales agent (e.g., Paschen et al. 2020). In a prevalent type of HSA-ASA combinations, the HSA manages customer communication to elicit initial product interest and contact information in the very early stages of sales processes, while the ASA is deployed to take care of more analytical and operational tasks, such as specifying sales offers in the later sales stages. As such, these HSA-ASA combinations comprise “two parties—one human, one automated—each doing what they do best to close the deal” (Exceed.ai 2019). Both research and practice anticipate orders-of-magnitude increases in productivity and high synergy potentials through allocating HSAs and ASAs to distinct yet complementary roles (e.g., Rai et al. 2019, Jain et al. 2021, Fugener et al.¨ 2022). Therefore, HSA-ASA combinations have the potential to outperform both single sales agents and teams of same-kind sales agents (e.g., two HSAs) and thus, may be particularly appreciated by both customers and vendors.

Although existing studies on differential customer responses to sales rep types (i.e., ASA versus HSA versus HSA-ASA) provide an initial understanding of the effects of ASAs while accounting for the presence or absence of HSAs, the differential effects of sales rep types are still largely underexplored, resulting in “a surprising lack of empirical research investigating the differences” (Xu et al. 2017, p. 852). The recent surge in research on the effects of sales rep types coincides with the fact that ASAs increasingly permeate technologymediated communication channels (e.g., email, phone, chat), particularly because of their advances in conversational capabilities (e.g., natural language processing and understanding) (e.g., Benbya et al. 2021, Schanke et al. 2021). In these channels, customers are even often unable to distinguish ASAs from HSAs (e.g., Luo et al. 2019b). Yet, despite this technological progress, evidence that customers consistently appreciate ASAs over HSAs in sales processes is largely lacking. In fact, customers have indicated that they prefer HSAs to ASAs, particularly in hedonic-focused situations that are, for example, highly subjective and emotion oriented (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022). Moreover, research has mostly neglected HSA-ASA combinations in which HSAs and ASAs perform complementary tasks and are presented as one team to customers, in contrast to studies on HSAs and ASAs performing identical tasks (e.g., Xu et al. 2020) or being on different status levels (e.g., Longoni et al. 2019, McLeay et al. 2021). Lastly, previous research on customer responses to sales rep types has mainly treated sales processes as a single-stage phenomenon but rarely considered sales processes with multiple, successive stages (e.g., Xu et al. 2017, Castelo et al. 2019), although customers in the process of exploring, evaluating, and purchasing an object of interest often face multiple decisions and thus, go through different decision stages (e.g., Choudhury and Karahanna 2008). Consequently, potentially shifting customer responses to sales rep types along the decision stages in sales processes are likely and important to consider, yet hitherto, they have been largely under-investigated. Against this backdrop, it is of scholarly interest to elucidate whether, how, and why the effects of sales rep types on customer responses persist or change across consecutive decision stages of sales processes.

## 2.2. Customer Information Processing and Hedonic-Utilitarian Attribute Trade-Offs in Sales Initiation Stages

According to information processing theory (e.g., Newell and Simon 1972, Simon 1978), humans process the information they receive rather than merely responding to it. Information processing hereby mainly follows a linear sequence: humans <sup>fi</sup>rst perceive a large amount of information and process it at a super<sup>fi</sup>cial level. For deeper processing, they then prioritize the information and use attention to separate relevant from irrelevant information. Consequently, information processing is mainly about trade-offs, in that humans can only deeply engage with information when prioritizing and considering a small amount of the available information.

The marketing and information systems literature (e.g., Bettman et al. 1998, Lambrecht et al. 2011, Huang

et al. 2019) has drawn on information processing theory as a theoretical lens to postulate that as customers move through different decision stages in the sales process, they face different decisions and thus, have different priorities, trading-off information in a way that is relevant for the respective decision in the decision stage. One such trade-off is the consideration of the hedonic and utilitarian attributes of an object (e.g., good, service) (e.g., Dhar and Wertenbroch 2000, Khan et al. 2005). Hedonic attributes of an object (e.g., an appealing design of a laptop) signal hedonic value in the form of affective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that re<sup>fl</sup>ect customers’ perceptual and experiential pleasure, focusing on how the object is rewarding in itself; utilitarian attributes (e.g., reliable performance of the same laptop) signal utilitarian value in the form of cognitive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that re<sup>fl</sup>ect customers functional and instrumental (i.e., goal-related) deliberations, assessing the object as a means to an end (e.g., Botti and McGill 2011). Although customer decision making can comprise the consideration and processing of both hedonic and utilitarian attributes, customers often base a decision on the importance of either hedonic or utilitarian attributes in the respective decision stage and thus, make hedonic-utilitarian attribute trade-offs in their information processing (e.g., Khan et al. 2005, Bhargave et al. 2015). Moreover, customers in a mental state of discovering and exploring an object of interest do not randomly display hedonic-utilitarian attribute trade-offs in the decision stages but rather follow systematic trade-off patterns, in that customers usually first prioritize hedonic attributes to formulate initial preferences before they subsequently consider utilitarian attributes to engage in more granular cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t assessments (e.g., Dhar and Wertenbroch 2000, Lambrecht and Tucker 2013). For example, in e-commerce, customers tend to look <sup>fi</sup>rst to signals for hedonic attributes (e.g., word of mouth, popularity cues) and associated hedonic value (e.g., joy, excitement) to generate initial interest in the object before they look to signals for utilitarian attributes (e.g., shipping costs, price promotions) and related utilitarian value (e.g., ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t) to inform their cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis of the object (e.g., Liu and Goodhue 2012, Huang et al. 2019).

In our paper, we presume shifting customer responses to sales rep types (i.e., ASA, HSA, and HSA-ASA) in common and salient customer decisions in the email sales initiation stages because of customers’ hedonicutilitarian attribute trade-offs. In these sales settings, vendor companies typically organize the email sales initiation using a series of email exchanges, in which customers face two main customer decisions (e.g., Peppers and Rogers 2004, Exceed.ai 2019, Woodpecker 2022). Customers successively decide (1) whether to indicate their initial interest in a new sales offer in the <sup>fi</sup>rst step (i.e., an initial interest indication) and (2) whether to provide contact information to schedule a sales appointment meeting via more personal communication channels (e.g., phone, live chat) in the second step (i.e., contact information provision).<sup>2</sup> Given these two salient decisions, customers have two different guiding questions and priorities that in<sup>fl</sup>uence their hedonic-utilitarian attribute trade-offs, whereby customers care about both the sales object itself and the sales rep making the sales offer. In their decision about whether to indicate their initial interest, customers most often focus on whether the email signals hedonic attributes to assess the hedonic value (e.g., warmth) to be initially interested in building a trusting sales relationship. Subsequently, in their decision about whether to provide contact information, customers usually care about whether the same sales offer signals utilitarian attributes to assess the utilitarian value (e.g., ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency) that justify the investment of further time and effort in subsequent interactions (e.g., Dhar and Wertenbroch 2000, Dimoka et al. 2012).

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses Development

Building on customer information processing and hedonic-utilitarian trade-offs in email sales initiation stages, we argue that the sales rep type (ASA versus HSA versus HSA-ASA) in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer decision outcomes.<sup>3</sup> The gist of our theorizing is that sales rep types signal different hedonic and utilitarian attributes that customers appreciate based on the respective hedonic-utilitarian attribute trade-off in the given decision stage, causing shifting customer responses across stages. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, as depicted in our research model in Figure 2, we argue that sales rep types have differential effects on the initial interest indication (Hypotheses 1, (a) and (b)) and contact information provision (Hypotheses 2, (a) and (b)). For each stage, we hypothesize about the effects of HSAs relative to ASAs (Hypotheses 1(a) and 2(a)), hence focusing on the comparative effects of human versus automated single sales agents (e.g., Rai et al. 2019, Jain et al. 2021). Additionally, we hypothesize about the effects of HSA-ASAs relative to ASAs (Hypotheses 1(b) and 2(b)), highlighting the potential of human-machine combinations in comparison with full automation (e.g., Daugherty and Wilson 2018). Moreover, we develop our mediation hypotheses that shed light on the role of social presence as a crucial customer perception of hedonic attributes driving initial interest indication (Hypotheses

1, (c) and (d)) and of performance expectancy as well as effort expectancy as crucial customer beliefs regarding utilitarian attributes that are factored into customers’ costbene<sup>fi</sup>t calculus driving contact information provision (Hypotheses 2, (c) and (d)).

## 3.1. The Effects of Sales Rep Type on Initia Interest Indication

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of an email sales initiation, customers usually receive an email from a sales rep and are asked to indicate their initial interest in a sales offer by answering the email. At this very <sup>fi</sup>rst encounter, customers usually ask, “Do I want to begin a business relationship with the sales rep?” To answer this question, customers in a mental state of discovering an unexpected object tend to follow a systematic trade-off pattern in their information processing by <sup>fi</sup>rst looking for hedonic attributes that indicate hedonic value (e.g., affective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, experiential pleasure) to formulate initial preferences before they consider utilitarian attributes that indicate utilitarian value (e.g., cognitive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, goal achievement) to engage in more granular costbene<sup>fi</sup>t analyses (e.g., Dhar and Wertenbroch 2000, Bleier and Eisenbeiss 2015). For instance, research has shown that customers must <sup>fi</sup>rst deem the sales initiation to be trustworthy before they are open to considering the sales offer and thus, proceeding toward indicating their initial interest (e.g., De Wulf et al. 2001, Liu and Goodhue 2012).

We argue that customers respond more favorably to HSAs than to ASAs in the <sup>fi</sup>rst encounter of a sales initiation and thus, are more likely to indicate their initial interest to an HSA (versus an ASA). This is because customers are more likely to perceive more hedonic attributes (e.g., personal attention, empathy) that signal the hedonic value in an HSA (versus an ASA), which customers appreciate in business relationships (e.g., Xu et al. 2014, Tan et al. 2019). Indeed, the relationship marketing and social psychology literatures (e.g., Snyder and Fromkin 1980, De Wulf et al. 2001) indicate that customers tend to discount standardized and cold services and instead, appreciate personalized and warm interactions in the very early stages of sales processes. In these decision stages, customers usually tend to watch out for social cues as hedonic attributes that signal this desired personalized care—cues that are particularly rare and thus, sought after, particularly in text-based, technology-enabled communication (e.g., Gefen and Straub 2003, Tan et al. 2019). We assert that one of the few available social cues is the type of sales rep. Accordingly, we expect that customers interpret ASAs as capable of operating in a standardized and repetitive manner that treats every case similarly, with little to no marginal costs for the vendor company, whereas they are likely to view HSAs as a hedonic attribute signal of the vendor company’s investment to meet the unique needs of individual customers (e.g., Leung et al. 2018, Castelo et al. 2019). This is in line with previous studies pointing toward higher acceptance of HSAs (versus ASAs) in relational and personal settings (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022). Given the arguments and empirical evidence, we propose that customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest to HSAs (versus ASAs).

Hypothesis 1 (a). Customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest in email sales initiations when encountering an HSA rather than an ASA.

How do customers respond to an HSA-ASA combination relative to an ASA alone in terms of initial interest indication in email sales initiations? For HSA-ASA combinations, one widely used practice in email sales initiations is to introduce two collaborating sales agents (i.e., an HSA and an ASA) to the customer in the email who are collectively in charge of addressing customer needs (e.g., Exceed.ai 2019, Paschen et al. 2020), whereby the HSA is responsible for the customer communication and the ASA is responsible for the preparation and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the sales offer, which is presented to the customer in later stages of the sales process (Online Appendix A presents examples). As such, both sales agents assume complementary roles and thus, can signal advantages because of their specializations (i.e., HSA: hedonic value; ASA: utilitarian value) (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022).

We argue that customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest to an HSA-ASA (versus an ASA). The indication of an HSA specializing in the task of communication evokes the perception that the HSA can fully focus on providing hedonic value, allowing for more personal interactions with customers than an ASA alone, which is not only expected to signal less hedonic value than an HSA does but also has to cover both roles. Given that customers face a primarily hedonic-focused decision when expressing their initial interest in a sales initiation encounter and therefore prioritize primarily hedonic attributes, the HSA's specialization in the role of a personal contact person makes the HSA-ASA combination particularly appealing.

Previous research supports this argument. For instance, Longoni et al. (2019) demonstrated that patients accepted medical advice from a recommendation system less often when the recommendation system derived the medical advice alone relative to a human doctor deriving the medi cal advice together with a recommendation system. Thus, we predict that customers will be more likely to indicate their initial interest when experiencing an HSA-ASA combination (versus an ASA alone).

Hypothesis 1 (b). Customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest in email sales initiations when encountering an HSA-ASA combination rather than an ASA.

Why exactly should customers respond differently to HSAs (and HSA-ASAs) versus ASAs and thus, be more likely to indicate their interest? As elaborated, custom ers who follow systematic trade-off patterns in their information processing usually base their decision on hedonic attributes in very early stages of decision making to formulate initial preferences. Thus, any signal of a human and warm touch that allows the customer to infer some sense of personal commitment, investment, and relationship should increase customers’ likelihood of indicating an initial interest. Consequently, we expect that social presence (Short et al. 1976, Gefen and Straub 2003)—the perceived sense of personal and warm interactions with another human being— will be a crucial customer perception that explains why customers are more or less likely to indicate their initial interest. In general, online environments, such as email communications, are considered to lack social presence (e.g., Hassanein and Head 2007). Yet, adding digital social cues through text and pictures can convey social presence, which in turn, can lead to desirable cus tomer responses, such as trust and purchase intentions (e.g., Cyr et al. 2009, Qiu and Benbasat 2009). We argue that just such a digital social cue is the type of sales rep. As such, we hypothesize that customers perceive that social presence is largely missing in the presence of a “cold and cost-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient” ASA but is more salient in the presence of a “warm and costly” HSA (e.g., Pavlou and Gefen 2004, Dimoka et al. 2012). Consequently, customers are likely to feel a more personal connection when they recognize the presence of an HSA (versus an ASA), and thus, they will be more willing to invest further time and effort in the interaction. This is in line with previous research indicating that customers are more likely to accept recommendations for chocolate bars—a product related to hedonic consumption—the more the recommender (i.e., human, machine, or machine-human hybrid) is associated with assessing and providing hedonic value (e.g., Longoni and Cian 2022). Thus, the more a sales rep evokes social presence, the more likely a customer is to indicate their initial interest. As such, customers should be more responsive (i.e., indicate higher interest) to HSAs and HSA-ASA combinations than to ASAs alone because of their higher levels of social presence.

Hypothesis 1 (c). Social presence mediates the effect of sales rep type on initial interest indications, such that a customer is more likely to indicate their initial interest to an HSA rather than an ASA because of higher levels of social presence.

Hypothesis 1 (d). Social presence mediates the effect of sales rep type on initial interest indications, such that a customer is more likely to indicate their initial interest to an HSA-ASA combination rather than an ASA because of higher levels of social presence.

## 3.2. The Effects of Sales Rep Type on Contact Information Provision

After their initial interest has been indicated, in the follow-up email communication, customers usually need to decide whether they are willing to agree to schedule a sales appointment, resulting in the provision of their contact information to switch the communication channel from emails to cue-richer communication (e.g., live chat or phone) with the same sales rep (e.g., Peppers and Rogers 2004, Woodpecker 2022). Such an appointment for an initial sales meeting and related contact information provision are non-binding and allow the customer both to get to know the sales rep better and to more easily scout possible sales offer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations (e.g., Xiao and Benbasat 2007), all before the customer engages in more serious negotiations and faces the binding decision on whether to complete the purchase. In this non-binding situation, customers may implicitly ask themselves, “Do I want to provide contact information to this sales rep?” The decision to engage in the scheduling of a sales appointment builds on the mental processing of utilitarian attributes that goes beyond the hedonic attributes perceived when deciding to indicate their initial interest. Accordingly, at this subsequent decision stage, customers typically look for signals that justify the time and effort that must be invested in making a sales appointment with a sales rep (e.g., Bettman et al. 1998, Lambrecht et al. 2011).

We argue that customers have a lower likelihood of providing their contact information to schedule a sales appointment when they encounter an HSA (versus an ASA). Our rationale is that customers faced with the organizational and operational tasks of contact information delivery tend to focus on utilitarian rather than he donic value, such as effort minimization, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and performance, which ASAs (as opposed to HSAs) in particular provide (e.g., Huang and Rust 2018, Castelo et al. 2019). Accordingly, customers in this stage tend to process information following a trade-off pattern in favor of utilitarian rather than hedonic attributes. Literature on the phenomenon of automation (e.g., Daugherty and Wilson 2018, Rai et al. 2019, Jain et al. 2021) particularly indicates the operational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of ASAs over HSAs, which allow for vast ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains for customers and thus, for minimizing customer effort and maximizing customer convenience and performance. Moreover, ASAs are less likely to make careless mistakes or have bad days in operational tasks than HSAs (e.g., Huang and Rust 2018, Schanke et al. 2021). Therefore, we suggest that customers associate less utilitarian value with an HSA than an ASA when scheduling a sales appointment meeting, thus being less likely to provide contact information to an HSA (versus an ASA).

This argument is in line with previous research (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Logg et al. 2019) indicating that algorithms (versus humans) performing analytical, objective, and automatable tasks are not only superior in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and accuracy but are also often preferred in these areas by users. In the same vein, users typically turn to automated agents for their operations-oriented capabilities to increase ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and performance, such as setting an alarm with Siri, turning on the lights with Alexa, or getting directions from Google. In sum, we propose that customers are less likely to provide contact information to an HSA (versus an ASA).

Hypothesis 2 (a). Customers are less likely to provide contact information in email sales initiations when encoun tering an HSA rather than an ASA.

How do customers respond to an HSA-ASA combination relative to an ASA alone in terms of contact information provision? When comparing HSA-ASA combinations with ASAs, we argue that a customer is less likely to provide contact information when the sales rep is an HSA-ASA combination (versus an ASA alone). This is because customers at this decision stage tend to prioritize utilitarian over hedonic attributes and thus, believe that HSA-ASAs (versus ASAs) are less adept in providing utilitarian value that is, for example, associated with scheduling a sales appointment. Even if the HSA in the HSA-ASA combination is completely devoted to the task of customer communication and thus, to scheduling sales appointments, the HSA (versus an ASA only) is still subject to various human <sup>fl</sup>aws in performance, such as natural response delays, limited time resources, and proneness to mistakes (e.g., Huang and Rust 2018, Schanke et al. 2021). Thus, having the HSA specializing in the role of the communicator does not make the HSA-ASA combination more appealing than an ASA alone when scheduling a sales appointment. Based on the logic, we propose that a customer has a lower likelihood of providing contact information when encountering an HSA-ASA combination (relative to an ASA alone).

Hypothesis 2 (b). Customers are less likely to provide contact information in email sales initiations when encountering an HSA-ASA combination rather than an ASA.

Why exactly should customers be less likely to provide contact information when encountering an HSA (and also, an HSA-ASA combination) rather than an ASA alone? As mentioned earlier, customers at this later stage of the email sales initiation face a utilitarianfocused decision and typically follow an information trade-off that prioritizes utilitarian over hedonic attributes to engage in more granular cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t assessments. Accordingly, any signal of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and seamless goal achievement allows the customer to infer some sense of performance maximization and effort minimization, which increases customers’ likelihood of engaging in the cumbersome task of scheduling a sales appointment. Consequently, we expect that performance expectancy, a customer’s belief that the interaction with the sales rep will help to attain gains in performance (Venkatesh et al. 2003), and effort expectancy, a customer’s belief that the interaction with a sales rep is free of effort (Venkatesh et al. 2003), are two crucia beliefs that explain why customers are more or less likely to provide contact information when scheduling a sales appointment. ASAs—particularly contemporary ones (e.g., Luo et al. 2019b, Schanke et al. 2021)—are known for their operational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts over HSAs, which allow for vast ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains for customers and thus, for maximizing performance (e.g., Daugherty and Wilson 2018, Rai et al. 2019, Jain et al. 2021). Accordingly, ASAs executing operational tasks are not limited by well-known human idiosyncrasies, such as making careless mistakes, having bad or sick days, and becoming frustrated or tired (e.g., Huang and Rust 2018). Moreover, ASAs are much more <sup>fl</sup>exible and easier to use for operational routines, can better focus on the customers’ organizational demands, and require less communication with the customer to move on in the sales process. For instance, whereas an ASA can make the sales appointment meeting at any time proposed by the customer and may even talk to several customers at the same time, HSAs in charge of and even specialized in communication (i.e., in the HSA-ASA combination) are restricted to their schedule and thus, have limits (e.g., time and length of calls). Furthermore, interacting with ASAs does not require several exchanges and con<sup>fi</sup>rmations regarding the scheduling of the sales appointment meeting. Additionally, the emergence of missed phone calls by the sales rep and the potential necessity to reschedule a call are less likely with an ASA (versus an HSA). Given these differences in performance expectancy and effort expectancy, customers are less likely to provide their contact information and thus, engage in the task of scheduling a sales appointment meeting in the presence of an HSA (versus an ASA).

This is in line with previous information systems research demonstrating that performance expectancy and effort expectancy are two of the strongest predictors of users accepting technologies, particularly in utilitarian contexts (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2003, Cenfetelli et al. 2008). Relatedly, customers were found to be more likely to accept product recommendations in utilitarianfocused decisions the more the recommender (i.e., human, machine, or machine-human hybrid) was considered to be able to assess and provide utilitarian value (Longoni and Cian 2022). Thus, the more a sales rep evokes performance expectancy and effort expectancy, the more likely a customer is to provide contact information. Therefore, customers should be less responsive (i.e., at providing contact information) to HSAs and HSA-ASA combinations than to ASAs because of their lower levels of performance expectancy and effort expectancy.

Hypothesis 2 (c). Performance expectancy and effort expectancy mediate the effect of sales rep type on contact information provision such that a customer is less likely to provide contact information to an HSA rather than an ASA because of lower levels of performance expectancy and lower levels of effort expectancy.

Hypothesis 2 (d). Performance expectancy and effort expectancy mediate the effect of sales rep type on contact information provision such that a customer is less likely to provide contact information to an HSA-ASA combination rather than an ASA because of lower levels of performance expectancy and lower levels of effort expectancy.

## 4. Research Methodology

We employed a multi-method approach (Mingers 2003, Venkatesh et al. 2013) and used the complementary properties of a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment and a follow-up randomized online experiment to test our hypotheses. The <sup>fi</sup>rst study addressed the direct effect hypotheses (i.e., Hypotheses 1, (a) and (b) and 2, (a) and (b)) and was a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment (Study 1) based on a sales initiation via email communication of a real company to examine the main effects of our research model in a setting with high ecological validity and generalizability. Subsequently, we conducted a randomized online experiment (Study 2) to investigate the direct effect hypotheses in a setting with high internal validity. In addition, we aimed to explore the mediating mechanisms underlying the effects of the sales rep type on the initial interest indication (Hypotheses 1, (c) and (d)) and contact information provision (Hypotheses 2, (c) and (d)), respectively.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section 5, we describe the research context and experimental design of the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment (Study 1), followed by the experimental procedures and results. In Section 6, we present the re<sup>fi</sup>ned experimental design and measured variables for the follow-up online experiment (Study 2), concluding with the results. Finally, we discuss the implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and draw conclusions in Section 7.

## 5. Study 1: Randomized Field Experiment 5.1. Company Background and Context

We partnered with a leading European pharmaceutical company, Pharma,<sup>4</sup> to conduct a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment. Pharma makes more than <sup>e</sup>10 billion in sales every year, mainly through sales to other businesses; it sells puri<sup>fi</sup>cation and monitoring technologies (among other products) as well as related supplies and services for laboratory applications to its customers. These customers are from different geographic regions as well as from the pharmaceutical and related technological sectors. Thus, the optimal puri<sup>fi</sup>cation and monitoring technology and related supplies and services for each customer highly depend on customer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements (e.g., the chemicals used, daily puri<sup>fi</sup>cation volume needs, monitoring requirements, necessary certi-<sup>fi</sup>cations), so that sales reps usually help customers in specifying and selecting the best sales offers to optimally ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the customers’ speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs.

As a result of our cooperation with Pharma, we gained access to existing customers of the organization that had acquired puri<sup>fi</sup>cation and monitoring technology at some point between 2006 and 2018. At least one year after the sale, these customers usually require further and/or different supplies (e.g., chemicals) as well as services (e.g., repair and maintenance) for their puri<sup>fi</sup>cation and monitoring technologies, presenting additional sales opportunities for Pharma. These sales usually range between <sup>e</sup>5,000 and <sup>e</sup>20,000 without considering other potential sales, including cross-selling and after sales. However, the required supplies and services rely on open standards and thus, can easily be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed and provided by competitors, hence not representing safe sales for Pharma. Moreover, presenting such sales offers to customers usually resembles <sup>fi</sup>rst-time vendorcustomer encounters (and not encounters in long-lasting vendor-customer relationships) because (1) a long time period (i.e., at least a year) has elapsed since the sale of the puri<sup>fi</sup>cation and monitoring technology, usually without any vendor-customer interaction in between, and (2) there are high <sup>fl</sup>uctuations on both the sales agent and customer sides (e.g., job rotation, promotion, turnover). For more information on the legal and ethical situation of sales initiations via email communication particularly in Europe, see Online Appendix I.

Recently, Pharma tested deploying ASAs at various stages in their sales processes. The customer sales and service centers were at the forefront of these business transformations, so Pharma could assign any type of sales rep (i.e., ASA, HSA, or HSA-ASA) to its email sales initiation stages. In collaboration with Pharma, we designed and executed a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, unbeknownst to the participants under study (i.e., customers). The customers in our sample were observed while making real-life decisions with serious consequences for both them and Pharma. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study are subject neither to the reporting biases and demand effects inherent in survey and laboratory research nor to issues of self-selection, as we were able to observe subjects even when they did not respond to the emails at all.

## 5.2. Experimental Design

To investigate our hypotheses, we randomly assigned customers to one of three experimental conditions in a between-subjects design, where each customer received emails from one of three different sales reps (i.e., ASA Only, HSA Only, and HSA-ASA). In the baseline condition, ASA Only, the sales rep was a single sales agent that represented an automated identity, whereas in the HSA Only condition, the sales rep was a single sales agent who represented a human identity. In the HSA-ASA condition, the sales rep consisted of a team of two sales agents, where the HSA was presented as being in charge of the customer communication and the ASA was presented as being responsible for the sales offer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

In the emails to the customers, the sales reps presented identical sales offers (e.g., content, presentation) and saliently indicated their identity several times (i.e., in the introduction, farewell message, and signature). Online Appendix A presents examples of the sent emails. To rule out any confounding effects because of previous interactions between the sales reps and the customers, we ascertained that no type of sales agent had previous direct (e.g., email, call, meeting) or indirect touch points (e.g., Twitter, LinkedIn) with the contacted customers and that Pharma had no commercial contact with the customers since its sales of the puri<sup>fi</sup>cation and monitoring technology.

To ascertain the validity of the conditions, we conducted two workshops and one pilot study with Pharma. Thus, we ensured that the procedure and sent emails included content that was representative of the usual Pharma sales emails. Moreover, each email contained the same content, except for the features related to the type of sales rep. Consistent with previous research (Senecal and Nantel 2004), we ran a pretest to ascertain that customers could identify the respective sales rep type as intended and differentiate it from any other relevant sales rep type (see Online Appendix B). Consistent with previous research (Senecal and Nantel 2004), we also ran a pretest with participants from Amazon Mechanical Turk to ascertain that customers could identify the respective sales rep as intended and that they could differentiate it from any other relevant sales rep (see Online Appendix B for details). Finally, we made sure that no effects were subject to time-related confounds by verifying that all emails were sent between 10 a.m. and 11 a.m. during usual working hours, when most customers would be at their workplace.

## 5.3. Experimental Procedure and Sample Description

The experimental procedure comprised four steps, allowing us to directly examine how different sales rep types in<sup>fl</sup>uenced customer decisions in early sales initiations via email communication.

1. Pharma sent each customer an email with a general sales offer from one of our three sales reps. In this email, the sales rep requested that the customer respond by stating whether he or she was interested in a personal sales offer.

2. Each customer could indicate initial interest by answering the email directly or by clicking one of two implemented buttons (i.e., “request a sales offer” or “not interested”) within the email.

3. Customers who expressed an initial interest received another email from the same sales rep type. This time, they were required to provide both their phone number and time slots when they would be available for a sales appointment meeting via phone with the respective sales rep to specify the sales offer to meet the customers’ needs.

4. Each customer could provide the requested contact information by answering the email directly.

At this point, our data-gathering process stopped.

We applied strati<sup>fi</sup>ed random sampling to a pool of Pharma’s customers, where we ensured that each condition had the same number of customers and similar characteristics regarding gender and education. We recorded all variables via email responses. A total of 786 customers from different geographic regions and sectors in Germany were contacted. Of these, 94 (12.0%) were dead ends (i.e., mail delivery failures or customer representatives not in charge anymore), 367 (46.7%) did not respond, and 325 (41.3%) responded. Of the 325 who responded, 87 (26.8%) indicated an initial interest in the sales offer, and 238 (73.2%) indicated that they had no initial interest in the sales offer. Of the 87 who indicated their initial interest, 54 (62.1%) provided their contact information. More information on the descriptive statistics and further analyses are provided in Online Appendix C.

## 6. Study 2: Randomized Online Experiment

## 6.1. Purpose and Methods

The randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in Study 1 was characterized by high ecological validity thanks to the real-life context in which contemporary ASAs are practically applied. Yet, the Study was constrained in terms of internal validity, and we were unable to assess the underlying mechanisms that explain the direct effects of sales rep types on customers’ initial interest indication and contact information provision. We intended to address these limitations in Study 2 by corroborating the high external validity of the <sup>fi</sup>rst Study within a controlled, randomized online experiment with high internal validity. Additionally, we aimed to explore the key explanatory mechanisms underlying the direct effects of sales rep types in each of the two stages.

Against this backdrop, we designed Study 2 analogously to Study 1 regarding the experimental procedures, the design and timing of the outgoing emails, the manipulations of the sales rep types, and the dependent variables. We created vignettes and related emails according to the characteristics and context of Study 1. Study 2 mainly differs from Study 1 regarding some aspects of the experimental procedures, the sample, and the measured variables (i.e., the mediators, controls, and attention checks). The following sections will detail these differences. Online Appendix F provides more details as well as supplementary analyses and robustness checks for the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in Study 2.

## 6.2. Experimental Procedures

Following established guidelines for experimental designs (e.g., Aguinis and Bradley 2014), we used a scenario-based vignette for our randomized online experiment to simulate realistic decision making. In this vein, we instructed participants to imagine that they were going to receive a sales offer via email from a well-known company that provides products and services for puri<sup>fi</sup>cation and monitoring technologies. Thus, we created a scenario in which participants had no previous contact and hence, no purchase history with not only the sales rep but also the vendor company. We randomly assigned participants to one of three conditions that differed in terms of the sales rep type; these were identical to the three conditions in Study 1 (i.e., ASA, HSA, and HSA-ASA).

To collect sample data comparable with Study 1, we reached out to a European market research <sup>fi</sup>rm and recruited participants who worked in sales settings (i.e., relationship management, negotiation, buying products and services). We incentivized participation in two sub sequent randomized online experiments about email sales initiations with <sup>e</sup>5 remuneration. Similar to the timing in Study 1, we ensured that all links for the experiment were sent out at about 10 a.m. for the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of the experiment (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>rst decision stage) and again at 10 a.m. the day after (i.e., second decision stage). In the experiment, participants went through an experimental procedure similar to the one in Study 1, in that participants made a decision about (1) whether they would indicate their interest after the <sup>fi</sup>rst email and (2) whether they would provide contact information for scheduling a sales appointment meeting after the second email. In contrast to the experimental procedure in Study 1, however, after the participants provided their

Conversion Rate 2 (Contact Information Provision)

answers, their participation did not end. Instead, we instructed the participants to <sup>fi</sup>ll out a questionnaire, including the measures for our mediators, controls, and attention checks. Moreover, for the second decision stage, we also invited all participants who decided not to indicate their interest in the offer in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage to pretend that they had indicated their interest (e.g., Luo et al. 2019a). The reason for including interest-indicating and noninterest-indicating participants in the second stage was to analyze the hypothesized effects of sales rep type on contact information provision independently from customers’ information processing, priorities, and thus, decisions in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, thereby offering a more robust explanation for contact information provision and allowing for a more formal quanti<sup>fi</sup>- cation of the hypothesized effects of sales rep types in the second stage (e.g., Morgan and Winship 2015).

## 6.3. Dependent Variables, Mediators, Controls, and Sample Description

For our dependent variables, Conversion Rate 1 (i.e., initial interest indication) and Conversion Rate 2 (i.e., contact information provision), we used the same binary variables as in Study 1, except that we asked participants to answer a binary question (yes or no) for each decision instead of tracking real email interactions. We also accounted for and measured sample characteristics and controls (i.e., Age, Gender, Education, Company Type, Technology Sector, Technology Use at Work, Computer Programming Experience, and Prior Knowledge about Automated Agents) to check for the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Additionally, we used and adapted established scales to measure Social Presence (Gefen and Straub 2003) and Performance Expectancy as well as Effort Expectancy (Venkatesh et al. 2003) to conduct the mediation analyses as well as Curiosity (Real et al. 2000, Lee and Chen 2011) and Moral Awareness (Reynolds 2006) to rule out alternative explanations (e.g., see Online Appendix I for ethical issues on deploying ASAs in Europe). We mainly used seven-point Likert-type scales ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Finally, we included two attention checks.

## 7. Discussion

How and why do customers respond to different sales rep types (i.e., ASAs, HSAs, and HSA-ASAs) across sales stages? Our paper offers notable <sup>fi</sup>ndings that support our assertion that sales rep types have differential effects on customer decisions across consecutive sales stages. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in two studies about email sales initiations, we consistently <sup>fi</sup>nd support that (1) customers are about 95% (Study 1) and 195% (Study 2) more likely to indicate their initial interest to HSAs (versus ASAs), whereas (2) they are about 81% (Study 1) and 60% (Study 2) less likely to provide contact information to HSAs (versus ASAs). We reveal that customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest to HSAs (versus ASAs) because of HSAs’ higher levels of social presence, yet they are less likely to subsequently provide contact information because of lower levels of performance expectancy and effort expectancy associated with HSAs (versus ASAs). Although we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the shifting effect patterns for HSA-ASAs (versus ASAs) across the email sales initiation stages resemble those of HSAs (versus ASAs) in the online experiment, the results in the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment diverged. More precisely, HSA-ASAs and ASAs exhibited no different effects on initial interest indications and contact information provision. Overall, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly advance the <sup>fi</sup>eld of study on customer responses to human as opposed to computerized sales agents.

## 7.1. Contributions to Research and Practice

Our article extends the research in three important ways. First, we advance prior work by suggesting that customer responses to HSAs versus ASAs can shift across sales stages. Previous research on customer acceptance of HSAs versus ASAs embraced a fairly monolithic view of sales processes, implicitly assuming that the effects of sales rep types on decision outcomes are uniform and stable for the entire sales process (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022). Yet, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that customer responses to sales rep types cannot simply be extrapolated from one stage to another. In particular, we demonstrate whether and how customer responses to HSAs versus ASAs can shift and even reverse across subsequent stages. Our study thus paints a more nuanced picture of customer responses to sales rep types across sales stages.

Second and relatedly, we advance our understanding of the effect mechanisms that explain customers’ shifting responses to HSAs and ASAs across sales stages. Whereas previous studies have extensively investigated the direct effects of different sales agent types (e.g., Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022), the question of why these agents affect decision outcomes across sales stages has remained largely unanswered. By proposing shifting customer priorities in the consideration of sales reps’ hedonic and utilitarian attributes across sales stages, we provide a window into the mechanisms that explain why the effects of sales rep types can differ across stages. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we reveal that customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest to HSAs versus ASAs because of HSAs higher levels of social presence, whereas they are less likely to provide contact information because of HSAs lower levels of performance expectancy and effort expectancy. Explicitly revealing these hitherto missing explanatory mechanisms is important for understanding why sales rep types may operate in a more or less effective manner across sales stages.

Third, we go beyond the study of customer accept ance of ASAs in comparison with HSAs (e.g., Luo et al. 2019b) and in isolation from other sales rep types (e.g., Li and Karahanna 2015). To date, there has been little research on the “missing middle,” where HSAs and ASAs are deployed together rather than alone (e.g., Daugherty and Wilson 2018). Against this backdrop, we shed light on the effects of HSA-ASAs, where both agents specialize in complementary activities that each one is considered to do best. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our two studies reveal that we cannot unequivocally conclude whether customers respond differently to HSA-ASAs versus ASAs. We surmise that these diverging results can be explained by contextual features in the <sup>fi</sup>eld set ting (e.g., Hong et al. 2014). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in conversations with customers in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, we learned that they paid only limited attention to the profound recognition and assessment of the HSA-ASA because of the constraints of their daily work environments (e.g., lack of time and cognitive resources to process the subtleties of their emails). As such, customers tended to focus on the more outstanding and salient sales agent in the HSA-ASA (i.e., the ASA), which probably overshadowed the nuances of the combination. This observation is in line with previous research on hedonic-utilitarian decision making in that the saliency of attributes can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the trade-off between hedonic and utilitarian attributes (e.g., Dhar and Wertenbroch 2000). The dependence on such salient attributes demonstrates that although pro mising HSA-ASA combinations (such as those in which HSAs and ASAs assume distinct yet complementary roles) can be viable options for single ASAs, customer responses to HSA-ASA combinations (versus ASAs) are not simple and context-agnostic. Instead, contextual fea tures of the sales setting can crucially affect the custome responses to HSA-ASA combinations in each sales stage, and eventually, the response shifts across stages. Expos ing such diverging customer responses and thus, varying response shifts across studies represent a useful contribution because it serves to identify potential boun dary conditions (the when) of our theorizing.

Our insights also provide valuable practical lessons for vendor companies that consider presenting ASAs in several sales stages to improve customer responses and ultimately, increase sales. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings on how and why the effects of sales rep types persist or change across sales stages are important because without proper forethought, vendor companies may assume that customers will consistently appreciate ASAs in situations where the exact opposite is true. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our high-stakes <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment are particularly useful for vendor companies because they build on real customer responses. Our insights may also extend to other sales stages and contexts where ASAs and HSA-ASA combinations are increasingly emerging and consecutive customer decisions are most common, such as in credit approval or online investment processes (e.g., robo-advisory services).

Our research demonstrates that employing ASAs— either separately or jointly with HSAs—can be more or less bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in sales processes depending on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c stage of customer decision making in which they are deployed. In this vein, vendor companies need to consider not only that different sales rep types bring distinct attributes to the table but also that customers’ appreciation of these attributes shifts across the stages of the sales process. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, customers are more likely to indicate their initial interest when encountering an HSA, which emphasizes customers’ preference for rather personal human-human relationships and thus, for hedonic value during the initial sales contact. Yet, once customers are asked to provide their contact information to schedule a business appointment, they seem to prioritize utilitarian value regarding performance and effort when communicating with ASAs. Overall, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings inform vendor companies about customer responses to ASAs, as one and the same ASA can either help or hurt customer responses depending on what stage customers have reached in the sales process and what their hedonicutilitarian attribute trade-offs are. Given these differential customer responses, vendor companies are well advised to consider whether they need to emphasize hedonic value (i.e., social presence) or utilitarian value (i.e., performance and effort) when deploying different types of sales reps in their sales processes.

## 7.2. Strengths, Limitations, and Directions for Future Research

The present manuscript has various strengths, including the complementary features of a high-stakes randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment and a controlled online experiment, insights from objective customer behaviors, and the investigation of two highly consequential but under-researched consecutive customer decisions and their underlying mechanisms. Yet, like any paper, our research has limitations that provide fertile ground for future research.

First, we conducted our research in a sales setting with high transaction volumes and quite complex products. Although we believe that the core patterns of <sup>fi</sup>ndings—shifting customer responses to ASAs versus HSAs across early decision stages—can generalize to lower-stakes sales settings with less complex processes and products, future research should examine the boundary conditions of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings across different processes and product settings. More research is also required to test the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to different domains (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nance, e-government, and healthcare) and cultural and geographic contexts (e.g., the United States and China). Similarly, although our research focused on sales initiation via emails, a channel with growing relevance and broad applicability, we encourage future researchers to examine the effects of sales rep types on customer responses via alternative sales channels (e.g., chat or social media). Future research is also warranted to explore other sales-setting speci<sup>fi</sup>cities, such as sales initiator (e.g., vendor-initiated versus customer-initiated), artifact design (e.g., text- versus voice-based ASAs), and conversation content (e.g., sentiment, topic, communication style).

Second, although we found that customers responded differently to HSA-ASAs than to ASAs in the online experiment, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd these differential effects in the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment. As conjectured, the results in the <sup>fi</sup>eld suggest that customers are primarily affected by the contextual features in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and the mere presence of an ASA in the HSA-ASA combination, while the nuances of the combination were largely ignored. We encourage future research to investigate whether and how boundary conditions in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer responses in each stage as well as the resulting emergence and direction of response shifts across sales stages. Moreover, it will be interesting to investigate whether and how customer responses to ASAs and HSA-ASA combinations will change once ASAs have further developed their capabilities and/or customers have tested, experienced, and acquainted themselves more with ASAs. In the same vein, ethical concerns will increase in relevance, such as the truthful disclosure of the sales rep identity (see Online Appendix I for an extended discussion). Taken together, we highlight the importance of conducting additional <sup>fi</sup>eld studies, such as those based on longitudinal and qualitative research designs, to enhance our understanding of the reasons for customer responses to ASAs and HSA-ASA combinations.

Third, we mainly focused on two salient consecutive decisions in the early stages of email sales processes, namely the initial interest indication and contact information provision, which are highly consequential for later stages of the sales process. Future studies could explore customer decisions after the email sales initiation (e.g., evaluation and purchase decisions) and beyond the sales closure (e.g., customer complaints and product returns). Accordingly, researchers could investigate other dependent variables (e.g., brand perceptions, revisits, and repurchases), mediators (i.e., customer engagement and behavioral uncertainty), and moderators (e.g., customer traits, such as social anxiety or need for control) that may be more pertinent in other decision stages and product settings. In this vein, future research may also investigate how to shape the hedonic and utilitarian attributes of the sales rep type to improve customer responses, such as emphasizing social cues (e.g., anthropomorphic features or avatar representations) to increase customers’ perceptions of social presence in hedonic-focused decisions or utilitarian cues (e.g., fast delivery speed or high service quality) to evoke performance and effort expectancies in utilitarian-focused decisions. Moreover, it would be interesting to see whether it is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial or detrimental for vendor companies to keep the sales rep type consistent throughout the email sales initiation stages (which was the focus of our paper) or to dynamically alternate between sales rep types across stages. In this way, vendor companies may explore what factors customers value most when making their respective decisions and thus, determine the best sales rep types for each stage, adhering to the principle of “deploying the right sales rep at the right time” to best meet customer needs. In this regard, more in-depth research is also warranted to investigate differences in customer responses to varying combinations of HSAs and ASAs (e.g., in complementary or substitutive peer roles and in subordinate or superordinate roles). Finally, researchers may go beyond the sales context, as it is likely that responses shift across stages in entirely different decision contexts, such as responses of employees to human versus automated managers in organizational settings (e.g., Benlian et al. 2022) or patients responding to human versus automated physicians/assistants in medical care (e.g., Longoni et al. 2019).

In conclusion, we believe that there is much to learn about the applicability of human and/or automated agents in sales processes and beyond, a topic that is being increasingly discussed across industries and geographies. We have only scratched the surface of the myriad challenges and opportunities faced by customers and vendors. More collaborative work between academics and practitioners is needed to help decision makers leverage and extend our insights. We offer this work as an important step toward building the foundation for future research and practice in this arena.
