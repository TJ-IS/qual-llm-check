# Value of Information Sharing via Ride-Hailing Apps: An Empirical Analysis

Abstract. A ride-hailing platform is an app-based, two-sided platform that matches riders with vehicles via information technology (IT). In 2015, the Shanghai government introduced a policy to restrict taxi drivers’ access to and acceptance of ride requests via ride-hailing apps during certain hours. We conceptualize this policy shock as the restricting of information sharing enabled by IT and collect comprehensive data on various uses of transportation to gauge the economic benefits of this information sharing for existing capacity and its subsequent externalities on other transportation. Through a time series analysis, we identify significant decreases in the ridership of an affected taxi fleet during times of enforcement but significant increases at some times of nonenforcement postlaunch. Furthermore, the traffic on public transportation, via the city’s transportation cards, and the congestion on the surface streets and expressways significantly increase after launching the policy during both enforcement and most nonenforcement times. These suggest that information sharing via ride-hailing apps can improve the utilization of existing taxi capacity, which further alleviates traffic during alternative times and the burden placed on alternative transportation modes. Interestingly, our mechanism analysis shows decreased profitability after the restriction, which supports the notion that information sharing via ride-hailing apps reduces drivers’ search cost and thus enables them to match not only with more orders but also with those of higher marginal profit. This study contributes to the literature on ride-hailing platforms’ impact and the economic value of information sharing and IT by dissecting the compound ride-hailing’s impact to extract the value of information sharing enabled by IT and to reveal the underlying mechanism. Practically, we evaluate the policy studied, make postre vision suggestions for general contexts, and provide managerial insights on precise policy making that best extracts the economic value of information sharing in ride-hailing and general forms of online two-sided platforms.

## 1. Introduction

A ride-hailing platform, such as Uber, Lyft, Hailo, and DiDi, is a two-sided platform that connects two distinct user groups, namely drivers and riders, via information technology (IT) (California Public Utilities Commission 2013). The app-based mobile IT enables information sharing that allows drivers to receive information about potential riders (e.g., pick-up points, destinations) and riders to access the driver’s information (e.g., wait time, nearby vacancy, vehicle information, rates). Users of one side can reach those on the other side who are otherwise unreachable in traditional practices (e.g., roadside hailing). This information sharing also can enable or synergize other advanced IT features, such as a global positioning system (GPS), digital maps and routing, and automatic online pay. Thus, the value of such information sharing is of interest to information systems researchers (OECD/ITF 2016, Deighton-Smith 2018).<sup>1</sup>

This value, however, is easily overshadowed by the potentially stronger impact of the ride-hailing platform, specifically the rapid rise in the supply of ride sharing. This increase in supply is often perceived as the rise of illegal taxis due to lagging regulation, which disrupts the incumbent industry and causes concerns about rider safety, increased traffic congestion, and reduced use of public transportation, among others (see Online

Appendix A1 for a review). Such concerns influence public perceptions of the societal impact of ride-hailing platforms and affect platform design and policymaking by regulators regarding the use of such platforms. For instance, in 2016, Austin, Texas, banned the operation of the two biggest ride-sharing companies, Uber and Lyft, for refusing to abide by the city’s regulation that required drivers’ fingerprints as a means to protect public safety (McPhate 2016). Policies such as this, however, undermine the value of information sharing. To best extract the economic value of ride-hailing platforms while eliminating their adverse effects, practitioners must disentangle the economic value from different aspects to formulate more precise policies.

This study examines how information sharing via ride-hailing apps improves the utilization of fixed capacity to justify the economic value of information sharing and IT. Specifically, we leverage a natural policy shock that restricts taxi drivers’ access to and acceptance of ride requests via ride-hailing apps during certain periods of the day that was launched in Shanghai, a well-controlled ride-hailing market in which the platforms work mainly with licensed taxi drivers. The capacity of the policy-affected taxis does not vary, with said taxis’ operating traditionally without information sharing powered by ride-hailing apps, resulting in a policy shock that can be conceptualized as a restriction on information sharing via ride-hailing apps (see Section 2 for the conceptualization). We then build an empirical strategy based on time series analysis to draw causal evidence on the contemporaneous effect and temporal spillover of information sharing via ridehailing apps on the utilization of fixed capacity. To obtain strong evidence, we further investigate the secondary impact of the policy, that is, the externalities on alternative transportation uses.

The estimation results reveal findings that remain robust under multiple specifications and falsification tests. First, we show a significant decrease in operational efficiency for the taxi fleet during the enforcement time when information sharing via ride-hailing apps is restricted but a significant increase during some nonenforcement times. Second, we find that the traffic on various forms of public transportation significantly increases after launching the policy during both enforcement and most nonenforcement times. Finally, we show that the congestion on the surface streets during most times and the traffic on the expressway during both the policy enforcement periods and some nonenforcement times significantly increase after launching the policy, indicating increased use of vehicles other than the affected taxis. All the significant changes during nonenforcement times are more salient when they occur closer to the enforcement periods and gradually diminish as they are more distant from the enforcement periods. The findings suggest that restricting information sharing via ride-hailing apps decreases the utilization of fixed taxi capacity and causes traffic spillovers to alternative times and transportation modes, which generates a burden on the use of alternative transportation. Hence, the findings successfully isolate and extract the efficiency gain attributed to information sharing via ridehailing apps.

We find further support for the underlying mechanism of increased search costs by proposing and testing changes to drivers’ average travel distances that reflect the changes to the quality of orders. Specifically, con sidering that the taxi-fare system provides higher marginal profits for orders with a shorter distance (i.e., “high type” orders), drivers with shared information, including the order distance, ride-hailing apps that operate during rush hour (i.e., low risk of being idle) can select and, thus, more easily (i.e., with low search costs) match with their “high type.” The policy that restricts this information sharing is therefore expected to increase drivers’ average travel distances. Our empirical evidence is in accordance with our expectation and thus, from the quality perspective, supports the mechanism explanation that information sharing via ride-hailing apps reduces drivers’ search costs and thus improves allocational efficiency.

The primary contribution of this study is to provide among the first field-based estimates of the value and externality of information sharing, including various IT artifacts enabled or synergized by such sharing, in ride hailing apps.<sup>2</sup> Earlier studies on the value of ride-hailing platforms examine the combined economic value of the availability of ride-hailing services and attribute their value primarily to the rapid increase in service capacity that ride sharing promotes, or its consequences, for example, substitution or complementary effects (Gong et al. 2017, Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Pan and Qiu 2018, Li et al. 2021a, 2021b, Zhang et al. 2022). Another research stream contrasts ride-hailing services with their traditional counterpart, that is, taxis (Zheng et al. 2020) and explains the difference between the two through the combination of IT, a compensation model (Angrist et al. 2017), regulation (Hall et al. 2018), flexibility (Crame and Krueger 2016), and other factors. Broader research considers the value-added of features of ride-hailing, such as dynamic pricing (Cohen et al. 2016, Lam and Liu 2019) and dispatching/matching (Feng et al. 2021). Despite being an essential element in ride-hailing platforms and a key concern of information system research ers, the value of information sharing and IT has been understudied in the extant literature (OECD/ITF 2016, Clewlow and Mishra 2017). Our study dissects the combined value of ride-hailing platforms to extract the value of information sharing and IT in ride-hailing apps. Doing so not only deepens the understanding of ridehailing platforms’ value creation but also justifies the value of IT in ride-hailing across the broader transportation spectrum and thus enriches the literature on the economic value and societal impacts of IT (Banker et al. 1990, Oliner and Sichel 2000, Cheng et al. 2020).

Our second contribution is to provide among the first empirical evidence that information sharing via a digital infrastructure can reduce seller-side search costs and gain allocational efficiency in on-demand platforms. Earlier studies on the value of information sharing for sellers consider mainly the operating cost reduction from an interorganizational perspective (Barrett and Konsynski 1982).<sup>3</sup> Regarding the search cost reduction, the literature focuses primarily on the buyer side in online marketplaces (Bailey and Bakos 1997, Bakos 1997, Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000). The conclusions, however, do not suit our context due to differences in the situation and aptitude under study. Unlike reducing buyers’ search cost, which is deemed to increase allocational efficiency by alleviating the information asymmetry problem (Akerlof 1978, Bakos 1997), reducing the search cost of sellers in an on-demand platform may induce adverse behaviors in sellers’ “cherry-picking” regarding that efficiency, including strategic idling (i.e., waiting for a better ride) in ride-hailing contexts, which, in some cases, lessen the total efficiency (Chu et al. 2018). Allon et al. (2012) demonstrate a difference in allocational efficiencies between on-demand platform designs and the traditional ones where buyers make seller selection decisions, using analytical modeling. In addition, the ride-hailing situation also differs from that of online marketplaces, not only in terms of background factors that can be synergized by the information sharing but also in terms of features enabled by the information sharing.<sup>4</sup> These factors and features can have salient effects, including moderation effects and mediation effects, and thus result in findings that are quite different from those seen in the extant literature. Our empirical investigation fills in the gaps on both directions and adds the ride-hailing context to the literature on the value of information sharing.

Finally, our findings provide important policy implications for ongoing discussions on the value and designs of information sharing. It provides an evaluation of the information sharing restriction policy (hereafter, “the policy” or “ISRP”) and thus suggestions for its postimplementation revision. It also provides managerial insight into how policymakers should enact precise policies to best extract the economic value of information sharing for ride-hailing platforms and, more generally, other forms of online two-sided platforms.

## 2. Information Sharing Restriction Policy and Effect Conceptualization

This study leverages the ISRP issued by the Shanghai Municipal Transportation Commission (SMTC), the department that oversees the city’s transportation, as a quasi-natural experiment to isolate the impact of information sharing via ride-hailing apps. The SMTC found that, after the entry of ride-hailing apps, roadside riders experienced increased difficulty in hailing a taxi. To make taxis more available to roadside riders, the SMTC reset the market structure to how it was prior to the use of ride-hailing apps. Under the reset policy, 30,000 taxis that belong to Shanghai’s five largest taxi companies, which account for three-fifths of the taxi capacity in the city, are equally divided into five groups, based on the last digit of the taxi’s plate number. Every workday, the taxis in one group, roughly 6,000 total (12% of all taxis in Shanghai), are restricted from accessing ride information and accepting ride requests via a ride-hailing app during the periods of 0730–0930 and 1630–1830 hours, when roadside hailing is in high demand, and must accept riders in the traditional manner, for example, roadside hailing and assignments from dispatch centers. This policy was issued as an executive order, effective April 13, 2015 (Yue 2015), and was immediately enforced by participating companies, drivers, and traffic police.<sup>5</sup>

We conceptualize this policy as a restriction on information sharing via ride-hailing apps during the enforcement times, wherein information sharing is defined as drivers’ access to the demand information (i.e., ride requests) and riders’ access to the information about drivers through such apps. Although ride-hailing apps in our context are similar to Uber and Lyft in terms of information sharing, functionality, ride-hailing process, and a strong presence, these apps differ from Uber and Lyft in terms of service providers: due to regulation, only taxi drivers can provide ride-hailing services, and their capacity/supply is unaffected by the policy.<sup>6</sup> Also, at the focal time of this study (i.e., mid-2015), the ridehailing practices were still at their early-mid stage, and for drivers, the major function was to enable their access the ride requests beyond the traditional ones (e.g., roadside hailing). Other features/functions, as summarized in Table A2, are limited. In fact, the policy does not restrict drivers from using other functions in ride-hailing apps that can be used separately from information sharing or other apps on their smartphones or smart devices. Hence, the policy is equivalent to the elimination of information sharing associated with roughly 6,000 ridehailing service providers via their apps but does not change the ride-hailing (taxi) capacity. The affected drivers cannot access the demand information (i.e., ride requests) that are available only through a ride-hailing app, and, due to the absence of the affected drivers from such apps, the drivers’ information also is not available to rider-side users of the apps.

Nonetheless, it is worth noting that the value of information sharing further encompasses the value of a wide range of functions that are derived from or fully enabled by information sharing and the synergies between other functions and information sharing, that is, the differences between when the other functions are synergized with the information versus when they are not (e.g., separately used in different apps).<sup>7</sup> Regarding the synergies, although some functions (e.g., online payment, navigation) are available to drivers for their traditional operations via alternative apps, they may become more convenient to use when integrated with the information shared. From a causality perspective, the effects of such enabled functions and the synergy correspond to the mediation effect and moderation effect, respectively, and are parts of the causal effect of information sharing. Beyond the synergies and mediators, the policy incurs no other changes. Specifically, due to the tight regulations on taxi/ride-hailing markets, the policy does not trigger any differences in service quality, dispatching methods, fare rates/metering, payment methods, commissions, and so forth.<sup>8</sup>

## 3. More Context, Data, and Model-Free Evidence

Another policy change, namely the Rush Hour Extension (hereafter, “the extension” or “RHE”), may confound the ISRP. This policy change was launched by the SMTC two days after the ISRP policy, on April 15 (Yan 2015), and extends the rush hours from 0730–0930 and 1630–1830 hours every workday to 0700–1000 and 1600–1900 hours. Residents of the city rely heavily on the expressways for daily travel between their workplace and home. To ensure smooth trips in the city, private vehicles registered outside of the city of Shanghai, temporarily registered vehicles, vehicles operated by new drivers, and vacant taxis are restricted from driving on the expressways during the rush hour periods designated by the SMTC. This policy thus aims to reduce traffic congestion on the expressway during the extended hours. In Sections 4.1 and 4.2, we present how we tease out the potential confounding effect of the policy. In addition to this change and the ISRP, no other transportation-related policies came into effect during our observation window.

To estimate empirically the policy impacts on macrolevel transportation systems, we construct a unique data set that contains information from four official sources for an observation window of April 1–30, 2015. Table 1 shows the four organizations that host raw data, and Online Appendix A2.2 provides the details of their data collection. Shanghai Qiangsheng Holding Co., Ltd., is the largest taxi company in Shanghai and a major participant in the ISRP, with a fleet that accounts for roughly 43% of the affected taxis. Although the data do not capture all the taxis in Shanghai, it has fair representability due to the high degree of homogeneity across taxis from different companies and the fact that the policy is homogeneously applied among the participating taxi fleets across different companies.<sup>9</sup> Shang hai Public Transportation Cards (SPTCs) are the only issued transportation cards in Shanghai and account for the majority of Shanghai’s public transportation ridership. In 2015, the year of our observation, more than 80% of the ridership on buses and subways was through SPTCs, and this percentage is more than 90% during the morning and evening rush hours.<sup>10</sup> Thus, the data provide an ideal representation of the general public transportation use.<sup>11</sup>

We construct a half-hour index i by dividing 24 hours into 30-minute intervals. For instance, the half-hour index for 0100–0130 hours is zero, 0130–0200 hours is one, and so forth. The day index t is each day in April 2015, ranging from 1 to 30. As a result, we have 48 halfhour indices for 30 days, resulting in 30 × 48 � 1,440 time-series observations. Each observation corresponds to the dependent variables (used in the main analysis, robustness checks, mechanism analysis, and extended analysis), the independent variables, and control variables, as described in Table 2 and Online Appendix A2.3 (which provides a detailed textual description). The summary statistics are found in Table A2.

We develop visualized baseline estimates to explore the impact of the ISRP on the dependent variables. Recall that the RHE is applied two days after the ISRP. We could therefore compare the average Taxi for each half-hour over days under one of the three following conditions: when no new policy applies (i.e., before day 13); when only ISRP applies (i.e., day 13 and 14); and when both the ISRP and the RHE apply (i.e., after day 14). The ridership differences between the conditions involving the ISRP and those not involving the policy indicate the impact of ISRP. Using the same approach, we also plot the trends of average public transportation ridership via SPTC (including Metro, Bus, Ferry, and Park & Ride) and average congestion level (including CongFree and CongSurface) to examine the externality of the ISRP on public transportation and overall vehicle use. The results are shown in Figure 1. The solid, dashed, and dotted curves represent the ridership/ congestion levels when no policy is enforced, when only the ISRP is activated, and when both the ISRP and the RHE are applied, respectively. The two narrow areas between the pairs of vertical dashed lines represent the two enforcement periods of ISRP, that is, 0730–0930 (13≤ i ≤ 16) and 1630–1830 hours (31≤ i ≤ 34). The dashed curves (i.e., only ISRP) are calculated as the average of data points over only two days, which is much less than the other two curves and thereby exhibits more randomness and violent fluctuations. Therefore, we focus more on the comparison between the solid curve (i.e., no policy) and the dotted one (i.e., two policies).

Figure 1(a) shows the average Taxi for each half-hour over days under the three different conditions and, surprisingly, suggests no salient pattern, as we may have expected. The dotted curve is generally above the solid curve throughout, which counterintuitively suggests that the two policies, when applied simultaneously, significantly increase the taxi ridership, even during their nonenforcement times. One possible confounder that causes this is time trend/seasonality. For example, the days used to calculate the dotted curves may be busier overall or may include more Fridays, which are typically busier than other days of the week. Hence, we attempt to remove the time trend by fixing the shape of each curve and parallelly moving both the dotted and dashed curves until the cumulated spaces under the three curves are equal, that is, the average daily ridership under the three conditions is the same. It is worth noting that this processing largely resembles the demean approach for teasing out time-fixed effects in econometric models.

The resulting average Taxi after adjusting the time trends is shown in Figure 1(b). The dotted and dashed curves drop below the solid line during both enforcement periods in the results, suggesting that the two policies decrease the taxi ridership on the fleet. Interestingly, both the dotted and dashed curves immediately pick up and are higher than the solid one right before and after the morning enforcement period and then gradually diminish as time elapses, which implies the temporal spillover effects. There is, however, no similar phenomenon during the afternoon enforcement period; the dotted curve largely overlaps with the solid one outside the enforcement period.

## 4. Models

Considering public transportation ridership via SPTC, Figure 1(c) shows that the dotted and dashed curves are both higher than the solid one and that the differences are particularly salient during both enforcement periods, indicating that the ISRP and the RHE significantly increased the public transportation ridership via SPTC. Moreover, we find the differences extend beyond the enforcement periods to nearby ones, especially during times before the morning enforcement and after the afternoon enforcement, and gradually diminish in magnitude as time elapses, indicating the temporal spillover effects on public transportation. The city-wide congestion level, as seen in Figure 1(d), exhibits comparable patterns as those for public transportation via SPTC, indicating similar impacts on total vehicle use. We omit the processing for Figure 1(b) because the differences in both figures during the enforcement periods and those that closely precede or follow are more salient and are therefore expected to exist even after moving the curves in parallel to tease out the time trends.

Despite our preliminary insights, concern remains because we cannot cleanly isolate the ISRP effect from that of the RHE by comparing the solid curve with the dotted one. Theoretically, we can further leverage the dashed line to difference out the ISRP from the RHE effect. However, such a scheme is challenged and blurred by the violent fluctuations of the dashed line caused by the small size of its sample. Moreover, our visualization can hardly avoid the confounder of halfhour specific unobserved heterogeneity. We therefore construct an econometric model to cleanly isolate the impact of the ISRP.

Our core interest is to examine the effects of information sharing via ride-hailing apps on the utilization of existing taxi capacity. The ISRP only limits existing taxi drivers’ access to information sharing via ride-hailing apps. Thus, we can attribute the effects and externalities of the ISRP (policy effects) to those of information sharing via ride-hailing apps. In particular, we capture four dimensions of the policy’s effects: main, horizontal, vertical, and mixed effects. We define main effects as the direct and contemporaneous impact of the policy on Taxi when enforced and horizontal effects as contemporaneous policy effects on the variables of alternative transportation modes, that is, Metro, Bus, Ferry, and Park & Ride, when enforced. Vertical effects represent the impact of the policy on Taxi at times with no policy enforcement, which captures temporal spillover effects. Finally, mixed effects are the impacts of the policy on variables of alternative transportation modes at a time without policy enforcement, which is the combination of horizontal and vertical effects.<sup>13</sup> We discuss how we specify and identify these effects next.

## 4.1. Main and Horizontal Effects of the ISRP

We introduce our identification strategy for the main and horizontal effects of the ISRP. These two types of effects use the same identification strategy but for different transportation mode variables, that is, Taxi and variables of alternative transportation modes, accord ingly. We combine the discussions of their identification strategies, and, in the following, we discuss only the main effects. Using a within-day half-hour index i on the x axis and a daily index t on the y axis to represent the observation period in a two-dimensional space, we visualize our identification strategy in Figure 2. 14

Our focal ISRP has two enforcement periods, 0730– 0930 and 1630–1830 hours, starting on April 13, 2015, which are denoted by a dummy indicator T (and the corresponding shading), as specified in Section $3 , ^ { 1 5 }$ for each 30-minute period. The major identification challenge stems from the potential confounding effect of the RHE that starts two days after the implementation of the ISRP, on April 15, 2015. Although the policy does not significantly or directly affect taxi operations, it has an indirect effect by having an impact on the operation of other vehicles. In doing so, changes to the congestion level on the surface streets and expressways occur, which has an impact on the taxis. In addition, although imposing mainly a change at the next half-hour immediately preceding and following each enforcement period of ISRP (i.e., 0700–0730, 0930–1000, 1600–1630, and 1830–1900 hours), as denoted by the dummy indicator RE and the corresponding shading (see the definition in Table 2), according to the policy description (see Section 3), the RHE also can impose a subsequent spillover effect to the nearby time periods, such as the enforcement periods of ISRP after the RHE, denoted by the dummy indicator RO (and the corresponding shading).<sup>16</sup> As observed, T overlaps largely with the RO; thus, a large portion of T is double-shaded (i.e., as $T + R O ) . ^ { 1 7 }$ This spillover effect (i.e., the impact of RO), if omitted, could cause biased estimates of the main effects (i.e., the impact of T). Such a spillover effect can be different from the impact of the RHE during RE (i.e., the impact of RE) and thus should be specified differently.

Accordingly, the impacts of T, RE, and RO are denoted as $\beta _ { 1 } , \beta _ { 3 } ,$ and $\dot { \beta _ { 4 } } , ^ { 1 8 }$ respectively. By taking the difference between $T + R O$ and the nontreated (non shaded) areas, we can identify the combined effect of T and $R O \ ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \beta _ { 1 } + \beta _ { 4 } )$ ). We also can, however, identify $\beta _ { 1 }$ separately by taking the difference between the nonoverlapping $\dot { T }$ (with only T shaded) and the nontreated (nonshaded) areas and further isolate $\beta _ { 4 }$ by taking the difference between $\beta _ { 1 } + \beta _ { 4 }$ and $\beta _ { 1 }$ . We need to clarify that, due to the “within” nature of RO that overlaps with T, an interactive effect between these two policy changes is absorbed by $\beta _ { 4 }$ and cannot be separated from the effect of RO. However, our objective is to identify the causal effect of T that does not coexist with RO counterfactually, and RO is included to control for con founding bias.<sup>19</sup> This identification strategy satisfactorily addresses our needs.

Furthermore, we note that day-specific and timespecific fixed effects could be incorporated into the model so that T (also RE and RO) are not confounded by day-specific unobserved factors and time-specific unobserved factors. Because we can always find within-halfhour variations and within-day variations in $T , T + R O ,$ and $R E , ^ { 2 0 }$ all $\beta _ { 1 } , \beta _ { 1 } + \beta _ { 4 } ,$ , and $\beta _ { 3 }$ are identifiable after subtracting the day-specific average across half-hour periods and the half-hour specific average across days. It is worth noting that, after teasing out the effects of the RHE, the remaining effect of the rush hour restrictions (which also applies to days before RHE) is time specific, persistent across days, and therefore part of the seasonality/time trend. The fixed effects eliminate the time trend/seasonality, including such remaining effect. Finally, the identification of $\beta _ { 3 }$ is straightforward. Although it does not matter with the identification of the main effects, teasing it out results in a cleaner identification of the vertical and mixed effects, which are discussed next.<sup>21</sup>

## 4.2. Vertical and Mixed Effects of the ISRP

Both the vertical and mixed effects are policy effects on alternative periods but differ on the targeted variables of transportation modes. Therefore, similar to the identification strategies for main effects and horizontal effects, we apply a similar specification of vertical effects on alternative transportation modes to identify the mixed effects. Thus, in the following, we only discuss the vertical effects.

We denote the vertical effects as $V E _ { i t }$ and take into account four considerations in its construction. First, by our definition, vertical effects occur only before or after the time ranges of policy enforcement. Thus, no vertical effects are present during the policy enforcement periods. Hence, we define $D _ { 1 i }$ and $D _ { 2 i }$ as the temporal distances between half-hour i and the 0730–0930 hours enforcement period and the 1630–1830 hours enforcement period, respectively; $D _ { 1 i }$ and $D _ { 2 i }$ are positive integers, as they are temporal distances that indicate absolute differences between half-hour indices.

Second, we assume that each vertical effect is monotonically decreasing.<sup>22</sup> The nature of vertical effects is the substitution of time to travel, so we expect riders will prefer alternative periods that are closer to their planned periods. That is, a rider who originally planned to travel at 0900 hours might be more likely to travel at 1000 hours than 1800 hours when affected by the policy. Therefore, we hypothetically expect diminishing vertical effects as time progresses further away from one enforcement period and for absolute-monotonically decreasing functions to apply, for example, exp $( - D _ { \cdot i } )$ and ${ D _ { \cdot i } } ^ { - 2 }$ , on temporal distances to capture the diminishing shape, denoted as $f . ( D _ { \cdot i } )$ . These shape functions increase when period i approaches the corresponding enforcement period but decrease as i moves further away. A magnitude parameter to estimate, $\beta _ { 2 \cdot \prime }$ , is further associated with $f . ( D _ { \cdot i } )$

Third, because the enforcement periods are the commuter time, and people cannot get to work late or leave early, we allow the vertical effects to be an asymmetric or even a uni-directed shape before and after each enforcement period. To accommodate this, we allow the vertical effects to be different in terms of shape functions (i.e., diminishing rates) and magnitude before and after each enforcement period and across the two enforcement periods. We represent the vertical effects derived from the 0730–0930 hours enforcement period on times before and after as $\beta _ { 2 1 } \cdot f _ { 1 } ( D _ { 1 i } )$ and $\beta _ { 2 2 } \cdot \bar { f } _ { 2 } ( D _ { 1 i } ) .$

namely vertical effects 1 and 2, respectively, and those from the 1630–1830 hours enforcement period on times before and after as $\beta _ { 2 3 } \cdot f _ { 3 } ( D _ { 2 i } )$ and $\beta _ { 2 4 } \cdot \bar { f } _ { 4 } ( D _ { 1 i } )$ , namely vertical effects 3 and 4, respectively. We allow f $. ( \cdot ) , f _ { 2 } ( \cdot )$ $f _ { 3 } ( \cdot ) ,$ , and $f _ { 4 } ( \cdot )$ to differ from each other to accommodate asymmetry in terms of shape and provide the degree of freedom for the difference across the parameters to estimate, that is, $\beta _ { 2 1 } , \beta _ { 2 2 } , \beta _ { 2 3 } .$ , and $\beta _ { 2 4 }$ , which accommodates the asymmetry in terms of effect magnitudes. Model specification tests are used to select the specifications for $f _ { 1 } ( \cdot ) , f _ { 2 } ( \cdot ) , f _ { 3 } ( \cdot )$ , and $f _ { 4 } ( \cdot )$ from the candidate functions that lead to the best model fit (in terms of likelihood, Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which we report in Section 5.1. It is worth noting that each transportation mode has its freedom in the specification of $f . ( \cdot )$ and the identification of $\left[ \beta _ { 2 } \right]$ , so the mixed effects on any transportation mode and the vertical effect can differ from the others.

Fourth, because the times around 0100–0130 hours have the furthest temporal distance from any enforcement periods, and the vertical effects diminish as the time elapses further from the enforcement periods, the vertical effects at those times could be minimal. Intuitively, individuals who originally planned to travel during the enforcement periods but were forced to change their plans due to the policy are unlikely to pick a time close to 0100–0130 hours as an alternative because it does not overlap with working hours. For a clearer identification, we allow 0100–0130 hours and its nearby times, for example, 1230–1300 and 1330–1400 hours, to receive zero vertical effects. We further use model specification tests to determine which times receive zero vertical effects by comparing different specifications of times with zero vertical effects and finding the best fit option. The results are reported in Section 4.1. Because these periods neither receive the policy effects, they are free from any effects and consequently serve as a “control” baseline, which better justifies the isolation of both the policy effects and the vertical/mixed effects.

Finally, as the ISRP is enforced over two different periods, 0730–0930 and 1630–1830 hours, the two vertical effects derived from each enforcement period overlap at each half-hour i. After taking the four considerations into account, the final vertical effect is

$$
\begin{array}{l} V E _ {i} (f (\boldsymbol {D} _ {i}), \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {2}) \\ = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \beta_ {2 1} \cdot f _ {1} (D _ {1 i}) + \beta_ {2 3} \cdot f _ {3} (D _ {2 i}), & \text { if } \tau_ {1} \leq i \leq 1 1, t \in T _ {p}; \\ \beta_ {2 2} \cdot f _ {2} (D _ {1 i}) + \beta_ {2 3} \cdot f _ {3} (D _ {2 i}), & \text { if } 1 6 \leq i \leq 3 1, t \in T _ {p}; \\ \beta_ {2 2} \cdot f _ {2} (D _ {1 i}) + \beta_ {2 4} \cdot f _ {4} (D _ {2 i}), & \text { if } 3 6 \leq i \leq \tau_ {2}, t \in T _ {p}; \\ 0, & \text { else }, \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{1}

$$
\begin{array}{l} V E _ {r, i t} (f _ {r} (d _ {i}), \boldsymbol {\beta_ {5}}) \\ = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \beta_ {5 1} \cdot f _ {r 1} (d _ {1 i}) + \beta_ {5 3} \cdot f _ {r 3} (d _ {2 i}), & \text { if } \tau_ {1} \leq i \leq 1 0, t \in T _ {r}; \\ \beta_ {5 2} \cdot f _ {r 2} (d _ {1 i}) + \beta_ {5 3} \cdot f _ {r 3} (d _ {2 i}), & \text { if } 1 7 \leq i \leq 3 0, t \in T _ {r}; \\ \beta_ {5 2} \cdot f _ {r 2} (d _ {1 i}) + \beta_ {5 4} \cdot f _ {r 4} (d _ {2 i}), & \text { if } 3 7 \leq i \leq \tau_ {2}, t \in T _ {r}; \\ 0, & \text { else }. \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The nonparametric identification of the vertical (and mixed) effects is essentially the same as that of the main (and vertical) effects. The vertical (and mixed) effects can also be taken as an interaction term between the policy treatment and a function of temporal distance between a specific half-hour and the enforcement time, such that the parametric identification comes from the variation provided by the temporal distance, in addition to that of the policy effect, as previously discussed. Also, both the enforcement times and the temporal distances of the RHE differ from those of the ISRP. This indicates that, for a particular half-hour, both policy treatments and functions of the temporal distance vary across these two policies, which provide the additional variation for separate identification of the vertical effects of the RHE. In fact, our design can be taken as an adapted interrupted time series (ITS), where the hypothesis being tested is whether the ISRP changed the time ser ies ridership for each considered transportation mode.

## 4.3. Regression Specification

We use time-fixed effects time series models, which are close to that in Dahl and DellaVigna (2009). To estimate the main and vertical effects of the ISRP, we specify Taxi as the dependent variable for Model 1a. Considering the nature of the primary dependent variable, we use negative binomial regressions because it better accommodates overdispersion in the count distribution, which is found in our data through the overdispersion test (Cameron and Trivedi 1990) (Z-score is 19.95, with $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The equation is as follows:

$$
P (y _ {i t} \mid \lambda_ {i t}, r) = \frac {\Gamma (y _ {i t} + r)}{\Gamma (r) \Gamma (y _ {i t} + 1)} \cdot \left(\frac {r}{r + \lambda_ {i t}}\right) ^ {r} \left(\frac {\lambda_ {i t}}{r + \lambda_ {i t}}\right) ^ {y _ {i t}},\tag{3}
$$

where $\lambda _ { i t } = \beta _ { 1 } \cdot T _ { i t } + V E _ { i } ( f ( D _ { i } ) , \beta _ { 2 } ) + \beta _ { 3 } \cdot R E _ { i t } + \beta _ { 4 } \cdot R O _ { i t }$ $+ V E _ { r , i t } ( f _ { r } ( d _ { i } ) , \pmb { \beta _ { 5 } } ) + \pmb { \beta _ { 6 } } \cdot C t r l _ { i t } + \gamma _ { i } + \tau _ { t } , \gamma _ { i }$ is half-hourspecific fixed effects that control for the half-hour specific unobserved heterogeneity, and $\tau _ { t }$ is day-specific fixed effects that control for unobserved, common across all $i ,$ daily varying trends and seasonality. $C t r l _ { i t }$ includes Precipitation<sub>it</sub>, and Temperature<sub>it</sub>. Finally, r is the dispersion parameter, and $[ \beta _ { 1 } , \pmb { \beta } _ { 2 } , \beta _ { 3 } , \beta _ { 4 } , \pmb { \beta } _ { 5 } , \pmb { \beta } _ { 6 } ]$ are the coefficients among which $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\pmb { \beta } _ { 2 }$ are of interest. Equations (1) and (2) can be reformatted in a linear additive form with four coefficients each. Equation (3) thus can be reformatted as a standard generalized linear form and estimated via a standard estimator for negative binomial regression. Also, although the specifications for the main and vertical effects are pooled in one regression, their identifications do not affect each other due to clear isolation between them (see endnote 21).

## 6.2. Mechanism Analysis

To investigate how information sharing via ride-hailing apps affects taxi operations, we next propose and empirically test a possible underlying mechanism. We expect that the effect of the policy mainly pertains to the reshaping of drivers’ operations, particularly because the design of the policy only restricts the use of the app for drivers.<sup>26</sup> Specifically, prior literature shows that information sharing in electronic marketplaces creates value through reducing the consumer’s search cost and enabling allocational efficiency, that is, consumers can find and match with “high type” products that bring higher utility (Bailey and Bakos 1997, Bakos 1997, Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000). We therefore expect that limiting information sharing via ride-hailing apps increases search costs for drivers to find “high-type” riders, and consequently, drivers have a higher chance to pair with “lowtype” riders or nothing. Our main analysis has shown a higher chance of not pairing, that is, fewer riders and more idleness. Hence, we focus more on testing whether the affected drivers pair more with low type riders. Notably, such changes to the quality of riders can hardly be explained by driver-side mechanisms other than the increased search costs.

The regulated pricing scheme for the taxi industry in Shanghai encourages drivers to profit more from shorter orders. The marginal revenue for taxi rides that are less than 3 km is more than 4.66 RMB/km, whereas for taxi rides that are more than 3 km, the marginal revenue is only 2.40 RMB/km. Also, the time intervals we considered, that is, the enforcement periods of the ISRP, occu during rush hour when taxis are in great demand, and drivers should have fewer concerns regarding demand/ idleness. The taxi drivers, given their limited capacity, are expected to prefer shorter-distance orders, that is, high type, than long-distance ones, that is, low type.<sup>27</sup> In sum, with information shared, including the distance, via ride-hailing apps, drivers can select and match more frequently with the short distance trips, whereas under the policy, such an option is no longer available.

To test our proposed mechanism, we apply the model specified in Equation (5) with the dependent variable of average travel distance per ride, given that the distance is also a continuous variable that exhibits a normal shape after a log-transformation. We also consider the total distance to build a connection between the analysis of the total taxi ridership and that for average travel distance. The estimation results are shown in Table 6. The average travel distance per transaction increased significantly $( \beta _ { 1 i } = 0 . 0 1 0 , p < \bar { 0 } . 0 1 )$ , whereas the total distance decreased significantly $( \beta _ { 1 h } = - 0 . 0 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , possibly because of the decreased ridership shown in our main analysis. This complements the main result from a quality perspective to further support the mechanism of limiting information sharing via ride-hailing apps as increasing drivers’ search cost, leading to more failed matches and worse matching outcomes. Notably, mechanisms other than that of search cost and allocational efficiency can hardly explain such simultaneous changes to matching quality and the number of successful matches.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusions

Ride-hailing platforms have created a significant economic impact and resulted in externality in terms of existing transportation systems. An important question for information systems researchers is whether these effects are attributable to information sharing, including its synergized or enabled IT features, via ride-hailing apps. This study answers the question, leveraging a quasi-natural experiment of the ISRP and investigates the underlying mechanism. We find evidence that information sharing significantly improves the efficiency of existing taxi capacity. Specifically, a fixed taxi fleet within a fixed time can undertake more ridership by sharing information. Information sharing further alleviates the traffic during alternative times and the burden placed on other transportation modes, that is, metro, bus, ferry, park & ride, and non–public transportation vehicles. The mechanism analysis reveals that drivers with information sharing are advantaged, not only in terms of the quantity of matched rides, but also in terms of the quality (profitability) of the matching outcomes, which supports the mechanism of the increased search cost for drivers and thus lowered allocational efficiency when information sharing is restricted.

The qualitative findings can be considered generalizable to multilevels of broader contexts. First, although the magnitude and shape of spillover specific to a transportation mode may depend on a city’s transportation structure, we expect similar efficiency gain in the taxi operation and positive spillover to alternative time or transportation modes when equipping the taxi drivers in that city with information sharing and its enabled features in ride-hailing apps. Second, the qualitative findings also hold for ride-sharing platforms such as Uber, given the similar information sharing, its synergized or enabled IT features, and the search-cost mechanism. If ride-sharing drivers are forced to not access information sharing but rather to practice call-center or roadside hailings, the operational efficiency and thus the profitability of drivers can be lowered, which may force drivers to quit the platform and induce ridership spillover. To a broader extent, the findings of the allocational efficiency gain and positive spillover due to information sharing may apply to other on-demand, two-sided platforms in which the information sharing can reduce the search cost and empower the selectivity of the seller side (service providers). Exemplary practices include independent delivery platforms (e.g., Instacart, DoorDash, Grubhub), freelance marketplaces (e.g., Freelancer.com, Amazon Mechanical Turk), and health service marketplaces (e.g., nomadhealth).

The findings also have important implications from a theoretical perspective. Different from other studies that examine the compound impact of the availability of ridehailing services (e.g., the entry of Uber), the efficiency changes and the externality that we model and isolate are attributed to the changes in information sharing and thus are separate from alternative explanations, such as changes in the regulation, compensation, and capacity, that the flexibility of ride-sharing promotes. These changes justify the economic value of information sharing and IT and deepen the understanding of how ridehailing platforms create value. In addition, we model the spillover on the temporal dimension and its combination with the horizontal (across transportation mode) dimension, including not only magnitude but also decay shape. The empirical evidence on the presence of such spillover suggests that ignoring this dimension may lead to a salient undervaluation of the externalities. In sum, our comprehensive measures of both the intrinsic impact and the externalities of information sharing via ride-hailing apps contribute to the literature on the value of ride-hailing services and of IT.

Another salient distinction of this study, compared with prior studies on the value of information sharing, is that we consider an on-demand platform in which search cost and selectivity apply mainly to the seller side (i.e., drivers). Although information sharing can reduce the seller-side search cost for efficiency gain, as it does for the buyer side in the traditional online marketplace (Bakos 1997), it also may induce adverse behaviors in sellers’ selectivity that theoretically lead to efficiency loss (e.g., strategic idling in ride-hailing; see Chu et al. 2018). Our empirical evidence suggests that efficiency gains dominate, although they may be subject to context. Nonetheless, our study is among the first to empirically show that information sharing enabled by IT can reduce the seller-side search cost and enhance allocational efficiency in on-demand platforms, which adds to the literature on the value of information sharing. In addition, the ride-hailing situation largely differs from that of online marketplaces in terms of information-sharing-enabled features and background factors that can be synergized. Thus, this study also adds the ride-hailing context to this literature stream.

The findings also provide managerial insight into policy evaluation. Although the initial objective was to protect offline riders, the cost of the policy, shown as the decreased ridership of the affected taxis and negative externality to the incumbent users of other time or transportation modes, was exorbitant, as it indicates that there is an even lower chance of taking a taxi for the entire taxi market and more congestion in other transportation modes. To this end, encouraging and supporting offline riders to be involved in using ridehailing services is a better option than restricting drivers use of these services. This implication could be applied to various ride-hailing (including taxi) markets similar to our context<sup>28</sup> and even other IT-disrupted, two-sided markets when investigating whether to restrict IT to protect users who are particularly disadvantaged by IT.

Regarding broader policymaking, our evidence suggests that policymakers and regulators should exempt the information-sharing aspect from restrictive policies that aim to prevent any negative and disruptive impact of ride-hailing services, as such an exemption is not only valuable for ride-hailing users but also efficient for the overall transportation ecosystem. Instead, they should disentangle the impact of other aspects and design policies that more specifically restrict the harmful aspects. For example, to address the issues attributed to the increase in capacity due to ride-sharing providers’ entry, policymakers could regulate total capacity through licensing, instead of completely banning ride-hailing services, which simultaneously restricts information sharing. Similarly, to avoid the unfairness to riders due to drivers’ selective fulfilling, policymakers can adjust pricing schemes to direct drivers’ preferences to be aligned with the public’s interests rather than restricting the use of ride-hailing apps. This implication also applies to policy designs for broader online two-sided platforms.

Finally, based on our findings, we recommend digitizing traditional ride-hailing services (i.e., taxis) with the ride-hailing app to harness the economic value of information sharing and its enabled IT features. This strategy has been successfully implemented in several countries and is being embraced by some of the strictest regulators in the United States, such as those in New York and San Francisco.<sup>29</sup> A similar strategy can be generalized to other on-demand service contexts in which search costs apply primarily to sellers; IT-based information sharing should be offered to more such service providers.

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, as a quasi-natural experiment, our investigation is subject to natural interventions. Future research can explore a more nuanced intervention to isolate the value of other aspects of ride-hailing or disentangle the effects of different IT features or mechanisms enabled by information sharing. Second, there are certain caveats regarding the data. Data of taxi ridership come from one of the five companies affected by the ISRP, whereas data on public transportation ridership comes only from SPTCs. The congestion data cannot tease out the traffic attributed to the taxi fleet. Although we argue for the representativeness of data in capturing the city-wide transportation sive or precise data for replication. Third, we observed only 17 days after the policy shock, which might fail to capture the long-term dynamics of the transportation ecosystem driven by the demand side’s reaction. Future studies with longer observation windows and richer information could address our limitations.
