# Value Implications of Sourcing Electronic Health Records: The Role of Physician Practice Integration

Abstract. Should hospitals source electronic health records (EHR) systems from a single vendor or multiple vendors to deliver high-value care? We study hospitals’ EHR sourcing strategies based on their degree of integration with physician practices and its impact on the value of healthcare delivered. We propose a novel framework to define healthcare value as the extent to which a hospital effectively expends clinical resources to deliver services that improve patient outcomes. Drawing on modular systems and transaction cost eco nomics theories, we propose a moderated-mediation model that explores the pathways through which EHR sourcing strategies can create value in healthcare. We test our research hypotheses on a large, longitudinal sample of U.S. hospitals and observe that hospitals with EHR configurations closer to single sourcing strategies exhibit greater health information sharing compared with hospitals with multisourced EHR systems. Furthermore, we find that hospital-physician practice integration moderates the impact of single sourcing on health information sharing, which in turn, improves value. Specifically, tighter integration between hospitals and physician practices can create greater value if it is aligned with hospitals’ EHR sourcing strategies. As the healthcare industry moves toward value-based payment reform, our findings provide a useful roadmap to practitioners and policy makers to improve the performance of hospitals and healthcare providers.

## 1. Introduction

The U.S. healthcare system has witnessed greater interest in value-based care to improve resilience and care quality in the light of the pandemic (Burns-Lausch and Chakmakian 2021). Healthcare value is a notion that emphasizes “health outcomes achieved per dollar spent” (Porter 2010, p. 2477). Compared with traditional fee-for-service models, in a value-based payment model, providers are incentivized based on the value of care delivered in terms of the overall costs incurred to deliver a threshold level of health outcomes (Bethke et al. 2020). In other words, value-based payment models incentivize providers to deliver cost-effective and highquality care that improves patient health outcomes. Toward this goal, electronic health records (EHRs) offer significant potential to improve clinical decision making, care coordination, and reduce healthcare spending through better information sharing (Ayabakan et al. 2017b , Atasoy et al. 2021).

An EHR is an enterprise-level, information system that consists of multiple distinct, yet interrelated modules (Wowak et al. 2022). The modular nature of EHR systems offers hospitals an opportunity to adopt a multisourcing (MS) strategy by sourcing applications from multiple vendors. Hospitals may also adopt a singlesourcing (SS) strategy to implement their entire EHR configuration from a single vendor (Angst et al. 2017). Despite the recent trend toward hospital deployment of an SS strategy, there remains much debate regarding the right EHR sourcing approach for the transition to valuebased care (Payne et al. 2012). On one hand, proponents of SS strategy argue that applications from the same vendor are better integrated and thus offer greater interoperability, leading to greater efficiency due to lowe integration costs and access to patient health information across modules (Conn 2013). On the other hand, opponents insist that an MS strategy can also provide fluid data exchange and best-of-breed functionality when combined with application programming interfaces that connect two or more EHR modules (Glaser and Gardner 2022). Furthermore, an MS strategy can offer more affordable solutions that align with the functional needs of providers and better satisfy the needs of individual business units or departments (Hamadi et al. 2022). Therefore, the central research question “which EHR sourcing strategy can deliver greater healthcare value?” is critically important and remains an under-explored area.

Although the relationship between EHR sourcing strategy and the value of care delivered is not straightforward, this relationship may depend on several organizational factors, such as hospital-physician practice integration. This is an important issue because the role of hospital-physician integration has resurfaced due to the emergence of value-based care initiatives, such as accountable care organizations (ACOs) (Kocher and Sahni 2011). However, there is scant empirical evidence about the contextual role of practice integration in determining healthcare value. The literature suggests that practice integration is an anticompetitive tactic that is intended to reduce competition and increase one’s market power, resulting in higher healthcare prices but little improvement in patient outcomes (Post et al. 2018). In theory, one can also argue that practice integration may reduce inefficiency and align financial incentives when exchanging patient data and coordinating care with other healthcare organizations (Baker et al. 2014). Because hospitals are typically involved in various types of practice integration, it is important to study their impact on healthcare value and identify boundary conditions that may impact the association between organizational EHR sourcing strategies and the value of care provided.

Our study is intended to address these research gaps by examining the value implications of EHR sourcing strategies through the lens of information sharing, which may be contingent on the type of practice integration model. We focus on the system interoperability perspective and draw on modular systems and transaction cost economics theories. Specifically, we focus on three research questions. (a) Does EHR sourcing strategy impact health information sharing? (b) Does the type of practice integration model moderate this relationship? (c) Does health information sharing mediate the impact of EHR sourcing strategy on the value of healthcare delivery?

To answer our research questions, we leverage multiple data sources to construct a large panel data set containing 1,919 hospitals in the United States across nine years. We implement a two-stage research design to measure healthcare value using data envelopment analysis (DEA) in the first stage, followed by panel data econometric estimation in the second stage. We observe that hospitals that deployed an SS strategy were more likely to exchange patient medical information with other hospitals and ambulatory clinics than their MS counterparts. Broader information sharing, in turn, enables providers to effectively use clinical resources and manage their patient health outcomes to deliver greater value. This result suggests that health information sharing is a critical mediation pathway through which SS strategy improves healthcare value. Furthermore, we find that hospitals that deployed tight practice integration models were more likely to leverage SS strategies to enhance the level of health information sharing. Our empirical analysis using a moderated-mediation framework suggests that the interoperability advantages of SS strategy are more salient when hospitals are fully integrated with physicians, which explains about 50% of the improvement in healthcare value. In sharp contrast, SS strategy does not contribute to information sharing when a hospital has none or low levels of practice integration. Our results indicate that practice integration is a vital moderating factor that incentivizes providers to effectively use SS strategy to reap the full benefits of interoperability.

Our study makes several important contributions toward research and practice. First, we propose and test a research framework to measure healthcare value based on patient health outcomes and the cost of resources used, which aligns with the overall goal of value-based care and offers valuable insights for healthcare providers to act on. Second, ours is one of the first empirical studies to examine the impact of EHR sourcing strategies on healthcare value. Unlike earlier health IT studies, our results indicate that it is not sufficient to consider EHR adoption as a binary choice when examining its implications. How EHR systems are sourced, configured, and integrated, is an important question that IT professionals and researchers should consider when making health IT decisions. Specifically, our research reveals information sharing as a critical mediation mechanism through which the impact of EHR sourcing strategy on the value of care is observed. However, the strength of the mediation link depends on the type of practice integration model between hospitals and physicians. Therefore, our findings indicate that hospitals should prudently evaluate their EHR sourcing strategies based on their practice integration model. Otherwise, they may not be able to reap the full interoperability benefits of an SS strategy, especially in low practice integration settings. Our results underscore the need to promote a universally accepted data exchange standard for healthcare pro viders to achieve the right balance between interoper ability and functionality goals of EHR systems.

## 2. Related Literature

In this section, we first propose a framework to measure healthcare value, followed by a description of EHR sourcing strategies and the role of practice integration in driving healthcare value.

## 2.1. Healthcare Value

The healthcare literature defines “value” as the health outcomes achieved for every dollar spent (Porter 2010). Value is not only about cost reduction nor simply about the quality of care; instead, it is composed of both dimensions. For instance, Teisberg et al. (2020, p. 682) observed that “ … reducing costs is important but not sufficient. If the real goal of value-based health care were cost reduction, pain killers and compassion would be sufficient … .” It has also been argued that value should not be just determined solely by the quality of care because quality improvement efforts may not necessarily improve health outcomes, primarily when they are geared toward operational excellence (Teisberg et al. 2020).

Therefore, healthcare value should be considered a composite measure that represents the multi-input, multioutput nature of healthcare (Hollingsworth 2008, Bao and Bardhan 2022). The inputs refer to the clinical resources necessary to deliver healthcare services, including operational expenses, capital investments, and staff utilization. In contrast, outputs represent a variety of patient outcomes that measure health status achieved or sustained and the process of recovery. Accordingly, we conceptualize healthcare value as the extent to which a hospital expends its input resources to deliver services that improve patient outcomes. This notion aligns with the value-based healthcare literature as well as the goal of recent valuebased care initiatives (e.g., ACO and hospital value-based purchasing programs) that reimburse providers based on their cost savings and patient outcomes. It also unites all actors' interests, including patients, providers, payers, and society (Porter 2010, NEJM Catalyst 2017).

Prior research has used DEA and stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) to address the multidimensional nature of healthcare services (Cantor and Poh 2018, Kohl et al. 2019) but has focused primarily on outputs such as the quantity of services provided (Bao and Bardhan 2022). Although these approaches measure the efficiency of resource utilization to produce more services, they are insufficient to draw a complete picture of the value of care. To date, few studies have empirically investigated healthcare value and the role that information technology (IT) sourcing plays in creating healthcare value. Our study intends to fill this void.

## 2.2. EHR Sourcing Strategy

Healthcare providers have increasingly implemented EHR systems to capture and exchange patient data, spurred by the financial incentives provided by the Health Information Technology for Clinical Health (HITECH) Act. The EHR is a modular medical record consisting of separate but interrelated applications, such as clinical documentation, decision support, order entry, and data repository.

Modularity allows hospitals to “mix-and-match” vendors for different applications to meet the business needs of each department. Hospitals may adopt an MS strategy and use more than one vendor to source their EHR appli cations. Conversely, hospitals may also choose to license their entire suite of EHR applications from a single vendor, termed as a prototypical SS strategy. Without loss of generality, we consider EHR sourcing strategy as the degree to which a hospital adopts EHR applications from a single vendor (i.e., the degree of single sourcing). An SS strategy provides a more straightforward approach to build a prototype against which we can compare different configurations and measure the extent of sourcing strategies adopted at hospitals (Angst et al. 2017, p. 1132). This conceptualization allows us to construct a single continuous variable with larger values indicating greater similarity with a prototypical SS strategy. Hence, we anchor on SS strategy, not because of any a priori expectations but simply due to empirical considerations.

There is no consensus on using SS or MS in the prior research because each strategy offers several advantages and disadvantages (Angst et al. 2017). For instance, an SS strategy supports consistent user interaction across applications, reducing the learning burden for end users (Ford et al. 2010). In addition, an SS strategy provides greater ease of integration across applications, reducing the effort needed to implement and update the system (Bapna et al. 2010, Roskill 2014). On the other hand, an MS strategy includes discretion over each application's function, requires less effort to re-engineer business workflows, provides greater IT flexibility, and costs less to switch vendors to improve functionality. Despite this, hospitals have increasingly adopted SS strategies in recent years, likely driven by concerns over ease of implementation and integration (Privis Health 2018). Whether an SS strategy is associated with better healthcare value remains unclear. Although a few studies have examined this topic, they primarily focus on productivity-based measures, clinical outcomes, or user satisfaction, offering limited guidance to managers in a value-based care era (Fareed et al. 2012, Ford et al. 2013, Srivastava et al. 2022, Wowak et al. 2022).

Given the significant debate in the healthcare industry on digital strategies for healthcare transformation, there is an urgent need to examine the impact of EHR sourcing strategies on the value of care (Privis Health 2018). Because proponents of an SS strategy have argued for its interoperability benefits, we examine the role of information sharing as the primary mediation mechanism that explains the impact of SS on healthcare value and the strength of this relationship under different contextual settings (Conn 2013).

## 2.3. Practice Integration

Hospitals achieve alignment with physicians through various contracts to improve patient care. One popular approach to achieve alignment is physician practice integration (Cullen et al. 2012). We focus on eight common types of integration models identified in the American Hospital Association (AHA) survey and frequently used in other healthcare studies (Ciliberto and Dranove 2006, Short et al. 2017). This subsection introduces each model from the least to most tightly integrated. To be parsimonious in our analyses, we group similar integration models based on their focus, incentives, and the level of clinical integration.

Independent practice association (IPA) and group practices without walls (GPWW) represent loose contractual networks. These are nonexclusive arrangements where hospitals only assist individual physicians in obtaining managed care contracts. They have no obligation related to clinical practice and have arguably little or minor implications for health information sharing. Therefore, we group these two models with none or little integration (NA) and classify them as “low integration” models.

An open physician-hospital organization (OPHO) is a joint venture contract in which hospitals assist physicians in negotiating with health plans, provide administrative services, and help manage ambulatory care facilities. A closed physician-hospital organization (CPHO) is similar to an OPHO but is more selective based on physician quality and cost performance. Furthermore, CPHOs form exclusive relationships such that physicians generally do not sign similar contracts with other hospitals. Management service organizations (MSOs) are similar to CPHOs except that they may involve acquisition of the assets of physician practices. Compared with low integration models, these three types of contracts represent closer relationships between hospitals and physician groups for patient care coordination. However, physicians maintain autonomy over their practice and clinical operations compared with full integration models. Following prior research, we classify these three models (OPHO, CPHO, and MSO) into the “medium integration” category (Bazzoli et al. 1999, Burns et al. 2000).

Fully integrated organizations include the integrated salary model (ISM), equity model (EM), and foundation model (FM) of practice integration. The full integration model is the most tightly integrated form where hospitals hire physicians as salaried employees and acquire the entire practice. In other words, the relationship between hospitals and physicians is no longer contractual; instead, it becomes an ownership model. Clinically, this has the highest potential for patient care coordination and improvement of healthcare value due to better alignment of financial incentives (Cuellar and Gertler 2006). Hence, we classify these three models as “high integration” models.

Although previous studies observed that greater practice integration is consistently associated with higher healthcare prices, its association with clinical outcomes is ambiguous (Post et al. 2018). For instance, a change in ownership will result in higher costs incurred by the hospital that may be attributed to employee wages, benefits, IT investments, facility upgrades, and medical malpractice coverage requirements (Cullen et al. 2012). Practice integration allows hospitals to control patient referrals and compete against rivals by discouraging integrated physicians from referring patients outside their organizations (Baker et al. 2014), which has been found to distort physicians’ treatment choices and increase outpatient charges for Medicare patients (Richards et al. 2021). With respect to clinical outcomes, Koch et al. (2021) observed that hospital acquisition of physician practices had a minor impact on the progression of Medicare hypertension and diabetes patients, whereas Godwin et al. (2021) called for further research to investigate the quality effects of hospital-physician integration.

Furthermore, we observe that value-based care initiatives, such as ACO programs, have increasingly shifted financial risk to hospitals, spurring them to integrate with physician practices (Neprash et al. 2015). Therefore, it is of paramount interest to study the implications of practice integration on the value of care delivered. Our study providers greater clarity to the extant debate by examining how hospital-physician practice integration influences the value of care, primarily through its moderation effect on the relationship between EHR sourcing strategies and health information sharing.

## 3. Theoretical Foundation

Although an SS strategy has several advantages and disadvantages compared with the MS approach, we focus on the extent to which health information sharing mediates the impact of sourcing strategy on the value of care for different practice integration models. We draw on modular systems theory (MST) coupled with transaction cost economics (TCE) theory to help us better understand this phenomenon. MST describes the disaggregation of tightly integrated systems into loosely coupled compo nents (Tanriverdi et al. 2007, Campagnolo and Camuffo 2010). The term “loose coupling” indicates that individual components are linked together while maintaining a certain degree of independence in performing tasks (Salvador 2007). Systems modularity determines whether the disaggregated modules can be recombined to reap the advantages of modular design (e.g., configuration flexibility and division of labor) without compromising the original functionality (Schilling 2000). Accordingly, MST posits that the system architecture needs to encompass interfaces and standards to enable system modularity (Sanchez and Mahoney 1996).

Because patients may be transferred from one clinical setting to another, often within the same hospital or across hospitals, the need to integrate healthcare necessitates data integration at two levels, internal and external (Raghupathi and Tan 2002). Internal integration refers to the integration between disparate EHR applications and the information flows between providers in a hospital. External integration goes beyond hospital boundaries and refers to routine medical data exchange with providers outside the focal hospital or in different health systems. It builds on internal integration and further requires resolving transaction costs arising from opportunistic behavior and incentive misalignment between organizations (Barki and Pinsonneault 2005, Hagel and Brown 2005).

## 3.1. Implications for EHR Sourcing Strategy

An EHR is a modular system that hosts disparate patient data, such as laboratory tests, radiology images, and clinical records, that are typically coded in different formats. The complexity of health data standards leads to implementation of various data standards by EHR vendors, resulting in a lack of interoperability between different vendor products. As a result, when adopting EHR applications from multiple vendors, hospitals may need to develop appropriate interfaces to access heterogeneous data sources (Walker et al. 2005). These system integration efforts may exceed the resources required to implement the EHR due to the complexity of interfaces needed for information exchange (Sharif et al. 2007). MST suggests that, in the absence of a standardized IT system, greater integration costs may create barriers to data communication across modules, which in turn, reduce the benefits of modularization (Tanriverdi et al. 2007). Therefore, MST provides an appropriate theoretical lens to explain the relationship between different EHR sourcing strategies, health information sharing, and healthcare value.

## 3.2. Implications for Practice Integration

TCE suggests that when organizations maintain independence over their governance structure, they may act in their own interests at the expense of their partners (Narayanan and Raman 2004). This concern is more salient in our context, where exchange of patient medical data across providers is the core unit of interest. Specifically, interorganizational information sharing may place providers at a competitive disadvantage, because sharing patient data may allow competitors to poach patients and gain insight into a provider’s operations and strategy (Adjerid et al. 2018). Furthermore, exchange of patient data imposes liability risks and security concerns on providers if patient information is breached while being used by other providers. Adjerid et al. (2018, p. 344) observed that “ … [this] requires a shift towards the appropriate coordination model. In particular, when market transactions occur frequently, TCE advocates pursuing a systematic approach that includes contractual agreements that establish long-term relationships between individual market actors … .” Hence, providers may align incentives and mitigate transaction costs through appropriate practice integration models that encompass process, governance, and contractual relationships.

## 4. Hypotheses Development

In this section, we develop our research hypotheses on the key relationships between EHR sourcing strategy, health information sharing, and healthcare value, accounting for the role of practice integration.

## 4.1. Impact of EHR Sourcing Strategy on Health Information Sharing

Information sharing across organizations first requires internal integration to ensure that patient health data are integrated across diverse EHR applications and databases within a hospital or health system (Raghupa thi and Tan 2002). In the absence of uniform standards, these applications are often structurally different from one vendor to the next, such that when a hospital pursues an MS strategy, its IT infrastructure may consist of dozens of specialized interfaces connecting disparate applications to a federated data hub (Salvador 2007). Each customized interface requires IT personnel to have technological and domain knowledge to analyze relevant clinical workflows and develop the appropriate functionality (Williams et al. 2012). Once interfaces are implemented, considerable resources are expended to monitor their performance and resolve technical issues (Ruppel et al. 2020). Because the sheer number of application interfaces can grow exponentially, the associated costs may daunt healthcare providers to prioritize “workaround” routines that are often paper-based (Walker et al. 2005). Hence, a lack of integration support for application interfaces impedes adopters of multisourcing strategies from sharing patient health data.

Furthermore, MS adopters may need to open a cascade of disparate applications to access parts of a patient’s health record, especially when the data are imported from external sources (Payne et al. 2012, Adler-Milstein and Wang 2020). This adds to workflow complexity and additional latency as providers have to switch from one application to another, which contributes to provider fatigue and negatively impacts the efficacy and efficiency of data retrieval (Ford et al. 2013, Ruppel et al. 2020). As a result, staff dissatisfaction and resistance may hinder effective interactions necessary to communicate patient health information (Srivastava et al. 2022). On the other hand, EHR systems from the same vendor are usually designed with uniform user interfaces, which bends the learning curve for SS adopters to share and access patient health records (Payne et al. 2012).

Adoption of an MS strategy may also complicate implementation of new software releases because ad opters have to upgrade data standards, access rights, and authorizations on different applications (Hermann 2010). As IT asset specificity increases, periodic application updates may impose considerable switching costs for MS adopters (Ford et al. 2010, Adjerid et al. 2018). In contrast, software development and integration can be more effectively conducted using an SS solution due to the ease of knowledge transfer, problem-solving, and conflict resolution (Tanriverdi et al. 2007). Thus, sourcing applications from a single vendor is likely to favor ease of patient health information communication.

Hence, we argue that hospitals that pursue SS strategies face fewer technical challenges in managing and integrating various applications, leading to streamlined workflows and effective patient data exchange with external entities, compared with an MS strategy (Gold and Mclaughlin 2016). MST posits that when the nature of interaction between individual applications is uncertain, as is the case with EHR applications, customers are likely to favor integrated solutions from a single vendor to optimize their performance (Schilling 2000, p. 322). Therefore, we hypothesize that hospitals with EHR configurations that are similar to an SS strategy are more likely to communicate and share information with outside providers due to greater interoperability.

Hypothesis 1. The degree of EHR single sourcing strategy within hospitals is positively associated with their breadth of health information sharing.

## 4.2. Moderation Effect of Practice Integration

Patients with multiple comorbidities may seek care from numerous provider organizations such as hospitals and outpatient clinics. Ideally, their health data should follow them as they are transferred across organizational boundaries to ensure continuum of care. However, sharing patient health information with outside providers is challenging due to friction caused by opportunistic provider behavior and transaction costs. Hence, an SS strategy may not necessarily lead to greater interorganizational information sharing, despite its interoperability advantages. The core tenets of TCE theory suggest that tighter practice integration can contribute to greater incentive alignment between healthcare providers such that single-sourced EHR systems can be utilized effectively to facilitate health information sharing.

When hospitals are loosely integrated with physicians as independent entities, information sharing can be subject to competition concerns because each party is motivated to direct patients to their individual facilities. In contrast, when hospitals form joint ventures with physicians in moderately integrated practices, the tighter relationship facilitates greater care coordination and information sharing (Ciliberto and Dranove 2006). In fully integrated models, physicians are employed by hospitals and share common organizational goals, thereby reducing the likelihood of opportunistic behavior and fostering mutual trust between the two parties (Dennis et al. 2012). Therefore, as providers engage in tighter practice integration models, they have less conflict of interest and their incentives are better aligned through joint financial risk sharing, thereby lowering organizational barriers to information sharing.

As healthcare contracts are mostly incomplete, they can lead to ex post opportunism (Cuellar and Gertler 2006). Furthermore, an effective governance mechanism should include a precise clinical performance specification and demand conformance with clinical protocols for each party involved, which increases the need to share clinical data (Campagnolo and Camuffo 2010). This incentivizes hospitals to conduct routine exchange of patient health records with physician practices that are tightly integrated, even though they may incur higher transaction costs. However, IT can be an effective tool in reducing transaction costs due to its ability to facilitate information sharing across multiple partners (Kleis et al. 2012). For instance, providers within the same health network may have incompatible IT infrastructure leading to systems interoperability challenges, which can be offset by developing specific interfaces for data exchange. The coordination costs to resolve these challenges are daunting to independent healthcare practitioners due to weaker governance ties and misaligned incentives (Tanriverdi et al. 2007). On the contrary, it is easier to overcome governance barriers and resolve incentive conflicts, when there exist closer interorganizationa relationships (Barki and Pinsonneault 2005). As a result, hospitals with tighter practice integration are more likely to benefit from SS strategies as it enhances their information sharing capabilities and monitor provider adherence to clinical protocols (Simpson and Coate 1998).

When hospitals are tightly integrated with physicians, they are more likely to refer patients to other hospitals and affiliated practices within the same network (Neprash et al. 2015). This creates a strong work interdependence and the need to share patient health data. Furthermore, tight practice integration also allows providers to internalize network externalities from coordinated patient care (Atasoy et al. 2017). Hence, the value of clinical information increases under tighter practice integration, which motivates providers to use SS strategies to support health information sharing. We argue that hospital-physician practice integration resolves incentive issues and helps providers to leverage the interoperability benefits of an SS strategy to facilitate greater health information sharing. In other words, we posit that the impact of an SS strategy will be amplified when hospitals and physician practices are more tightly integrated.

Hypothesis 2. Hospitals that are tightly integrated with physi cian practices are more likely to exhibit a positive association between EHR single sourcing strategy and health information sharing, compared with hospitals that are less tightly integrated with physicians.

## 4.3. Mediation Effect of Health Information Sharing

EHR sourcing strategies may influence clinical operations and thus hospital performance through numerous mechanisms (Angst et al. 2017, Srivastava et al. 2022). In this research, we focus on the enablement of information sharing as one mediation channel, through which SS strategy may improve healthcare value. Specifically, we argue that enhanced exchange of patient data, through adoption of a hospital SS strategy, is associated with better patient outcomes and lower health expenditures, thus improving the value of care.

First, patient mobility creates a fragmented healthcare delivery environment where services are delivered by multiple, often unaffiliated providers, requiring routine communication of patient health records among caregivers to ensure care coordination (Ayabakan et al. 2017b). Timely access to laboratory tests and radiology reports allows providers to make informed clinical decisions and avoid duplicate tests (Walker et al. 2005). Lammers et al. (2014) reported that information sharing enabled by health IT reduces the incidence of repeat imaging tests by more than 50%. These redundant procedures consume healthcare resources but have little impact on health outcomes; hence, they do not create value. On the other hand, when providers have access to patients’ medical data, they can prospectively stratify patients based on risk severity, enabling them to prioritize high-risk patients for value-based care (Bao and Bardhan 2022).

Access to patient health records also allows providers to prevent avoidable adverse events. For instance, providers usually wait for hours to receive test results in a nonemergency setting. This prolongs patient length of stay in hospitals and increases their risk of hospitalacquired infections, which can be avoided if other providers share these results in a timely manner. Furthermore, providers can use decision support algorithms to identify and prevent potential medical errors, such as drug allergies and drug-drug interactions, based on medication and allergy information (Atasoy et al. 2019). Such adverse events not only deteriorate patient health outcomes but also consume extensive clinical resources, thereby reducing overall value delivered to patients.

Monitoring patient health requires processing of large volumes of clinical information, especially for chronic diseases (Atasoy et al. 2019). By integrating patient data from various sources, hospitals can develop a patientlevel dashboard to better track and manage patient care (Levinson 2019). This allows providers to identify gaps in care, such as preventive checkups that are overdue. It can also send early warnings to providers based on patient health data such that nurse practitioners can intervene and take timely corrective action for at-risk patients (Chiedi 2019).

Overall, the ease of access to shared patient health data resulting from single-vendor EHR solutions improves diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision making, leading to higher healthcare value. Thus, we posit that adoption of an SS strategy will facilitate greater exchange of health information between providers, which in turn, is likely to improve the overall value of care. In other words, health information sharing mediates the impact of EHR sourcing strategies on the value of care delivered by the hospital.

Hypothesis 3. Health information sharing mediates the positive effect of EHR single sourcing strategy on value.

It highlights the impact of EHR sourcing strategy on healthcare value through the pathway of health information sharing and the moderating role of practice integration. We are particularly interested in the extent to which information sharing mediates the effect of EHR sourcing strategy on the value of care under various practice integration scenarios.

## 5. Data and Variables

We collected data from multiple sources on a panel of 1,919 hospitals across nine years from 2008 to 2016. EHR adoption and vendor selection data were obtained from the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) database. We merged it with health IT data from the AHA Annual Survey IT Supplement database to measure the type and breadth of health information sharing among healthcare providers. Using the primary AHA survey data, we collected hospital-leve contractual arrangements with physicians to measure practice integration (Baker et al. 2014, Post et al. 2018). We further sourced other primary data on hospital expenses from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Cost Reports, staffing levels from the AHA Survey, and quality performance from the CMS Hospital Compare database to measure healthcare value. Finally, we supplemented data on other hospital-level operational and environmental characteristics from the CMS Impact Files and Dartmouth Atlas of Healthcare.

## 5.1. EHR Single-Sourcing Strategy

To construct the EHR sourcing strategy, we draw on prior research by Angst et al. (2017), who proposed a measure of EHR sourcing based on five EHR modules, including Clinical Data Repository, Clinical Decision Support System, Order Entry, Computerized Physician Order Entry, and Physician Documentation (Angst et al. 2017, Atasoy et al. 2017). We considered the status of a specific EHR module as adopted if a hospital reported its status as “live and operational” in a given year. Based on supplier information for adopted modules, we constructed SS as the degree to which a hospital sources its EHR portfolio from a single vendor. Specifically, we implemented a two-step procedure based on Angst et al. (2017). In the first step, we determined the dominant supplier of the majority of EHR modules for each hospital-year unit. We then derived a prototype SS sequence with all modules adopted from the dominant vendor. In the next step, we compared the actual EHR configuration with the SS prototype by measuring their similarity based on the extant modules supplied by the dominant vendor. We normalized the similarity measure by dividing it by the total number of modules.<sup>1</sup> The variable SS is bounded between zero and one, with larger values indicating an EHR configuration that is closer to an SS strategy.<sup>2</sup>

## 5.2. Health Information Sharing

We draw on a composite AHA IT survey question to construct the health information sharing variable: “which of the following types of patient data does your hospital electronically exchange/share with one or more of the provider types listed below.” The questionnaire considers five categories of patient data. These are (1) patient demographics, (2) laboratory results, (3) medication history, (4) radiology reports, and (5) clinical/summary care records in any format.<sup>3</sup> For each data type, hospitals report their sharing status (yes/no) with four types of providers: (1) hospitals inside your system, (2) hospitals outside your system, (3) ambulatory providers inside your system, and (4) ambulatory providers outside your system. Hence, we develop a construct, InfoShare, by cal culating the percentage of “Yes” responses for each hospital-year observation (Vest and Simon 2018).<sup>4</sup> A larger value of this construct indicates broader health information exchange between the focal hospital and other providers.

## 5.3. Practice Integration

In the AHA survey, hospitals reported their participation status in eight contractual arrangements with physicians. To be parsimonious, we grouped similar integration arrangements based on their focus on integration, incentive alignment, and ownership of physi cian facilities (Cuellar and Gertler 2006, Baker et al. 2014). We defined a three-level indicator variable, Integration, with higher values indicating hospital participation in a tightly integrated practice model. Specifically, Integration is set to one if the hospital did not participate in any integration model or in low practice integration models related to contracting and administrative serv ices (i.e., IPA and GPWW). It is assigned a value of two for moderate integration where the hospital participated in joint venture contracts with physicians (i.e., OPHO, CPHO, and MSO). Finally, a value of three was assigned if the hospital acquired physician practice(s) and hired physicians as salaried employees (full integra tion models, i.e., ISM, EM, and FM). If a hospital partici pated in more than one integration model, they were classified based on the most common integration reported (Short et al. 2017).<sup>5</sup>

In a separate robustness check, we created a practice integration index by calculating the average value of integration, weighted by the number of physicians in each class. We also simplified the construct by using a binary indicator for participation in a joint venture with physicians.

## 5.4. Healthcare Value

It is challenging to quantify healthcare value because it entails measurement of multiple inputs and multiple outputs. Furthermore, complex interdependencies exist among these dimensions and the underlying production function is not observable. These concerns make simple ratio or regression approaches implausible to measure healthcare value and motivate us to draw on DEA to operationalize Value (Cantor and Poh 2018, Kohl et al. 2019). DEA, which has been widely used to analyze hospital productivity, is a nonparametric optimization approach that uses linear programming techniques to determine the optimal value at which input resources are used to produce outputs (Hollingsworth 2008, Tiemann and Schreyogg 2012).

Following the healthcare literature, we considered five inputs to capture resource utilization related to deployment of capital assets (Capital Expense), expenses (Operating Expense), and labor (No. Physicians, No. Nurses, and No. Other Clinicians). We also used three types of patient outcomes (Agha 2014, Senot et al. 2016). Experiential Quality is an average score of six survey questions related to patient satisfaction of communication and responsiveness of caregivers (Senot et al. 2016). Mortality Rate is calculated as the average of individual mortality rates for heart attack, heart failure, and pneumonia, weighted by the number of patients with each condition. Similarly, Readmission Rate is calculated as the weighted average of readmission rates for these three conditions. Mortality and readmission rates are risk-adjusted based on patients' age, gender, past medical history, and comorbidities, consistent with the literature (Mccallion et al. 2000, Porter 2010). We also normalized input resources based on the number of encounters, leading to “pervisit” measurements as the unit for each resource variable to account for the differences in hospital sizes.<sup>6</sup> Because hospitals intend to maximize production of quality outputs in the DEA model, we use the inverse of mortality and readmission rates as outputs (Tiemann and Schreyogg 2012).

We used the Banker-Charnes-Cooper (BCC) model, accounting for variable returns to scale in the DEA production function (Banker et al. 1984), as specified in Equation (1).

$$
\begin{array}{c} M i n _ {\theta , \lambda} \{\theta - \varepsilon (1 ^ {\prime} s ^ {-} + 1 ^ {\prime} s ^ {+}) | X \lambda + s ^ {-} = \theta x _ {o}; \\ Y \lambda - s ^ {+} = y _ {o}; \quad 1 ^ {\prime} \lambda = 1; \quad \lambda \geq 0 \} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

This linear programming formulation is used to identify a Pareto efficient frontier across all data points and compute a radial efficiency score for each hospital (Ayabakan et al. 2017a). X and Y represent the vectors of hospital inputs and outputs, respectively, and θ represents the DEA-based measure of value. Because hospi tals typically exercise control over their deployment of input resources, we adopted an input-oriented BCC model. The derived Value score is a continuous variable, bounded between zero and one, where a larger number indicates greater value of care delivered and accurately represents a measure of inputs expended to obtain healthcare outcomes.

## 5.5. Control Variables

To mitigate concerns of omitted variable bias, we capture a broad spectrum of hospital-specific controls that may affect EHR sourcing strategy and hospital performance (Dranove et al. 2014, Senot et al. 2016, Angst et al. 2017, Atasoy et al. 2017, Adjerid et al. 2018). We include the size of the hospital and health system, crowdedness, teaching intensity, geographic location, for-profit status, wage index, case mix index, operational disturbance, market share, and year of operation. We also control for environmental factors in the hospital referral region (HRR). An HRR is a well-defined local healthcare market where patients are likely to receive a bulk of their healthcare services, requiring health information sharing among providers within the region (Miller and Tucker 2014, Adjerid et al. 2018). We specifically include the density of primary care physicians, specialists, and nurses per population and a Herfindahl-Hirschman index based on inpatient admissions (Everson and Adler-Milstein 2016). Table 1 provides the variable definitions along with their corresponding data sources, and Table A1 in the online appendix presents the correlation matrix of our model variables. The variance inflation factors of key variables are less than five, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a serious concern.

## 6.2. Endogeneity and Identification Strategy

The decisions to adopt a single-sourcing EHR strategy and exchange patient health data are not random. Although we have included an extensive set of controls and two-way fixed effects, they may still be subject to endo geneity issues, leading to biased ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation. Hence, we deployed an instrument variable (IV) approach to address identification concerns. We draw on geographic variation in hospitals belonging to multihospital health systems to construct our IVs (Dranove et al. 2014, Atasoy et al. 2021). Specifically, we identified other partner hospitals affiliated with the same health system located in different HRRs (hereafter referred as remote partner hospitals) for each focal hospital. We then defined the average rates of SS strategy adoption and health information sharing among these hospitals as the IVs for SS and InfoShare, respectively.

Based on institutional theory and network effects, we argue that higher the ratio of remote partner hospitals that adopt an SS strategy and exchange patient data, the more likely it is that the focal hospital will also deploy an SS configuration and share patient health information (Angst et al. 2017, Lu et al. 2017). To ensure the validity of our IVs, we test the association between each IV and the corresponding endogenous variables and report the estimation results in Table A2 in the online appendix. We observe that remote partner hospitals’ decisions to deploy an SS strategy and exchange health information strongly influence the focal hospital’s decisions. The large F statistics assure the inclusion restriction and address weak IV concerns.

An exogeneity concern may arise if remote peer hospitals’ IT decisions affect the focal hospital’s care delivery and performance. Accordingly, we performed several tests to mitigate this concern. In particular, we argue that partner hospitals’ decisions related to SS and information sharing should not affect service at the focal hospital, because patients typically receive most of their treatments from hospitals within the same HRR (Miller and Tucker 2014, Atasoy et al. 2017, Adjerid et al. 2018). Prior research suggests that patients typically receive treatment within a 10-mile radius and do not travel more than 20 miles to seek care (Yen 2013). Therefore, we examined the association between our IVs and the focal hospital market standing, service level, and patient complexity, and present these exogeneity test results in Table A3 in the online appendix. We observe that our IVs are not significantly associated with focal hospital market share, number of outpatient and inpatient visits, and the case mix index. This suggests that a cross-HRR boundary spillover effect is unlikely, and the decisions of remote partner hospitals are exogenous to care quality and value at the focal hospital.

Overall, our IVs meet the relevance and exogeneity criteria. A two-stage least square (2SLS) estimation with fixed effects provides a valid approach to estimate the above equations (Angrist et al. 1996).

## 6.5. Moderated Mediation Model

We show that health information sharing serves as a mediation mechanism through which EHR sourcing strategy improves healthcare value and demonstrate that the positive impact of SS on information sharing is moderated by the level of hospital integration with physician practices. In this section, we study both effects (i.e., mediation and moderation) and examine the research model shown in Figure 1 with a moderated-mediation framework (Muller et al. 2005, Edwards and Lambert 2007). We are particularly interested in quantifying the strength of health information sharing as the mediation channel that explains the impact of SS on healthcare value for different levels of practice integration (i.e., moderation effect). We follow the approach described in Hayes (2013) and estimate the following equations:

$$
\begin{array}{c} I n f o S h a r e _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S S _ {i t} + \sum_ {j} \beta_ {2 j} S S _ {i t} \times D (I n t e g r a t i o n _ {i t} = j) \\ + \beta_ {3} I n t e g r a t i o n _ {i t} + \theta X _ {i t} + \delta Z _ {h t} + \alpha_ {i} + \lambda_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Value} _ {i t} = \rho_ {0} + \rho_ {1} S S _ {i t} + \sum_ {j} \rho_ {2 j} S S _ {i t} \times D (\text {Integration} _ {i t} = j) \\ \quad + \rho_ {3} \text {InfoShare} _ {i t} + \rho_ {4} \text {Integration} _ {i t} + \theta X _ {i t} \\ \quad + \delta Z _ {h t} + \alpha_ {i} + \lambda_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array} \tag {8}\tag{8}
$$

We use a categorical Integration variable and include a series of dummy variables Integration<sub>it</sub> � j, where j � 1, 2, or 3 was ordered from low to high. We treat low practice integration as the reference group (j � 1). For a particular integration model j, $\rho _ { 2 j }$ captures the direct effect of SS on value after controlling for InfoShare, and the estimated $\beta _ { 2 j } \times \rho _ { 3 }$ measures the indirect effect of SS on value through information sharing. Hence, the moderated mediation model includes both moderation and mediation components that are estimated in Equations (7) and (8).

For brevity, we present the estimated values of the direct, indirect, and total effects in Table 4. Our primary interest is the portion of the total effect that is explained by the indirect effect, shown as indirect effect/total effect in the last column. We observe that when hospitals are more tightly linked with physicians, the indirect effect of SS on value (through information sharing) signifi cantly increases to 0.030 for medium integration (i.e., level 2) and 0.064 for high integration (i.e., level 3). Specifically, when hospitals form joint ventures with physicians using OPHO, CPHO, or MSO models, adoption of an additional module from the dominant EHR vendor improves healthcare value by 3%, of which about onethird (i.e., 34.1%) can be attributed to greater information sharing. However, tighter hospital integration with physician practices, based on an ISM, EM, or FM structure, results in 6.4% improvement in value, with 50.4% of this improvement attributed to greater information sharing. Overall, our results indicate that deployment of a single-vendor EHR solution alone may not result in greater value. Rather, the impact of EHR sourcing strategies on healthcare value through information sharing is contingent on the level of practice integration.

## 7.2. Stochastic Frontier Estimation

As an alternate approach to DEA, we also implemented stochastic frontier estimation (SFE) to measure healthcare value (Hollingsworth 2008). Compared with DEA, SFE is a parametric approach where a stochastic frontier represents the best practice frontier of decision-making units in terms of their efficiency in converting available input resources to produce an attainable level of outputs (Greene 2011). Following this notion, we propose that healthcare value can be defined as a hospital’s efficiency in converting inputs into outputs relative to those on the efficient frontier. An inherent limitation of SFE compared with DEA is that it was developed to handle a single output (Li et al. 2010). However, in recent years, scholars have adopted a multi-output, distance production function to accommodate multiple outputs using SFE (Hamidi 2016). This approach uses a translog distance functional form, a Cobb-Douglas production function generalization, that provides more flexibility through its second-order approximation (Coelli et al.

1999).<sup>7</sup> Accordingly, we implement the multioutput, distance production function framework to estimate healthcare value, as shown in Equation (9).

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {l n y _ {3 i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {2} \alpha_ {1 j} l n y _ {j i t} ^ {*} + \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {2} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {2} \alpha_ {2 j k} l n y _ {j i t} ^ {*} l n y _ {k i t} ^ {*}} \\ & {\qquad + \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {5} \alpha_ {3 m} l n x _ {m i t} + \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {5} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {5} \alpha_ {4 m n} l n x _ {m i t} l n x _ {n i t}} \\ & {\qquad + \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {5} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {2} \alpha_ {5 m j} l n x _ {m i t} l n y _ {j i t} ^ {*} + V _ {i t} - U _ {i t}} \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

The output variable $y _ { 3 i t }$ represents Experiential Quality, and $y _ { 1 i t } ^ { * }$ and $y _ { 2 i t } ^ { * }$ represent the corresponding ratios of Mortality Rate and Readmission Rate to Experiential Qual ity for hospital i in year t, respectively.

Our input variables, $x _ { 1 i t } - x _ { 5 i t } ,$ , represent Capital Exp enses, Operating Expenses, No. Physicians, No. Nurses, and No. Other Clinicians of hospital i in year t, respectively; $V _ { i t }$ is a symmetric random error term with zero mean and unknown variance, and $U _ { i t }$ is the inefficiency term with a nonnegative normal distribution (truncated at zero). The SFE model assumes that the pattern of inefficiency change is the same for each hospital over time, that is, $U _ { i t } ~ = ~ U _ { i } e x p ( - \eta ( t - T ) )$ (Battese and Coelli 1995). We implemented SFE with a panel data setting in STATA using the xtfrontier module. We used the resulting SFE technical efficiency scores, that is, $V a l u e _ { i t } = E [ e x p ( - U _ { i t } )$ $/ ( V _ { i t } - U _ { i t } ) ]$ , as a proxy for hospital healthcare value and re-estimated our main models. We report the SFE results in Table 6 and observe consistent findings as our main results using DEA (Table 3). Overall, our robustness tests using SFE suggest that the DEA-based approach to measure healthcare value is robust to parametrization.

## 7.3. Internal and External Health Information Sharing

Hospitals may share health information with other healthcare providers either within their health network or across different health networks (or both). Even though hospitals may adopt an SS strategy to integrate patient data, information sharing with providers outside their health system is challenging due to organizational barriers. Hence, we decompose InfoShare into two variables, Internal and External InfoShare, which measure the breadth of patient health information shared with hospitals and ambulatory providers within and outside the focal hospital's health network, respectively, that is, intra- and interorganizational sharing. We replaced Info-Share with these variables and re-estimated Equations (2)–(6).

Table 7 presents the estimation results. Panel A shows the results for within-health system or internal information sharing. We observe qualitatively consistent coefficients, indicating that adoption of SS strategy is associated with broader information sharing within a health network, which in turn improves care value. Tighter practice integration has a positive moderation effect, allowing hospitals to reap greater value by deploying an SS strategy. Panel B presents the results for health information sharing with external providers. We observe that the coefficient of SS is insignificant in columns (1) and (2), indicating that an SS strategy is not significantly associated with information sharing with providers outside the health system. However, Integration is a positive moderator, suggesting that SS contributes to value creation when hospitals are tightly integrated with physician practices. Furthermore, we observe that information sharing with external providers consistently improves healthcare value, even though the magnitude of its impact is significantly lower compared with the impact of internal information sharing.

Collectively, the results suggest a nuanced difference between intra- and interorganizational information sharing. Although an SS strategy supports greater information exchange, its direct impact on the value of care is observed only in the external information sharing scenario (Panel B). When patient data are shared internally, the impact of EHR sourcing strategy on value is mediated through information sharing.

7.4.2. Alternate DEA Models. We also implemented different DEA models to test the robustness of our findings and reported these results in Table A5 of the online appendix. First, we examined an output-oriented DEA model to account for the possibility that some hospitals maximize patient outcomes given limited resources. These hospitals may participate in value-based care initiatives so that they are required to achieve a level of health outcomes to earn incentive payments. Second, we used a window analysis approach described in Cooper et al. (2011) to account for temporal trends when assessing healthcare value (Cantor and Poh 2018). Specifically, we ran DEA on each two-year window and measured a hospital’s relative healthcare value by comparing its performance against its own performance in a different year and with other hospitals. Third, the DEAbased production frontier may be upward-biased because the data from the best performers may be inherently unobservable or dropped during preprocessing (Kohl et al. 2019). We deployed a bootstrap DEA algorithm proposed by Simar and Wilson (1998) to mitigate this concern. As shown in Table A5 in the online appendix, the estimation results using alternate DEA models yield broadly consistent findings as our main results.

7.4.3. Alternate Second-Stage Regression Models. We also deployed alternative models for the second-stage econometric estimation. First, we used a panel Tobit model with hospital and year fixed effects because our measures of DEA-based healthcare value and health information sharing are bounded between zero and one. Because the Tobit model has a nonlinear function form, a typical 2SLS estimator will yield biased results (Terza et al. 2008). Hence, we adopted a control function approach (Wooldridge 2010) using the same IVs from 2SLS as the exclusion criteria in the first stage. We observe qualitatively similar results, as shown in Panel A of Table A6 in the online appendix.

It is also possible that hospitals participate in practice integration with physicians to reduce friction when coordinating care with outside providers, leading to endogeneity concerns (Cuellar and Gertler 2006, Post et al. 2018). We first explored variations in Integration and Value across time in our sample. We observe that the average value of integration exhibits a steady time series, whereas Value fluctuates significantly with a decreasing trend over the years. This observation is consistent with the prior literature and indicates that the value of care may not drive practice integration contracts (Short et al. 2017). Nevertheless, we implemented a 2SLS model to mitigate this concern and adopted a similar preference-based IV for Integration, based on the average integration level of remote partner hospitals (Atasoy et al. 2021). We present the 2SLS estimation results in Panel B of Table A6 in the online appendix and observe qualitatively consistent results with an amplified effect of practice integration on information sharing and value.

7.4.4. Alternate Practice Integration Constructs. We also used alternative measurements for practice integration to check whether the variable construction is driving our results. First, we created an Integration Index by using the average level of integration model participation, weighted by the number of physicians at each level of integration, to account for the extent to which a hospital engages in each integration model. Second, we define a binary variable for joint venture participation, which equals one if the hospital has a joint venture arrangement with physicians or physician groups and zero otherwise. We present the estimation results associated with these measures of practice integration in Panels A and B of Table A7 in the online appendix, respectively, and observe qualitatively consistent results.

## 8. Discussion

Our research contributes to the extant literature on the business value of IT in a value-based healthcare environment. Although prior research examined the performance implications of health IT, they primarily studied individual performance dimensions, such as cost, readmission rate, mortality rate, and patient satisfaction and did not consider the interdependencies between these measures (Agha 2014, Adjerid et al. 2018). In the healthcare literature, it is well documented that there exist tradeoffs between cost and quality objectives because hospitals are often forced to make resource allocation decisions due to budget constraints. To succeed in a value-based care era, providers often need to balance these tradeoffs because value-based incentives are contingent on achieving both cost savings and patient outcomes (Bao and Bardhan 2022). We propose a novel framework to define healthcare value as the extent to which a hospital effectively expends clinical resources to deliver services that improve patient outcomes (Porter 2010, Teisberg et al. 2020). Furthermore, we operationalize and empirically test our conceptualization of value using a multi-input, multi-output research framework that takes into consideration both cost and quality objectives associated with hospital resource allocation and health outcomes. Our proposed framework unites al stakeholders' interests and contributes to a more sustainable healthcare system (NEJM Catalyst 2017). Therefore, our study opens a new avenue for future research on the value of healthcare.

Second, prior health IT studies have primarily studied investments and adoption of the entire IT system as an artifact (Atasoy et al. 2017, Bao et al. 2020), whereas there has been limited research on evaluating the role of a hospital’s EHR sourcing strategy (Fareed et al. 2012, Ford et al. 2013). This is a critical research issue considering the growing debate on the choice of appropriate sourcing strategies for value-based healthcare models. Drawing on modular systems theory, we find that an SS strategy is associated with greater information sharing compared with an MS strategy. Enhanced health information exchange, in turn, improves the value of care delivery. Our research provides empirical evidence to substantiate this critical mechanism on how EHR sourcing strategy can drive value in healthcare (Adjerid et al. 2018). Importantly, our research suggests that health information sharing is a critical mediator through which EHR sourcing strategy affects the value of care. The mediation mechanism accounts for a 29% improvement in healthcare value attributed to SS strategy. Our findings imply that measuring EHR adoption as a binary choice may not always be the most accurate approach. Instead, researchers should study how EHR solutions are sourced and what types of modules should be implemented to support improvement in health information sharing and value, an overlooked issue in previous studies.

Furthermore, we observe that hospitals with greater practice integration are more likely to share patient health information at higher levels of single sourcing. Figure 2 highlights an increase in the level of information sharing for medium and high levels of practice integration as hospitals source a greater proportion of EHR systems from a single vendor (SS > 0.8). In other words, when hospitals are tightly integrated with physician practices, they can effectively deploy an SS strategy to exchange patient medical data with other providers. The strength of information sharing as the mediation mechanism varies across different integration models, accounting for 50% of improvement in healthcare value for full integration models to a negligible proportion for low integration models, such as IPAs and GPWWs (i.e., group practices). We draw on TCE theory to argue that tighter integration enforces better alignment with modular EHR systems, leading to frictionless information sharing. Our findings provide empirical evidence to test this theory with a better understanding of the role of practice integration in value-based care. Our observed moderation effect also underscores the necessary conditions for EHR sourcing to improve information sharing across providers’ organizational boundaries as they move toward tighter practice integration models (Post et al. 2018).

## 8.1. Practical Implications

As the U.S. healthcare system moves toward value based care reimbursement models, healthcare providers are often required to practice under multiple constraints that emphasize the triple aim: care experience, population health, and per capita costs. For instance, the mandatory hospital value-based purchasing program withholds 2% of payments for inpatient stays in acute care hospitals as an incentive for hospitals to achieve greater value, that is, better outcomes and lower cost. Our measurement of value provides a useful framework for providers to inform clinical practice and compete in a value-based care environment through resource reallocation, elimination of non–value-added services, and addressing gaps in patient care.

Better coordination of care delivery across clinical settings lies at the heart of many value-based care initiatives. For instance, CMS requires all participating entities in ACOs to effectively coordinate care delivery processes across the continuum of care to provide efficient, highquality care. This requires routine exchange of patient medical information, a critical ingredient for providers to manage population health across otherwise disparate provider organizations. Our research findings highlight the interoperability advantages of the SS strategy and support the observations by Hagland (2018) on the state of Beth Israel Deaconess Care network:

“ … [the] data resides in different systems. We have 46 different EHRs, and 150 different installs. There is data in core EHRs, but also in lab, radiology, and infusion systems. You want to try to get your hands on all that data, and that can be a challenge in and of itself. … All systems store data differently, and there is no fully embraced HIE standard. What’s more, an overall, industry-wide vision of interoperability has still not been achieved. And there remains a lack of vendor support, as well as fragmented access to EHR data … .”

On the other hand, the prevalent shift in healthcare payment models creates new requirements for EHR systems that can support the delivery of high-value care. Our results suggest that it is not optimal to source al EHR applications from a single vendor. Adoption of an SS strategy requires each hospital department to give up the flexibility to select EHR applications that best fit their business needs. Figure 2 suggests that when physician practice integration is low, hospitals are better off choosing a best-of-breed (i.e., multisourcing) EHR sourcing strategy, because low integration models dominate medium and high integration models with respect to the level of health information sharing across providers. In other words, despite a growing movement among hospitals toward adoption of an SS strategy, our results suggest that adoption of an SS strategy by itself is not sufficient to foster interorganizational information sharing due to significant organizational barriers.

When hospitals integrate with their physicians tightly, they share common interests that resolve transaction cost issues and incentivize providers to use their health IT capabilities to exchange patient data. However, practice integration also requires extensive capital investment and training on infrastructure management to better connect with physician practices. Kocher and Sahni (2011) reported that employing a single physician increases hospital expense by \$150,000 to 250,000 annually. Hence, tighter integration alone may not contribute to greater healthcare value due to a corresponding increase in resource inputs. Our findings echo the observations of Cullen et al. (2012) based on their study of physician integration models:

“ … The success of an organization’s physicianintegration efforts is substantially contingent on the effectiveness and reach of its clinical systems. Valuebased care requires functional clinical systems that provide interoperability between ambulatory and acute settings. Physicians will not want to use more than one EMR platform … .”

We observe that adoption of an SS strategy together with greater practice integration is associated with higher levels of information sharing. Specifically, Figure 2 indicates that when more than 80% of hospital EHR applica tions are sourced from a single vendor, then tighter integration between hospitals and physician practices outperforms loosely coupled partnerships with respect to health information sharing. In the AHA survey, we observed that more than 15% of hospitals planned to make significant changes to their primary EHR vendors within the next 18 months. Our results suggest that the hospital EHR sourcing strategies should be contingent on organizational choices related to their physician practice integration models. This is particularly true because a large share of hospitals have no to low practice integration (Short et al. 2017). In such situations, adoption of an EHR sourcing strategy without taking into consideration the organizational model for integrating physician practices may only provide marginal benefits with respect to information sharing and the value of care delivered.

## 9. Conclusions

Should hospitals source EHR systems from a single vendor or multiple vendors to deliver high-value care? This is a critical question for health IT managers and hospital executives with the emergence of value-based care initiatives in recent years. Our research shows that there is no one-size-fits-all solution. An SS strategy can facilitate information sharing, which improves healthcare value. However, this phenomenon is pronounced only when hospitals and physician practices are tightly integrated. Hospitals that adopt an SS strategy may not realize the full interoperability benefits of their EHR configuration without tighter practice integration because of incentive misalignment. Hence, it is of considerable importance for healthcare providers to prudently rethink their EHR sourcing strategies and account for their practice integration model with physicians. As the industry strives to achieve a vision of best care at lower cost, our research provides a roadmap for practitioners and policymakers to create a thriving value-based care environment.

Our study has several limitations. Our informationsharing construct is based on five categories of patient health data exchanged between ambulatory providers and hospitals. We acknowledge that this measurement only represents the breadth of information exchange and call for future studies to explore other dimensions to fully assess the impact of EHR sourcing strategy on the effectiveness of information sharing pathways. Second, our study primarily focuses on health information sharing as the mediator, although EHR sourcing strategies may affect hospital operational performance in many other ways, such as system usability and functionality. To the best of our knowledge, we are unaware of existing longitudinal, national data sources that provide such relevant data. Future field studies may be needed to examine the extent and intensity of EHR use and study their impact on healthcare value across different hospital sourcing strategies and practice integration models. Finally, our empirical analyses assume a linear effect of EHR sourcing on value, which may not be valid across the continuum of sourcing strategies. Future work can reveal the nonlinear impact of EHR sourcing strategies to offer richer insights. Overall, our study opens several exciting avenues for future scholarly work related to EHR sourcing strategy and healthcare value.
