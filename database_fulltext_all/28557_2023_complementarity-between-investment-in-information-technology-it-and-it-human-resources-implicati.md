# Complementarity Between Investment in Information Technology (IT) and IT Human Resources: Implications for Different Types of Firm Innovation

Abstract: It is well acknowledged in the information systems (IS) literature that information technology (IT) plays an important role in firm innovation. A small but growing body of empirical evidence points to the importance of IT employees as an important complementary resource, particularly for IT-related innovation. However, there is an open question as to whether investment in such resources is complementary across a range of innovation types. This research note aims to provide theoretical and empirical clarity by emphasizing the complementary nature of firm investment in IT and in IT human resources (HR) in affecting firm innovation performance across four types of innovation: incre mental, radical, non-IT related and IT related. Drawing on the theory of complementarities and theorizing about the operand and operant nature of resources, we argue that firm investment in IT HR enables firms to gain additional innovation value from their IT invest ments and vice versa. Using a sample involving 36,812 firm-year observations, we find that firms with robust investments in IT and in IT human resources produce more patents with greater value. Furthermore, we find that such firms produce more patents that emphasize new knowledge and those that build on existing knowledge. Finally, we find that this com plementarity promotes IT-related and non–IT-related innovation. A series of analyses dem onstrate the robustness of these results. We discuss implications for theory and practice.

## Introduction

Innovation—the creation of new products, services, or processes aimed at adding value to the market—is central to firms’ long-term competitiveness and sustainability (Grover and Kohli 2012, Nambisan 2013). Across a broad range of industries, information technologies (IT) are increasingly becoming an important part of enabling firms to innovate (Tilson et al. 2010). Although this has been true for some time, there is a sense that it has intensified over the last decade following several technological advancements, including big data analytics, artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and digital open innovation platforms, among others (Fang et al. 2018, Kohli and Melville 2019). These advances, combined with much improved computing power, have encouraged firms to think differently about the possibilities in innovating new products, processes, and services. To aid in their innovation efforts, some firms use artificial intelligence to facilitate drug discovery (Chen et al. 2018) and others leverage digital simulation technology to run hundreds of new product design experiments (Leonardi 2012). Other firms—such as Wealthfront—use digital technology to automate financial advice services to average consumers (Viceira and Ciechanover 2017). These represent just a fraction of the examples of ways in which firms are integrating IT into their efforts to create product and service innovations.

A rich body of literature in information systems (IS) has underscored the role of IT investment in enabling firm innovation performance (Xue et al. 2012). Some of this work has conceptualized IT investment as encompassing both IT infrastructure (such as hardware and software) and IT staff (Aral and Weill 2007, Xue et al. 2012). Other work has focused on IT investment strictly in terms of computing assets such as hardware and software and examined it as a driver of firm innovation performance (Joshi et al. 2010, Gao and Hitt 2012, Kleis et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2017). Recent research has drawn attention to investments in IT and investments in IT human resources (HR) as being distinct and complementary rather than independent or substitute drivers of firm innovation performance (Svahn et al. 2017, Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018), drawing attention to the benefit of keeping the two as conceptually distinct.

Consistent with this view, we define investment in IT as firm investment in technology hardware and software (Kleis et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2017), and we define investment in IT HR as firm investment in training and development of IT employees (Bapna et al. 2013, Mehra et al. 2014). Recognizing that investments in IT alone may not fully account for firm innovation performance, this view has sought to highlight the role that IT HR plays in innovation. Such research has noted that the increasing prevalence of IT in innovation processes combined with the observation that products are increasingly embedded with digital components underscores the need for an active role of IT HR to participate in sensing, sensemaking, and implementing such innovations (Gordon and Tarafdar 2010, Tarafdar and Tanriverd 2018). By decomposing how IT and IT HR investments relate to each other in affecting firm innovation performance, recent research has enabled IS scholarship and management practice to be more deliberate in explicating how IT-related investments shape firm innovation outcomes.

Building on this emerging view of the complementary role of investment in IT and investment in IT HR in firm innovation, this research note aims to theorize and empirically examine whether such complementarities are salient for different kinds of innovation. Specifically, we decompose firm innovation along two key dimensions: incremental versus radical innovation and non–IT-related versus IT-related innovation. Radical innovations involve creating new knowledge that is substantially different from a firm’s existing innovations, and incremental innovations mostly build on a firm’s existing innovations (Dahlin and Behrens 2005). IT-related innovations involve digital components as a major aspect of the product or service (Nambisan et al. 2017, Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018). In contrast, non–IT-related innovations do not substantively encompass digital components in the product or service.

It remains an open and unresolved theoretical and empirical question as to whether investments in IT and in IT HR have complementary effects on all these different forms of firm innovation. For instance, their role in incremental and non–IT-related innovation has been understudied because their role in such innovation is not obvious. Yet, such distinctions may be important to managers’ ability to organize for and align their investment decisions with their innovation objectives (Crossan and Apaydin 2010). It brings to light the riskreward tradeoffs that managers must make when considering their IT and IT HR investments relative to firm innovation objectives. On one hand, complementarity between IT and IT HR investments can serve as an operant resource to spur radical innovation—considering that investments in both enable the firm to expand research boundaries and use new knowledge. However, although the potential payoff for radical innovations is high, the costs of organizing operant resources and the risks of failure are also quite high (Kaplan and Vakili 2015). On the other hand, complementaries between IT and IT HR as operand resources can be crucial for the firm to incrementally improve upon existing knowledge and support ongoing innovation programs. Although the costs of organizing operand resources and the risks of failure are low, the potential payoffs for incremental innovations tend to be lower and easily replicable by competitors (Ettlie et al. 1984).

Examining the effect of IT and IT HR complementarities on IT-related and non–IT-related innovation is also important. One may think that complementarities between IT and IT HR are not necessary to produce non– IT-related innovation (e.g., new drugs, machinery, etc.). However, maximizing non–IT-related innovation requires a supporting digital technology infrastructure and knowledge on how to use such technologies effectively. As operand resources, IT and IT HR support the firm’s ability to harness, process, and communicate information to generate new non-IT-related products and services. The costs and risks of organizing for this kind of innovation tend to be lower. For IT-related innovations, it may require both IT and IT HR as operant resources to facilitate IT product innovation and to integrate IT components into products (Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018). Such innovations incur greater costs and risk as IT component interfaces and standards are prone to evolving in rapid cycles (Carrillo 2005). These discussions remain an open question in the otherwise robust literature on IT investment and firm innovation. Thus, this research note aims to illuminate whethe complementarities are necessary for producing positive outcomes for all types of innovations or only specific types.

To inform our theorizing about the potential differential implications of IT and IT HR investment complementarities across different types of innovations, we leverage theory on complementarities (Milgrom and Roberts 1995, 1990) and integrate theory on their role as operand versus operant resources in innovation (Nambisan 2013). Furthermore, recognizing the fast-paced and continuous evolution of technological landscapes that leads IT skills to atrophy (Bapna et al. 2013), we leverage theorizing on the importance of investment in IT HR, which emphasizes firms’ efforts to invest in their IT employees through continuous training programs, creation of incentive programs for professional development, the establishment of merit-based bonuses and promotion, and identification of career development paths (Ferratt et al. 2005, 2012). Such investments are essential for promoting the development of IT skill and understanding of contemporary IT (Bapna et al. 2013, Mehra et al. 2014). Our thesis is that such investments enable firms to get more out of their IT investments than investments in IT alone and are germane to incremental, radical, non–IT-related, and IT-related innovation.

To test our core thesis, we construct a panel data set for the period 2003 to 2012 using data from four different sources. First, we use Computerworld’s Annual 100 Best Places to Work in IT list to identify companies that have made successful investments in IT HR. This list considers key aspects along which firms invest in their IT employees, including career development, retention and promotion, benefits, and training. Second, we use the InformationWeek 500 list to capture companies that have made successful investments in IT. Information-Week 500 evaluates core areas of IT investments, including the IT budget, infrastructure, strategy, and deployment. Third, we collect data on patent activity from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). Finally, we use the CRSP/Compustat database to determine the value of patent filings and to construct control variables.

## Conceptual Background and Hypothesis Development

It is widely acknowledged that investment in IT has become a core part of firm innovation (Kleis et al. 2012, Xue et al. 2012, Nambisan et al. 2017, Fang et al. 2018, Kohli and Melville 2019). IT has become deeply entwined with processes that generate innovations. Numerous empirical studies across a wide variety of industries have illuminated how IT is implicated in innovation, including how it enables firms to orchestrate innovation (Nambisan 2003, Xue et al. 2012, Svahn et al. 2017), access external knowledge for innovation (Tambe et al. 2012, Trantopoulos et al. 2017), identify new patterns for innovation (Kyriakou et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2017), and establish new innovation activities (Boland et al. 2007, Dougherty and Dunne 2012). These and other studies clearly implicate IT as a key factor enabling firms to augment their innovation performance.

Investment in IT HR is conceptualized as the firm’s efforts to attract, develop, and motivate their IT employees through devotion of resources to skill-based training, merit-based incentive schemes, promotion, and career pathways (Ferratt et al. 2005). Agarwal and Ferratt (2001) identify this as a “long term investment” configuration in which firms place greater emphasis on career development, employment security, and concern for the IT employee. Ferratt et al. (2005) note that such investment—which emphasizes a greater focus on promotions and investments in training and development—reflects a long-term orientation and a relational focus in a firm’s strategy for leveraging its IT HR.

The knowledge-based view of the firm suggests that firms serve as an ideal means for integrating the knowledge that is embedded in human resources (Grant 1996). They can orchestrate the integration and application of specialized expertise to problem domains through a variety of mechanisms, including rules (Demsetz 1991) and routines (Pentland and Rueter 1994). To the extent that innovation is an exercise in coordinating and applying IT resources with specialized IT expertise and the domain expertise of new product development teams to innovation opportunities, firms serve as an ideal form of organizing to promote such activity (Nickerson and Zenger 2004). This suggests that investments in IT and investments in IT HR are complementary to each other in affecting innovation.

## IT and IT HR Investment Complementarities for Innovation

Milgrom and Roberts, (1990) theory of complementarities posits that firm investments in resources do not just produce independent effects but rather function as complements insofar as an investment in any one resource enhances the value gained from other resources. Importantly, they point out that a defining attribute of such complementary activities is that “if the levels of any subset of the activities are increased, then the marginal return to increases in any or all of the remaining activities rises” (Milgrom and Roberts 1990, p. 514). Milgrom et al. (1991) leverage this theory to observe the complementarities between advanced IT and flexible manufacturing in affecting manufacturing outcomes.

IS research has begun to theorize the complementarities between IT and IT HR in affecting IT-related innovation. IT HR is broadly characterized as playing a role in enabling firms to bridge the conceptual gap between the technical knowledge needed to make sense of IT and business knowledge needed to leverage them in context (Swanson and Ramiller 2004, Peppard et al. 2011, Peppard 2018). In light of this role, IT employees have been viewed as a strategic resource for firms (Ferratt et al. 2005, 2012). At the firm level, empirical and analytical research shows that investment in engaging with software developers improves firm software innovation performance (Stam 2009, Parker et al. 2017). Other research has identified and theorized the role of IT employees in sup porting the innovation of products embedded with IT components (Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018). Although this stream of research clearly demonstrates the core role that IT HR plays in IT-related innovation, it has not explicitly tended to the complementary nature of their relationship to IT in affecting other types of innovation.

Investments in IT and IT HR are particularly unique and noteworthy because they embody versatility in their nature as resources. Specifically, Nambisan (2013)

has drawn attention to the nature of IT-related resources as being operand or operant resources. Operand resources are those resources that are acted on in support of innovation (Nambisan 2013, Lusch and Nambisan 2015). Operant resources are resources that act on other resources to produce innovation. A firm’s IT investments can fluidly function as operand resources or as operant resources. Nambisan (2013) points out that innovators can leverage IT investments to support their innovation activities. Indeed, a majority of the empirical research on IT investments and firm innovation performance theorizes IT as an operand resource that supports innovation (Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Kleis et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2017). Nambisan (2013) points out that IT investments can also function as operant resources, triggering innovation. Here, IT investments initiate product or service innovation (Boland et al. 2007, Dougherty and Dunne 2012). Similarly, investments in IT HR can function as operand resources—where IT employees use their IT skills to support innovators in their sensemaking about how to leverage IT in their product or service innovation process (Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018). Conversely, they can function as operant resources, such as when IT employees create innovations (Fayard et al. 2016, Parker et al. 2017).

As our baseline hypothesis, we posit that, given their fluidity as operand and operant resources, investments in IT and IT HR are complementary in affecting firm innovation. Drawing on the theory of complementarities (Milgrom and Roberts 1990), investments in either IT-related resource enables firms to gain more in innovation from their investments in the other. Investments in IT enable firms to derive greater innovation benefits from their investments in IT HR. More robust IT resources provide a stronger technological foundation for IT employees to put their contemporary IT skills to use in supporting or initiating innovation. Such investments create new innovation possibilities for IT employees who are continuously developing their own IT skills through training and development. Conversely, investments in IT HR ensure that IT employees remain up to date in their IT skills (Bapna et al. 2013, Mehra et al. 2014), putting them in a position to get the most out of their IT investments in supporting or initiating innovation. Such investments curtail the erosion of innovation given the rate at which IT skills atrophy. In sum, investments in IT and IT HR are mutually complementary in affecting firm innovation.

Hypothesis 1. Investment in IT and IT HR will have a complementary association with firm innovation.

## Operant IT and IT HR Investment Complementarities

Operant IT and IT HR complementarity reflects resources acting on each other to unlock new innovation.

Investment in such resource combinations triggers innovation in unanticipated ways (Nambisan 2013). In this form, investments in IT and IT HR enable firms to augment and leverage new knowledge in the creation of new ideas. By way of illustration, Zymergen, a synthetic biology and material science firm, leverages investments in big data infrastructure, machine learning, software engineers, and data scientists to produce new materials constructed at the molecular level (Voigt 2020). By digitizing genetic sequences and encoding their material properties, the firm can simulate the recoding and experimentation of millions of alternative sequences and use machine learning to identify and isolate the most promising ones that meet prespecified material properties. Because they are engineered at the genetic or molecular level, these products represent radical departures from existing products. They are fundamentally new materials that did not previously exist in nature (Voigt 2020). Complementary investments that Zymergen makes in IT and IT HR are germane to being able to identify and produce such innovations, hence serving as operant resources (Nambisan 2013).

Although operant IT and IT HR complementarity seems appealing, it encompasses significant risk that may not make it worthwhile. First, operant IT and IT HR resources are costly. They are a manifestation of specialized knowledge or skill that is not easily accessible (Lusch and Nambisan 2015). They also require investments in time and effort due to experimentation. Second, their outcomes can be unpredictable and are not always positive (Bailey et al. 2012). This means that routines and adjacent resources must be reconfigured to make the most of these wakes of innovation (Boland et al. 2007). This entails centralized forms of organizing for innovation, which involves increased coordination costs (Crossan and Apaydin 2010). Despite their costs, payoffs from such investments are not assured.

Effects on Radical Innovation. Investments in operant IT and IT HR are expected to be complementary in affecting firms’ radical innovation. Radical innovation reflects a drastic change in a product or service (Anderson and Tushman 1990). Given this magnitude, such innovation often draws on new knowledge and capabilities (Dahlin and Behrens 2005). Radical innovations are viewed as creating technical discontinuities as they embody a marked departure from a firm’s existing knowledge base (Tushman and Anderson 1986).

Firms that invest in IT HR should expect to see increasing returns to their investments in IT (and vice versa) in the form of radical innovation. Here, these investments function as operant resources in relation to innovation activity; triggering, rather than supporting, product or service innovation (Nambisan 2013). They do so in two notable ways. First, they trigger new ways of innovating that facilitate the creation of innovations that would not otherwise exist. Dougherty and Dunne (2012) find that the digitization of scientific elements in drug discovery has created new knowledge for scientists that would not be possible in the absence of IT. This new knowledge—triggered by IT—makes it possible for scientists to envision new possibilities and formulate new chemical compounds for novel drugs (Dougherty and Dunne 2012). Conversely, they also find that scientists’ IT skills and knowledge base enable them to encompass precision, modularity, and standardization when imagining new molecular structures. Consequently, their knowledge enables firms to generate innovative returns from their IT investments in new drug discovery. This demonstrates the complementarity of IT and IT HR investments. Second, investments in operant IT and IT HR create an integrative networked environment that facilitates flows of new knowledge that are germane to radical innovation (Di Benedetto et al. 2008). For instance, Huang et al. (2022) find that understanding IT enables firms to generate new service innovations from their IT investments. Their study of WeCash revealed that the firm was able to repurpose its credit scoring technology to devise a whole new service innovation in farm loans. Their understanding of the IT’s data needs to generate accurate credit scoring led them to adapt their data generation and capture processes and build new data processing and management modules to execute this service innovation. In another example, Lou and Wu (2021) find that investments in AI infrastructure, combined with investment in employees who possess AI and domain knowledge, are more likely to identify new drugs. They argue that AI infrastructure enables innovators to search innovation landscapes at a large scale in a way that humans cannot replicate. They also point out that employees who possess a combination of AI skills and domain expertise are better able to calibrate and refine the AI models to improve new drug discovery.

Hypothesis 2. Investment in IT and IT HR will have a complementary association with firm radical innovation.

Effects on IT-Related Innovation. We expect investment in operant IT and IT HR to be complementary in affecting IT-related innovation. IT-related innovations are products or services that involve IT as a major component (Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018, Forman and Goldfarb 2020). Nambisan (2013) notes that IT is being embedded across a range of products and services. Such products or services involve software and hardware components that enable the product to engage in data flows (Yoo et al. 2010, Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018).

Investments in IT and IT HR as an operant resource are foundational to triggering IT-related innovation. This is attributable to the increased embedding of IT infrastructures (e.g., Internet of Things) into a wide range of products and services that operate in the physical world (Kallinikos et al. 2013, Faulkner and Runde 2019). The ontological ambivalence of such IT components in products and services means that they can be perpetually morphed and repurposed into new innovations. Furthermore, in light of IT infrastructures such as cloud technology, networks, and sensors, the data streams generated by such products or services can spark new product innovations by illuminating information about use patterns and market opportunities (Nambisan 2013). Further reflecting this complementarity, research on IT-embedded product innovations (Yoo et al. 2010, Taraf dar and Tanriverdi 2018) argues that IT employees play a direct role in developing new features in products for which IT is a core component. Such products are de signed to be programmable, editable, and reconfigurable (Kallinikos et al. 2013). Implementing and maintaining new features requires IT employees to be up-to-date on current programming and hardware standards for pro duct components that are affected by software (Bapna et al. 2013). The Huang et al. (2022) study of WeCash finds that its core IT developed for microlending was repur posed to create a service innovation for cellphone pur chase loans. In doing so, they learned that a photo ID was essential for credit scoring and risk management. Conse quently, WeCash was able to create a new service innovation by adding an image recognition module to the IT’s data collection workflow (Huang et al. 2022). Indicating that IT and IT HR may have an important complemen tary role in contributing to innovation processes, Bran stetter et al. (2019) find that software engineering intensity has progressively become positively associated with innovation outcomes in manufacturing firms as a range of products in the automotive, medical, and aero space industries incorporate software components.

Hypothesis 3. Investment in IT and IT HR will have a complementary association with firm IT-related innovation.

## Operand IT and IT HR Investment Complementarities

Operand IT and IT HR complementarity reflects resources that are acted on in the process of generating innovations (Nambisan 2013). They both function as enablers of the innovation process and support the innovation activity of new product or service teams (Gordon and Tarafdar 2010). Operand resources play a supporting role in enabling innovators to execute their innovation activity (Nambisan et al. 2017). In this form, investments in IT and IT HR enable firms to innovate using familiar activities and existing routines. Consistent with Nambisan (2013), we argue that both types of investment function are enablers of the innovation process, supporting the innovation activity of new product or service teams (Gordon and Tarafdar

2010). As an illustration, an ethnography by Leonardi (2012) recounts how an automobile manufacturer’s software engineers enabled the automotive design team to create safer cars by using simulation software CrashLab to innovate how they conducted crash tests for their new designs. Through IT as an enabler and the support of IT HR, the manufacturer was able to run hundreds of crash tests more cost-effectively, allowing them to improve the safety of their car designs (Leonardi 2012).

Investments in operand IT and IT HR resources may be appealing as their application is relatively straightforward, involving minimal disruption of organizational routines and the payoffs from such investments are much clearer (Crossan and Apaydin 2010). However, the innovation payoffs from such investments tend to be smaller in scale and the IT and IT HR resources tend to be imitable (Nambisan 2013). Consequently, although beneficial, such investments may not confer sustained innovation advantage for firms.

Effect on Incremental Innovation. Investments in IT and IT HR are expected to be complementary in affecting firms’ incremental innovation. That is, an increase in IT HR investments lays the foundation for firms to enjoy greater returns to investments in IT (and vice versa) in the form of incremental innovation (Milgrom and Roberts 1990). Incremental innovation represents small changes to a product or service (Tushman and Anderson 1986, Anderson and Tushman 1990, Ritala and Hurmelinna-Laukkamen 2013). Such innovations draw heavily from a firm’s existing knowledge base to introduce slight improvements or extensions in its products or services (Garcia and Calantone 2002). They are often characterized as reinforcing an existing technical order (Rosenbloom and Abernathy 1982).

Investments in operand IT and IT HR provide the important informational infrastructure that is central to innovation (Tilson et al. 2010). Innovation activity requires information on product or service specifications and communication connectivity between innovators (Xue et al. 2012). Innovators need to be able to collect, manipulate, and process product or service information. They can also benefit from visualizing such information, running diagnostics, and experimenting with potential improvements (Kleis et al. 2012). IT HR plays a key role in enabling firms to bridge the conceptual gap between the technical knowledge needed to make sense of IT and business knowledge needed to leverage them in context (Swanson and Ramiller 2004, Peppard et al. 2011, Peppard 2018). In keeping with the operand resource view, Xue et al. (2012) argue that investment in customer relationship management systems enables firms to solicit and capture customer preference information to identify an opportunity for product or service improvements (Xue et al. 2012). Ravichandran et al. (2017) point out that research and development (R&D) activities are informationally intensive and that IT investments enhance firms’ capacity to process information to get the most out of such activities (Ravichandran et al. 2017). IT HR bridges this knowledge gap, enabling new product or service development teams to be more effective in using their existing knowledge supported by the informational infrastructure of IT (Pavlou and El-Sawy 2006, Gordon and Tarafdar 2010, Peppard 2018). Tarafdar and Gordon (2007) highlight IT HR as a key aspect of firms’ IS competencies necessary to leverage knowledge assets in the process of product or service innovation. IT HR facilitate access to and integration of firms’ existing knowledge assets through devel opment or configuration and deployment of knowledge management systems (Tarafdar and Gordon 2007). This visibility of existing knowledge assets enables firms to connect otherwise isolated pockets of innovation, improving the efficiency of innovation (Tarafdar and Gordon 2007).

Hypothesis 4. Investment in IT and IT HR will have a complementary association with firm incremental innovation.

Effect on Non–IT-Related Innovation. We expect investments in operand IT and IT HR to be complementary in affecting firms’ non–IT-related innovation. Non–ITrelated innovations broadly characterize new products or services that do not incorporate IT as an embed ded component—for example, physical machinery parts and consumer packaged goods (Milgrom and Roberts 1990).<sup>1</sup> The design of new non–IT-related products or services still requires the ability to search for, capture, and integrate information (Tarafdar and Gordon 2007). Operand IT and IT HR resources facilitate this (Pavlou and El Sawy 2006). To illustrate, Saldanha et al. (2020) identify the role of enterprise social technologies (e.g., Wikis, social media) in enabling firms to collate and aggregate these unstructured data so that they can be used to gen erate new product ideas. Consistent with Tarafdar and Gordon (2007), they also point to the role of such IT in facilitating weak ties between innovators, enabling them to engage in knowledge collaboration to envision new products or services.

Investments in operand IT and IT HR provides firms with needed innovation infrastructure to meet informational demands. To create non–IT-related innovations, innovators need to scan for and capture in formation on customer needs (Xue et al. 2012, Saldanha et al. 2017). They may also need to digitize product or service information (e.g., specifications) to visualize, share, and manipulate their real-world representation. For instance, Kyriakou et al. (2017) examine how metamodels in three-dimensional (3D) printing communities on Thingiverse are used to visualize and customize the design of physical products. Kyriakou et al. (2017) point out that these metamodels contain the necessary information to print a family of products or parts. Innovators are able to share and recombine these metamodels to create new physical products (Kyriakou et al. 2017). Customization and integration of IT investments with existing infrastructure are often necessary before they can be leveraged for innovation.

Hypothesis 5. Investment in IT and IT HR will have a complementary association with firm non–IT-related innovation.

## Method

To test our main hypotheses on the complementarity of investments in IT and IT HR in affecting firm innovation performance, we construct a panel data set using four different sources. We now describe our operationalization approach for the main variables in our model.

## Investment in IT

Annually, InformationWeek ranks companies that use IT in innovative ways. As explained by the publisher, The InformationWeek 500 list (IW500) “tracks the IT practices of the nation’s most innovative IT organizations, providing a unique opportunity to understand and examine the business practices of these firms across core areas of operations, including technology deployment, IT budgets, business-technology infrastructure, and IT strategies.” InformationWeek invites organizations to fill out a comprehensive survey, written by InformationWeek editors, about their IT strategies, initiatives, and practices. Based on the information gathered, the editorial panel selects and ranks organizations included in the annual IW500 list. In our sample, companies included in the IW500 list are designated as those who have made successful IT investments. This list is the most commonly used in research on IT investment and firm innovation performance (Joshi et al. 2010, Ravichandran et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2017).

## Investment in IT HR

Computerworld is a leading source of technology news and information for IT influencers worldwide: its website, publications, conference series, and custom research form the hub of the world’s largest global IT media network (Computerworld 2014a).<sup>2</sup> As explained by Computerworld, the Annual 100 Best Places to Work in IT (CW100) list “showcases the organizations that excel at keeping their employees engaged and loyal with compensation, training, and access to hot technologies (Computerworld 2014b).”<sup>3</sup> Each year, Computerworld conducts a survey to identify the 100 best places to work for IT professionals. Computerworld invites its readers, professionals, and other interested parties to nominate organizations they consider great employers for IT workers. In selecting the annual final top 100 companies to work for in IT, Computerworld emphasizes key areas, including career development, retention, benefits, and training.

Another important feature of Computerworld’s methodology is the procurement of feedback from actual employees of companies that have been nominated (Computerworld 2014b). Upon completion of the survey, all participating companies are required to obtain feedback from a random sample of their U.S.-based full- and part-time IT employees. The survey considers IT employees who provide technology support and services to their companies. This aligns with our theorizing about IT employees enabling their firms to innovate by leveraging IT support. In scoring the responses from the company and employee surveys, results are weighted based on the importance ratings provided by respondents to the employee survey. Hence, the CW100 list balances the benefits that have been claimed by the company with the perception of actual employees. We refer to companies that appear on this CW100 list as firms that have made substantial investments in their IT HR.

## Innovation Outcomes

We operationalize firms’ innovation performance in the domain of patenting activities (Kleis et al. 2012, Nambisan 2013). Following the patent literature, we include a list of measures to capture innovation outcomes across several dimensions. The first set of innovation measures is related to the quantity and importance of companies’ patenting activities (Fang et al. 2014, Tian and Wang 2014, Mukherjee et al. 2017). Consistent with prior IS research, we measure the quantity of patents (Patent Count) as a count of the number of patents that were filed during the fiscal year (Gao and Hitt 2012). Next, we augment this operationalization by following Kogan et al. (2017), who use the stock market reaction around its patent grant announcement as a proxy for the market value of its patent, which we refer to as the Value of New Patents.<sup>4</sup> The USPTO publishes newly granted patents and the details of the patents through the Official Gazette every Tuesday unless there is a federal holiday. We obtain patent assignee names, three-digit patent technology classes, patent filing date, patent grant date, and which previous patents have been cited by each patent, and the citations received by each patent in the future. For each individual patent, Kogan et al. (2017) estimate the value of a patent as the change in total market capitalization in a three-day window, starting from the release day of the Official Gazette (each Tuesday in the week) to Thursday in the same week, after adjusting for idiosyncratic stock return volatility and aggregate stock market movement. According to Kogan et al. (2017), the stock market reacts to the issuance of a new patent i by adjusting the value of the firm that owns patent i as follows:

$$
\Delta V _ {i} = (1 - \pi_ {i}) \xi_ {i},\tag{K1}
$$

where π<sub>i</sub> is the market’s ex ante belief that the applica tion for patent i is eventually granted and $\xi _ { i }$ is the economic value of patent i. To determine the economic value of this patent i, Kogan et al. focus only on the component of the idiosyncratic return. This is represented in the firm’s return minus the return on the market portfolio around patent issuance events that are directly related to the value of the patent. They decompose the idiosyncratic return for a given firm around the time of the issuance of its patent i as

$$
R _ {i} = v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i},\tag{K2}
$$

where $v _ { i }$ represents the stock return that is related to patent $i ,$ and $\varepsilon _ { i }$ represents the component of the firm’s stock return that is not related to patent i. The economic value of the patent $i , \xi _ { i } ,$ is the product of the estimated stock return due to the value of the patent, $v _ { i } ,$ , and the market capitalization $M _ { i }$ of the firm owning patent i on the day prior to the announcement of the patent issuance:

$$
\xi_ {i} = (1 - \overline {{\pi}}) ^ {- 1} \frac {1}{N _ {i}} E (v _ {i} \mid R _ {i}) M _ {i},\tag{K3}
$$

where $\overline { { \pi } }$ represents the unconditional probability of a successful patent application, which is approximately 56% based on all patent applications from 1991 to 2001, and $N _ { i }$ is the number of patents issued to the same firm on the same day as patent i. We aggregate the value of all patents issued by a given firm at the patent application year to calculate the economic value of patents in that particular year. 一

The second set of innovation measures is related to the knowledge used to develop patents. We separate the patents into two categories: New Knowledge Patent (radical innovation) and Old Knowledge Patent (incrementa innovation). Dahlin and Behrens (2005) suggest a similar approach to operationalizing incremental versus radical innovations. Following Brav et al. (2018), we define a patent as New Knowledge Patent (Old Knowledge Patent) if 80% of its citations are based on new knowledge (existing knowledge). Existing knowledge includes all the patents that the firm has invented and all the patents that were cited by the firm’s existing patents over the past five years. New knowledge includes all the patents that the firm did not invent and all patents not cited by the firm’s patents filed over the past five years. We then create two separate measures at the firm-year level. The New Knowledge Patent (Old Knowledge Patent) at the firm-year level is defined as the natural logarithm of the total number of newly filed new (old) knowledge patents in year t + 1.<sup>6</sup>

The last set of innovation measures is related to the technological classes of patents. We separate the patents into two categories: IT-related Patent and Non–ITrelated Patent. Following Forman and Goldfarb (2020), we classify a patent as IT-related Patent if the patent is related to the computers and communication category as defined in Hall et al. (2001).<sup>7</sup> All patents that do not meet the classification requirements of IT-related

Patent are considered as non–IT-related. Similar to the previous set of innovation measures, we create two separate measures at the firm-year level. The dependent variables, IT-related Patent and Non–IT-related Patent, are measured in year t + 1 in natural logarithm form.

## Discussion

This research aims to contribute by theorizing and empirically examining the complementarity between investment in IT and IT HR in facilitating different types of firm innovation—incremental, radical, non-IT related, and IT related. To this end, we draw on the theory of complementarities (Milgrom and Roberts 1991) and theory on operand and operant resources (Nambisan 2013) to articulate how investments in IT and IT HR jointly affect these different types of innovation. We empirically examine these complementary impacts on innovation performance in the form of patents generated from 2003 to 2012. Consistent with our main thesis, we find that firms that make superior investments in IT and IT HR achieve higher innovation performance overall. Furthermore, we observe these gains across all four types of firm innovation. The findings are consistent across a range of robustness tests.

## Theoretical Contributions

The findings in this research contribute to the literature in several significant ways. First, this research contributes to IS research on IT-related investment and firm innovation. Research in this stream has cumulatively demonstrated how IT investments contribute to firm innovation performance (Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Barczak et al. 2007, Nambisan and Baron 2009, Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2017). More recently, this work has drawn attention to the complementarity between investment in IT and IT HR in affecting firm innovation performance (Svahn et al. 2017, Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018, Branstetter et al. 2019). The unresolved theoretical and empirical question in this literature was whether such complementary investments are robust across different types of firm innovation. Prior work either examined firm innovation performance as unitary (without distinguishing the type of innovation) or examining one type (principally IT-related innovation), leaving open the question of whether complementarities matter for other types of innovation. We contribute to the IS literature by theorizing that complementary investments in IT and IT HR are associated with firm innovation performance across four different types of innovation that have traditionally been studied. This finding adds to the growing body of work on the complementary role of IT and IT HR in firm innovation, draws attention to the need for future research to theoretically and empirically distinguish the type of firm innovation under study, and suggests that complementarities in IT-related investments should be investigated across a broader range of innovation types.

Second, our findings contribute to the literature on strategic IT HR. Prior research has laid a firm foundation for understanding the value of investing in IT employees (Ang and Slaughter 2004, Ferratt et al. 2005). For instance, research has focused on the returns to the training of IT employees in the form of productivity (Bapna et al. 2013), wage gains (Mithas and Krishnan 2008), and firm productivity gains (Mehra et al. 2014, Tambe 2014). This extant research offers a less clear view of the theoretical role of investment in IT HR to promote different types of innovation. This research represents among the earliest attempts to theorize and demonstrate empirically that investments in IT HR can promote different types of firm innovation performance through its complementary effects on IT investment. This is a significant theoretical shift as it suggests that investing in IT HR is not only germane to IT-related innovation but that it plays a significant role in enabling firms to make the most of their investments in IT to promote less obvious types of innovation like incremental, radical, and non–IT-related innovation. IT HR has been implicitly assumed to play a lesser role in promoting these types of innovation—the presumption being that IT alone provides the necessary foundation for new product development teams to create such innovations (Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018). The findings suggest that future research should embrace an expanded role of investments in IT HR in contributing to firm innovation.

Finally, our research provides a robust operationalization of firm innovation performance beyond common practice. Although a majority of the research in the innovation domain has focused on patents (Joshi et al. 2010, Saldanha et al. 2017) to operationalize innovation at the firm level, there have been notable empirical limitations that we address in this work. A majority of prior work has not differentiated between different types of innovations—including differentiating patents that leverage a firm’s existing knowledge versus innovations based on new knowledge, and patents that are IT related versus those that are non-IT related. In this research, we enrich the operationalization of firm innovation performance in two consequential ways. The first is by focusing on the value of patents. This is particularly important because patent filings alone do not fully reflect the value that firms derive from their innovation activity. Our approach offers a more complete accounting of innovation performance compared with a majority of prior research in this domain. The second is in distinguishing between patents that build on a firm’s existing knowledge and those that are based on new knowledge and those that are IT related versus those that are non-IT related. This is important because it explicitly acknowledges that innovation output is not monolithic but rather varies in the degree of newness and IT relatedness. The online appendix illustrates how our operationalization compares to that of recent research.

## Practical Implications

The primary practical implication of this research for managers is that investments in IT and IT HR are complementary in nature across a range of innovation types. As firms invest in IT and IT HR to augment their innovation efforts, they can put themselves in a good position to gain value in their innovation efforts from those investments through complementarities. When making such investments, it is important for managers to consider the associated risk-reward tradeoffs for different types of innovations. Managers pursuing radical or ITrelated innovations need to weigh the costs of organizing for such innovations against the potential payoff. Such innovations tend to have larger payoffs if successful, but they are also at high risk of failure. In contrast, incremental and non–IT-related innovations are less costly in terms of organizing and the payoffs are much clearer. However, the payoffs tend to be smaller and shorter lived. Regardless, our research suggests to managers that IT and IT HR investments go hand in hand. A second important implication is that managers need to be deliberate about deploying their IT HR as operand versus operant resources. Their orientation toward the innova tion process is quite distinct and managers should act accordingly. In operand roles IT HR primarily function as enablers or supporters of innovation, implementing or configuring necessary information infrastructure. In contrast, in operant roles, IT HR need to be deployed as active participants in the innovation process, generating solutions, engaging in collective sense making and analysis of information while being embedded in innovation teams. In such cases, IT HR training may need to incorporate some knowledge of the problem domain.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

This research is subject to limitations. We rely on Computerworld’s Annual 100 Best Places to Work in IT to identify companies that exhibit superior investment in IT HR. This list may not comprehensively capture companies with superior investment in IT HR because it only includes 100 organizations annually. Moreover, due to data availability, our analyses are limited to publicly traded companies that make the list. It would be useful for subsequent research to investigate whether an investment in IT HR is beneficial for innovation efforts in other settings, including privately held companies, governmental entities, universities, and other academic institutions.

We rely on the InformationWeek 500 list to identify companies that have made substantial investments in IT and hence have high IT capability. It is possible that this list (to some degree) also captures some elements of IT HR investments. However, to the extent that the InformationWeek 500 list already identifies those with high IT HR investments, this would bias against us finding significant results for the complementarity variable. We use the Computerworld Best Places to Work list in our analyses because it primarily focuses on IT HR practices.

There are several dimensions to innovation productivity. Future work can examine other dimensions of patents that are not investigated in this study. For example, Wu et al. (2020) measure the novelty of innovation, which is based on the concept that novel ideas can be detected through changes in the vocabulary of the patent disclosure. They examine the appearance of new words or topics in the patent abstract. This and other dimensions of innovation can be examined further in future work.

In this research, we only focused on innovation outcomes. We did not uncover the mechanisms by which investments in IT and IT HR exert their complementary effects to generate innovation outcomes. Furthermore, our research design is not causal in nature. Future work should apply other research methods (e.g., field study, natural experiments) to pinpoint precisely how such investments enable IT and IT employees to contribute meaningfully to firm innovation efforts.

## Conclusion

Firms increasingly rely on IT in their innovation. This has elevated the importance of IT HR to support or trigger firms’ innovation efforts. In this research, we highlight the role of investment in IT and IT HR in enabling firms to realize greater innovation performance across different types of innovations. Our empirical study of 6,234 firms over the period 2003 to 2012 supports this complementary relationship. The results show that firms with robust investments in IT and IT HR achieve greater incremental, radical, non–IT-related, and IT-related innovation performance in the form of patents and the value associated with them. The findings highlight the importance for firms of providing a holistic approach to investing in IT capabilities across different types of innovations.
