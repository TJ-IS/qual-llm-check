# Does Social Influence Change with Other Information Sources? A Large-Scale Randomized Experiment in Medical Crowdfunding

Abstract. We examine how social influence interacts with other information sources to affect user behaviors in the context of medical crowdfunding. We conduct a large-scale randomized field experiment on a leading medical crowdfunding platform, showing friends donation information to donors in the treatment group and not showing such information in the control group, and examine how the likelihood to donate differs. In addition, we conduct a survey on Amazon Mechanical Turk to evaluate the informational value of different case attributes in conveying the patients’ need for help to donors. We find that for cases containing attributes with high informational value (e.g., minor patient, severe conditions), social influence is insignificant. In contrast, for cases lacking attributes with high informational value, social influence significantly increases donors’ likelihood to donate. Overall, our results show that the impact of social influence depends on the informational value of other information sources, suggesting that the social influence in our context is primarily informational. Our findings indicate that rather than generating an entrenchment effect, where cases with attributes of high informational value attract disproportionate ben efits, social influence can increase donation likelihood to cases that lack such attributes, promoting more equal access to resources overall.

## 1. Introduction

In an increasingly interconnected world, users’ behaviors are influenced by the actions of others both online and offline (Turner 1991). Previous studies have examined whether social influence exists and how its impact varies under different conditions (Aral and Walker 2011, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Huang et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2018). However, no research has systematically studied how social influence interacts with multiple other information sources. This paper aims to fill this research gap. Using a large-scale randomized field experiment in the context of medical crowdfunding, we explore how the impact of social influence on donation decisions varies depending on the informational value of case attributes.

Medical crowdfunding is a major type of donationbased crowdfunding, where fundraisers seek financial help from donors to cover medical expenses (Young and Scheinberg 2017). It has grown rapidly in scale and popularity in recent years; for example, about a third of all crowdfunding cases on GoFundMe are for supporting healthcare costs, raising around \$650 million in donations each year (Charles 2021). However, not all fundraisers are equally successful in convincing potential donors to offer help, and large disparities usually exist in the attention and support that different cases receive (Kim et al. 2016). Unlike traditional charity, where donations are collected for a general group of recipients, each medical crowdfunding case raises funds for an identifiable patient. To present a compelling case to donors, medical crowdfunding fundraisers need to understand how different case attributes (such as patient age, medical condition, and gender) affect users’ willing ness to donate and whether social influence interacts with this effect. Without such an understanding, a blanket implementation of social influence can be ineffective and even cause a negative impact in practice (Zhang and Liu 2012, Burtch et al. 2013).

Prior work has not examined in detail how social influence changes depending on the informational value provided by other sources (Connelly et al. 2011). It is possible that social influence is more salient in cases that have attributes with high informational value, since prior work has shown that social connections sometimes generate an entrenchment effect, as better-connected individuals receive more resources (Wu and Kane 2021). It is also possible that in the presence of case attributes with high informational value, the informational role of social influence is mitigated, as prior work has shown that when multiple signals are present, users tend to focus on signals that are more salient or have higher priority (Morton and Podolny 2002, Higgins et al. 2011). In this study, we aim to fill the research gap and systematically examine how social influence changes depending on the informational value of other information sources.

We conduct a large-scale donor-level randomized field experiment involving more than 700,000 donors on a leading medical crowdfunding platform in China. The platform sets up the web page for medical crowdfunding cases and handles the donations, but the case information is disseminated entirely by users sharing case links with their social connections. Thus, donors can only access a case page by clicking the link shared with them and not through searching or browsing the platform. This feature makes this platform an ideal context for studying social influence and how it interacts with informational value in case attributes. To estimate the impact of social influence cleanly, during the experiment period, we randomly assign users to treatment and control groups the first time they click to view the details of a case page. This assignment is fixed for a given user across all subsequent case page visits for the duration of the experiment. For the control group, the case page displays the case title, target funding amount, and a text description; for the treatment group, the case page displays the same information as that for the control group, plus an extra line of information showing the donation contributed by the friend from whom the focal donor receives the case link (i.e., the social influence treatment).

To characterize the informational value of case attributes, we conduct an online survey using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), where we list all the available case attributes and ask respondents to evaluate how each case attribute would influence their perception that the case is in genuine need of help. The results indicate that three case attributes—patient age, severe diseases and whether the patient has commercial insurance—have strong impact on donors’ perception that the case is in genuine need of help, whereas other case attributes— such as patient gender, target donation amount, the length of the case description, and whether the case provides many photos—have a weaker impact. Based on the results, we examine how social influence differs depending on the informational value of different case attributes.

Our results show that for cases that have an attribute with high informational value (e.g., severe conditions), social influence does not have a significant impact on donation likelihood. In contrast, for cases that only have an attribute with low informational value (e.g., female patient), social influence has a strong and positive impact on donors’ donation likelihood. In addition, we find that the combination of multiple case attributes with low informational value provides high informational value jointly, consequently mitigating social influence. Furthermore, using out-of-sample data from the same source, we train a model to compute the informativeness index of a case, measuring the overall aggregative informational value in all case attributes. We observe consistent results that social influence increases a donor’s donation likeli hood and donation amount, but the impact is reduced for cases with higher informativeness index.

Our findings provide important insights for medical crowdfunding platforms and fundraisers by systematically examining how social influence interacts with the informational value of case attributes in driving donation decisions. We demonstrate that social influence helps funnel attention and funding, particularly for cases that lack attributes with high informational value. In addition, our findings can generalize to other contexts, such as peer-to-peer platforms and prosocial campaigns, and provide insights into how social influence and other information sources jointly influence user behavior.

Section 4 describes the experiment design, the empirical analysis framework, and the MTurk survey that establishes the informational value of various case attributes. Section 5 presents our main results. Section 6 reports additional analyses for underlying mechanisms and robustness checks. Finally, Section 7 discusses our contributions and the managerial implications.

## 2. Literature Review

In this section, we review prior studies from the three streams of research that are related to our paper: crowdfunding, charitable giving, and social influence.

## 2.1. Medical Crowdfunding and Information Asymmetry

Medical crowdfunding (i.e., raising donations from the general populace for healthcare-related purposes) provides an alternative solution to alleviating financial shocks from unexpected medical conditions (Young and Scheinberg 2017). By making donations to medical crowdfunding cases, donors provide financial support for cases they find compelling. A major challenge for medical crowdfunding is the information asymmetry problem, arising from the difficulty to convince users that the patient’s need is genuine—that is, the severity of the patient’s condition is accurately described, and the patient does not have alternative resources to finance the medical treatments (Kim et al. 2016). Furthermore, donors typically lack relevant medical expertise to evaluate specific medical conditions, which potentially prevents them from evaluating the severity of the condition and the patient’s need for help effectively (Kolata 2011). Since medical crowdfunding mainly relies on the information presented in the case descriptions to motivate donors to make donations, it is important to examine how different sources of information influence donation decisions.

The emerging literature stream on donation-based crowdfunding mostly focuses on evaluating the aggregate financial or societal impact. For example, Burtch and Chan (2019) examine how medical crowdfunding influences individual bankruptcy filings. Gao et al. (2020) demonstrate how educational crowdfunding for teachers’ projects influences student performance in school. The few existing studies that examine the factors that influence the credibility of medical crowdfunding campaigns mostly rely on aggregate-level data or interview data (Kim et al. 2016, Young and Scheinberg 2017), limiting their ability to identify the impact of specific case attributes on donation decisions. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no study has explored how social influence and different information sources impact donations in the medical crowdfunding context. Our study fills this gap by conducting a large-scale donor-level randomized field experiment and analyzing how social influence affects the likelihood to donate, contingent on the informational value of case attributes.

## 2.2. Charitable Giving and Recipient Attributes

Most of the existing studies on charitable giving focus on how various donor attributes affect donation intentions (Andreoni and Vesterlund 2001, Einolf 2011) and how factors such as compassion mediation, cost of prosocial behavior, and donor power moderate such effects (Ashar et al. 2016, Kessler and Milkman 2018). Other studies investigate how external factors such as advertising content and designs of the campaign influence donors’ likelihood to contribute to charitable campaigns (Karlan and List 2007, Sudhir et al. 2016). Prior work on charitable giving mostly treats fundraisers as a homogenous group and does not examine how various fundraiser attributes may change donors’ likelihood to donate. In contrast, medical crowdfunding provides great variations on the patient side such that we can explore how patient attributes can influence donations. Using a randomized field experiment and detailed donor-level data, our study extends this literature stream by examining how social influence varies depending on the attributes of the patient and the medical crowdfunding case.

Past work on charitable giving has separately examined the impact of different recipient attributes on donations.

Relating to the age of the recipients, studies have found that children have generally been considered a vulnerable population in need of care and help (Landrigan 2004), and charity for children are found to be particularly popular among donors (Chapman et al. 2020). These findings suggest that young patient age likely has high informational value in convincing donors of the case’s need for help. In contrast, whereas gender has been shown in prior studies to signal founder trustworthiness in the reward-based crowdfunding context (Johnson et al. 2018), mixed evidence exists regarding gender differences in healthcare outcomes. On the one hand, studies indicate that disparities exist in access to healthcare for women (Rosen and Schneider 2004, Manuel 2018). On the other hand, studies find that women have longer life expectancy and smaller chances of suffering from fatal conditions in general compared with men (Regitz-Zagrosek et al. 2010). These contrasting findings suggest that patient gender likely has low informational value in convincing donors of the case’s need for help.

Findings from these separate studies generally support the observation that various case attributes have different informational value in informing donors of the need of the recipient. However, no study has compared such informational value across attributes or systematically examined how they moderate the impact of social influence on donations. We contribute to this stream of studies by examining the informational value of different attributes for medical crowdfunding cases and exploring how social influence varies depending on the informational value of the case attributes.

## 2.3. Informational Social Influence

Prior work has shown that an individual’s behavio is affected by others’ behaviors, which is referred to as social influence (Aral and Walker 2011, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Zhang et al. 2018). Through informational social influence, individuals infer information from others behavior to reduce uncertainty, using social influence as a credible source of information (Cohen and Golden 1972, Burnkrant and Cousineau 1975). Huang et al. (2020) find that social influence is moderated by product category in social advertising, as status goods rely more on normative social influence, and experience goods rely more on informational social influence. Iyengar et al. (2015) find that informational social influence dominates in trial stages to reduce the risk for drug adoption by physicians, whereas normative social influence exerts a stronger influence on increasing conformity through repeated usage. Qiu et al. (2018) find that informational social influence through friends’ checking-in on location-based social networks encourages users to try new restaurants. Dewan et al. (2017) examine the role of social influence through different types of network positions in conveying information and influencing online music consumption. However, no studies have examined how social influence changes, contingent on the presence of various information sources. In this study, we fill this gap and examine whether social influence differs depending on whether attributes with high informational value are present, using medical crowdfunding as the research context.

## 3. Development of Hypotheses

Perceived need for help is one of the main motivations that drive donation behavior (Bekkers and Wiepking 2011). However, information asymmetry can mitigate the efficiency of the need of the patient being conveyed to the donors. Donors likely rely on the case attributes to infer whether the patient is in genuine need of help, and this results in disproportionate donations to cases with certain types of attributes, as observed in industry reports and confirmed in our analyses (Kim et al. 2016). Displaying friends’ donation information can influence users’ donation decisions through informational social influence. This is because friends who donated likely have outside private information, such as relevant medical knowledge about the disease, or have social network connections with the patient to validate the case. Therefore, focal donors likely perceive friends’ donations as an endorsement that the case is in genuine need of help and increase the chances that they donate to the case. Thus, we have our first hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. Social influence increases the likelihood that a focal user donates to a case.

We propose that social influence interacts with the informational value of case attributes. The main reason is that the marginal value of informational social influence is contingent on the available alternative information that case attributes provide. Past studies on information literacy show that signals can have different informational values depending on their levels of priority and saliency (Gulati and Higgins 2003, Ramaswami et al. 2010, Connelly et al. 2011). When multiple signals are present, users potentially discount or disregard lowpriority information in the presence of a superior information source (Morton and Podolny 2002, Higgins et al. 2011). The informational value of a signal becomes redundant when the underlying quality it seeks to reflect can be better captured by other signals from more direct sources (Pollock et al. 2010). We argue that the impact of social influence on donations is contingent on the informational value of the case attributes. For cases that have at least one attribute with high informational value, which serves as strong evidence that the case is in genuine need of help, donors likely rely on such case attributes as a direct source of reliable information, rendering the informational value from social influence redundant. In contrast, when no such attributes with high informational value are present, donations from the focal user’s friends present a valid source of information for donors to evaluate the genuine need of the case; thus, social influence is likely more salient for these cases.

Furthermore, we hypothesize that for cases that lack attributes with high informational value, social influence is also mitigated in the presence of multiple attributes with low informational value. Prior work has shown that the informational value of additional signals increases when each additional signal contains unique and sub stantive information (Pollock et al. 2010). Studies have also shown that multiple signals can complement each other when they provide congruent information and the information contained in one signal does not fully encompass the information in the other signal (Stern et al. 2014, Steigenberger and Wilhelm 2018, Bapna 2019). When a case lacks a dominant attribute with high infor mational value to convey the need of the case clearly, dif ferent case attributes with low informational value can each provide unique information in support of the need of the case. Therefore, we expect to see that the margina value of informational social influence is mitigated, as multiple attributes with low informational value add up to provide higher informational value overall. In sum, we hypothesize that social influence is stronger when case attributes contain low informational value overall and is mitigated in the presence of a case attribute with high informational value or multiple case attributes with low informational value.

Hypothesis 2. The positive impact of social influence on donation likelihood is mitigated when the informational value in case attributes is higher.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Research Context and Experiment Design

We collaborate with a leading medical crowdfunding platform in China to conduct a large-scale randomized field experiment. The platform has a “keep-it-all” rather than an “all-or-nothing” model, allowing fundraisers to receive the amount of donations raised regardless of whether the fundraising goal is fully met. The platform does not collect fees, and the entire sum of donations collected is sent to fundraisers. As a fraud-prevention measure, a seven-day grace period begins after the con clusion of each campaign, which allows the platform to investigate reports on case qualifications before the funds are transferred to fundraisers. In 2019, the platform funneled a total of 10.6 billion RMB (around 1.7 bil. lion USD) in donations, a funding level comparable to other national philanthropic organizations.<sup>1</sup> The platform has over 400 million active users, accounting fo about 50% of internet users in China. The platform’s significant scale in user base and funds raised makes it a representative research context.

In the first step to launch a case, fundraisers submit case information and supporting materials for the platform’s review. The fundraisers can be the patients themselves or friends and family members acting on the patient’s behalf. Typical documentation includes identification, patient condition, patient photos, insurance status, financial status, and medical notes from the hospital where the patient is treated. The platform then conducts a thorough review of the case qualifications. Fraudulent cases, such as those failing to provide valid documentation or instances where patients are found to have sufficient funds to cover the medical expenses themselves, are screened out during this process. Once the case passes the review, its profile page becomes active, and a unique link to the page is generated, to be distributed to potential donors. Although the platform does not stipulate a limit on the length of time that a case can stay active, most cases stay open for about six weeks and then conclude to draw the funds.

The platform employs a unique approach for case dissemination, relying almost exclusively on users sharing the cases through their social networks on a third-party social media platform. Unlike charity fundraising websites or reward-based crowdfunding platforms, this platform does not list cases on its website. Access to a case is only granted through clicking its distinct case link. This link is created when the case is published and then disseminated by fundraisers sharing the link through social media posts or direct messages to their friends or family members, who, in turn, can distribute the link further through their social networks. Therefore, someone who is not directly connected to the fundraiser can only view the case if its link is shared by his or her friends either through direct messaging or posting, but not through other means such as browsing or searching. This design helps improve the chances that potential donors see cases relevant to them, involving patients they are connected to, and reduces the chance of wide distribution of fraudulent campaigns. This unique design of the crowdfunding platform also makes it a highly suitable context to examine the impact of social influence on donations without confounding factors such as search ranking and content promotions.

Our randomized experiment spanned four days, from December 7, 2017, to December 10, 2017.<sup>2</sup> This sample period did not overlap with public holidays or special events that may confound our findings. All active cases during the period are included in the experiment. The randomization is performed on the donor level. Users (potential donors) are randomly assigned to the treatment or the control group the first time that they click a case link during the experiment period, and this assignment is fixed for the user throughout the experiment period. When the page is loaded, donors in the control group see the case details. Donors in the treatment group see the same case details as the control group, except for one additional sentence, “person X donated Y amount,” which is displayed underneath the case’s fundraising goal, as illustrated in Figure 1. The friend displayed here is the person who sent or shared the link that the focal donor clicked. The case information pages are identical for the treatment and control groups otherwise.

## 4.2. Data and Measures

Our data consist of around one million page views that occurred during the experiment period. We remove observations for cases that did not pass the platform’s internal evaluation criteria and exclude observations involving gender-specific diseases, such as prostate cancer and cervical cancer. We include page views through links shared by friends and remove 95,827 observations where the page views occurred through platformgenerated messages sent directly to the donors. We also remove observations for cases that have a target amount or case description word count in the lowest fifth percentile as potential outliers. These data preparation procedures allow for more representative analyses of how multiple information sources interact in influencing donors’ likelihood to donate. Then we aggregate the data to the donor-case level to avoid giving higher weights to donors visiting the same case page multiple times.<sup>3</sup> Our final data set contains 757,094 donor-case-level observations from 722,179 viewers to 15,768 cases. A total of 348,295 unique donors are assigned to the control group and 373,884 unique donors to the treatment group. The main dependent variable is Donation, a binary indicator for whether a donor eventually donated to a case. We also use the amount of money that a donor contributed to a case, Donation Amount, as an alternative dependent variable in the robustness checks.

We collect data on all available case attributes on the medical crowdfunding platform for each case in our sample. Female Patient indicates whether the patient is biologically female. Based on patient age information, we create an indicator variable, Minor Patient, to indicate whether the patient is younger than 18 years of age. Severe Disease is an indicator based on the patient’s condition. It takes the value of 1 if the description of the patient’s condition contains the keywords “severe,” “acute,” or “malignant.”<sup>4</sup> Commercial Insurance indicates whether the patient has valid commercial insurance. Target Donation Amount reflects the fundraising goal of the case. Time Since Case Creation represents the number of days elapsed since case creation up to the point of the donor’s visit. Word Count for Case Description represents the length of the text entry for the case description. Number of Pictures Uploaded reflects the number of photos that the fundraiser uploaded, which may include patient photos and/or photos of supporting documents.

We also collect a comprehensive set of donor-level data. These include Access Channel, a binary variable with the value of 1 if the donor accessed a case link that was shared in social media posts, and 0 if the link was received through direct messages.<sup>5</sup> Prior Donations is the amount of donations raised at the time that the donor accessed the page. First-time Visit indicates whether the donor is visiting the platform for the first time. Time Since First Visit reflects the length of time measured in days since the donor’s first visit to the platform, which takes the value of 0 for new donors. Previous Donor is a binary variable that indicates whether the donor has previously donated to cases on the platform. Colocation specifies whether the donor is from the same geographic area as the patient. Definitions and summary statistics of these variables are reported in Table 1.

To verify that users are randomly assigned to the treatment or control condition, we perform a balance check and examine whether significant differences in the case- and donor-level variables exist across the treatment and control groups. The results in Table 2 provide strong evidence that donors randomly assigned to the two groups are comparable, showing balanced attributes overall. Only two case-level variables, Target Donation Amount and Access Channel, have statistically significant mean differences. For these two variables, the differences are small across the control and treat ment groups and comparable in scale to the statistically insignificant mean differences of other variables. According to prior literature, such observation suggests that the two significant t-test results are most likely due to the very large sample size and unlikely to cause significant biases or influence the results (Lin et al. 2013, Bloom et al. 2015). As an additional robustness check, we perform within-case propensity score matching on the donor-level variables to verify that our results are consistent on a matched sample where all the control variables are well balanced for the control and treatment groups.

## 4.3. MTurk Survey—Informational Value of Different Case Attributes

To determine the informational value of various case attributes, we design an online survey based on the likelihood ratio concept and collect responses using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). The likelihood ratio is a popular measure to evaluate the probative value of evidence by comparing how much more or less likely the results are under the conditions of one proposition than the other proposition (Horrocks and Walsh 1998). In a similar spirit, we evaluate the infor mational value of case attributes by how likely one value set convinces donors of the genuine need of the case compared with an alternative value set.

We encoded the responses to the questions using reverse encoding and combined the responses to both versions of the questions. For example, responses for the patient age attribute ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” are encoded from �3 to +3 for the first version (“A minor patient is in stronger need of help than an adult patient”), and responses to the second version (“An adult patient is in stronger need of help than a minor patient”) are encoded from +3 to �3. The encoded values of the responses combined across the two versions represent the extent to which respondents find the patient being a minor as a stronger indication of the need of the case than the patient being an adult.

Responses to questions on the other attributes are encoded similarly. We then used two-sided t-tests to examine whether the values of encoded variables are significantly different from 0. If so, then we infer that the value set that leads to a significantly higher perceived need of help has high informational value. If the t-test result is not significant, then we infer that the attribute has low informational value.<sup>6</sup> The summary statistics and t-test results are reported in Table 3.

Based on the results in Table 3, we observe that three attributes have high informational value: minor patient, severe diseases, and having commercial insurance. Specifically, respondents perceived that minor patients and patients with severe diseases are more in need of help (mean values are significantly positive) and that patients having commercial insurance are in less need of help (mean value is significantly negative). In contrast, female patient gender, higher-than-average target donation amount, more-than-average number of photos, and longer-than-average case description are considered as attributes with low informational value because they lead to higher levels of perceived need for help compared with alternative values, but the differences are not significantly different from 0.

We next examine how social influence interacts with case attributes containing different informational value using the results from the randomized field experiment.

## 4.4. Framework for Main Analysis

To examine how social influence varies depending on the informational value of the case attributes, we partition the donor-case-level data into subsamples based on whether case attributes with high/low informational value are present. The validity of this subsample analysis is ensured since the randomization of our experiment is performed on the donor level, and the randomized assignment of control and treatment groups also holds for any subsample of cases. Equation (1) outlines the regression framework for our main analysis, using a logistic model to relate the likelihood of donation to the treatment indicator, control variables, and their interactions with the treatment indicator. We include the full list of control variables reported in Table 1 and their interaction terms with the treatment condition in all estimations. In addition, whereas our main analysis focuses on the likelihood to donate, we verify that the findings are consistent using the amount donated as the dependent variable in the robustness checks section.

## 5. Results

In this section, we report our main results on how social influence interacts with case attributes of varying degrees of informational value to affect donation decisions. Given that cases have different attributes with varying degrees of informational value, we take the following approach to demonstrate their interaction effect step by step. First, we compare the effect of social influence for cases that have a certain attribute with high informational value versus that for cases that do not have such an attribute, going through the three attributes with high informational value (minor patient, severe diseases, and having commercial insurance) one at a time. Second, we focus on cases that do not have any of the three attributes with high informational value and examine how social influence changes depending on whether the case has a certain attribute with low informational value or not, going through the attributes with low informational value (female patient gender, higher-than-average target donation amount, more-than-average number of photos and longer-than-average case description) one at a time. Third, we demonstrate that when a case has no attribute with high informational value but has two or more attributes with low informational value, the impact of social influence is mitigated, just as for cases with an attribute of high informational value. Finally, we evaluate the overall informational value of all case attributes. We employ a model we trained based on another data set with a similar structure to predict the likelihood for a case to be successful in attracting donations. We develop the informativeness index as an aggregate measure of the overall informational value of all case attributes. With this aggregate measure, we consistently observe that the impact of social influence is mitigated when the overall informational value in case attributes is higher.

## 5.2. Social Influence and Case Attributes with Low Informational Value

We next examine how social influence interacts with case attributes of low informational value to affect donations. Since prior work has shown that the impact of a signal becomes redundant when another signal conveys superior information (Morton and Podolny 2002, Hig gins et al. 2011), we focus on observations for cases that have no attributes with high informational value $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ removing observations for cases that contain the attributes of minor patient, severe diseases, and has commercial insurance, as included in columns 2, 4, and 6 in Table 4) and examine how social influence changes in the presence of case attributes with low informational value. When a case does not have attributes with high informational value, we expect to see that social influence has a significant impact on donation likelihood. For this subsample of cases, results confirm that social influence sig nificantly increases donation likelihood by 20.9% (i.e., $\mathrm { e } ^ { 0 . 1 9 0 } - 1$ , based on column 1 of Table 5). We further partition this subsample according to the presence of case attributes with low informational value and examine how the impact of social influence varies.

According to the MTurk survey results in Table $^ { 3 , }$ Female Patient is a case attribute identified as having low informational value. Some donors may perceive female patients as in more need of help, while other donors may perceive male patients to need more help. Therefore, the informational value of patient gender in convincing donors of the genuine need of the case is low, and donors likely refer to other information sources in the presence of the female patient gender attribute. Results confirm that social influence increases donation likelihood by 18.5% $( \mathrm { i . e . , e ^ { 0 . 1 7 0 } } - 1 .$ , based on column 2 in Table 5) for cases involving female patients, and this effect is statistically significant.

Having More Photos and Having Longer Text Descrip tions are also identified as case attributes with low informational value. Donors likely have different interpretations of the genuine need of a case based on different types of data presented. This potentially makes the informational value from one type of data insufficient to determine donation decisions and drives donors to seek other information. We used a binary cutoff based on the mean value to define cases with more photos and cases with longer case descriptions. For cases with more photos, results show that social influence still increases donation likelihood by 25.5% $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ e ^ { 0 . 2 2 7 } } - 1 .$ based on column 3 in Table 5). For cases with longer descriptions, social influence still increases donation likelihood by 19.1% $( \mathrm { i . e . , e } ^ { 0 . 1 7 5 } - 1$ , based on column 4 in Table 5).

## 5.3. Social Influence and the Presence of Multiple

Case Attributes with Low Informational Value Whereas the informational value from one case attribute with low informational value can be insufficient to influence donation decisions by itself, it is possible that multiple such attributes can present sufficient information jointly and mitigate social influence. This prompts us to examine the impact of social influence for cases that have two case attributes with low informational value. The same four case attributes with low informational value shown in Table 5 are used for this estimation. The results are reported in Table 6. Interestingly, we observe that for all possible combinations of two case attributes with low informational value, social influence becomes insignificant in their presence. For example, for cases involving female patients and showing more photos in their case profiles, social influence does not further increase donation likelihood (column 1, Table 6). This suggests that the combined informational value of female patient and more photos is likely sufficient to determine donation decisions, mitigating the impact of social influence. The results are consistent when we examine different pairs of case attributes with low informational value (columns 2 to 6, Table 6). These results support Hypothesis 2, showing that the impact of social influence is also mitigated in the presence of two case attributes that have low informational value individually, similar to the situation where a case attribute with high informational value is present. We also examine possible combinations of three or all four case attributes with low informational value and evaluate how the impact of social influence changes in the presence of these case attributes. The results are reported in Table B2 in Online Appendix B. These results provide further support to Hypothesis 2, that social influence is mitigated in the presence of a combination of multiple (two or more) case attributes with low informational value.

In sum, we have shown that social influence increases the likelihood to donate overall, and this effect is stronger in the absence of case attributes with high informational value. Whereas the positive impact of social influence is still significant when the case only has one attribute with low informational value, this effect is mitigated in the presence of a combination of multiple case attributes with low informational value.

## 6. Robustness Checks

We conduct a series of robustness checks to validate our findings. First, we verify whether our main results are replicated if we evaluate the overall informational value of all case attributes, rather than focusing on a few case attributes at a time, and that our results are robust using alternative outcome variables, such as donation amount. Second, we verify that alternative explanations (e.g., motivations to show off on social networks) are not the main driver of our results. Third, we conduct withincase matching to confirm that the likelihood of different cases to be shared by viewers on their social networks does not confound our results. Last, we verify that potential concerns related to the reflection problem are unlikely to confound our findings.

## 6.2. Subsequent Sharing and Impact of Social Influence

One alternative explanation for our results is that when focal users see the treatment condition, where friends donation information is shown, they anticipate that their donations will be shown to their friends in turn and decide to donate, driven by the intention to show off. We first note that whereas this alternative explanation can possibly explain the main impact of social influence, it cannot explain our findings that social influence is mitigated in the presence of case attributes with high informational value because a donor’s “show-off” intention should not significantly vary with the informational value of a case attribute.

We conduct another robustness check to rule out this alternative explanation by comparing social influence for donors who clicked to share the page link and those who did not.<sup>9</sup> According to the “show off” explanation, donors who subsequently shared the link would be more likely to donate compared with donors who did not subsequently share the link. Therefore, we repeat the estimation on the two subsamples of donors, respectively, and report the results in columns 3 and 4 in Table 7. We observe that the coefficient estimates on the two subsamples are in the same direction and very similar in scale. ## 6.4. Reflection Problem

One major challenge in establishing the effect of a group on an individual is the reflection problem (Manski 1993). Individuals in the same group can behave similarly due to three effects: endogenous effects (which is the impact of social influence that we want to evaluate), exogenous (contextual) effects, and correlated effects. Therefore, exogenous and correlated effects need to be accounted for, to identify the effect of social influence (Manski 1993). In our context, exogenous effects could arise when social influence is caused by case-level attributes that influence the donations of both the friend and the focal user. Therefore, the inclusion of case-level fixed effects can account for such homophily driven by case-related features (Kim and Viswanathan 2018). Correlated factors refer to similar individual characteristics or institutional characteristics that influence the behavior of the friend and the focal user, for example, their socioeconomic status. To address these concerns, we include regional fixed effects based on donors’ locations to control for common factors in the donor’s regional level that can influence the friend and the focal user’s donations. Following a similar approach used in prior work (Kim and Viswanathan 2018), we account for both case-level fixed effects and donor regional-level fixed effects and report the results in Table 8. We note that the main effect of the informativeness index is absorbed by the case-level fixed effects in columns 1 and 3 in Table 8.<sup>10</sup> We observe that our main results are robust after accounting for these potential sources that cause the reflection problem.

## 7. Discussions and Conclusion

## 7.1. Summary of Findings and Contributions

This paper is one of the very first studies that systematically examines the role of social influence in medical crowdfunding and how it interacts with other information sources with varying levels of informational value. We conduct a survey using MTurk to examine the informational value of each case attribute through how different value sets of the case attribute compare in affecting the perceived need for help. Using a large-scale randomized field experiment involving over 700,000 users, we examine the impact of social influence on the likelihood to donate for cases containing attributes of different levels of informational value. For cases that contain attributes with high informational value (e.g., minor patient), we find that social influence does not significantly affect donation likelihood. In contrast, for cases that only have an attribute with low informational value (e.g., female patient), social influence significantly increases donation likelihood. For cases that have multiple attributes with low informational value, the impact of social influence is mitigated, just like in the presence of case attributes with high informational value. Overall, we find that the impact of social influence on donations decreases as the informational value in case attributes is higher.

Our findings imply that the social influence we observe is mainly informational, since its impact is contingent on the informational value of the case attributes. In contrast, if normative social influence has a dominating effect, we would expect to observe relatively comparable impact of social influence for cases with attributes of different informational value (Deutsch and Gerard 1955, Iyengar et al. 2015). Furthermore, we note that these findings are based on the medical crowdfunding context, where contributions are mainly driven by nonpecuniary motivations. Arguably, in other contexts where users have stronger motivations to engage in cost-benefit analysis to maximize returns, they likely expend more efforts in processing different sources of information. Thus, a stronger moderating effect between social influence and other informational channels can be expected. Our findings highlight the importance of future studies to account for the varying impact of social influence depending on the presence of other information sources across different contexts.

This study makes theoretical contributions in the following areas. First, we contribute to the stream of literature on social influence (Aral and Walker 2011, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Zhang et al. 2018, Huang et al. 2020). Using the medical crowdfunding context as an example, we demonstrate how social influence is contingent on the informational value of other sources of information. Our findings highlight the importance of exploring how social influence interacts with other information sources to impact user behavior in different contexts, where information asymmetry and uncertainty are major issues. Second, we contribute to the literature on medical crowdfunding (Kim et al. 2016, Young and Scheinberg 2017, Burtch and Chan 2019) by using a large-scale randomized field experiment to examine the factors that influence users’ willingness to donate. Whereas existing studies mostly examine how donor attributes influence donations, we focus on how the impact of social influence on donations is contingent on the presence of case attributes with different informational values. The field experiment presents a clean setting to evaluate how the impact of social influence on donations varies depending on the presence of informational cues, and provides new insights on the conditions under which social influence has a strong impact on donations.

## 7.2. Limitations and Future Extensions

The explanation we propose for our results is that informational social influence and informational value in case attributes jointly influence donors’ perception for whether the case is in genuine need of help. One potential concern is that donations can be driven by different motivational factors besides perceived need of help, such as solicitation, altruism, cost and benefit, values, reputation, and efficacy (Bekkers and Wiepking 2011). To address such concerns, we conduct another online survey on MTurk to examine the main mechanisms that drive donation decisions. We asked respondents to indicate, on a seven-point Likert scale, the importance of various mechanisms in their decisions on whether to donate to a case. The results reported in Online Appendix A confirm that the perceived genuine need of the case is the mechanism respondents regard as the most important in their donation decisions. At the same time, we acknowledge the limitation of our data in teasing out the effect of potential alternative mechanisms that drive donors’ decision-making processes. Future studies are needed to unveil the underlying psychological process that determines how donors process information from friends’ donations with other case information.

There are some limitations of our study due to data availability that future research can further expand upon. First, our data do not include detailed information on the identity of the friends or their social connections. The attributes of the influencer, including influencer identity (Strodtbeck et al. 1957, Chaiken 1979), recipient identity (Falomir and Invernizzi 1999), and relationships between the influencer and the recipient (Haslam et al. 2004, Aral and Walker 2014), likely moderate social influence. Future studies can explore how the impact of social influence on donations is moderated by the attributes of the friends’ social networks. Second, donor anonymity is not clearly identified in our context since donors’ screen names are displayed on the case pages, which are easily recognizable by their friends but would be considered anonymous for people outside their social networks. Future studies can also examine whether anonymity influences how donors process social and nonsocial cues in their donation decisions. Third, the amount donated by the friend potentially has an anchoring effect on donations by the focal user. Data limitation prevents us from examining this effect in detail. This is another area of potential extensions for future research. Last, in our experiment setting, since both the control group and the treatment group get access to the cases by clicking on the links shared by friends, this layer of social influence is the same across the two groups. The difference in donation likelihood that we observe is attributed to the second layer of social influence, that of showing friends’ donation amount on the case information page. Future studies can extend from our analyses and potentially tease out the impact of social influence on sharing decisions and how cases are disseminated through social networks.

## 7.3. Managerial Implications and Generalizability of the Findings

The managerial insights from this study are as follows. First, our findings provide a potential solution to those in need of help from medical crowdfunding and whose cases do not have eye-catching information to stand out from other cases. Social influence has a strong impact on increasing donations to these cases that lack attributes with high informational value. These fundraisers can rely on their social networks and use social influence to lend credibility for their need of help. This likely increases the attention to the case and donations from potential donors. At the same time, for cases that have attributes with high informational value, we show that social influence does not reduce their chances to get support. Such effects lead to a more dispersed distribution of donations across different cases. In our data, the entropy measure in the amount of donations to different cases is 23.9 in observations from the treatment group and 27.9 in observations from the control group. Lower entropy measure in the treatment group means that distribution of donation across cases is more evenly distributed. Rather than leading to more concentrated donations to the strongest cases that attract more attention, social influence benefits weaker cases more. Overall, we find that social influence promotes more equal chances for patients to get the financial support they seek.

Second, our findings indicate that social influence is not a universal solution to all types of problems that require persuading users to take certain actions. Instead, a more detailed examination into the quality of the information available in the specific context is required to evaluate whether social influence has a significantly positive impact. These insights have broader implications to a more general context of studies, across other types of platforms where users are exposed to multiple sources of information, including e-commerce, online content promotions, and peer-to-peer exchange platforms. Our findings have significant practical value in guiding businesses to identify where social influence likely has a larger impact in persuading users to take certain actions depending on the value of other sources of information available.
