# Optional Verification and Signaling in Online Matching Markets: Evidence from a Randomized Field Experiment

Lanfei Shi,<sup>a,</sup>\* Siva Viswanathan<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Online matching platforms lack common informational mechanisms such as ratings and reviews that serve to reduce information asymmetry in transactional platforms. The lack of verified information about participants further exacerbates issues of information asymmetry in such markets. This study focuses on a novel role of verification in such matching markets—its ability to serve as a credible signal for a user when such verification is made optional and visible to other users. In collaboration with a leading online dating platform with no reputation mechanisms and where most of the information is self-disclosed, we design and conduct a randomized field experiment to examine not only who chooses to verify but also, the effectiveness of such optional verification for different types of users. Interestingly, we find that users on the two sides use the same signal very differently. Males act consistent with the conventional prediction of signaling, with high-type males being more likely to opt in to verification. As for females, we find that medium-type females are the most likely to opt in to verification as compared with high-type females. We also find that such differential opt-in decisions are related to the differences in the credibility of the existing key attribute of each side (viz., income for males and beauty for females). In examining the outcomes of verification, we find that verified users receive more contacts from higher-type users, with the high-type males and medium-type females benefitting the most. More interestingly, we find that verified users become more proactive and reach out to more and better potential partners. Further, the introduction of this voluntary verification facilitates desirable matching outcomes and benefits the platform as a whole. These findings have useful implications for research as well as practice.

## 1. Introduction

Online matching platforms have proliferated over the past decade, and a central challenge faced by these platforms is how to mitigate information asymmetry and facilitate matching among users. These markets typically lack traditional informational mechanisms, such as reviews and ratings, that serve to effectively reduce information asymmetry in transactional markets. Such information asymmetry issues are further exacerbated for participants in online dating platforms in particular because of the lack of verified information about participants in these markets. Our study focuses on a novel role of phone verification in such markets—its ability to serve as a credible signal for potential partners when such verification is made optional and visible to other users.

Mandatory phone verification of registrants, when implemented, increases the overall credibility and security of the platform, as it serves to verify user identity and helps ensure that the users are real and prevents them from changing identify via multiple accounts (Tolentino 2015).<sup>1</sup> However, making verification mandatory may suppress useful information that could otherwise be conveyed by participants when platforms allow users to opt in to be verified. The focus of this study is to understand an alternate role of verification that has not been studied previously—its ability to serve as a credible signal for individual users when such phone verification is made optional and visible to other users on the platform. A simple-to-implement mechanism, such as phone verification, when made optional can take on additional significance in platforms that lack alternate reputation and transaction-assurance mechanisms (e.g., online reviews, escrow services, and moneyback guarantees) and where participants on the platform often have to rely largely on self-disclosed information by potential partners.

Unlike previous studies of informational mechanisms that primarily focus on their ex post effectiveness, our study also examines the ex ante choices of users opting into verification. Our study focuses on a two-sided matching market wherein both sides have the choice of opting into the same signal. Because of the gender differences in online dating (Fisman et al. 2006, Histch et al. 2010b, Ong and Wang 2015), it is likely that women are different from men in how they adopt these signals. Therefore, we are interested in the differential opt-in choices as well as implications for the two sides of the platform, as the value of such verification could differ across user types as well as across the two sides of the platform. We also focus on the role of paid verification wherein the platform charges a fee for verifying users, given its potential benefits to verified users (Ba et al. 2003, Goes and Lin 2012). We compare this with free verification, wherein there are no monetary costs to users for verification. More specifically, our study seeks to answer the following questions. Given the option to verify, who chooses to verify, and how do the opt-in decisions differ across the two sides of the platform? What is the underlying mechanism driving such decisions? We then examine the impact of such verification and ask the following questions. How does verification influence the potential partners of verified users? How does verification impact the verified users themselves? Finally, we seek to understand the impact of optional verification on the platform-level outcomes. How does verification affect matching between the two sides of the platform? In examining these questions, we also compare paid verification with free verification.

To examine these questions, we collaborate with one of the leading online dating platforms in the United States. We choose an online dating market in particular for the following reasons. Online dating markets have gone mainstream and have received increasing attention from researchers and marketers. One in five new relationships and one in six new marriages begin online.<sup>2</sup> As a business, it is worth \$2.5 billion annually in the United States alone.<sup>3</sup> In addition, given the lack of any other credible informational mechanism and the fact that al user information is self-reported and unverified, the online dating platform we collaborate with provides an ideal environment to study optional verification and its differential impacts on the two sides. Lastly, although there have been empirical studies in other types of matching markets, conducting randomized field experiments in these markets (e.g., kidney exchange and school applications) is often infeasible because of legal and ethical concerns (Coles et al. 2010; Hitsch et al. 2010a, b).

We design and conduct a randomized, controlled field experiment. We introduce optional phone verification to users in the online dating platform and randomly assign existing users to one of the three groups: one control group and two treatment groups. Treatment I (T1) is a one-time invitation for verifying the user’s account through their mobile phone with a charge of 50 virtual coins (equivalent two dollars), whereas Treatment II (T2) is the same invitation with no monetary cost. Upon verification, the verified users get a badge prominently displayed on their profiles indicating that their phone number is verified.

We draw upon signaling-related theories to guide our empirical investigation on the ex ante opt-in decisions as well as the ex post verification impacts of optional verification. In examining the opt-in decisions, we find that only 8% of the users choose to pay a fee to verify in T1, whereas 10% of the users choose to verify for free in T2. Sharing personal information is costly, and additional monetary charges result in lower opt-in rates. We are particularly interested in understanding how the two sides on this platform—males and females—make verification choices and how it affects their outcomes. We segment females and males into three tiers—low (L), medium (M), and high (H) types— based on their popularity on the platform.<sup>4</sup> Interestingly, we find that among males, higher-type males are more likely to opt in to verification in keeping with the conventional prediction of signaling theories. However, in the case of females, we see a nonmonotonic pattern of verification. We find that M-type females are more likely to opt into verification relative to L- and H-type females.

We then examine the factors contributing to these differential opt-in patterns among males and females. Consistent with signaling theories, we find that the deci sion to opt in to verification (i.e., the user’s likelihood of adopting an additional signal) is associated with how credible their existing dominant signal is (namely, beauty for females and income for males in our context).<sup>5</sup> Beauty for females, assessed through images in their profiles, is more credible and easier to verify compared with income among males, a self-disclosed value range. We apply a state-of-the-art deep learning frame work to measure beauty using the photos of female users. Specifically, we find that H-type females who score high on beauty are less likely to verify, whereas M-type females are more likely to verify and differenti ate themselves further from L-type females. On the other hand, as self-disclosed income is noisy and not easily verifiable, we find that H-type males are more likely to verify as compared with M-type and L-type males. Unlike beauty for women and income for men, which serve as dominant signals that contribute to overall popularity, we find that phone number verification serves as a new signal that helps opt-in users to further differentiate on a different dimension—their willingness to share personally identifiable information, which is valued in online anonymous environments like online dating.<sup>6</sup> Online surveys provide supporting evidence that users who opt into phone number verification are also more likely to share other personally identifiable information and that users who chose not to opt in do so because of privacy concerns.

In examining the outcomes of such verification on the potential partners of verified individuals, we find that verified users do benefit as they receive more contacts from higher-type users, with the tiers that are the most likely to verify (i.e., H-type males and M-type females) benefitting the most. More interestingly, we find that upon verification, verified users become more proactive and are more likely to initiate messages to higher-type potential partners. In examining the impact of verification on the platform, we find that it facilitates better matching between the two sides. Matching platforms often seek to balance the attention among superstars and average users and to promote high-type users when the market is crowded and competitive (Roth 2015). We find that upon verification, M-type females receive more attention from the male users. In addition, verified H-type males further stand out from the crowd and receive contacts from females. Such improvements in matching outcomes stem not only from the signaling effect of verification but also, from increased proactiveness among verified users. Finally, in examining paid versus free verification, we find that free verification has similar outcomes as paid verification, although the effects are weaker—highlighting the trade-off between higher coverage of free verification and more user proactiveness resulting from paid verification.

Our study makes a number of theoretical and empirical contributions. First, in contrast to previous studies, we examine verification in a context where there are no alternative reputation mechanisms or third-party signals that can mitigate information asymmetry and profile information is mostly self-disclosed and unverified. Our study shows that when phone verification for users is made optional in such a context, it has the potential to serve as a valuable signal to individual users. Second, our study examines users’ decisions to opt into verification as well as whether and how optional verification can serve as an effective signal for users on both sides of the platform. Although prior research has examined the effectiveness of information mechanisms such as word of mouth (WOM) and third-party certificates (e.g., Antony et al. 2006, Bu¨ ttner and Go¨ritz 2008) in other contexts, there are hardly any studies that examine users adoption decisions of such mechanism. Our study contributes to this literature on information mechanisms by examining both the user’s adoption decision as well as its impacts on user outcomes. Third, prior studies examining the role of information mechanisms have largely focused on when a signal is introduced to only one side of the platform and the response of the other side is examined (e.g., the seller side) (see Urban et al. 2009, Anderson and Simester 2014). As the existing theories and empirical findings may not be readily applicable to our context where both sides adopt the same signal, our study adds to the vast body of work in signaling by examining the differential choices as well as the differential impacts of verification across the two sides of an online platform. Fourth, we also contribute to emerging research on applying deep learning techniques to business problems (Malik et al. 2019) by using deep learning to estimate beauty and examine how beauty impacts females’ adoption of verification.

Finally, our study also generates interesting implications for the design of verification mechanisms for online matching platforms. It shows that offering optional verification to users in a platform’s early years can serve a very useful purpose—as a signal that helps those who opt in better differentiate themselves from others— without imposing a significant cost on all users seeking to join these platforms. Optional verification is particularly significant when platforms lack other credible mechanisms to verify important information about participants. Our findings also provide insights into how optional verification has heterogeneous impacts on different platform users and can also benefit the platform as a whole. Our findings also pave the way for examining other similar verification mechanisms (e.g., optional verification for social media profiles) as complementary for even mature platforms that require mandatory phone verification.

## 2. Related Work and Theoretica Underpinnings

Our work builds on and contributes to multiple streams of literature as detailed.

## 2.1. Matching and Marriage Markets

Research on matching markets, particularly those relating to market design, focus on designing efficient/stable matching mechanisms (Coles et al. 2010; Hitsch et al. 2010a, b; Roth 2015). Some examples include kidney transfer, matching of medical interns to hospitals, etc. However, online dating markets have not been studied with equal vigor, despite their popularity. Online dating markets, in particular, suffer from significant information asymmetries because of self-disclosed information with low credibility as well as from the lack of alternative signals. This study is among the first to examine the role of optional verification in an online matching market.

In particular, our study draws upon prior research on specific matching markets for marriage and online dating. Most previous work on marriage markets has examined matching and sorting patterns (Hitsch et al. 2010a, b; Abramitzky et al. 2011; Bruch and Newman 2018). This stream of research identifies gender differences in sorting preferences across cultures and in both offline as well as online settings. These studies find that males place a higher value on the physical appearance of females, whereas females place a higher value on the income and financial prospects of males (Buss 1989, 1994; Li et al. 2002; Fisman et al. 2006; Histch et al. 2010b; Ong and Wang 2015). Consistent with these findings, our study finds that the popularity of females is correlated with their beauty scores, whereas the popularity of males is correlated with their income. Our study also adds to this stream of research by showing how these differences in the attributes most preferred by males and females are related to their differential opt-in decisions for verification.

Our findings also contribute to the growing research on online dating markets in information systems (Jung et al. 2019). For instance, Bapna et al. (2016) investigate how viewing a potential partner’s profile anonymously works as a weak signal of preference. Additionally, Huang et al. (2022) study the disclosure of user demand information and find that it significantly affects users choices of partners and subsequent matching outcomes. Jung et al. (2022) examine the effect of different choice capacities on the number of choices and matches in a dating platform. Our study in comparison examines another important signal in online dating platforms— signals of willingness to share personal information, an important attribute that users look for in their potential matches in online dating platforms.

## 2.2. Signaling and Verification

Our study also builds on earlier research relating to verification as a basic mechanism to reduce information asymmetry. Researchers have studied the effect of verification on a number of different outcomes (Forman et al. 2008, Anderson and Simester 2014, Mayzlin et al. 2014). For instance, verified reviews are found to have a greater impact on subsequent review behaviors and product sales (Forman et al. 2008). The impacts of verification in these studies have been mostly examined within the context of a reputation system. However, our study focuses on a strategic role that optional verification may play in a market where there are no alternative informational signals (e.g., WOM).

Our study of optional verification as a signal in the online matching market is grounded in seminal theoretical and empirical studies of signaling (Spence 1978, Heil and Robertson 1991, Riley 2001, Connelly et al. 2011). In brief, the signalers seeking to differentiate from others disseminate private unobservable information (e.g., productivity or desirability) to the signal receivers. Multiple signals may be used collectively to help with differentiation on multiple dimensions. For instance, in online dating, people may want to convey their desirability by signaling multiple attributes, such as income, appearance and willingness to share privacy-sensitive information. In college applications, students highlight SAT (the college admission test in the United States) scores as well as extracurricular activities to signal their academic excellence as well as leadership, respectively (see the report by Warikoo and the State of College Admission Report 2019).<sup>7</sup> Further, studies have shown that the impacts of the signaling mechanisms depend on the credibility of the signal; when the signal is more uncertain and less reliable, it tends to have a weaker impact on signal receivers (Kreps and Wilson 1982, Davila et al. 2003). For instance, Davila et al. (2003) find that the signaling effect of a venture capital event increases as the credibility of the signal improves.

Theoretical models of signaling generally assume that signalers are rational; their ex ante decisions of adopting a signal are based on a cost-benefit analysis, and the ex post outcomes should confirm their expectations on the payoff. Therefore, traditional signaling models suggest that it is the higher types (high in productivity, wealth, or other valued attributes) that are more likely to send a costly signal to differentiate themselves from lower types (Spence 1978, Jovanovic 1982, Riley 2001), and these higher types should benefit more. However, there is emerging literature that suggests H-type users sometimes are less likely to signal compared with M-type users (Feltovich et al. 2002, Gambetta and Sze´kely 2014, Luca and Smith 2015). For example, the study by Luca and Smith (2015) finds that the top business schools are much less likely to advertise their ranking compared with middle-tier schools as middle-tier schools have a stronger incentive to signal their type and differentiate themselves from low-tier schools. This phenomenon can occur when H-types already have an existing signal that is credible and effective (for instance, in the study by Luca and Smith (2015), top schools already have a well-established reputation).

In general, the decision to adopt a new signal depends on the additional value of the new signal compared with the existing signal. Users resort to a new signal when it is relatively more credible and when it serves to provide further differentiation (Boulding and Kirmani 1993, Wells et al. 2011). H-type users may not have a need to adopt a costly new signal if their existing signal is a sufficiently strong differentiator. To the best of our knowledge, existing studies that document such nonmonotonic patterns largely focus on theoretical models or laboratory experiments. More importantly, prior studies introduced the signal to one side and examined its impact on the other side. In contrast, our study examines a context where both sides have the choice of opting into the same signal, and it is ex ante unclear whether and how the differences in subpopulation characteristics (e.g., gender differences) would impact user decisions. In doing so, our study is among the first to document an interesting heterogeneous pattern across two sides of the same market—wherein females and males strategically use the same signal differently.

Our study also draws upon prior work that compares voluntary and mandatory quality disclosure mechanisms.

Recent studies have identified the role of voluntary mechanisms as a signaling device in other contexts. For instance, Lennox and Pittman (2011) find that firms that choose to audit when auditing is no longer legally required attract upgrades to their credit ratings, as such voluntary auditing sends a positive signal about the firm. Our study contributes to this thread of literature by examining the role of optional verification and its ability to serve as a credible signal for individual users in a matching market, particularly when there are no alternative assurance mechanisms and users have to largely rely on self-disclosed information.

Further, the literature on signaling mostly focuses on examining how others react to the focal users’ signals, whereas we expand the investigation to examine the behaviors of focal users as well. In line with signaling theories, as focal users acquire the signal based on their cost-benefit calculus, they are likely to change their own behaviors in keeping with the newly acquired signal (Lazear 2000). In a classical example of education as a signal of ability, based on simple rationality arguments, an individual who has invested time and effort into (say) higher education would find it rational to seek more and/or better jobs in keeping with his or her newly acquired signal. Whether and how this manifests in a particular setting are empirical questions, and our analyses provide evidence of these impacts in our specific context.

Finally, our study also draws upon the emerging body of work that focuses on applying deep learning models in business research. We adopt state-of-the-art deep learning techniques to construct beauty scores for females using their photos. Deep neural networks have been applied to various image-related tasks, such as image classification and face recognition, because of their superior ability to learn discriminative features (Krizhevsky et al. 2012, Taigman et al. 2014). Recent studies in computer science (Liang et al. 2018, Xu et al. 2018) have focused on developing better algorithms to predict beauty from images. Researchers in social sciences have also begun to value this technique’s high scalability and consistency to mine valuable attributes from pictures (Malik et al. 2019). We contribute to this emerging line of work by applying a state-of-the-art deep learning architecture to estimate beauty of female users in our samples and examine the implications of beauty for the user’s decisions to opt into verification.

## 3. Randomized Field Experiment 3.1. Research Context

To examine the research questions highlighted earlier, we collaborate with one of the leading online dating platforms in the United States, which has over 1 million registered users. Similar to most online dating websites, it offers the following features to its users. Users create their online profiles where they introduce themselves as well as state their preferences relating to the partners they are seeking. User profiles typically also include photos. All profile information is self-disclosed by users themselves. The introduction of phone number verification in this study is the first time that users see public verified information of others. The platform offers a decentralized system wherein users perform a targeted search for potential partners that filters the profiles by age, location, and other demographic variables. Users are able to browse others’ profiles without limitations and at no cost.

## 3.2. Experimental Design

We randomly select over 20,000 users for our experi ment and randomly assign each user into one of three groups—a control group with no verification options as well as two treatment groups offering free and paid ver ification, respectively. Both paid and free mechanisms are commonly adopted by practitioners in various contexts to shed light on the impacts of costs on the out comes of interest. The treatments are verification invitations sent just once to each user who is cur rently online. This is designed to maximize the proba bility that the subject gets the message and reads it as compared with offline messages or emails. Acknowledging gender differences as documented in the litera ture, we block on gender to make sure males and females are distributed proportionally across groups (Fisman et al. 2006, Histch et al. 2010b). The invitation message describes that the verification uses one’s phone number and that the purpose of the verification is to reduce “scams” and to make sure that others know that “you” are real. The message for T1 states it will charge users 50 virtual coins (equivalent to two dollars) if they pass the verification, whereas the message for T2 vali dates the phone number for free. We also emphasize, in the message, that the platform will never disclose o share this information with anyone else. Each phone number can be used to verify only one user’s profile. Specifically, if one chooses to verify, the user is directed to a phone verification page upon clicking the verifica tion link in the message. Once the user successfully vali dates the verification code sent to the phone through short messaging services (SMS), he or she gets a verifica tion badge that is displayed on his or her profile page as well as on the search page.<sup>8</sup> The detailed design is illustrated in Figure 1, and the verification badge is as shown in Figure 2. The badge clearly shows that the user’s phone number is verified. This verification badge is accessible to everyone in the experiment. It allows us to account for the incentives of sending messages to veri fied users when examining related outcomes of interest. Finally, we make sure there are no parallel experiments on the platform during the duration of our experiment to prevent interference.

## 4. Data

## 4.1. Numeric Variables

As noted earlier, we track three sets of outcomes. We track the verification choice decisions for all subjects in the treatment groups. We also track postintervention messaging behaviors among participants as online conversations indicate strong initial interest, which could potentially lead to offline dating and matching. Specifically, as shown in Table 1, we track message initiation of each conversation that focal users engage in. If a conversation is initiated by potential partners, we included it in msg\_received to measure the initial interest of others in a focal user. If a conversation is initiated by focal users, we included it in msg\_sent to measure the proactiveness of focal users. In other words, a conversation between a focal user and a potential candidate is captured either by msg\_received or msg\_sent, depending on whether the potential match or the focal user initiates the message. We further draw from previous literature (Hitsch et al. 2010a, Bapna et al. 2016) to define a conversation match as a three-round back-and-forth conversation. Similarly, matches can be initiated either by potential partners (i.e., match\_received) or by focal users (i.e., match\_sent). We calculate these outcome variables within a week, represented by the notation outcome\_1week. For instance, we use msg\_sent\_1week and msg\_received\_1week to measure the messages initiated by focal users and messages initiated by potential matches within a week, respectively. We log transform all messaging outcomes as they are count numbers. We also use absolute count numbers to conduct negative binomial regressions as robustness checks. All variables relating to user characteristics (e.g., income) are snapshot data recorded when the users were randomly assigned to one of the experimental groups as subjects. Hence, these covariates reflect the original user type (e.g., one’s popularity prior to verification) and are not influenced by the assignment. The detailed coding of each variable is listed in Table 1.

## 4.2. Beauty Scores Using Deep Learning

Beauty is a valuable trait for users seeking a match. However, beauty is a more important attribute for females, as it ranks highest in terms of what males look for in their potential partners (Fisman et al. 2006, Hitsch et al. 2010b). To obtain this measure, we use a deep learning technique to predict the beauty of female users in our sample based on their photos. There are a number of techniques to code beauty, including manual coding. However, such manual coding techniques do not scale well and also lead to privacy concerns. Increasingly, supervised machine learning approaches, rather than human raters, are growing in popularity because of their high scalability and consistency on rating criteria. We choose deep learning over other models because of its superior performance on image-related tasks (Krizhevsky et al. 2012, Taigman et al. 2014, Malik et al. 2019). We follow a recent paper that tackles the problem of facial beauty prediction (Liang et al. 2018). Liang et al. (2018) collect a data set of facial images and annotate each image with a beauty score between one and five. They use deep neural networks for the task because of their ability to learn powerful image representations. We use their publicly available data set to train our own deep neural networks to predict beauty scores without having to risk unintended leakage of private user data. For our task, we only consider the female faces in the data set that have the same racial composition as in our test examples. We modify the last layer of DensetNet-121 (Huang et al. 2017), one of the state-of-the-art deep learning models, to directly output a scalar value as the predicted score. We also experimented with a few other models and found that DenseNet-121 performs the best because of its correspondence with the size of our training set and its ability to avoid both underfitting and overfitting. The trained model predicts for each input image a score between one and five. Table 2 shows the results obtained from fivefold crossvalidation. Our final model achieves a mean absolute error of 0.24 and a mean squared error of 0.10. Both errors are close to what the authors report in their paper (Liang et al. 2018).

## 5.1. Verification Decisions

5.1.1. Given the Option to Verify, Who Chooses to Verify? $$

5.1.2. What Contributes to the Differential Opt-In Decisions? We further examine what contributes to the differential opt-in decisions among males and females. In particular, why are H-types females less likely to opt in as compared with M-type females, whereas the opposite is the case with males. It is natural to attribute these differences to the higher demand for females on these platforms, as there are generally more male users than female users on most online dating platforms<sup>13</sup> (Jung et al. 2022). However, the overall higher demand for females over males cannot explain why among females, M-types females are more likely to opt in as compared with H-type females. We, therefore, draw upon the theoretical literature on signaling, which shows that H-type users are more likely to adopt a new signal as compared with M-type or L-types users primarily when their existing signals are noisy or weak and when the new signal adds value to their existing signals or attributes (Spence 1978, Jovanovic 1982, Riley 2001). However, the opposite is more likely when the existing signal is strong and credible (Feltovich et al. 2002, Gambetta and Sze´kely 2014, Luca and Smith 2015). In other words, when their existing signal already enables the H-type users to sufficiently differentiate themselves from M-type and L-type users, they would be less likely to adopt an additional signal given the costs involved. Based on these theories, we expect that the likelihood of a user opting into verification depends on his or her existing dominant signal.

To this end, we start by identifying the existing dominant signal for males and females. Prior literature has extensively examined gender differences in dating and marriage (Buss 1989, 1994; Li et al. 2002; Fisman et al. 2006; Histch et al. 2010b; Ong and Wang 2015), and it finds that men, more than women, desire partners who are physically attractive and good looking and that women, more than men, desire partners who have good financial prospects. We validate this gender difference using our data by examining the correlations between individual attributes and their overall popularity. In keeping with the documented evidence in prior research, we find that beauty (income) scores have the highest correlation to overall popularity for women (men), qualita tively consistent with earlier studies (e.g., Walster et al. 1966) as shown in Table A2 in the online appendix. Naturally, these dominant signals—beauty for women and income for men—drive the attractiveness of each side and also, determine the properties of their demand distributions.

Interestingly in our context, these two dominant signals—beauty for women and income for men—differ in their signaling attributes. In our matching platform, beauty is assessed from pictures, and income is provided by the users as a range of values (e.g., the highest range available is “>\$300,000”). Although both the pictures as well as the income ranges are provided by the focal users, it is much easier for a potential partner to verify a user’s beauty and attractiveness than it is to verify a user’s income. Consequently, beauty for females as assessed through their pictures is more credible and a less noisy informational cue than is income for males. This is also reinforced by prior research (Berger and Calabrese 1974, Metzger 2007, Metzger and Flanagin 2013), which finds that visual information is associated with more ease of evaluation and less perceived risk, and users perceive online information as more credible and informative if it is easier to validate offline.

We further examine if this is indeed reflected in our data. Following the signaling literature (Kreps and Wilson 1982, Davila et al. 2003), because of the difference in the credibility of the dominant signals, income for men would have a weaker signaling effect than beauty for women, leading to the demand for males having lesser differentiation than the demand for females. In other words, we should observe a smaller difference in average demand for high- and medium-type male subgroups segmented based on income, compared with the difference in the average demand for high- and medium-type female subgroups segmented by beauty scores, prior to the introduction of verification. We segment females (males) into low, medium, and high subgroups based on the mean and standard deviation of the beauty (income) scores.<sup>14</sup> We do find such patterns in our data that there is a significantly greater demand difference between the average beauty\_High and beauty\_Medium females, compared with the demand difference between the average income\_High and income\_- Medium subgroups of males, as shown in Table $6 . ^ { 1 5 }$ It indicates that the beauty scores of females serve as a more credible and sufficient signal than the income of males that helps with user differentiation. We include the income of females and beauty scores of males in the regression as control variables to account for their role in demand. This evidence is robust to alternative ways of segmenting beauty—high/medium/low for females—and income— high/medium/low for males (see Section 6.4).

As noted earlier, when the existing signals are noisy, we would expect to see a monotonic adoption pattern for a new signal. Thus, although H-type males have a higher income and popularity, given the uncertainty and noisiness of income as a signal, H-type males have a stronger incentive to adopt the new signal to complement their existing attributes and to further differentiate themselves from lower types. On the other hand, H-type females with their stronger existing signals would be less likely to adopt the new signal compared with M-type females. As shown in Figure 3 and Table 5, H-type men and women do have differential opt-in decisions. More directly, we further seek to validate whether beauty scores and income are associated with the identified differential opt-in patterns, respectively. As shown in Tables 7 and 8, we do find that females exhibit a nonmonotonic pattern in that women with higher beauty scores (i.e., beauty\_High) are less likely to verify compared with those with lower beauty scores. On the other hand, we find that among men, higher-income males are more likely to opt into verification (i.e., income\_High) as compared with those with lower incomes. Our robustness checks using continuous measures of beauty scores and income also show that income (beauty) contributes to the monotonic (nonmonotonic) opt-in patterns among males (females) in Section 6.4. In summary, we find that, in keeping with the predictions of signaling theories, the decision to adopt a new signal in our context depends on the strength of the existing signals for each side.

## 6. Additional Analyses and Robustness Checks

## 6.1. Manipulation Checks

We conducted manipulation checks on Amazon Mechanical Turk to support that the treatment messages are interpreted as a potential signaling device. We detail the steps and survey questions in Online Appendix A5. We conducted two independent surveys to verify whether T1 and T2 convey the information effectively. We excluded subjects who are under eighteen and/or fail to pass the attention check. We find that 91% of people responded “yes” in survey 1 for paid verification and 86% of people responded “yes” in survey 2 for free verification. These statistics confirm that subjects do perceive verification as an opportunity to differentiate from others on the platform.

## 6.2. Underlying Mechanisms

One alternative mechanism to tease out from signaling is homophily, where (un-)verified users are more likely to reach out to (un-)verified users. It could also lead to an increase in messages received in the treatment groups. However, the key difference is whether such an increase comes from verified versus unverified senders. We hence develop a new outcome, ratio\_msg\_received, to measure the ratio of the messages initiated by verified users to the messages initiated by unverified users. If homophily is at work, we would observe ratio\_msg\_received to be different between the control group and the treatment groups as users in the treatment groups would receive more messages from verified users, whereas users in the control group would receive more messages from unverified users. On the contrary, the signaling mechanism would lead to a nonsignificant difference between the two groups because both verified and unverified users value verification. We confirm that the results align with the signaling mechanism, as shown in Table A6 in the online appendix.

Another natural question that arises is how phone verification serves as a signal for users on this matching platform. Prior research finds that a willingness to share highly personal and private information plays a key role in fostering closeness among individuals (Gibbs et al. 2006). This is particularly highlighted as an integral part of romantic relationships and intimacy by Greene et al. (2006). However, paradoxically, users on online dating platforms are generally reluctant to share private information, especially personally identifiable information (PII) such as phone numbers and real names (Gross and Acquisiti 2005, Cobb and Kohno 2017, Nandwani and Kaushal 2017). Phone numbers are particularly sensitive (Jones 2016); the leakage or misuse of such PII may lead to malicious attacks from the real world and cyberspace (Sullivan 2000, Krishnamurthy and Wills 2009). Surveys and studies have consistently shown that users have privacy concerns about sharing their phone numbers and other PII online and with others, especially before meeting them in person (Lee 2018). Thus, phone verification, when made optional, can serve as a useful mechanism in such settings as it provides users the ability to signal their willingness to share PII to establish credibility, without having to reveal such information publicly to all users on the platform.

Even sharing such PII is costly to users,<sup>19</sup> consistent with our findings of a low opt-in rate. We hence conjecture that only users who have a higher willingness to share such personally identifiable information to establish their credibility are more likely to opt in to phone verification. If our conjecture holds, then the volume of information shared on user profiles among those who chose to verify should already reflect such willingness to share before verification is made available. Using the snapshot data tracked right before the experiment, we do find that verified users on average share 27% more information about themselves (i.e., self-introduction. than unverified users. We also conduct a brief survey to obtain insights into why, despite the obvious benefits of phone verification, the majority of the users chose to opt out of phone verification, even when it was offered for free. As suggested by prior work,<sup>20</sup> the survey specifically seeks to examine whether privacy concerns are a significant factor in driving users’ decisions. Following prior research (Jiang et al. 2013) on privacy and information disclosure, our survey states “Please check the reason why you did not opt in to phone verification” with four choices. Of the 172 respondents, the majority of them (63%) of them chose “I was concerned about disclosing my phone number,” indicating that privacy concerns are a key reason why people are reluctant to verify.

To further validate our conjecture, we conduct a short on-site survey to investigate users’ willingness to opt in to share other PII with the platform. We expect that users who opt into phone verification would also be more likely to opt into verify their LinkedIn accounts. The survey asks participants to verify their LinkedIn accounts through an authentication process similar to phone number verification. We emphasize in the survey that the information will be encrypted and will not be stored.<sup>21</sup> Among the 3,450 respondents (14% response rate), 59% are men, 41% are women, and 15% of them have verified their phone numbers. Eight percent of the 3,450 respondents choose to verify their LinkedIn account. We confirm that users who opt in to phone verification are more likely to opt in to verify their LinkedIn account as well (Table 15).

## 6.3. Alternate Model Specification

We first assess the possibility of interference among subjects because social interactions could potentially confound the identified effects. We approach this question by testing if the subjects who are more socially connected with verified users would experience a differential impact on their messages received and sent both quantitively and qualitatively compared with less socially connected others, detailed in the online appendix. According to the nonsignificant interaction terms in Tables A7 and A8 in the online appendix, we validate that the social connectedness with verified users does not play a role in subjects’ subsequent messaging behaviors.

We then test the equivalent LATE framework as the alternative specification, which is an instrument variable (IV) approach that uses the treatment assignment as the IV to estimate the causal impact of the treatment on the compliers (i.e., the verified users in the treatment groups in our context). We confirmed the consistent results in Tables A9 and A10 in the online appendix. Moreover, we ran alternative negative binomial regressions for count variables and validated the robust results as in Table A11 in the online appendix.<sup>22</sup>

## 6.4. Additional Robustness Checks

We further conduct a series of robustness checks.<sup>23</sup> First, we test the possibility that the control group gets discouraged and initiated fewer messages upon seeing others’ verification badges. We compare their message initiation during the experiment (i.e., msg\_sent\_1week) with that in the prior week (i.e., msg\_sent\_pre\_1week) and confirm no significant drop (Table A12(A) in the online appendix). We also zoom into msg\_sent\_1week and confirm that subjects in the control group who have more exposure to verification did not initiate fewer messages compared with those who have less such exposure (Table A12(B) in the online appendix). We use whether a focal user has received any messages initiated by a verified user to proxy this cutoff of high/low exposure. Both findings suggest that the users in the control group did not become less engaged in the experiment.

The second test is to assess the possible simultaneity between msg\_sent and msg\_received. Note that these two outcomes are measured independently, where the former (later) captures outgoing (incoming) message initiation. We further conduct subsample analysis by only including outcomes generated by those unverified subjects. This helps to control the other side’s ability to verify, hence accounting for potential simultaneity and homophily. Specifically, we generated msg\_received\_unverified and msg\_sent\_unverified to measure the number of messages that unverified users initiated to focal users and the number of messages that focal users initiated to unverified candidates, respectively. By examining these two outcomes, we verify that the signaling effect and focal users’ increased proactiveness still hold (Table A13 in the online appendix).

Moreover, we test the alternative way of equally splitting male and female users to segment H/M/L types.<sup>24</sup> We find that the differential opt-in patterns remain consistent (Table A14 in the online appendix). Following the same rationale, we further validate our findings in Table 6 with this alternative equal split to segment male users using income and female users using beauty scores (Table A15 in the online appendix). Further, we replace the dis crete user types by using the continuous measure of popularity and its quadratic forms. We confirmed the inverted U pattern among females, whereas male users exhibit the monotonic upward pattern (Table A16 in the online appendix). More importantly, we validate directly that income (i.e., income) is associated with the monotonic opt-in decisions among male users, whereas beauty score (i.e., beauty) is associated with the nonmonotonic opt-in decisions among female users (Table A17 in the online appendix). This strengthens that the dominating signals of each side play a role in the differential opt-in decisions. Relatedly, we also test the mediation effects with beauty scores/income as the independent variable, v0erification decisions as the dependent variable, and popularity as the mediating variable (detailed in Table A18 in the online appendix). The results further validate that the differential opt-in decisions between men and women are driven by the dominating signal of each side through one’s popularity. Finally, we enhanced our randomization checks by confirming our sample is balanced across finer subgroups (female/male × L/M/H type) as shown in Table A19 in the online appendix.

## 7. Discussions and Conclusion 7.1. Key Findings and Implications

Our study is among the first to examine the effectiveness of a voluntary verification mechanism as a signaling device in an online matching platform with significant information asymmetries. By modifying the more commonly used mandatory phone number verification to a voluntary option and making the verification publicly visible, we provide users with a potential signaling mechanism and examine its adoption and impacts by users on both sides of the platform.

Several interesting findings emerge from our study that provide valuable insights for theory and practice.

Phone verification is a relatively simple to implement, cheaper, and scalable mechanism for platforms as compared with the verification of drivers’ licenses or other user-related information. Our study is among the first to demonstrate that allowing users to opt in to phone verification, rather than mandating it, transforms this mechanism into a valuable signal for individual users. More interestingly, we find that despite the phone verification mechanism being the same for all users on the platform, users on the two sides of the platform make opt-in decisions very differently. We provide new empirical evidence based on a large-scale field study to add to the emerging line of work on signaling that, sometimes, M types have a stronger incentive to signal.

In delving deeper, we find that such differential optin decisions are related to the differences in the strength and credibility of existing dominant signals for the two sides (viz., income for males and beauty for females). Although a number of prior studies have examined the adoption and impact of signals by users on a platform, our study is among the first to provide evidence of a differential adoption pattern of the same signal by users on the two sides of the same platform.

These differential opt-in decisions among males and females also benefit the platform as a whole. Specifically, our findings suggest that introducing optional verification can help enhance M-type females’ profiles and increase their demand without hurting H-type females. It also helps H-type males, arguably the more competitive side, to further differentiate themselves from the rest, making it easier for female users to identify better candidates.

In further examining the signaling properties of verification, we find that a user’s willingness to be verified signals a greater willingness to share personal information. This is particularly the case in our setting, where a user’s phone number is the only verified attribute that is closely tied to one’s real-world identity. Our surveys provide supporting evidence as users who are more willing to opt in to phone verification are also more willing to opt in to verify their LinkedIn accounts. Signaling one’s willingness to share personal information with strangers is particularly valuable for potential partners in a matching platform, as it helps foster trust, closeness, and intimacy among individuals (Gibbs et al. 2006, Greene et al. 2006, Marcus and Marcus 2015). Optional phone verification, as compared with other valuable attributes such as income or beauty, serves to signal an orthogonal attribute that is also valued by potential partners.

We also find that optional phone number verification also triggers an interesting behavioral change among verified users; they become more proactive in reaching out to potential partners. Although prior studies of signaling mechanisms have uncovered the impact on signal receivers, our study is among the first to uncover the impact on signal senders. Verified users benefit from a two-pronged effect of becoming more proactive in initiating contacts to as well as from receiving contacts from more and better potential matches. Thus, optional verification positively impacts the messaging funnel and benefits the platform as a whole.

Finally, our findings of paid and free verification provide actionable implications. We find that although they generate qualitatively similar results, there is an important trade-off between coverage and intensity. Free verification induces higher coverage of opt-in users, whereas paid verification leads to a higher level of proactiveness of focal users. Therefore, nascent platforms would benefit from free verification, whereas more established platforms would benefit from paid verification.

## 7.2. Contributions to the Literature

Besides the aforementioned empirical contributions and implications, our study also makes significant theoretical contributions to the literature on signaling. First, we extend the examination of signaling to a less-investigated context of two-sided matching markets with unique characteristics; we focus on online dating platforms where user profiles are mostly self-reported without credible information mechanisms (Forman et al. 2008, Lennox and Pittman 2011, Anderson and Simester 2014, Mayzlin et al. 2014). In such a context, we demonstrate that by making mandatory information option, it takes on additional significance as a signaling device and a strategic differentiator. In addition, our study sheds light on the nonmonotonic opt-in patterns that are different from the conventional prediction. We find that the opt-in patterns on each side of the market depend on their existing dominant signals. Although the emerging literature on this topic is mostly analytical, our findings provide further support for and insights on the theoretical prediction (Feltovich et al. 2002, Gambetta and Sze´kely 2014). Lastly, although the existing literature on signaling mostly examines how others react to the focal users’ signal, we are among the first to also investigate the impact of adopting a signal on focal users themselves (Lin et al. 2013, Luca and Smith 2015). The identified increase in proactiveness enhances our holistic understanding on the impact of adopting a signal among focal users.

## 7.3. Contributions to the Practice

Our study provides valuable insights to platform practitioners when designing information mechanisms. First, our findings demonstrate that the value of offering optional verification to users during the early years of these platforms can serve a very useful purpose—as a signal that helps those who opt in better differentiate themselves from the others—without imposing a significant cost on all users seeking to join these platforms.

<sup>2</sup> See the article by Stewart at https://blog.marketresearch.com/ dating-services-industry-in-2016-and-beyond.

Optional verification could take on particular significance for contexts that lack credible signals and suffer from the dilemma that users tend to be reluctant to disclose personal identifiable information up front. Our findings are potentially generalizable to other two-sided matching markets that share similar characteristics of lacking of reputation systems and/or credible signals, such as those earlystage e-commerce platforms and job matching platforms. Moreover, our findings suggest that by making a mandatory piece of information optional, it enables its additional role of signaling under such contexts. In particular, optional phone number verification has its merits of low implementation cost and scalability. Besides optional phone number verification, we would expect that other types of optiona verification signals that convey one’s willingness to share would make similar positive impacts on the platform (e.g., LinkedIn verification badge and address verification badge). Even in later stages of growth when platforms have implemented mandatory verification, optional verification mechanisms, such as verification badges for Facebook/LinkedIn/Instagram, that serve a similar purpose as phone verification can serve as valuable signaling mechanisms for individual users on these platforms. However, platform designers should adopt such signals with caution and consider the costs of such implementation.

Third, we believe that such an optional verification mechanism works not only for platform users but also, for the platform as a whole because it also increases user proactiveness upon verification as well as aligns with two pressing goals of matching platforms in general (Roth 2015). On one hand, it facilitates desirable matching by helping H-type male users to further stand out, making it easier for female users to identify H-type candidates. On the other hand, it has the potential to improve matching coverage as it helps the M-type female users to get more attention. Further, we strongly encourage platform designers to exercise caution when designing and introducing a new information signal. In particular, we recommend that the practitioners take the existing dominant signal on each side into consideration in order to make sure the expected outcomes would align with the current objectives of the targeted platform; depending on the effectiveness of the existing dominant signals, the new signaling mechanism may manifest differential opt-in patterns and outcomes on the platform users. Specifically, if the existing dominant signal is not as credible, the newly introduced signal would help H-type users to stand out. If the existing dominant signal is relatively sufficient, H-type users might not be interested in this new signal, and it would be the M-type user who benefits the most from this new signal. Finally, weighing between more coverage afforded by free verification and greater effect intensity afforded by paid verification, platforms can choose the design that best suits their current need.

## 7.4. Limitations and Future Research

This study is not without limitations. They provide interesting opportunities for future research. First, if a longer time span of observation is feasible, future research can examine the potential learning effects. In addition, our study focuses on examining optional phone verification as a new signal orthogonal to existing ones. It would be worthwhile to explore user behavior when multiple signals are made available (e.g., “more responsive” user badges). Finally, for generalization purposes, it would help to extend our study to other matching platforms in their early stages of growth.
