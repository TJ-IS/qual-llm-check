# Departmental Boundaries and Knowledge Sharing in Corporate Online Communities

Abstract. An increasing number of companies are launching corporate online communities to facilitate knowledge sharing. A corporate online community is different from traditional communication channels, as its social-media atmosphere physically eliminates organizational boundaries. However, studies have shown that organizational boundaries still hinder knowledge exchange in corporate online communities. In this study, we propose that both knowledge-focused and social-related motivations exist, underlying the impact of organizational boundaries on knowledge sharing, and the former is not necessarily detrimental. We focus on the departmental boundaries and propose that employees are more inclined to share knowledge with the same-department colleagues (i.e., intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination). Using the data set of a large corporate questionand-answer (Q&A) community, we first demonstrate the existence of intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination with a potential-dyads approach and a series of robustnes checks. We further apply an empirical design using the same-name feature to strengthen the identification. Our mechanism checks provide evidence of the existence of both knowledge-focused and social-related motivations underlying this inclination. Although the proposed two types of motivations also exist for the knowledge-sharing inclinations subject to other organizational boundaries (e.g., location boundaries), the intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination is unique because it is driven more by the knowledge-focused motivations (versus social-related motivations). This emphasis on knowledge-focused motivations should not necessarily hinder the knowledge exchange, and our analyses verify that the intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination is not detrimental in terms of work performance or effort in knowledge input.

## 1. Introduction

Companies chronically struggle to take full advantage of knowledge dispersed across the organization (Zahra et al. 2020), as traditional knowledge sharing is often constrained by organizational boundaries (e.g., Kleinbaum et al. 2013, Srivastava 2015). To overcome these barriers, companies are using corporate online communities: internal forums for knowledge exchange beyond formal boundaries. Already, 93% of managers used social tools such as corporate question-and-answer (Q&A) communities for internal communication in

2016 (Bughin et al. 2016). Among these, corporate Q&A communities have gained popularity, featuring open discussion boards on which users can voluntarily pose and answer questions (Peng et al. 2020). Companies anticipate these platforms to mitigate traditional barriers such as geographical separation and role-specific silos.

However, recent studies indicate that organizational boundaries still significantly influence knowledge sharing in these new communication channels. For example, Hwang et al. (2015) show that employees in corporate

Q&A communities are more likely to share knowledge with colleagues from the same location and of the same hierarchical rank. Similarly, studies on corporate online discussion groups and wiki systems have demonstrated fewer interactions with higher-ranked employees (e.g., Argyris and Ransbotham 2016, Meske et al. 2020). The general tone from the literature is that organizational boundaries hinder knowledge exchange, still leading to the phenomenon of “knowledge silos” that online communities have been expected to dissolve (e.g., Hwang and Krackhardt 2020).

In this study, we are interested in the role of departmental boundaries in shaping knowledge sharing in corporate online Q&A communities. Specifically, we investigate the research question: In a corporate Q&A community, are knowledge providers affected by the seekers department information and more inclined to share knowledge with those from the same department (versus with seekers from different departments) regardless of explicit content? We term the phenomenon that employees are more inclined to share knowledge with the samedepartment colleagues regardless of the content as “intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination.”

The existing studies mainly attribute the influence of organizational boundaries on knowledge sharing to social-related factors, such as asymmetric power across hierarchy (Meske et al. 2020) or in-group favoritism stemming from the shared social identity (Hwang et al. 2015, Hwang and Krackhardt 2020). In this study, we propose a more comprehensive mechanism framework that integrates knowledge-focused and social-related motivations underlying the intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination. Corporate online communities encompass both functions of knowledge exchange and social interaction. Employees preferentially share knowledge with same-department colleagues because they believe it will be more helpful to colleagues with similar backgrounds, making their input more valuable. Moreover, social-related motivations, such as shared identity, homophily, social capital, and adherence to social norms, can also drive the knowledge-sharing inclination.

The relative salience of knowledge-focused versus social-related motivations likely varies depending on the type of organizational boundaries. Specifically, departmental boundaries, which divide employees by functional areas, may emphasize more about knowledge specializations, whereas location boundaries may place greater emphasis on social and cultural factors. Therefore, compared with the inclination subject to location boundaries, the intradepartmental knowledgesharing inclination should be driven more by knowledgefocused motivations, whereas less by social-related motivations. In this light, the observed intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination should not be necessarily detrimental to knowledge exchange in online communi ties, as previous studies suggested.

We obtain a unique data set from the online Q&A community of the marketing segment of a large company in China, comprising 16 departments with geographically dispersed branches. Each question page in the corporate Q&A community includes question text, posting time, and real names of the knowledge seekers and providers. A separate user information page allows users to view the organizational attributes of all participants. Using a potential-dyads approach (e.g., Xue et al. 2018), we examine whether a knowledge provider, facing all available questions, is more likely to reply to questions from same-department (versus non-same-department) knowledge seekers, controlling for factors such as question content and employee knowledge/experience through a series of text mining techniques. Whereas identification in such an organizational setting is challenging because departments are not randomly assigned, we have made rigorous efforts to show the robustness of our results across alternative econometric specifications, additional control variables, and various assumptions. We further validate the result with an empirical design using the “same-name feature,” which provides an exogenous source of variation, showing that knowledge providers indeed infer the seeker’s department from their name when deciding which questions to answer because they are also more likely to answer questions posted by the same-department colleague’s same-name peer(s) who are from a different department (i.e., a mistakenidentity scenario).

We also examine the mechanism framework we posit. We first show the existence of knowledge-focused motivations underlying intradepartmental knowledgesharing inclination, as we find that the inclination is related to question types and job function proximity. Social-related motivations also matter, as evidenced by the persistence of this inclination for nonprofessiona questions. Then, we compare the mechanisms between intradepartmental and same-location knowledge-sharing inclinations. Through a series of additional analyses, we show that intradepartmental inclination is more strongly driven by knowledge-focused motivations, and samelocation inclination is more dominated by social-related motivations. Furthermore, the intradepartmental inclination is not necessarily detrimental to knowledge exchange. Same-department contributions tend to be of higher quality, involve greater effort, and are not negatively related to employees’ future performance.

Our study complements the existing understanding of knowledge sharing in corporate online settings (e.g., Faraj et al. 2016, Meske et al. 2020) by analyzing the role of departmental boundaries. Unlike prior research (e.g., Hwang et al. 2015) emphasizing expertise similarity related to explicit knowledge, our study identifies an inclination toward intradepartmental knowledge sharing encompassing tacit knowledge and social behaviors. We highlight that intradepartmental knowledgesharing inclination exists even in the absence of explicit common ground, meaning it is independent of the content shared. This inclination is largely driven by knowledge-focused motivations centered on tacit common ground—knowledge that is unspoken and socially embedded—as well as by social-related motivations tied to relational dynamics.

Moreover, we contribute to the literature by proposing a comprehensive mechanism framework for intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination that integrates both knowledge-focused and social-related motivations, grounded in the nature of online communities that encompass both knowledge sharing and social engagement (Faraj and Johnson 2011, Faraj et al. 2016). We examine the existence of the two types of motivations underlying intradepartmental knowledgesharing inclination and extend the analyses to explain the well-known knowledge-sharing inclination subjected to location boundaries (e.g., Hwang et al. 2015). However, we argue that the relative emphasis on these motivations varies between intradepartmental and same-location knowledge-sharing inclinations, corresponding to their distinct characteristics. Departmental boundaries typically separate employees based on functional areas, leading to more specialized knowledge sharing driven by knowledge-focused motivations. In contrast, location boundaries divide employees by physical geography, often associated with social and cultural factors, where social-related motivations play a more prominent role in facilitating knowledge sharing. We provide empirical evidence to explain how distinct knowledge-sharing mechanisms are emphasized across these boundaries.

Finally, our findings contribute to the literature on the relationship between organizational boundaries and employee communication patterns (e.g., Liu and Srivastava 2015). Notably, employees are more inclined to share with colleagues in the same department but at different locations rather than with those at the same location, indicating that online communities can facilitate knowledge flow across location boundaries to some extent. We go one step further by empirically examining the relationship between intradepartmental knowledge sharing and important material outcomes. Whereas previous research on organizational boundaries has often highlighted negative aspects, such as asymmetric power in a corporate hierarchy (Meske et al. 2020) or in-group favoritism stemming from location (Hwang et al. 2015), we emphasize that intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination does not necessarily warrant intervention. The results also extend the literature on the relationship between knowledge sharing and outcomes for employees (e.g., Hann et al. 2013, Pu 2022).

This study offers practical insights for managers aiming to understand employees’ online knowledgesharing patterns at a time when large companies are increasingly turning to corporate online communities, especially those with geographically dispersed departments. Companies should not only motivate knowledge sharing across departmental boundaries but also recognize the value of intradepartmental knowledge sharing, where knowledge-focused motivations play a more significant role and the motivations are nondetrimental to the quality of sharing and the material outcomes for employees.

## 2. Literature Review

Organizational boundaries are standard features of a company. Boundaries demarcate the organization’s social structure, and activities operate under a specific logic of identity that shapes how things are done (Santos and Eisenhardt 2005), so organizational boundaries naturally play an important role in communication via traditional tools. In the offline setting, it is common sense that employees within the same organizational department or team would communicate or share knowledge more often because of the already-frequent interactions and proximate geographical locations (e.g., Hansen et al. 2005). The communications that happen via the telephone or email are mainly for formal work-related topics, and it is straightforward tha the within-department interactions are more frequent. Srivastava (2015) posits that email communication is more frequent among those who share a departmental affiliation. Hinds and Kiesler (1995) find that technical employees have more lateral communication (such as telephone and email) than administrators because of the collaborative nature of their work and the organizational structure of their work groups. Kleinbaum et al. (2013) analyze email logs and human resources records to reveal that homogeneous interactions are stronger within formal and quasiformal organizational units than across those boundaries. Organizational boundaries affect communications in traditional com munication channels because the interactions within the same geographical locations for the same-team members or these channels are for mandatory work related communications. The social-media style of a corporate online community represents an obvious departure from traditional communication tools, as it provides a space where employees can voluntarily share knowledge on a variety of both work-related and non-work-related topics in an informal way.

Corporate online communities are increasingly pop ular to link geographically separated employees with an open social-media-style atmosphere for knowledge sharing, but paradoxically, existing studies constantly find that online communities can also fragment interactions and continue to divide groups based on users characteristics (Van Alstyne and Brynjolfsson 2005). The mechanisms underlying the impact of organizational boundaries in corporate online communities are different from those in traditional communication channels. Specifically, knowledge sharing in corporate online communities is more likely to happen within location or hierarchical boundaries because employees share the same social identity stemming from the same organizational attributes, and they tend to express in-group favoritism via knowledge sharing (e.g., Behrendt et al. 2015, Hwang et al. 2015, Argyris and Ransbotham 2016, Hwang and Krackhardt 2020). Moreover, hierarchy affects knowledge sharing through asymmetric power and pressure. For example, Behrendt et al. (2015) find that corporate online wiki systems users are more inclined to discuss with colleagues with the same job rank (versus a different job rank). Pu et al. (2022) indicate that in a corporate Q&A community, employees are more likely to share knowledge with knowledge seekers with higher job ranks because of the potentia reputation benefits. By far, one of the implications of these studies has been that the influence of organizational boundaries on knowledge sharing leads to “knowledge silos” (Schnurman et al. 2017, Hwang and Krackhardt 2020), which is detrimental to organizations.

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to consider the effect of departmental boundaries, another fundamental type of organizational boundaries, on knowledge sharing in a corporate online community. Departmental boundaries differ from location and hierarchical boundaries in how organizational divisions are classified. Departmental boundaries are defined by functional areas (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967), whereas location boundaries are defined by physical locations and hierarchical boundaries with the various levels of authority and responsibility (Mintzberg 1989). Moreover, knowledge-sharing features vary across these boundaries because of their distinct classification. Departments are typically responsible for specific tasks and objectives, leading to knowledge specialization and a shared knowledge base (Tushman and Scanlan 1981, Carlile 2002). In contrast, location boundaries are shaped by location-specific factors such as culture, customer preferences, and regulatory environments (Bartlett and Ghoshal 2002). Employees within the same location engage more in social interactions, fostering stronger informal networks and social bonds (Sole and Edmondson 2002). These differences support our argument comparing the relative salience of mechanisms driving knowledge-sharing inclinations concerning two types of boundaries.

Departmental boundaries can be easily mistaken for “expertise similarity” (Hwang et al. 2015), which measures previous knowledge-sharing activities in the online community. However, they are distinct in theoretical explanation through the lens of common ground regarding explicit and tacit knowledge. Specifically, departmental boundaries encompass explicit knowledge, including organizational regulations and standard operating procedures, that are formally codi fied and can be reflected in the generated content. These boundaries also play a critical role in facilitating the sharing of tacit knowledge, such as shared culture, experiences, and work practices, which can be inferred from the context (Safavi and Ha˚ kanson 2018). Expertise similarity, however, especially measured based on online user-generated content, tends to be limited to explicit knowledge (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Ravichandran and Giura 2019). Additionally, departmental boundaries function as social units where employees interact, forming relationships that foster social effects, such as social identity and social capital (Katz and Kahn 2015). These effects are not typically present within the framework of expertise similarity, which lacks the depth of relational dynamics inherent to departmental settings. Our study notably identifies an inclination toward intradepartmental knowledge shar ing, even in the absence of explicit common ground. This extends beyond the scope of expertise similarity.

Additionally, in practice, departmental boundaries usually cannot be perfectly measured by users’ online knowledge-sharing content. First, departmental bound aries can exist simply for ease of management rather than division of job functions (Burton and Obel 2018). Second, employees from the same department can answer or post questions that are irrelevant to their department. Finally, one specific question, especially one that covers general topics, can attract employees across different departments with different levels of attention to answer it. Therefore, users’ previous knowledgesharing activities, as measured by their online expertise, do not reflect the departmental information well. ## 3.1. Knowledge-Focused Motivations

The core purpose of corporate online communities is to facilitate the exchange of information, skills, and expertise (Ma and Agarwal 2007). In line with this, we propose that knowledge-focused motivations drive intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination for shaping employees’ intentions to share helpful or perceived as helpful knowledge.

In corporate Q&A communities, when deciding to share knowledge, employees tend to rely on the available information and evaluate whether their contributions will be useful to the seeker. This is because the effectiveness of an answer depends largely on the provider’s ability to understand the seeker’s context (Peng et al. 2020). In this process, common ground between the provider and seeker plays a crucial role (Krauss and Fussell 1991, Hwang et al. 2015). Note that whereas individual capability may reduce the cost of answering questions, knowledge-focused motivations arise from the perceived utility of contributions. That is, employees prioritize sharing with same-department colleagues not merely because they can answer but because they anticipate their input will align with specialized needs rooted in common ground.

Generally, common ground operates in two dimensions: explicit common ground reflected in the generated content and tacit common ground inferred from the context. At the explicit level, employees within the same department share knowledge relevant to the department’s domain, such as specialized terminology or technical expertise. At the tacit level, they share a tacit understanding of departmental goals, challenges, and norms, which may not be explicitly communicated but can be inferred from the department information (Tsoukas and Vladimirou 2001). Although department proximity can result in greater explicit overlap in the seeker’s question content and the provider’s expertise and interests, the intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination identified in our study is primarily related to tacit common ground. This shared tacit understanding exists independently of explicit common ground, such as online expertise similarity or question content. It enables providers to better comprehend the intent and requirements of the inquiry (Hinds and Mortensen 2005), helping address coordination issues more effectively (Kraut et al. 2002), thereby increasing the likelihood of delivering more meaningful answers.

Moreover, perceived answer helpfulness enhances the knowledge provider’s expected benefits, further motivating knowledge sharing with same-department seekers. When the knowledge provider and seeker share the same-department context, the seeker is more likely to perceive the knowledge as particularly useful (Sedighi et al. 2016). The potential perceived useful ness can stimulate the desire to share knowledge, as providers anticipate their contributions will make a meaningful impact and enhance their reputation and credibility (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Engaging colleagues from the same department also allows employees to receive valuable feedback, expand their knowledge repository, and gain acknowledgment for potential collaborations (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Thus, sharing knowledge within the same department provides intrinsic satisfaction and fulfillment, as the provider believes one’s contribution will be particularly valu able (Wasko and Faraj 2000).

## 3.2. Social-Related Motivations

The other main function of corporate online communities is facilitating social interactions and building employee relationships. Corresponding with this, social-related motivations also drive intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination because of the social pressures or socialrelated purposes associated with sharing knowledge with same-department colleagues.

Belonging to the same group, such as a department, produces a positive feeling of social identity (Tajfel 1982, Charness and Chen 2020). According to social categorization theories, individuals reduce ambiguity and promote self-enhancement by categorizing colleagues into “in-group” and “outgroup” (Tajfel 1982, Charness and Chen 2020). The in-group/outgroup distinction triggers in-group favoritism, which involves preferential treatment of those in the same group and is evident in organizational settings for boundaries (Hewstone et al. 2002). Strong social identity within a group increases an obligation to participate in the collective adhere to in-group norms and create a psychological tie that facilitates conversations (Hinds and Mortensen 2005). In other words, intradepartmental knowledgesharing inclination can happen because providers feel more obligation to share and reap more enjoyment from sharing with same-department colleagues.

Moreover, individuals tend to associate with similar others (McPherson et al. 2001, Gu et al. 2014). This homophily effect enhances perceptions of trust and expertise (Casciaro and Lobo 2008) as well as certainty and safety toward the individuals involved (Prisbell and Andersen 1980). The same-department colleagues share similar traits, linguistic styles, and hobbies (Johnson et al. 2012), forming similarities in their interests and contributions over time. With an online community, employees would gradually form positive impressions toward colleagues with higher similarity and enhance communication frequency. In turn, the higher frequency of interactions further enhances the convergence of the employees’ characteristics, beliefs, and attitudes over time (McPherson et al. 2001). This dynamic trend encourages and sustains the inclination for intradepartmental knowledge sharing.

Finally, employees’ behaviors are shaped by the social capital they gain from interactions in the online community (e.g., Pu et al. 2022). Communicating with same-department colleagues may attract more attention from colleagues or managers from the same department, helping employees with better social images and status. Also, when other colleagues exhibit intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination, individuals should be naturally influenced by their tendency and follow this social norm. Adhering to the norm enables employees to better adapt to the social dynamics and expectations within their department, striving for rewards and avoiding penalties (Davis and Luthans 1980).

## 3.3. Main Propositions

Because of both knowledge-focused and socialrelated motivations associated with the departmental boundaries and knowledge sharing, we propose that knowledge sharing in a corporate online community is subject to departmental boundaries and the intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination exists: prospective knowledge providers are more inclined to share knowledge with seekers from the same department (versus those from other departments). Notably, the intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination identified in our study is primarily related to tacit common ground, in addition to the explicit common ground, such as online expertise similarity or question content.

We propose this mechanism framework grounded on the nature of online communities: they not only facilitate the exchange of knowledge but also foster online social connections among employees (Faraj and Johnson 2011, Faraj et al. 2016). Thus, we extend this framework to explain the knowledge-sharing inclinations subjected to other organizational boundaries (e.g., location boundaries). Our framework offers a more comprehensive perspective than existing studies focusing only on specific aspects of social-related motivations (e.g., Hwang et al. 2015, Hwang and Krackhardt 2020). Additionally, this framework allows us to compare the relative salience of motivations across the inclinations subject to different types of boundaries. We find that although intradepartmental inclination and same-location inclination are both related to knowledge-focused and social-related motivations, the emphasis on the two motivations is different. Departmental boundaries emphasize the speciali zation of knowledge, whereas location boundaries highlight social and cultural factors in communication. In this light, the knowledge-focused motivations are more salient for intradepartmental than same-location inclination, suggesting that the inclination observed in our study may not require special intervention.

## 4. Research Context and Data

The empirical context of this study is the marketing segment of a large company that markets and sells food and daily necessities in China. The marketing segment has five geographic sections, each with multiple work locations in different cities. Every geographic section has at least one employee in each department.

## 4.1. Departments

The organizational structure encompasses three main divisions: marketing, administration, and marketing support. These divisions are organized into six functionbased subdivisions or work types (with an additional breakdown within the product-based marketing division), which are further divided into 16 departments that have granular roles.<sup>1</sup> The product-based marketing, where sales representatives promote and sell products to the market, is divided according to different marketing techniques (X, Y, or Z) as functions. It adopts a matrix structure, combining technique specialization with product lines (A or B); that is, departments here apply the same marketing techniques across different product lines (see more detailed introduction for job functions in Section 6.1.1). The customer-based marketing subdivision is divided into four departments based on customer types, where representatives build relationships and market products to specific types of customers. The administration division is divided into training, business development, and other administrative departments. The marketing support division is composed of three departments that offer the necessary support for the sales representatives. Divisions of administration and marketing support are not further divided into subdivisions; their job functions are treated as equivalent to departments. ## 4.2. Corporate Q&A Community

The marketing segment launched a corporate Q&A community for knowledge sharing on topics such as products, marketing strategies, management, and leisure. Knowledge seekers post questions, and employees provide answers as knowledge providers. Figure 1 shows one example of a question page.<sup>2</sup> Notably, the question page displays the real names of the knowledge seekers and providers but does not provide personal information such as pictures or a link to a user profile page. In other words, prospective knowledge providers can only infer the seeker’s department from their name on the question page. Managers from the IT department report frequent visits to a separate page that aggregates users’ profile details, including their basic work profiles and aggregated community usages. A 2015 internal survey from the company also confirms that most users check their own or colleagues’ profiles after logging in.

The work information data, obtained from HR, includes each employee’s location, department, and tenure. We summarized the number of answers and questions per department, finding that, on average, employees answered 6.69 questions from the same department and 2.66 from other departments.

## 5. Estimation Strategies and Results

We use a potential-dyads approach to examine whether users of the corporate Q&A community are more likely to share knowledge with same-department seekers than with seekers from different departments. We also validate the findings with an empirical design involving the same-name feature, which occurs when two or more users have the same real name.

## 5.1. A Potential-Dyads Approach

In this approach, we construct a data set of all possible pairwise combinations of the questions posted during our study period and the users who provided at least one answer during our study period (e.g., Xue et al. 2018). This approach adopts the perspective of the knowledge providers, who can browse all available questions but ultimately will respond to only one or a few. A potential-dyads approach that considers all possible combinations is necessary for our research question because it can effectively control for providerand question-level variables and include individuallevel fixed effects to account for time-invariant factors.

To construct the dyadic data set, we match each question posted during our study period with each knowledge provider; knowledge sharing happened in some dyads but not in others. We identify the dyads in which knowledge sharing occurred by referring to the answer data set to determine which user(s) responded to each question. The unit of analysis is at the provider (i)–seeker (j)–question (k) level, where i refers to a knowledge provider, j refers to a knowledge seeker, and k refers to the question that seeker j posted. The dyadic data set contains $^ { 1 8 , 6 7 0 , 1 3 5 }$ dyads (14,759 questions × 1,265 knowledge providers), of which knowledge is shared in 687,083 dyads. The main dependent variable, $A n s w e r _ { i j k } ,$ equals one if i answered question k, and zero otherwise. Because the dependent variable is binary, we analyze knowledgesharing decisions with a logit regression model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\ln \left(\frac {\operatorname{Prob} (A n s w e r _ {i j k} = 1 | x)}{1 - \operatorname{Prob} (A n s w e r _ {i j k} = 1 | x)}\right)} \\ & {\qquad = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S a m e D e p _ {i j} + C o n t r o l _ {i j k} + \delta_ {i} + \theta_ {j} + \gamma_ {k} + \epsilon_ {i j k},} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $S a m e D e p _ { i j } ,$ the key independent variable, equals one if provider i and seeker $j$ are in the same department, and zero otherwise. We include fixed effects for the knowledge provider, $\delta _ { i } ,$ the knowledge seeker, $\theta _ { j } ,$ and the posting date of the question, $\gamma _ { k }$ . We estimate the model with heteroskedasticity-consistent robust standard errors.

We include a group of control variables, $C o n t r o l _ { i j k } .$ We include $Q L e n _ { k } ,$ the log-transformed number of Chinese characters in question $k ,$ as a proxy for the complexity of the question, which should relate to the effort required to answer the question. We include $E x p \_ A n s _ { i k }$ and $E x p \_ Q u e s _ { i k } ,$ the log-transformed sum of the number of answers and questions posted by i before question $k ,$ respectively, to quantify the knowl edge provider’s experience in knowledge sharing and seeking. We log transform $Q L e n _ { k } , E x p \_ A n s _ { i k }$ and Exp $\boldsymbol { \it Q u e s } _ { i k } ,$ as they are positive and count or quasicount in nature. We include a binary variable Previou-$s I n t _ { i j k } ,$ which equals one if seeker j shared knowledge with provider i before question k was posted; previous interactions between the knowledge provider and seeker may influence the answer decision. For example, a provider is more likely to provide answers to seekers who previously responded to the provider’s questions $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Johnson et al. 2014).

We include $I n t e r e s t R e l e _ { i k } ,$ , which captures the similarity between question k and provider i’s existing answers (Bapna et al. 2019), because a provider’s knowledge-sharing probability is likely affected by the extent to which the content of the question aligns with the provider’s interests. We use the latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) model to discover latent content topics and then obtain the topic distribution vector for each question and answer.<sup>3</sup> The LDA is a Bayesian statistical and information retrieval technique. We input the text of the questions and answers as the corpus and set a predefined number of topics $\left( T \right) . ^ { 4 }$ After model training, we obtain a set of words for each topic t and a T-dimensional topic distribution vector $< d _ { c , 1 } ,$ $d _ { c , 2 } , \dots , d _ { c , T } >$ for each piece of content $c ,$ where $d _ { c , t }$ represents the weight of content c on topic $t , 0 < d _ { c , t } < 1 .$ and $\textstyle \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { T } d _ { c , t } = 1$ . Higher weights indicate a higher likelihood that the content pertains to the topic. In Online Appendix A.3, we provide the top keywords for each topic generated by LDA and interpret the topic. We calculate $I n t e r e s t R e l e _ { i k }$ as the cosine similarity between the topic vector of question k and the average topic vector of i’s previous answers before responding to question k. Exp\_Ans<sub>ik</sub> and InterestRele<sub>ik</sub> also help isolate the effects from pure individual ability.

Finally, we include $O n l i n e E x p t S i m _ { i j k } ,$ , the similarity between provider $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ and seeker j’s online knowledgesharing experience (e.g., Hwang et al. 2015). We use the LDA model to generate a topic vector for each piece of content generated by i and j before question k was posted. Specifically, $O n l i n e E x p t S i m _ { i j k }$ is the cosine similarity between the average topic vector of i’s content before question k and the average topic vector of $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ content before question k. ## 5.2. Identification: An Empirical Design Using the Same-Name Feature

The potential-dyads approach may not fully address unobservable confounders because SameDep in Equation (1) may not be random. Unobservable confoun ders can be associated with both the provider’s answer decision and the fact that the provider and seeker are from the same department (the detailed discussions are in Online Appendix B). To alleviate this concern, we design an empirical test using the same-name feature, which occurs when two or more employees have the same real name (42 cases in our data set). Community users tend to infer the identities and attributes of other users from their names (Jiang et al. 2022). Because question pages only display names without IDs/ photos, as described in Section 4 and Figure 1, providers may mistake the same-name seekers’ departments from the question page. For example, as illustrated in Figure 2, if both a “Mike Wang” from departments A with ID 7886 and the other “Mike Wang” from department B with ID 1725 post questions, it is possible that providers from department A might misattribute both to department A, which we call a “mistaken-identity scenario.” If the seeker’s (inferred) department indeed affects providers’ answer decisions, then providers from department A should be more likely to answer not only questions posted by Mike Wang from department A (i.e., main effect) but also questions posted by Mike Wang from department B (i.e., the mistaken-identity scenario), compared with those from other departments.

We construct a new dyadic data set that includes only dyads in which the knowledge seeker has the same-name feature and the seeker’s department is not the same as the provider’s. The dependent variable, $A n s w e r _ { i j ^ { * } k } ,$ indicates whether provider i responded to question k posted by j\*. We generate a new independent variable, SN\_SameDep , which equals one if three conditions are satisfied: (i) seeker j\* has the same name as seeker j, (ii) seeker j is in the same department as provider i, and (iii) j\* is not in the same department as provider i. The first two conditions ensure that $S N \_ S a m e D e p _ { i j ^ { * } }$ captures the mistaken-identity scenario, and condition (iii) ensures that any captured effect is not attributable to the effect captured in our main analysis. Whether seeker j\* and seeker j are the same-name peers is largely exogenous to our research question because one’s name was given a long time ago, and it is nearly impossible for a user to change their real name for the online community. In other words, SN\_SameDep can be largely treated as exogenous, and the variable divides the samples into SN\_SameDep<sub>ij\*</sub> � 1 (i.e., the treatment group, where the mistaken-identity scenario can potentially happen) and SN\_SameDep<sub>ij\*</sub> � 0 (i.e., the control group, where the mistaken-identity scenario does not happen).

## 6. Mechanisms and Additional Tests 6.1. Mechanisms for Intradepartmental Knowledge-Sharing Inclination

In this section, we delve into the mechanisms underlying intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclinations, which can be classified into knowledge-focused and social-related motivations. We highlight knowledgefocused motivations for intradepartmental answer inclination, as existing studies have predominantly focused on social-related motivations in other organizational contexts, particularly regarding location (e.g., Hwang et al. 2015, Hwang and Krackhardt 2020). Therefore, we first design a series of analyses to show the existence of knowledge-focused motivations (Section 6.1.1), including examining the subsample of question types and the role of job function proximity. We also investigate social-related motivations, using a subsample analysis of professional and nonprofessional questions (Section 6.1.2). We also use the same-name design to confirm the mechanism analyses for intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination, and the results are in Online Appendix C.2.

## 6.1.1. Knowledge-Focused Motivations

6.1.1.1. Subsample of Question Types. If knowledge providers use seekers’ departments to infer whether they share the same background and aim to provide a good answer, then the question type should play an important role because providers need to assess how common background matters. However, if socialrelated motivations are the only driving force, the question content should not matter because favoritism or other social behaviors stemming from the member’s identity are independent of explicit content (Hewstone et al. 2002).

To examine our conjectures, we classify the professional questions (i.e., questions with a tag that is not “leisure related” or “administration”) into three categories: product related, customer related, and general, based on our custom dictionaries for the words involved. Product-related questions ask about product characteristics, ingredients, and usage. Customer-related questions concern the features and demands of specific customers. General questions are related to general marketing skills, difficulties, and strategies.

6.1.1.2. The Role of Job Function Proximity. In our context, the product-based marketing subdivision is structured into function-based subdivisions aligned with distinct marketing techniques. The departments within this subdivision adopt a matrix organizational structure (Ford and Randolph 1992), combining marketing technique (X, Y, or Z) with product line (A or B). Notably, two departments use the same marketing technique across different product lines. Figure 3 illustrates this matrix structure. The customer-based marketing subdivision comprises four departments unified under a single job function, all employing customercentric techniques to engage specific client segments. We leverage function-based subdivisions, which denote similar knowledge specialization and background within the units, to explore whether knowledge-focused motivations play a role in intradepartmental answer inclination. The online community displays department information but not job functions, meaning that users infer others’ job functions based on their department affiliations. For example, a provider from the department “Marketing Technique X-Product Line A (PM X-A)” would recognize a similar background with colleagues from the department “Marketing Technique X-Product Line B (PM X-B)” because of the shared marketing technique (job function). Therefore, we expect a shared job function, even across departments, to result in a common context with a higher probability of sharing knowledge.

In contrast, if social-related motivations are the only driving force, we would observe an inclination limited to the same department, not to the same job function.

To operationalize, we define a new binary variable: $D i f f D e p S a m e F u n c _ { i j }$ equals one if provider i and seeker j have the same job function but from different departments, and zero otherwise. The benchmark scenario, where both SameDep and $D i f f D e p S a m e F u n c _ { i j }$ equal zero, occurs when i and j are from different departments with different job functions. ## 8. Conclusion and Discussion

This study investigates whether knowledge sharing in a corporate online community is subject to departmental boundaries. By collaborating with a large company in China that uses a corporate online Q&A community in its marketing segment, we find that knowledge providers were 5.87% more likely to answer questions from same-department seekers than nonsame-department seekers. We confirm the relationship using a potential dyadic approach and an empirical design using the same-name feature. The results, along with an extensive set of robustness checks, consistently demonstrate an intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination, even though the open social-media style of the community is to eliminate physical and organizational barriers to knowledge sharing.

We verified the existence of knowledge-focused motivations underlying intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination. Specifically, we find that intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination is moderated by the question types, and it persists when employees have the same job functions but are from different departments. We reason that the importance of inferring background information for better knowledge sharing varies with the type of question, and knowledge sharing should also be more prevalent for employees with the same job function inferred from the displayed department information. Whereas this type of mechanism should not be unique in intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination, it should play a relatively more salient role compared with the knowledgesharing inclinations that are subject to other organizational attributes (e.g., geographical locations) because most work-related knowledge is accumulated from the job functions, which is closely associated by employees’ department but not their other organizational attributes.

The results contribute to the growing literature on knowledge sharing in corporate online communities (e.g., Hwang et al. 2015, Meske et al. 2020). Our exploration of departmental boundaries complements studies on geographical location and hierarchical rank as other important types of organizational boundaries. Moreover, the existing literature attributes knowledge sharing inclinations to undesirable social factors such as asymmetric power in a hierarchy (Meske et al. 2020) or in-group favoritism stemming from a shared social identity such as work location (Hwang et al. 2015). However, we emphasize knowledge-focused motivations that are not necessarily detrimental or undesirable, as they reflect the knowledge providers’ desire to provide helpful information to knowledge seekers. We further compare different types of organizational boundaries and demonstrate that the salience of knowledge-focused motivations should differ in different knowledge-sharing inclination types.

We find no evidence of negative consequences associated with intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination. A provider’s knowledge sharing with same-department colleagues or a seeker’s knowledge receiving from the same department is not negatively associated with their future performance. Also, providers do not seem to spend less effort or time answering questions from same-department (versus non-same-department) colleagues, suggesting that intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination is not impulsive or detrimental to the content quality. The result broadens the literature on the relation ship between knowledge sharing and outcomes for knowledge providers (e.g., Roberts et al. 2006, Chen et al. 2022).

The study has important managerial implications for knowledge communities and organizational management. Managers should consider our finding of an intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination when designing policies to promote knowledge sharing. For example, a company might achieve a more equal and open communication environment by incentivizing answers to questions posted by knowledge seekers from different departments and across organizational boundaries. Similarly, managers of online communities might incorporate users’ organizational attributes into the community’s recommendation system (which traditionally suggests questions and answers based on content alone). Managers may especially think abou mitigating the relevant social-related motivations. For example, whereas reward systems such as badges or virtual points can usually increase community engagement, such systems may motivate social-related motivations if certain rewards are compared across departments.

However, it is also worth noting that the intradepartmental knowledge-sharing inclination is mainly driven by knowledge-focused reasons, reflected by knowledge providers’ desire to share helpful knowledge. When designing incentives for knowledge sharing or recommendation systems, managers should recognize that providers want to infer seekers’ background information and organizational attributes to identify opportunities for effective knowledge sharing.

Also, it is somewhat reassuring that we do not find negative associations between intradepartmental knowledge sharing and individual material outcomes, although our analysis of future outcomes has several limitations, and we do not assess outcomes at the organizational level. Based on this finding, geographically dispersed companies should strive to balance intradepartmental and cross-departmental knowledge sharing in online communities, rather than exclusively prioritizing the latter. Additionally, companies should actively monitor intradepartmental knowledge sharing through metrics that track the flow and effectiveness of knowledge sharing within departments. This approach will help ensure that intradepartmental sharing does not result in detrimental outcomes in the long run.

Finally, our study shows that knowledge sharing is constrained by department boundaries to some extent, and we propose that the magnitude of the mechanisms should vary across different types of organizational boundaries. For example, we show that the salience of knowledge-focused motivations should be different, underlying the intradepartmental and the same-location knowledge-sharing inclination.

Our study is subject to several limitations that present future research opportunities. First, we broadly categorize the possible mechanisms into knowledgefocused and social-related motivations by emphasizing the key message that the knowledge inclination within organizational boundaries is not necessarily detrimental, and we do not aim to identify every specific mechanism or explore any new mechanisms. Second, our main purpose is to show the existence of knowledge-focused motivations; whereas we show that the salience of knowledge-focused motivations can be different across the knowledge-sharing inclinations by verifying how question content and job functions play a role, we are not able to directly compare the intensity of these mechanisms side by side. Third, we study only one common type of company that is implementing online communities: a large organization with geographically dispersed locations, and features of the context enabled us to strengthen the causal claim and explore mechanisms. However, readers should use caution when generalizing our results to other contexts. Fourth, despite an extensive set of robustness checks and the empirical design using the same-name feature, we do not claim causality because the observational data have their inevitable shortcom ings, and experiments are not feasible in answering our research questions. Finally, whereas the corporate online community serves as the primary formal Q&A knowledge-sharing platform, we lack detailed information about the informal communication channels that employees may use. This represents a promising avenue for future investigation into how formal and informal channels collaborate to facilitate knowledge sharing within departments.

In the second approach, we focus on the goal of deriving topics that differ from one another (Deveaud et al. 2014), and the approach derives the number of topics based on the information divergence (using Jensen-Shannon divergence) between all pairs of topics in a given model. The model assumes that LDA performs best with the number of topics achieving the maximum divergence. We run LDA and calculate the two metrics by varying the number of topics from five to 200. The results of first method suggests T � 5, and those of the second method suggest T � 10. In the main analysis we set T to 10. We achieve consistent results with T � 5, T � 15, and T � 20. The detailed descriptions for the 10 topics and robustness checks with different numbers of topics are presented in Online Appendix A.1.
