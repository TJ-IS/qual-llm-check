# The Costs of Ambiguous Information Disclosure: On the Unintended Consequences of Providing Restaurant Hygiene Scores on Platforms in the United States

Abstract. Information disclosure on digital platforms is ubiquitous and often well intentioned, yet it frequently results in unintended consequences. Given the prevalence of simplified cue prone to ambiguity in digital platforms’ information design, how such designs can induce ambiguity and consumer misunderstanding has not been closely evaluated. Yelp’s initiative to display hygiene inspection scores to restaurant webpages on Yelp across the United States, in response to consumers’ limited access to information about restaurant hygiene inspections online, provides a suitable context for us to examine the mechanisms by which simplified information disclosure can evoke misperceptions and result in unintended consequences. Using differences-in-differences (DID) analyses and online experiments, we study how the addition of hygiene scores on Yelp affects restaurant demand. Using a custom-built data set combining data from Yelp and SafeGraph foot traffic, we find that showing high (clean) hygiene scores on Yelp paradoxically reduces restaurant demand relative to restaurants for which Yelp does not show hygiene scores on their Yelp pages. To understand this effect, we propose a mechanism given how the hygiene information is displayed on Yelp—information ambiguity. We argue that consumers conflate online hygiene scores with the healthiness and tastiness of the food, thus intuitively leading to reduced demand. Results of two randomized experiments reveal that ambiguity appears to be the primary cause of misperceptions about hygiene scores. We also show that providing simple clarifications about the correct meaning of hygiene scores, as well a tweaking the displayed hygiene information to resolve ambiguity, helps mitigate consumer misperceptions. Finally, we show further support for this perspective through a differences-indifferences analysis confirming that partially remedying ambiguity can correct the negative effect on restaurant demand. Across two experiments and two regression models, our research informs theory on how valuable information can be presented online effectively in such a way as to reduce ambiguity, especially on social media platforms. We provide managerial implica tions for platforms such as Yelp and city municipal authorities that would like to disclose key information online, as well as for restaurant owners who may be affected by information disclo sure on their restaurants provided on similar platforms.

## 1. Introduction

Information disclosure on digital platforms is ubiquitous. Whereas it is often intended to help consumers make more informed decisions, the disclosure of some information may not necessarily lead to consumers making the expected inferences. Thus, observed behavior arising from information disclosure can significantly deviate from what was originally intended by platform owners or policymakers. For instance, when

Apple introduced its privacy labels where iPhone app developers self-declare the data collected by their apps, the featured blue checkmark with a “Data Not Col lected” label has often led users to mistakenly infe that Apple had thoroughly audited app security, which is not consistent with what the intended purpose of this information was originally (Fowler 2021). Similarly, LinkedIn launched a “Featured Job” label to spotlight employer-sponsored postings, yet this label is frequently misinterpreted as a marker of personalized or most suitable job recommendations for a given user (LinkedIn 2025). In yet another example, the “Amazon’s Choice” label, designed to denote popular products on Amazon, is often misperceived as a quality endorsement by the platform (Carbone 2019). Similarly, Zillow’s “Premier Agent” label has been mistaken to represent top-tier agent expertise, although it merely identifies agents who paid for advertising (Zillow 2025).

These examples highlight a prevalent yet largely underexplored phenomenon that occurs with some regularity on digital platforms: information is disclosed in a form that is ambiguous. Ambiguous information design—often in the form of simplified cues so as to allow users to process the information with ease—can, however, be a significant driver of consumer misperception or misunderstanding, leading to unintended outcomes that are not consistent with the original intention behind information disclosure. Simplified information disclosure is pervasive, especially as minimalist design principles dominate platform interfaces. We argue that such simplified disclosures could introduce significant ambiguity—defined as the susceptibility of information to incorrect understanding. Whereas prior research mainly focuses on information disclosure producing the intended, improved outcomes (e.g., Lansky 2002, Jin and Leslie 2003), in this paper, we address the flip side of information disclosure by studying how simplified disclosure of important information on platforms can actually evoke misperceptions because of ambiguity and yield unintended negative consequences. We investigate this understudied research question in the context of the disclosure of restaurant hygiene information on Yelp in the United States.

In the restaurant sector, disclosing hygiene quality information has been proven effective in reducing foodborne illness, stimulating higher demand for “clean” restaurants, and providing benefits accruing at the municipal level (Jin and Leslie 2003, Dai and Luca 2020). Consequently, in recent years, platforms such as Yelp have begun to incorporate hygiene scores into restaurant pages in the form of “health scores,” in addition to reviews and other relevant restaurant information. These hygiene scores are based on inspection data publicly reported by local government authorities but are not easily available online, a convenience that Yelp offers through its platform. This convenience notwithstanding, there has been considerable pushback from restaurants, arguing that the hygiene scores presented on Yelp can confuse consumers (Forbes 2017, Fleetwood 2019). Scholars, too, are skeptical about the implementation of hygiene information disclosure, suggesting that the design and presentation of the information can be misleading (Ho 2012, Ho et al. 2019, Kim and Martin 2021). This skepticism is particularly directed at the apparent “simplification of information disclosure,” which has the potential to introduce ambiguity in interpreting the information and contribute to misunderstandings among consumers (Ho 2012, Ho et al. 2019, Kim and Martin 2021). In this paper, we examine information ambiguity in the context of how the hygiene information presented on Yelp may paradoxically lower demand for restaurants with high hygiene quality, rather than enhancing their appeal.

In principle, restaurants are required to post officia hygiene inspection reports onsite, usually on or near the restaurant doors (Mejia et al. 2019). This inspection report follows a format mandated by city authorities and is designed to provide unambiguous information. However, when this information is reproduced on plat forms such as Yelp, the chosen format of disclosure does not always follow best practices. On Yelp, hygiene scores are provided using two common scales for cities in the United States. In San Francisco, for instance, a numeric score on a 100-point scale is used to report hygiene levels. Alternatively, in cities such as Los Ange les, a letter grade is employed rather than the corre sponding numerical scores (i.e., 90 to 100 points for an A, 80 to 89 for a B, 70 to 79 for a C). Restaurants that meet high standards in hygiene and food safety typically show a score of 90 points or higher in the former system and the “A” letter grade in the latter. In either context, theory would predict that displaying high hygiene scores, whether online or offline, should benefit restaurants by making them more attractive to custo mers, all else equal (Jin and Leslie 2003, Dai and Luca 2020). However, this logic assumes that consumers cor rectly interpret the information, an assumption that has not been tested thus far, particularly in the online context. Our focus is thus not on the efficacy of the existing hygiene scores per se (e.g., the scoring criteria, the precision of the scoring scale, the credibility of the scores), but on the effects of their presentation on platforms where the information is designed to be simplistic and quickly noted (e.g., three words: “Health Score A”). We thus ask: does the form in which this information is pre sented online engender misinterpretation of what is actually provided, leading to adverse consequences for restaurants?

Misinterpretation on social media is unfortunately common. Social media users today are known for their short attention spans and often do not spend adequate effort or deliberation in understanding content. Rather, they tend to “satisfice,” skimming content without engaging deeply with the materia (Duggan and Payne 2011), thereby allowing signifi cant scope for misunderstanding when the displayed information is ambiguous. Further, consumers are more likely to consult Yelp at the initial decisionmaking stage, where they search and rule out a large number of alternative restaurants in order to focus on a select few options to consider patronizing, rather than when they are physically at the door of the restaurant. Because the locus of decision making is further distant from the actual point of consumption, any ambiguity in how the information is provided can potentially have a stronger effect on subsequent purchase decisions. As a baseline, we thus examine whether restaurants displaying high hygiene scores, typically presented in the format of “Health Score A” on Yelp, experience any difference in demand as a result of this information appearing online.

Using a specifically curated data set on hygiene information gathered through Yelp from its partner firm (Hazel Analytics) and a differences-in-differences (DID) model (Study 1), we find that restaurants that have a high hygiene score in the form used on Yelp (“Health Score A”) added to their Yelp pages indeed receive fewer customer visits relative to a control group of restaurants without such information. We also find significant heterogeneity in the identified negative effect, driven by whether the restaurant serves fast food, offers drive-through service, or is oriented toward catering social events and entertainment. Essentially, we find that the reservations expressed by some in the industry about the provision of hygiene scores on platforms such as Yelp appear to be justified, given the lower demand observed for such restaurants. In explaining these counterintuitive results, we contend that it is highly unlikely that restaurant patrons find hygienic restaurants less attractive. Instead, we propose that these effects may result from consumers’ misperceptions arising from how hygiene information is presented on Yelp that may, inadvertently, lead to reduced demand.

Specifically, we theorize that the counterintuitive effects are driven by information ambiguity (Huckfeldt et al. 1998, Pike et al. 2018), where the ambiguous cue “Health Score” may be misunderstood to stand for the healthiness of the food served by the restaurant, rather than hygiene quality. This misunderstanding occurs because of the lack of readily accessible clarificatory information, as well as the tendency of users to choose to expend little effort in resolving ambiguity on platforms such as Yelp. Our proposed mechanism builds on a well-established empirical phenomenon: healthier foods are systematically perceived as less tasty and thus experience lower demand, all else being equal (Colby et al. 1987, Raghunathan et al. 2006). According to hedonic consumption theory, consumers food decisions are often driven by the hedonic goal of enjoying tasty, flavorful foods (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982). Dining out, in particular, is associated with pleasure and indulgence, making taste a paramount consideration. As such, when choosing a restaurant, consumers typically attend to information related to food attributes (Ozdemir and Caliskan 2014, Rhee et al. 2016) wherein food tastiness is thus consistently one of the most influential factors driving restaurant choice (Jung et al. 2015). Hence, when deciding which restaurant to visit, Yelp users particularly look for information that could provide clues about food tastiness. In such contexts, the information cue “Health Score” will likely and readily prompt the misperception of food healthiness, with the associated inference of less tasty food. Therefore, we contend that hygiene information presented on Yelp may be misperceived because of the resulting ambiguity, leading to lower demand for restaurants that show high hygiene scores.

Interestingly, this ambiguity in information can be largely mitigated by clarification, such as providing additional contextual information or modifying the manner in which information is provided. If the observed negative effect on demand is driven by ambiguity, as per our theorizing, adding a clarification should mitigate the effect. To test this specific argument, we implemented a follow-up randomized experiment (Study 2), where we randomly provided some subjects with clarifications. The results show that (1) the stimulus “Health Score A” does lead to misperceptions about the healthiness of the food served by the restaurant, confirming the proposition that users tend to mistake hygiene information presented on Yelp for food healthiness; and (2) a reduction in ambiguity, by adding clarifications, significantly reduces this misperception, providing evidence that ambiguity is indeed a key underlying mechanism driving the misperception. The direct implication of this finding is a potential remedy: less ambiguous hygiene information presented online helps reduce the misperception of food healthiness and thus mitigate the observed negative effect on restaurant demand.

We test this prediction by leveraging a unique real-world setting on Yelp. We discovered that on the Yelp pages of some (randomly selected) restaurants, hygiene information was mandatorily presented as “Health Score Inspected & Permitted” as opposed to the more common “Health Score A”. This unique setting resembles a “treatment” of less ambiguous hygiene information because “Inspected & Permitted,” on the margin, more clearly speaks to hygiene rather than unrelated food attributes. To test these arguments, we first conducted another controlled randomized experiment where we replaced the previous ambiguous stimulus with the less ambiguous stimulus “Health Score “Inspected & Permitted”” (Study 3). The results support our prediction, showing that the less ambiguous information “Health Score Inspected & Permitted” (versus “Health Score A”) significantly reduces misperceptions of food healthiness. Finally, we test these results using secondary data from the field to examine if these arguments manifest in observed restaurant demand. In a quasinatural experiment (Study 4) using hand-collected and curated data sets from Yelp, our differences-in-differences analysis—comparing restaurants that provide the less ambiguous information with a matched control group—reveals no significant effect on demand for high-hygiene restaurants. Collectively, across our four multimethod studies, we find consistent evidence indicating that information ambiguity is a key mechanism in this context. We also find that the resulting ambiguity can be mitigated, thereby reducing any adverse consequences from providing hygiene information online for stakeholders such as restaurant owners.

Our work makes three major contributions to literature. First, we identify ambiguity in information design as a systematic and critical mechanism by which well-intentioned disclosures can inadvertently lead to misperception and misattributions on digital platforms. This ambiguity mechanism is not limited to Yelp’s hygiene score context but is applicable to a wide array of information disclosure settings on digital platforms where minimalist or simplified cues (e.g., labels, badges, scores), whereas they are userfriendly in theory, often lack clarity or contextual grounding. We show that when ambiguity is not externally resolved on these platforms (e.g., clarification is not readily and easily available), users seem to resolve ambiguity by drawing on context-relevant heuristics to make inferences, offering insights into information systems (IS) research on how simplicity and streamlined interfaces may steer user decisions. Second, we provide some context for understanding how reliable hygiene information can be made available to consumers on platforms such as Yelp without the ill effects of ambiguity. To the extent that restaurant owners and consumers understand the implications of suitable hygiene disclosure and appropriately use them in decision making, we show that any fears of “clean” restaurants experiencing reduced demand may be controlled. Furthermore, for policymakers looking to reduce foodborne illnesses through broader access to information on platforms such as Yelp, we provide evidence for how such information cues provided online can be misleading and, importantly, how mistakes can be avoided. Third, our work speaks to a broader issue in social media research where abundant, potentially ambiguous information is provided to users who rarely invest time in deeper investigation. If the consumption of information on platforms is prone to fast and frugal processing, likely leading to misunderstanding, it is incumbent on platform owners and policymakers to ensure that clarity in messaging is given importance during design. In our specific case, we contend that it is incumbent on Yelp to ensure hygiene information is provided in a pithy but unambiguous manner so that such errors are avoided. Our work provides an example of where providing relevant information, such as hygiene, has adverse effects for restaurants yet can be easily avoided.

## 2. Theoretical Development

## 2.1. Background: Public Disclosure of Restaurant Hygiene Inspection Information

The public disclosure of hygiene inspection scores has been hailed as an information policy success in the U.S. restaurant sector. Beginning in the late 1990s, local municipal inspectors started requiring restaurants to display inspection cards conspicuously at their entrance, making hygiene grades salient to consumers (Jin and Leslie 2003, Fleetwood 2019). Anticipating that better-informed consumers would make choices accordingly, the policy helped exert market pressure on restaurants to improve hygiene. In a seminal study conducted in Los Angeles County, Jin and Leslie (2003) found that a mandatory “grades on doors” policy led to higher consumer demand for cleaner restaurants, a reduction in hospitalizations from foodborne illness, and an overall improvement of restaurant hygiene quality at the municipal level.

Given the clear benefits of this disclosure policy, a growing number of municipal entities have partnered with Yelp in recent years to provide hygiene scores on Yelp restaurant pages. Economic theory predicts that displaying hygiene scores saliently—such as on popular social media platforms such as Yelp, which consumers consult when making dining decisions—would be a straightforward, positive enhancement (Mejia et al. 2019). Building on this logic, Dai and Luca (2020) studied restaurants in San Francisco City and showed that displaying poor hygiene inspection scores on Yelp significantly reduced consumer purchase intention, measured as leads received from Yelp. Specifi cally, receiving a poor hygiene score of 70 or below (which accounted for 2.4% of city restaurants) led to a decrease in leads, while adding a “consumer alert” alongside poor scores further amplified this effect by making the information more salient. Together, prior research (Jin and Leslie 2003, Dai and Luca 2020) provided evidence that the disclosure of hygiene information can inform consumer decision making about restaurant choice.

## 2.2. Information Disclosure and (Incorrect) Consumer Inferences on Social Media

However, the efficacy of information disclosure hinges on a fundamental assumption: that the conveyed information is clear and correctly understood by consumers. We contend that this fundamental assumption may not necessarily hold true and requires further examination. In reviewing the information disclosure literature, Loewenstein et al. (2014) discuss a set of consumer psychological factors, such as limited attention and the tendency to jump to incorrect inferences, that can undermine the goal of promoting informed decisions, rendering disclosure ineffective or, in some cases, detrimental to the objectives of disclosure in the first place. In the particular case of restaurant hygiene information, research provides strong evidence that disclosure increases pressure on restaurants to improve hygiene quality, but it is much more ambivalent about how consumers respond to the form and manner in which this information is provided (Loewenstein et al. 2014). Indeed, some scholars have expressed skepticism by noting that the implementation of the hygiene information disclosure can be misleading because of the manner in which the information is provided (Ho 2012, Ho et al. 2019, Kim and Martin 2021). For instance, Ho (2012) assesses hygiene information disclosure in San Diego and New York and points out that flawed implementation likely inhibited the intended beneficial outcomes. Specifically, the design and presentation of the information, in which “simplification” is emphasized, could potentially contribute to greater misunderstanding of the information among consumers (Ho 2012, Ho et al. 2019, Kim and Martin 2021).

We further contend that the issue of incorrect inference or misunderstanding is more acute on social media, especially when information is viewed on mobile devices. Ideally, the quality of human decisions can be improved by providing more accurate and complete information, but this is hardly the case on most consumer-oriented social media, such as Yelp, where simplicity of information provision is stressed as a design goal. Simplified information presentation is a core principle of and almost synonymous with successful website design, especially when mobile and small-screen interfaces are used to access social media content. For usability considerations, simplicity is necessary to reduce cognitive load on users and win their acceptance (Robier 2016). Thus, design principles based on providing simplified information, often in the form of a few simple keywords, remain mainstream on user interfaces. Consistent with these principles, hygiene information on Yelp is presented simply (three words: “Health Score A”; see Figure 1(a) for an example), whereas the lengthy inspection details and hygiene violations are provided via a separate link and require the user to follow in a purposeful manner.

From an economic standpoint, such settings may appear superfluous, but in reality, they can impact consumer decision making profoundly because humans are boundedly rational decision makers who use limited cognitive resources to process and understand information (Simon 1972). Particularly on social media, individuals tend to be cognitive misers, skim-reading texts to just a satisficing amount (Duggan and Payne 2011,

## 2.3. The Role of Information Ambiguity as a Causal Mechanism

2.3.1. The Misperception of the Cue “Health Score” on Yelp. As discussed above, when acquiring information on social media platforms, individuals tend to “satisfice,” skimming content without engaging deeply with the text (Duggan and Payne 2011). Similarly, on Yelp restaurant pages, consumers tend to process information quickly and superficially, rather than comprehensively considering the information to make inferences (Luca 2016). This leads to our proposition that restaurant hygiene information displayed on Yelp may lead consumers to mistakenly perceive the information cue to refer to the healthiness of the food served by the restaurant, rather than correctly attribute the information cue to restaurant hygiene. We argue that this misperception is a function of information ambiguity, as described below.

Yelp’s interface provides hygiene information online in a simplified form—“Health Score A” for restaurants that score well on their hygiene inspections. Unfortunately, this information cue is ambiguous and open to multiple interpretations. Ambiguity in this context is defined as the proneness of information to result in incorrect understanding because of vagueness, low clarity, or a lack of context specificity. Ambiguity thus affects how individuals understand the information; ambiguous information muddies the understanding or interpretation of its correct meaning (Huckfeldt et al. 1998, Pike et al. 2018). In some cases, ambiguity arises from a lack of understanding of the specific context (Commerford et al. 2019). Communication researchers have argued that ambiguity may not be accidental but can also be strategic. For instance, political campaigns and change management efforts sometimes strategically introduce ambiguity into speech or messages, allowing people to fill in the context and meaning according to their perspectives (Gioia et al. 2012). Thus, ambiguity in interpersonal relationships, official communication, and firm-level information can be inadvertent, unrecognized, or deliberately strategic.<sup>1</sup>

In the context of restaurant selection, consumers typically start with the goal of enjoying a tasty meal and are likely to consult platforms such as Yelp to identify suitable restaurants. On encountering the ambiguous information cue “Health Score,” they are likely to infer the cue to refer to the healthiness of the restaurant food rather than the hygiene level. On social media, simplified information is often incomplete and ambiguous, and the consequences for misunderstandings are relatively small in most cases (Carpenter et al. 2018). In the case of Yelp, the average consumer is unlikely to invest significant time and effort to fully understand what the information means or pertains to exactly. Consumers thus tend to process ambiguous information by filling in the unclear context with the most readily accessible interpretation in their minds (Naylor et al. 2011). Thus, as an example of inadvertent ambiguous information presented on social media, the “Health Score” cue is likely to be interpreted in multiple ways. Of the multiple interpretations that could be applied to this cue, an inference about the restaurant’s food and its attributes is potentially the most easily accessible and relevant, thus leading the average consumer to misinterpret the cue “Health Score” to refer to the healthiness of the food served at the restaurant. How does this inference lead to lower demand? We address this link in our reasoning next.

2.3.2. The Phenomenon of “Healthy Food/Less Tasty fi Less Demand.” Healthy food is, by itself, not a negative characteristic. However, the literature shows that the perception of certain foods as being healthy can have a significant impact on whether individuals choose to consume that food, especially in a restaurant setting. This link between the healthiness of food and its eventual consumption is provided by a pervasive heuristic empirically documented repeatedly among American consumers: the belief that healthy food is less tasty (Raghunathan et al. 2006). The perception that healthy food is less tasty, and thereby experiences lowe demand in restaurant settings, has been observed in multiple settings and can be attributed to multiple theoretical perspectives, as explained below.

The notion that “healthy food is less tasty and thus enjoys lower demand” has been explained using a variety of theoretical perspectives across diverse literatures. In cognitive psychology, this relationship is explained through theories of associative memory and learning, which suggest that human cognition builds connections through repeated exposure to ideas, reinforcing associations over time (Shanks 1995,

Delamater and Lattal 2014). In modern society, advertising, cultural cues, and personal communication often imply that healthy foods are not tasty, thus reinforcing the association between healthiness of foods and their lack of tastiness. This association becomes automatic over time in our cognition and robustly influences our food choice, as shown in the literature (Raghunathan et al. 2006, Robinson et al. 2012, Hoogeveen et al. 2016, Briers et al. 2020, Garaus and Lalicic 2021, Turnwald et al. 2022, WHO 2022).

An alternative perspective on this relationship is provided in evolutionary psychology, based on the theory of evolutionary adaptation (Buss 2019), which posits that human beings have evolved to crave energy-dense foods. These flavorful foods, high in fat, sugar, and salt, are typically considered unhealthy by today’s standards, but these foods provided survival advantages in ancestral environments (Breslin 2013). This preference continues to shape our food perceptions and choices today, making healthier foods less appealing by comparison. Neuroscience further supports this explanation through theories of the brain’s reward systems (Pignatelli and Bonci 2015), indicating that unhealthy foods activate the brain’s pleasure modules by releasing dopamine, which drives preferences for these foods (Smith 1995, Wilson et al. 1995, Rolls 2015). Furthermore, we note that as per behavioral economics, drawing on the theory of present-biased preferences, people prioritize immediate gratification (such as taste) over long-term benefits (such as health) in their decision making (Loewenstein et al. 2001). Finally, psychophysiological theories argue that immediate visceral states, such as appetite or craving, often override cognitive assessments; thus, the anticipated pleasure of tasty food predominantly drives food choices (Mellers et al. 1999, Baumgartner et al. 2008).

Particularly relevant to our context (restaurant-based food consumption), hedonic consumption theory (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982, Dhar and Wertenbroch 2000) contends that besides utility, individuals are motivated to seek pleasure or hedonic value from certain types of consumption (Kivetz and Simonson 2002). For food, tastiness is a primary source of pleasure (Yeomans 1998, Avena 2015). When a food choice is perceived as less tasty, the expected hedonic value is reduced, leading to lower preference and demand (Yeomans 1998, Avena 2015), especially when dining out when food tastiness is consistently shown to be one of the primary considerations for restaurant choice (Jung et al. 2015).

Ample empirical evidence exists across the food sci ence and marketing literature showing support for the “healthy food/less tasty → less demand” relationship. Whereas a full discussion of this empirical literature is out of the scope of this paper, in the interest of space, we provide a representative sample of this research in Online Appendix 1. In the studies cited, demand for food has been assessed through various measures, such as actual food choices (Raghunathan et al. 2006), actual food consumption (Liem et al. 2012), self-reported food consumption (Briers et al. 2020), intention to purchase food (Garaus et al. 2023), and liking of the food (Tuorila et al. 1994). Across these multiple settings, there is robust support for the empirical relationship between the perception of healthiness of foods and the resulting expectations of tastiness and enjoyment. Notably, we observe that this link is particularly strong when the hedonic goal of enjoying food is salient, such as in a dining-out context (Oh 2020, Garaus et al. 2023), which is the focus of our current research.

Bringing these arguments back to our present case, we believe that a strong case can be made for the assertion that Yelp users who consult a restaurant’s page and encounter an ambiguous information cue such as “Health Score” are likely to mistakenly infer the restaurant’s food to be healthy and thereby choose alternative restaurants. Consequently, restaurants with a high health score (i.e., clean restaurants) are likely to see less demand, all else being equal, as a result of their hygiene information being disclosed on Yelp. This logic chain is displayed concisely in Figure 2 below. Alternatively, if displaying hygiene information on Yelp merely extends the disclosure from off line (on doors) to online—making the information more salient—we would expect a positive impact of hygiene disclosure on demand for high-score (clean) restaurants, consistent with prior work (Jin and Leslie 2003). To address this tension, we proceed with our empirical analyses, described next.

## 3. Empirical Analyses

## 3.1. Overview of the Empirical Studies

We use a combination of regression analyses based on field data and online experiments as our empirical strategy for this paper. Table 1 outlines the four studies we present, whereas Online Appendix 2 provides a detailed roadmap. By integrating multiple methodologies, we aim to triangulate on the relationships of interest in our context, acknowledging that any one methodology is unlikely to provide a complete narrative for the theorized effects. As a baseline, Study 1 uses a differences-in-differences design to establish the marginal effect of restaurants displaying “Health Score A” on their Yelp pages—a form of hygiene information disclosure prone to misperception—on their demand. We find that showing high hygiene scores on Yelp negatively impacts restaurant demand. To identify the proposed mechanism for this effect, we conducted two controlled online experiments. Study 2 tests the proposed causal mechanism of information ambiguity in our context, with the results showing that ambiguity does play a significant role in influencing consumer perceptions, as we theorized.

## 3.2. Study 1: Effect of the Displayed Hygiene Information on Restaurant Demand 3.2.1. Study 1 Data and Methods.

3.2.1.1. Identification Strategy. Yelp began to roll out hygiene scores widely across multiple U.S. states in $2 0 \dot { 1 } \dot { 6 . } ^ { 2 }$ However, within each state and the affected municipalities (cities), hygiene scores were added to the Yelp pages of restaurants in a manner that is largely unpredictable for both consumers and restaurant owners. Even though municipal codes require that all restaurants must be inspected and reported on and Yelp hygiene data were sourced from municipal reports, the process by which Yelp provided hygiene scores for some restaurants in a city on its platform, but not for others, is unspecified by both Yelp and the city authorities. As a result, some restaurants saw hygiene scores shown on their Yelp pages, whereas others in the same city did not (Carman 2018). In effect, the hygiene disclosure on Yelp represented an arguably exogenous event for both restaurant owners and consumers. Furthermore, because Yelp did not preannounce the provision of hygiene scores or give restaurant owners the ability to “turn off” such scores, any forward-looking strategic behavior by restaurant owners and consumers driven by the hygiene scores online was ruled out, allowing us to treat the appearance of the scores on Yelp as an exogenous “treatment” for purposes of our analysis. We rely on this relative exogeneity of the Yelp event for restaurants and consu mers alike. This identification strategy allows us to use a differences-in-differences framework where we com pare demand for the treated sample of restaurants whose hygiene scores were exogenously provided versus the control sample of similar restaurants in the same municipalities but without hygiene scores provided by Yelp, before and after the treatment.

3.2.1.2. Sample. Given the haphazard rollout of hygiene scores on Yelp across the cities, we were careful in establishing conditions under which the data set to be used was assembled. Although several states had hygiene scores added for restaurants on Yelp, we only included states in our analysis that met the following conditions: (1) hygiene scores were provided widely across major cities within the state instead of being pilot-tested in a single city (e.g., Chicago in Illinois), (2) news from Yelp and third-party sources could be used to verify the exact provision time/date across the state, (3) the proportions of restaurants with versus without hygiene scores at the time of rollout were relatively balanced, and (4) hygiene scores were shown as a number or letter (i.e., point/letter grade).<sup>3</sup>

The last criterion regarding the display format is important because our analysis is focused on the effect of displaying high hygiene scores in addition to simply providing hygiene scores. Furthermore, we ensured that the rollout of hygiene scores was the only systematic change to the Yelp interface in the states we focused on during the study period.<sup>4</sup> On the basis of these restrictions, our analysis was limited to data from three states—California, Oklahoma, and Texas (Figure 3). The selection of these states, rather than a much broader set of states, relies on a trade-off between good-quality data that can be verified versus a larger sample for which the precise conditions of the hygiene score rollouts are hard to verify. We thus err on the conservative side in constructing our data set here.

3.2.1.3. Data Collection. In each city in our study, we queried the complete restaurant list using Yelp’s proprietary location-based search engine. To ensure our data set is reasonably assembled for rigorous testing, we again systematically curated the data, striving to balance out ethical concerns of Yelp’s restriction on collecting large amounts of restaurant data against concerns of a lack of statistical power because of small sample sizes.<sup>5</sup> We ensured that our sample included only those restaurants that were verified to be operating—by observing that they received actual reviews—both before and after the treatment, leading to 8,037 restaurants in total, 1,357 (17%) of which were in the treated sample (hereinafter “total treated”) and the remaining 6,680 restaurants in the control sample (“total control”). Whereas we recognize that the sample is not exhaustive in terms of all restaurants within the states, it is adequate for the purposes of our analysis. Because the restaurant list returned by the Yelp search algorithm is unrelated to the actual hygiene scores (whether online or not), we have a seemingly random sample of restaurants from specific cities within the three states where there is variation across the treated sample.

3.2.1.6. Propensity Score Matching (PSM). Although DID analyses based on the complete sample benefit from a larger sample size and more statistical power, a concern is that the treated and control restaurants may not be comparable. Therefore, we employed PSM to control for possible bias and ensure that the control sample is a qualified counterfactual for the treated (Greenwood and Wattal 2017). We used 1:1 exact matching with these variables: restaurant location, business price, and prior average rating right before the treatment. We ensured that the matching procedure produced a sound balance between the treated and control units.<sup>13</sup> As a result, the matched-sample sizes used for analysis were lower than the full sample but provided cleaner inference given the balance between the treatment and control samples (sample statistics are provided in table notes).

3.2.2.2. Mechanism Tests. Recall that our arguments for the effect of hygiene information displayed through Yelp on restaurant demand are based on information ambiguity. To further establish the veracity of this proposed mechanism, we conduct a series of empirical tests based on heterogeneities in the estimated treatment effects. Specifically, building on hedonic consumption theory (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982), we start with the notion that restaurant customers are pursuing a hedonic goal—enjoying tasty, flavorful food— when dining out (Oh 2020, Garaus et al. 2023). Under these circumstances, the information cue “Health Score” is potentially misleading because it draws inferences about healthy and less tasty food. However, the effects we estimate are likely to be less pronounced in contexts where tasty food is not necessarily a dominant decision driver. In other words, in settings where consumers are looking to frequent restaurants where tasty food is not an important criterion, the effect of hygiene scores on Yelp and the resulting ambiguity may not affect demand as much. We can thus use these sources of heterogeneity as potential mechanism tests.

Based on this logic, we propose further analysis pertaining to three segments of restaurants: (1) fastfood restaurants, (2) restaurants that offer drive-through services, and (3) restaurants that cater to social events and entertainment (such as clubs and games). Our reasoning for these tests is as follows. Consumers typically go to fast-food restaurants for affordable food provided quickly, that is, for a utilitarian rather than a hedonic goal (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982). Therefore, the perception of “healthy foods/less tasty” is likely to be less relevant to consumers. Similarly, consumers go to restaurants with drive-through services for convenience, and entertainment-oriented restaurants typically serve social purposes (e.g., receptions, gatherings, and parties), making the tastiness of food less critical in restaurant choice. Therefore, within these segments, we should see a significant weakening of the estimated negative demand effect.

To empirically investigate this logic, we created dummy indicators for these segments of restaurants separately (D\_fast\_food, D\_drive\_through, and D\_entertain) by expanding our data collection to include these relevant attributes for restaurants in our sample. We then reestimated our analyses by adding interaction terms between the DID term and each of the dummy indicators, respectively, along with other appropriate interaction terms. The results of the full-sample and PSM-based analyses are shown in Tables 5 and $^ { 6 , }$ respectively. Given our focus on the treatment effect associated with the disclosure of hygiene grades on Yelp for high-hygiene restaurants, we highlight and discuss the heterogeneity analyses of the high-hygiene sample of restaurants here, whereas we provide the analyses of the low-hygiene restaurants in Online Appendix 5, along with the technical details about measures. There are two notable findings from Tables 5 and 6. First, the DID effect across the analyses is significant at least at the $p { = } 0 . 0 5$ level and remains consistent with our main findings discussed earlier in both the full-sample and PSM-based analyses. Second, as expected, the DID effect of reducing restaurant demand for high-hygiene restaurants is essentially mitigated for fast-food restaurants, restaurants that offer drive-through services, and restaurants catering to social events and entertainment occasions. Overall, these results help corroborate our hypothesized mechanism: to the extent that a highhygiene score disclosed on Yelp tends to have a negative effect on restaurant demand because of information ambiguity and the misperception of healthy/less tasty food, the effect is significantly diminished for restaurants where customers visit primarily for reasons other than tasty food.<sup>16</sup>

## 3.3. Study 2: Testing the Mechanism of Information Ambiguity

In this section, we move from secondary data to online experiments to specifically test for our hypothesized mechanism. Recall that we had argued that the negative effect of showing a high hygiene inspection score of the type “Health Score $\mathrm { ~ A } ^ { \prime \prime }$ on demand could arise from consumers’ misperception of food healthiness. In other words, we theorize that the stimulus “Health Score $\mathrm { A } ^ { \prime \prime }$ leads consumers to misperceive it as greater food healthiness, driven by information ambiguity. To further test this proposed mechanism, we ran an online experiment where we varied the stimuli shown to experimental participants. If the predicted effect—that “Health Score $\mathrm { A } ^ { \prime \prime }$ leads to a perception of greater food healthiness—is because of the ambiguity of this displayed stimulus, then adding a clarification specifying that “Health Score $\mathrm { A } ^ { \prime \prime }$ means “this restau rant meets high hygiene standards” should help mitigate ambiguity and thus attenuate the effect caused by ambiguity (i.e., show a significant moderation/interaction). We describe the experimental method conducted in Study 2 to test this reasoning.

3.3.1. Study 2 Method. Participants were 411 American adults from Prolific.com’s online panel $( M _ { \mathrm { a g e } }$ (standard deviation (SD)) � 37.89 (13.14), 199 males) and were randomly assigned to one of the four conditions in a 2 (hygiene information: yes, no) × 2 (clarification: yes, no) full-factorial between-subjects design. A prescreening ensured all participants were familiar with Yelp. The study scenario was straightforward: participants were told, “Imagine that you are on a trip to Los Angeles. You have decided to dine out tonight and are choosing possible restaurants. ## 3.5. Study 4: Testing the Effects of “Health Score Inspected & Permitted” on Restaurant Demand

As mentioned, Yelp provided, on the restaurant pages, the less ambiguous hygiene information “Health Score Inspected & Permitted” in a small number of U.S. states. Although Study 3 found that this hygiene information was less susceptible to being misperceived as referring to food healthiness in an experimental setting, it is unclear whether the finding will hold in the field. We leveraged this unique Yelp setting and again used DID to replicate this finding, that is, to test the marginal effect on restaurant demand.

3.5.1. Study 4 Methods. From the outset, the three states examined in this analysis cover the northeast and midwest of the United States and thus are geographically diverse, partially mitigating regional biases.<sup>20</sup> Given the relatively fine-tuned nature of this analysis, we ensured that the data set used was carefully assembled in order to provide robust results (more details about how we assembled the data set are provided in Online Appendix 8). For cities in these states, Yelp showed the less ambiguous form to a random subset of restaurants; these represent the treated sample consisting of restaurants with passing (high) and failing (low) hygiene scores, whereas the control sample consisted of other restaurants in the same cities but without hygiene data on Yelp. We obtained the full restaurant lists returned by Yelp’s search engine, conditioned on location. As in Study 1, we do not claim that this represents an exhaustive list of restaurants in these states but represents a random sample generated through Yelp’s search algorithms, which returned restaurants both with (i.e., treated) and without hygiene scores (i.e., control). As mentioned earlier, the restaurants returned by the Yelp search algorithm are systematically unrelated to the presence or absence of hygiene inspection data on Yelp, providing us with an unbiased sample for our purposes. Similar to Study 1, we collected SafeGraph data as well as data about hygiene scores and Yelp ratings for each restaurant in our curated sample. Some restaurants were dropped because of no Yelp ratings being received during the six months before and after the treatment. Our final data set for this study consists of 739 unique restaurants, representing roughly 5% of restaurants in these states.

3.5.2. Regression Results. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 7 list the DID results involving the less ambiguous form of showing hygiene information (e.g., “Health Score Inspected & Permitted”).<sup>21</sup> Interestingly, the results from both the full (column (1)) and PSM-based sample (column (2)) show that the previously observed negative effect of Yelp’s hygiene information disclosure on restaurant demand is absent. Thus, the results corroborate those of Study 3 and indicate that the marginal reduction in ambiguity of the hygiene information display causes less error in terms of invoking references to healthiness or tastiness of the food. In general, using secondary data from the field, we find that reducing the ambiguity of the hygiene information provided online, even marginally, is beneficial in aggregate in terms of its effects on restaurant demand.

## 3.6. Overall Summary of Empirical Findings

By combining curated secondary data and two online experiments, we show evidence that displaying hygiene information on Yelp in an ambiguous form (“Health Score A”) can have a detrimental effect on restaurant demand, especially for restaurants with high hygiene (Study 1). We propose that the negative effect on restaurant demand arises from a misperception of restaurants food healthiness because the ambiguous hygiene information “Health Score A” could lead consumers to misunderstand it as greater food healthiness. Meanwhile, providing a clarification alongside the hygiene information can help effectively mitigate such a misperception. The findings of our first randomized experiment (Study 2) confirmed our proposition in a causal framework. Moreover, we reason that even small changes in the form in which this information is displayed, whereby ambiguity is reduced, albeit marginally, can help mitigate such a misperception without needing additional clarification. Indeed, the results from our second experiment (Study 3) showed that the specific form of the hygiene information can significantly change how restaurants are perceived in terms of their food healthiness. Finally, we replicate this result in the field by using the unique Yelp setting and a carefully hand-curated data set (Study 4), bolstering the external validity of the laboratory result. To the extent that platform managers and policymakers can acknowledge the “satisficing” nature of how information is consumed online (Duggan and Payne 2011, Carpenter et al. 2018) and implement information disclosure in an appropri ate form, we believe many of the reservations expressed by restaurant owners about information disclosure on social media platforms can be addressed.

## 4. Discussion and Conclusion

Hygiene inspections in restaurants are a crucial public health activity because they are the first step toward preventing large-scale disease outbreaks (Mejia et al. 2019). Whereas it is broadly acknowledged that they are useful and that the information about restaurant hygiene should be shared with consumers, the specific form and modality in which it should be shared has remained a topic of some debate. Early work shows how hygiene grade cards displayed offline at the restaurant premises—in a form that is information rich and unambiguous—have been positively received (Jin and Leslie 2003), but as this disclosure process moves online, understandably, various members of the restaurant community have expressed reservations about how the inspection scores are displayed and about their potential downstream effects (Kim and Martin 2021). Whereas the scale and reach of platforms such as Yelp are certainly helpful in disseminating important information, the information, if displayed hap hazardly, might also be prone to misperceptions and misrepresentations (Sparrow et al. 2011). Our paper aims to resolve some of these open issues.

From a managerial viewpoint, our work provides some clear takeaways. First, platforms such as Yelp should consider the possible influence of ambiguity when they provide information online, especially important policy-related information such as hygiene inspection scores. Whereas simple and minimalist designs of information presentation are desirable, especially on smartphones where screen real estate is costly, simplicity can also lead to greater ambiguity and confusion. As we demonstrate, even small changes to the actual information presented can lead to significant behavioral changes. Second, for policymakers and municipal authorities who would like to ensure that important information such as hygiene is made easily available, a collaborative arrangement with platforms such as Yelp is feasible, provided efforts are made to ensure that the displayed information is consumed appropriately. There is considerable work showing how information online is either intentionally or unintentionally misused—in the specific context of hygiene, our work provides some visibility into how biases manifest and their mitigation.

Finally, restaurant owners have expressed reservations about hygiene scores being disseminated through Yelp, given potential problems in their interpretation. On the one hand, we show that their concerns are valid. On the other hand, we show that any shortcomings in the manner in which this information is displayed can be addressed. Ideally, Yelp and city authorities can work with restaurant owners in running pilot studies where they test different forms of information presentation with consumers and then adopt the most suitable form. At this point, our understanding is that hygiene data displayed on Yelp is largely at Yelp’s discretion rather than as a result of a collaborative effort. Given the importance of hygiene within the restaurant and the public health implications, we suggest that platform companies must work with restaurant owners and city authorities to define how and when this information is provided through their platforms, emphasizing clarity without sacrificing conciseness or precision.

The implications of our study could well generalize beyond American dining decisions in at least two dimensions. Information disclosure online continues to be driven by the need for simplicity and parsimony. Good web design requires that information be provided in ways that are easily understood. However, as we show here, information ambiguity can lead to variations in how the information is misperceived and used in downstream decision making. Our work here generalizes to a need for more fully understanding the implications of how information is displayed and for a more detailed evaluation of the unforeseen behavioral implications of the display modalities chosen. Moreover, information disclosure on security, safety, hygiene, risk, and potential harm is prevalent across online and mobile ecosystems. For instance, cybersecurity warnings and safety protocols with respect to online data are present in all online settings, especially in the context of sensitive healthcare and financial data. Similarly, risks from using specific apps are communicated online. AI algorithms that are made available to consumers often carry warnings or cautionary messages about the implications of their use. Hygiene and risk-related information is commonly provided in the case of certain services, such as restaurants. Research into how these messages are constructed, how users respond to them, and the behavioral and cognitive biases that manifest remains limited in IS. Our work addresses these issues within a specific context. However, more work is needed to fully understand how online (mobile) information disclosure can affect downstream behavior.

Our work here is subject to certain limitations. First, we do not measure actual demand but proxy for res taurant demand using SafeGraph foot traffic data. Despite this limitation, an increasing number of scho lars have recognized the value and reliability of such data in proxying business demand (Mitkina et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2022, Babar et al. 2023). That said, we caution that the estimated effects reported may potentially underestimate the true effects on restau rant demand because at least some mobile users are unwilling to share their location data with SafeGraph, even anonymously. Second, we do not possess a ful census of restaurants in our samples, especially when it comes to large cities. Whereas having access to the full census would be ideal, we are conscious of the data access restrictions imposed by platforms such as Yelp and the ethical issues involved in violating these usage norms. Across both our secondary data sets, we have endeavored to curate the data and ensure that our data sets are unbiased given the research question at hand. Furthermore, whereas we conducted two randomized experiments to causally identify our theo rized mechanism in the “laboratory,” we recognize that additional work is needed to fully establish the causal mechanism in the field. For instance, certain specially-designed cues incorporated on the platform, such as a cross symbol or a colored hyperlink to depict violation details, may serve as clarifications and help mitigate ambiguity. Yet further empirica testing, potentially with more complete data sets, is warranted before we can conclude that these cues are instrumental. Third, whereas our analysis helps pro vide strong evidence for the role of the ambiguity mechanism at play, it cannot completely rule out the possibility of other potential mechanisms. Again, more research is needed to fully disentangle these mechanisms, even as we show that the ambiguity mechanism does receive significant empirical support. Finally, the Yelp treatment effect can potentially spill over into the restaurants in the control group through individuals—individual patrons of restaurants may be more or less susceptible to ambiguity, leading to heterogeneity at the level of the individual. However, SafeGraph data do not provide visibility into individ ual behavior, and therefore, we do not have a clea way to identify individual sources of heterogeneity. This remains a topic for future research.

Our work also raises many interesting questions for future research. First, whereas we focus on how hygiene information provided on platforms broadly affects demand, we are not able to isolate the impact on new versus repeat customers—one could argue that the biases we discuss are more likely to occur with new customers, but this requires further study. Moreover, what happens if the hygiene information disclosed online becomes outdated and inconsistent with what is displayed offline at the restaurant? Do customers tend to disregard the information provided by platforms? Additionally, platforms such as Yelp are starting to provide hygiene information outside the United States, where the mindset that healthy food is less tasty may not apply in equal measure. For instance, Werle et al. (2013) argue that French consumers, because of their unique food culture where health and pleasure are culturally intertwined (e.g., emphasis on culinary heritage, small portion norms), may hold a counterpoint to the American perspective—where healthy food is assumed to be tasty as well (termed the “French intuition”).<sup>22</sup> Would we see similar results in such settings? Expanding our work to other areas of the world where dietary assumptions may differ from those in the United States represents a fruitful area for future research.

Furthermore, prior research has shown that the information displayed on platforms can be temporally bounded—that is, people do not always remember this information or may learn and update their understanding over time. Is this true of hygiene information as well? Or do the misperceptions engendered by hygiene information online remain salient for longer? Finally, we started our work here by highlighting the impact that ambiguity could have on information disclosure in online and digital markets across a plethora of settings—whereas anecdotal evidence exists for how these information cues (such as those provided on Apple, Zillow, and LinkedIn) can be misleading, systematic work studying the downstream effects of the resulting ambiguity is still absent in the literature. Information disclosure is, by definition, useful for markets and customers, but the form and structure of this information disclosure remain important design variables, especially in online settings. We suggest that much more work is needed before the various sources of information ambiguity visible in such online settings can be identified and corrected, whereas best practices for effective information disclosure online are devised. We believe our work is part of an ongoing and important stream of research addressing these important questions in the context of social media and digital platforms, where a lot of consequential economic activity is carried out.
