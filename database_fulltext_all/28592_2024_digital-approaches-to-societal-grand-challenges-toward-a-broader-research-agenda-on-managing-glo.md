# Digital Approaches to Societal Grand Challenges: Toward a Broader Research Agenda on Managing Global-Local Design Tensions

Abstract. Information systems (IS) scholars have pursued phenomenon-specific research to meaningfully engage with and contribute to addressing societal grand challenges (GCs). Although such efforts are invaluable, a broader framework that identifies generalized organizing problems in tackling GCs and reveals promising pathways for digital technologies to add value in addressing them could help establish an agenda for impactful IS research. In this article, we offer such a framework by drawing on Ostrom’s principles of public value creation and management research on organizational design. We identify a set of eight organizing design tensions that arise from employing global and local perspectives in addressing GCs. We consider digital approaches to resolving each of these design tensions and delineate a rich and diverse agenda for future research. We acknowledge prevailing socio-political structures and factors that may constrain the effectiveness of digital solutions and adopt a socio-technical design perspective to suggest alternative arrangements of digital and socio-political elements in GC settings. Our discussion foreshadows the considerable opportunity for IS research to contribute to the broader dialog on GCs by integrating existing IS theoretical perspectives and concepts with complementary theories from management and beyond. Our framework also informs and enriches GC phenomenon-specific IS research by helping to generalize the research questions and allowing for crossphenomenon mapping of critical study insights. More broadly, the pursuit of the research agenda proposed here, along with entering a broader GC conversation with other disciplines and stakeholders, may speed the identification and enactment of effective solutions to grand challenges.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade or so, persistent calls have been made for the information systems (IS) research community to meaningfully engage with and contribute to addressing broad-scope problems of social interest or societal grand challenges (e.g., Winter and Butler 2011, Lee 2016, and Majchrzak et al. 2016). Reflecting such an imperative, rapidly growing research streams in IS have focused on specific challenges—for example, climate change, environmental sustainability, and sustainable energy (e.g., Malhotra et al. 2014, Ketter et al. 2016, and

Seidel et al. 2017); societal health and well-being (e.g., Mirbabaie et al. 2020, Riemer et al. 2020, Ozer et al. 2023, and Tremblay et al. 2023); and social justice, equality, and inclusion (e.g., Andrade and Doolin 2016 and Aanestad et al. 2021).

Although such phenomenon-specific studies are invaluable, a broader framework that identifies general ized organizing problems in tackling societal grand challenges (GCs)—that is, problems that are evident across different phenomena—and reveals promising pathways for digital technologies to add value in addressing them could help establish an agenda for impactful IS research. Further, such a framework could also help anchor existing phenomenon-specific IS research in the ongoing broader conversation on GCs (e.g., George et al. 2016, Doh et al. 2019, George et al. 2021, and Fernhaber and Zou 2022) and make critical connections with related research in other fields and disciplines—both within business (e.g., management, entrepreneurship, international business) and outside (e.g., political science, sociology, community development). Such a framework is currently missing in IS research.

Grand challenges are “specific critical barrier(s) that, if removed, would help solve an important societal problem with a high likelihood of global impact through widespread implementation” (Grand Challenges Canada 2011, p. iv). GCs are often global, causally complex, characterized by significant uncertainty, consist of divergent stakeholder interests, and have intergenerational impact. These features of GCs, thus, require novel managerial solutions to organizing partnerships and galvanizing action toward solving them. Often, strategic discussions and policy formulation on how to address these GCs are held by global actors, such as multilateral agencies, standard-setting bodies, and multinational corporations, whereas implementation of this agenda mostly rests with local actors, such as nations, communities, businesses, social enterprises and citizens. For example, the Global Adaptation Mapping Initiative (GAMI) reveals a fragmentation among actors and interventions in climate change adaptation with sparse evidence of transformational adaptation. Partly, this is due to the unclear roles and responsibilities of global and local actors, which act as barriers to effective participation, governance, and implementation (Nalau et al. 2015, Reckien and Petkova 2019).

Thus, in multiactor situations that characterize GCs, a tension emerges between the conceptual clarity and agenda setting of global actors and the ground reality and implementation faced by local actors. Local actors are necessarily more varied because of their social, economic, technical, and cultural contexts in which they operate and their resources and capabilities to execute this agenda. The top-down decision-making approach of global actors (often situated in the Global North) might also exacerbate the tensions with local actors (for example, marginalized communities in the Global South, who often face the gravest consequences of GCs), who might consider recommended solutions as infeasible or inappropriate. This potential juxtaposition of grand global ambition with limited local capability and complexity of socio-technical systems in which they operate creates organizing design tensions that need to be considered before solutions can be enacted. In this article, we offer a framework by focusing on the tensions that arise from employing global and local perspectives in addressing GCs.

The global and local perspectives could lead to differ ent interpretations of the nature and structure of the problem that needs to be addressed, approaches to solving them, entities to be engaged with, and their agency/ decision-making rights, as well as feasible solutions. Consider the case of climate change. From a global per spective, the climate change problem has largely been viewed as one of mitigation (managing factors that cause climate change); the dominant entities have been the funding agencies (e.g., United Nations (UN), World Bank) and national/regional governments and nonprofit entities; and solutions have emphasized generalizability across geographies and scaling potential, with increasing focus on fit with contextual factors. In contrast, from a local-/community-level perspective, climate change is often viewed as one of active adaptation that emphasizes restorative and coevolutionary initiatives; connects closely with local (community) priorities and culturally grounded knowledge; and positions communities as the locus for collective action.

GCs, as complex problems with feedback loops and dynamics that cross global-local levels, call for simultaneously holding both these perspectives and resolving the tensions therein. For example, a global perspective may inform on the nature of the local problem (understanding the problem as a whole); local resources may need to be complemented by global resources; a local agency may be critical to effectively implement globally sourced solutions; global considerations may inform on scaling local solutions; etc. Global-local tensions of GCs are rooted in situations where local interests (e.g., clear ing weeds and waste from fields by burning them) conflict with global interests (e.g., reducing global carbon emissions). These types of problems, where there is a divergence between individual and group interests in societal common-pool resources, are referred to as collective action problems (Ostrom 1990).

We identify these global-local tensions in addressing GCs by drawing on Elinor Ostrom’s work on solving collective action problems (Ostrom 1990, Ostrom et al. 1994, Ostrom 2000, 2010). Specifically, we employ Ostrom’s eight general design principles to tease out the potential tensions between global and local perspectives in formulating and complying with rules that govern joint value creation—what we refer to as design tensions. We illustrate these global-local organizing design tensions using different GC contexts to indicate their generalizability and relevance. We then consider digital approaches to resolving each of these design tensions and identify a set of related exemplar IS research questions. We also suggest theoretical frameworks and con cepts from management, entrepreneurship, and other areas that complement and inform IS research perspectives. Figure 1 provides an overview of our framework.

In doing so, we seek to make two contributions to research in IS and beyond. First, we delineate a comprehensive and holistic research agenda for IS scholars that is founded on the broader issues and themes in organizing for GCs and yet connects with and builds on the ongoing GC phenomenon-specific IS research streams. Second, by identifying digital approaches related to specific organizing challenges, we offer a pathway for incorporating important digital technology-related theoretical themes and concepts in the broader dialog on societal grand challenges. More broadly, the pursuit of the research agenda proposed here, along with entering a broader GC conversation among other disciplines and stakeholders, may speed the identification and enactment of effective solutions to grand challenges.

## 2. Global and Local Perspectives in Societal Grand Challenges

Central to discussions of GCs is the concept of joint public value creation. Joint value creation refers to processes involving multiple parties who face high task and outcome interdependence in providing mutually supportive contributions to value creation (Bridoux and Stoelhorst 2016). The literature on public goods suggests that public value stems from the benefits accrued because a good is nonexcludable (available to all) and nonrivalrous (supply does not decrease with consumption) (Samuelson 1954, Ostrom 1990). Therefore, public value creation inherently offers collective implications, which call into question governance models for the deployment of resources and the coordination of effort (Cabral et al. 2019). Indeed, GCs require public value creation, but this is further complicated by GC features, such as divergent stakeholder goals, unclear definitions of outcomes, and difficulties in codifying standardized solutions. Because value creation in the GC context necessitates joint effort between public and private organizations, management researchers call for new models of collaboration that take into consideration the unique aspects of GCs as a managerial problem and the design complexity in the organizing needed to achieve it (George et al. 2024).

A critical aspect of this design challenge is the need for integration of global and local actors. To put this in context, global actors involved in addressing GCs include multilateral agencies and intergovernmental organizations and networks (e.g., the European Union (EU); the United Nations and its agencies; The African Union; the World Bank Group); multinational corporations; trade associations and standard-setting agencies (e.g., World Business Council for Sustainable Development, EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive); and global nonprofit and nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) (Amnesty International, Gates Foundation), among others. Local actors, for our purposes, include national governments; provincial states; policymaking and enforcement agencies; urban and rural governments and their agencies; communities; businesses; NGOs; and individual citizens. This variety of global and local actors—their attendant problems, goals, resources, incentives, and constraints—creates a unique set of collaboration and coordination problems in galvanizing joint efforts to address societal grand challenges.

## 2.1. Ostrom’s Design Principles and Global-Local Tensions in Joint Value Creation

Elinor Ostrom’s work on public goods and commonpool resources provides guiding principles that expose inherent tensions that underlie joint effort. Natural resource ecosystems, such as forests, land, water, and fisheries, are considered common-pool resources, where collective action and self-governance was required to ensure sustainable value creation. Ostrom’s primary goal was to understand why and how some commonpool resource systems, which require collective action, thrive, whereas others fail. Her path-defining contribution was the identification of design principles of robust, long-enduring, common-pool resource institutions (Ostrom 1990, pp. 90–102). These eight principles are (1) clear boundaries, (2) rules for division of joint value, (3) participation in decision making, (4) system for self-monitoring, (5) ability to sanction noncompliance, (6) mechanisms to resolve conflict, (7) right to selfgovern, and (8) nested decision making and governance structures with multiple stakeholders. Derived from a large set of empirical studies on common-pool resource governance, the eight principles were found to exist in robust institutions—but were notably absent in failed systems (Hess and Ostrom 2007).

Ostrom’s analysis of communities and their natural resources (e.g., Ostrom, 1990, 2000) and her behavioral experiments to isolate causal mechanisms (e.g., Ostrom et al. 1994) provide the necessary framework to curate a set of design principles to organize such public good and common resource projects (Ostrom 2010, Bridoux and Stoelhorst 2022). Although Ostrom’s work is indeed central to addressing GCs, especially with regard to common-pool resources (such as air, water, land, etc.), it is important to caveat that not all GCs impinge upon common-pool resources. For example, developing room-temperature vaccines for immunization or global health and wellness need not be directly tied to common-pool resources, even if a collective action problem and organizing design tension might exist. Indeed, management research has extended these principles beyond incentive conflict into the fundamental question of managing cooperation and coordination within an organization design framework (Gulati et al. 2012, Puranam et al. 2014, George et al. 2024). By complementing Ostrom’s work on public value creation with organization design theories, we can develop a more complete framework to study joint effort in public value creation to tackle GCs and the role that digital technologies can play in that.

Organizational design involves the processes by which collaboration is structured to address problems in the division of responsibilities and in the execution of coordinated action aligned with critical goals (Burton and Obe 1984). The diversity of global and local players in addressing GCs makes this collaboration, division of responsibility, coordinated action, and goal alignment into difficult organizing tasks. Ostrom’s (1990, 2000) eight principles essentially cover three building blocks for effective cooperation in public value creation: rules that govern stakeholders, compliance to these rules, and extension or scaling across larger institutional systems in order to have impact (Bridoux and Stoelhorst 2022). In Table 1, we highlight each of these design principles and how they may reveal misalignment or tensions between global and local actors. We discuss each design principle and use the example of sustainable develop ment goal (SDG) 13 on climate action to showcase how the principle could be relevant in organizing collective action. In Table 1, we provide additional SDG examples to illustrate the generalizability of these principles to a broader range of grand challenges.

Boundaries (design principle 1 (DP 1)) help define the problem or the organizing effort by identifying key stakeholders who are materially relevant to the problem and its solution—that is, it identifies who benefits and who is affected (March and Simon 1993). In GCs, global actors might frame and see problems differently from local actors, leading to differences in how goals are formalized and aggregated (upward) or disaggregated (downward). In complex problems, the beneficiaries and participant stakeholders might also not be immediately apparent because global actors might not have the visibility of local actors and their causes and preferences. For example, Sustainable Development Goal 13 on climate action specifies macro global targets on greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions that get cascaded downward into national- and community-level carbonmitigation projects that may not reflect priorities or resources of local actors. For instance, the Conference of the Parties (COP) 27 meeting held in 2022 to combat cli mate change disappointed many stakeholders with no new agreements on curbing emissions because coun tries could not align local developmental priorities with mitigation outcomes (WRI 2022). Hence, an emphasis on boundaries would reveal a design tension in goal clarity (design tension 1 (DT 1)), where global and local actors may not have congruent, specific, and clear goals.

Division of joint value created (DP 2) is the appropriation of private and public returns. It stipulates how much each participant benefits from joint effort. Division of rewards in group projects is often contentious, but this is exacerbated in GCs because of the mix of public and private value and the difficulty in effectively valuing public benefits (Cabral et al. 2019). There will likely be a tendency for local actors who are proximal to the solution implementation to overclaim returns and a similar likelihood for global actors to use the plurality of local actors as a negotiation strategy. For example, economic rights over saved emissions and the value of carbon emissions is uneven globally. Global carbon markets vary in prices, where companies buy carbon savings for a few dollars in undeveloped markets, and offset against emissions in more developed markets, where costs are significantly higher, where the rewards are not commensurate to effort in undeveloped markets, but become a lucrative arbitrage for those in developed markets. Researchers also find that the methods adopted by these registries in carbon credit markets failed to enforce criteria to ensure that projects make a tangible difference to carbon dioxide levels (Haya et al. 2023), turning many of these carbon offsets into junk credits, where value capture exists without the underlying value creation in climate mitigation. The principle of division of value created reveals the organizing design problem of reward provision (DT 2). The timely provision of commensurate rewards becomes the manifestation of the value appropriation principle.

Participation in decision making (DP 3) defines who has voice in discussions. Stakeholders from different communities likely differ in their interests and influence (Freeman et al. 2010). Pluralism in stakeholder concerns and their respective influence and power is manifested as participation and voice in decision making (Bashshur and Oc 2015). The multilayered repre sentation in global GCs could disempower local actors who are responsible for effort and solutions, leading to tensions and complaints of “not being heard” in discourses on GCs. In climate change discussions, small nations often get overlooked in setting global targets, yet they are often ones in the forefront of bearing the impact of climate change. For example, because of rising sea levels from climate change, 80% of the islandnation Maldives will likely no longer exist. Yet, Maldives by itself can do very little without the resources and efforts of larger countries. Participation in decision making is manifest in task allocation (DT 3) as a design tension, wherein certain stakeholders are responsible for the allocation of effort and delivery of outcomes.

Monitoring (DP 4) involves information collection and sharing to ensure compliances to rules and collective norms. Ostrom (1990) articulates that the governance of common-pool resources requires effective monitoring to ensure compliance to the rules. Indeed, global and local actors might have very different views on what monitoring is and how it is conducted. Actors may differ in their views of the degree of information required, the data collected, and their validity. Monitoring also requires global and local actors to converge on data handling, collection, and sharing practices to protect privacy and ownership rights. For instance, SDG 13 requires global firms to collect and aggregate valid data on carbon and GHG emissions and natural resource use. In stark contrast, inadequate local actor capabilities and norms may weaken data transparency and validity in areas such as emissions monitoring and energy consumption for carbon mitigation. The nascent Regenerative Finance (ReFi) movement seeks to transform the governance of global common-pool resources by leveraging digital monitoring, reporting, and verification. But, there is yet no clear path or models that can focus efforts on regeneration beyond mitigation (Schletz et al. 2023). Monitoring is effective only if there is valid and verifiable information, thus making information provision and transparency (DT 4) an organizing design problem to be addressed.

Sanctions (DP 5) are negative inducements to ensure compliance to rules and to dissuade free-riding behaviors. Although self-regulation can work for industry approaches to GCs (e.g., King and Lenox 2000), sanctions applied in a graduated manner act as disincentives (Ostrom 1990). Global actors could find arbitrage opportunities on compliance and sanctions by operating under multiple local jurisdictions, making compliance and sanctions harder. Local actors might have weak enforcement mechanisms for sanctions due to the lack of institutional enforcement infrastructure. For example, there is an inherent tension when local subsidies on petrol and kerosene for socioeconomic needs or political expediency become counter to the global goals for reduction of dependence on fossil fuels. Sanctions emphasize the disincentive to misbehave and a threat to ensure conformance and compliance, which makes a commensurate design tension as the formulation and application of penalties (DT 5) on noncompliant collaborators who do not contribute to the joint effort for public value creation.

Conflict resolution (DP 6) includes the governance mechanisms (and access to these mechanisms) to address disagreements on interpretation and enforcement of rules and regulations across global and local contexts (Ostrom 2010). Global and local actors have varying degrees of access to effective governance mechanisms to address dispute resolution, including arbitration and judicial processes, which may operate at varying time scales to offer meaningful remedies to conflicts when they arise. For example, multinational firms derive an advantage due to inadequate conflict resolution or weak enforcement at local jurisdictions by having higher GHG emission factories and underinvesting in carbon-mitigation technologies in production in weaker enforcement regimes. For conflict resolution to be effective, joint effort in collabora tion needs specific standards of conduct (DT 6). Public value creation may likely flourish only when standards of con duct become part of the organizing of joint effort.

Right to organize (DP 7) is the ability and right to selfgovern, which is to be able to make their own rules, processes, and mechanisms to collaborate, cooperate, and enforce effort (Ostrom 1990). Global and local actors might vary in their propensities and freedoms to self-regulate and govern based on their governing institutional environment. Global environments to selfregulate may differ drastically from the maturity and capacity of local institutions to enjoy autonomy and participation. Take, for example, pathways to achieve carbon neutrality in an economy with multiple industries. The choices include switching from carbon-heavy emissions to more environmentally friendly choices across sectors such as air transport, personal mobility, and work-from-home policies. But such coordinated efforts require the ability to self-govern and pace investments and collective action. Right to organize highlights a design tension on how tasks are divided and the autonomy to execute them. Task division and task autonomy (DT 7) highlight how the larger aggregated public value creation construct can only be achieved through effective division of the task and the freedom to exercise the effort to achieve it.

Polycentricity (DP 8) is the principle where economic activity can be governed by a mix of private and public actors with varying institutional arrangements. Publicprivate collaborations can vary in the degree of public/ state engagement and control on private-sector actors (George et al. 2024). Allowing private/market actors to collaborate with public/state actors on public value creation might create tensions on the structuring of such collaborations (Gupta et al. 2024)—for instance, efforts to repopulate mangroves and watersheds as carbon sinks and for private actors to capitalize on such investments. Local and regional laws and norms, however, may hinder partnerships with nonstate and market actors, which prevent effective utilization of global resources in local communities. Polycentricity as a design principle brings to the fore a tension on how relationships can be structured and operated across global and local actors. Formalizing and informalizing relationships (DT 8) become a key tension in how collective effort can be organized.

## 3. Digital Approaches to Managing Global-Local Design Tensions

The organizing of joint effort to tackle GCs requires innovative approaches to resolving these design tensions and paradoxes between global and local actors. We suggest that digital technologies portend important ways to address each of the above eight organizing design tensions. At the same time, existing sociopolitical structures and related vested interests, power asymmetries, and cultural barriers at different levels may inhibit the effectiveness of these tools. Further, deployment of digital tools may give rise to new sources of friction in addressing GCs. All of these imply the need to adopt a socio-technical design perspective and consider alternative digital approaches that incorporate different arrangements of digital and socio-political design elements. In this section, we outline these digital approaches and discuss their implications for future IS research (Table 2).<sup>1</sup> In doing so, we also connect with complementary theories and concepts in the broader management field.

## 3.1. Goal Clarity

Differences in the framing of problems by global and local actors on GC initiatives give rise to confusion on how goals and priorities become aligned and who benefits from public value creation. Such differences are further enhanced in the presence of geographic and cognitive distances between global and local actors. Digital platform-based approaches that allow for hosting initiative-specific interactions among global and local actors and the surfacing, sharing, and deliberating of goals, constraints, and priorities could help in managing such design tension. We discuss two alternative approaches—(a) informating platforms and (b) deliberation platforms—that respond to different socio-technical constraints and the nature of the problem to be addressed.

Informating platforms can serve as a knowledge portal and offer tools to enhance awareness and understanding of one another’s goals and efforts. For example, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) recently launched a collaboration platform, climahealth.info, to bring together global and local stakeholders related to climate and public health, including community-level health practitioners. Informating platforms could potentially reduce the invisibility of local problems and priorities, particularly in contexts where top-down goal setting is exercised by global and national organizations (e.g., WHO’s One Health initiative). However, they still may not give sufficient voice and agency to local entities in establishing initiative-specific goals and would likely only sustain existing power structures and asymmetries. Instead, a deliberation platform that brings together appropriate digital tools and socio-political structures for joint goal setting could emphasize not just knowl edge exchange, but joint knowledge deliberation as well to address goal clarity. Such deliberation platforms and tools may also allow for gaining and maintaining visibility into the whole-part relationships among global/local goals and thereby also into the relative rele vance of different value beneficiaries. Research on goa motivation in large group settings (e.g., Adler and Chen 2011) has shown that understanding how entity-leve goals relate to the broader set of goals (of a group) enhance goal and task commitment. New interactive visualization and hypermedia tools have emerged that enable users to view and connect multilayered knowledge/data elements (for example, data on global precipitation and local vegetation), thereby allowing for deeper understanding of global-local goal interdependencies. Studies (cf. Te’eni 1992 and Boland et al. 1994) show how visualization tools that facilitate “easy travel”—the ability to move back and forth from an overview of a concept or task to the granular goals and details—enable individual entities to discover and keep in view associations between their goals and the broader context (the whole-part relationships), along with the underlying assumptions.

These two alternative digital approaches underline a broader question: How can digital platforms be structured in a reimagined socio-political setting to enable global and local stakeholders to share, deliberate, and understand the diversity of priorities, resources, and constraints and engage in joint agenda-setting? As a corollary, what are the attributes of the GC problems that help define the specific types of tools and platforms as appropriate interventions? Recent research (Malhotra et al. 2021) has highlighted the promise of a socio-technical affordance lens in understanding how collaboration technologies and platforms may help diverse actors overcome knowledge challenges in large-scale collaborations. Affordance denotes “an action potential for a particular context offered by a set of technology features to meet the goals or needs of an individual, a group, or an organization” (Malhotra et al. 2021, p. 1378). In the current context, the affordance lens allows examining how different arrangements of socio (GC decision structures and processes) and technical (digital tools) elements can lead to different action possibilities related to knowledge exchange and deliberation, thereby addressing goal clarity in GCs. Future research may draw on this and related theoretical perspectives (for example, argumentation theory—de Moor and Aakhus 2006 and Iandoli et al. 2007) to identify specific affordances and related technical features that assume salience in the GC goal-setting context. In doing so, it may also be useful to draw on two research streams in management: organization purpose and cooperative behavior in groups. For example, affordances that help participants connect their goals with deeper duty-based purpose (George et al. 2023) may enable global and local entities to find common ground and build shared narratives beyond initiative-specific goals and priorities. Similarly, social identity has been shown to be a critical factor in determining cooperative behavior in large groups (e.g., Tyler and Blader 2013), and affordances that help highlight entity-level identity may allow for greater levels of cooperation in GC initiatives.

## 3.2. Provision of Rewards

The application of different and often-conflicting criteria in evaluating joint value creation can lead to disparities in the sharing of associated rewards. A critical challenge here relates to quantifying socioecological value created, particularly if such value cre ation occurs across global and local levels. A further difficulty is to attribute the public value created to individual entities. Digital approaches that encompass distributed ledger technology (blockchain), marketplace platforms, and artificial intelligence (AI) could help address the two underlying key issues—liquidity and attribution—and allow for more equitable provision of rewards. We discuss two alternative digital approaches—(a) token monetization platforms and (b)

intelligent valuation platforms—with different sociotechnical design implications.

The first approach involves blockchain-based tokenization of value created at different locations by different entities and the provision of a market-based settlement platform for local and regional entities to monetize such tokens. Tokenization is now evident in varied GC contexts, including climate change (carbon credits), sustainable energy production (renewable energy tokens), sustainable cities and communities (sustainable real estate tokens), and sustainable food production (agri-food supply chain tokens). Such tokenization, which involves partitioning into well-defined, small, fungible units, enhances the liquidity of value jointly created. When combined with digital marketplaces, such as Carbonplace or Handprint, it allows for easier allocation of associated rewards among diverse actors. Although token monetization platforms could enhance liquidity and enable faster reward allocation (e.g., Rossi et al. 2019 and Golding et al. 2022), inequities associated with existing power structures among GC participants may still remain and, in turn, raise important questions for future research. For example, who should establish GC token monetization platforms, and how should they be governed? In what ways can regional and local actors exercise agency in defining initiative-specific tokens? How would tokenization translate into GC contexts, such as refugee settlement and women’s empowerment, where quantification of value created is shaped by localized socio-cultural forces?

The second approach involves building intelligence into the valuation process and allowing for contingency payments systems that take into consideration entitylevel (localized) operational risks and constraints, thereby addressing value attribution. For example, AI technologies, when combined with high-resolution data on GC processes and outcomes, may enable incorporating the nature and extent of risks borne by individual entities involved in joint value creation and their specific institutional constraints. Such AI-based valuation could help ensure that rewards are equitable and localized, yet their effectiveness will depend on how consensus is built among GC participants on decisions related to the data to be used and the weightages assigned for the different risk factors. Lack of involvement and agency for local actors in devising such AI-based solutions could render them biased and/or less transparent, reducing trust in the provision of rewards.

More broadly, the two alternative digital design approaches underline a broader research question: what are the socio-political structural elements needed to complement digital tools designed to address liquidity and attribution issues in the provision of rewards in GC projects? In addressing this and related questions, theories and concepts related to incentives and joint value creation may be particularly helpful (Bridoux and Stoelhorst 2022). For example, social exchange theories or economic theories of exchange, utility, or subjective theories of value (e.g., Ga¨chter and Fehr 1999 and Kim et al. 2018) can provide a lens to understand normative value of the public good itself and the processes underlying exchange of such value.

## 3.3. Task Allocation

In GC initiatives, as global targets are translated into local projects, lack of channels for effective participation of all stakeholders in decision making may lead to misallocation of projects. Authority-based assignment and choice self-selection form the main two ways for task allocation (Puranam et al. 2014). In many GC initiatives, individual tasks or projects are assigned to local entities by global agencies through grant awards, where there could be a mismatch between capabilities or resources of local participants and overall objectives. Yet, opportunities for implementing different modes of self-selection mechanisms, which allow for greater levels of such fit and task motivation, are enhanced by digital platforms and technologies. We discuss two alternative digita approaches that cater to differing socio-political structures and levels of agency for local entities in task allocation—(a) quasi self-selection platforms and (b) task bidding and customization platforms.

Modular project architectures, which make visible the underlying fine-grained task structures, enable higher levels of transparency in large-scale GC projects, a key requirement for effective self-selection (Baldwin and Clark 2006, Puranam et al. 2014). Such project architectures, in combination with multiuser digital project management platforms (that bring together communication, big-data analytics, and coordination features), may offer global agencies greater information visibility into local participants’ goals, resources, and constraints, thereby allowing for informed task assignment. Further, based on analysis of historical data, such platforms may also enable global agencies to preclude self-selection to a limited set of local participants in a region, thereby implementing quasi self-selection mode in task allocation. Importantly, these techniques could potentially coexist with current top-down GC project-allocation structures, yet enhance the project fit with local actors priorities and constraints. The second digital approach, which involves online bidding and auctions, would enable greater agency for local actors in choosing projects, but warrant more radical changes in GC sociopolitical structures. For example, the use of e-auction platforms would allow local entities to “bid” for GC projects based on their localized resources and priorities and on the nature of expected socioecological returns. Going one step further, platforms and modular project architectures may also allow local entities to put together different configurations of individual tasks or activities, effectively creating customized GC projects that best fit with their local needs and resources.

These and other promising digital design approaches to task allocation that involve alternate mechanisms for self-selection raise a broader research question: what roles can digital technologies, platforms, and analytics play in giving greater levels of agency and decision-making power to local entities in task allocation in GC initiatives? How should global agencies adapt their structures and processes for grant awards and project management to incorporate such technology-enabled self-selection methods? What criteria should global agencies employ to preselect or invite local actors to GC project bidding? Insights from recent research on auctions in online service outsourcing platforms (e.g., Hong et al. 2016 and Liang et al. 2022) may be valuable in this regard. Future studies may examine the GC project contextual factors and policies that would render such self-selection approaches most effective. Recent research in organization science, specifically on job design and modularity in work processes, may inform such research. For example, it has been shown that, in addition to the benefits related to enhanced motivation and better project fit, self-selection is more effective when the context calls for highly specialized skills, when there is considerable uncertainty over participant availability (timing of participation), when there is less interdependence among tasks, and/or when the opportunities (projects) outnumber the participants (Ketkar and Workiewicz 2022, Raveendran et al. 2022). Future research may build on these and related ideas from distributed work and matching theory (e.g., Mitsuhashi and Greve 2009) to better understand the boundary conditions for digital approaches to task allocation in GC initiatives.

## 3.4. Information Provision and Transparency

Wide disparities in norms and capabilities for the collection, sharing, validation, and aggregation of information, particularly at local levels, form a barrier to monitoring of large-scale GC initiatives. Digital devices and platforms that enable (a) remote collection and (b) automated validation of high-resolution data could play a critical role in addressing the challenges related to information provision and transparency, with broader implications on the accompanying socio-political structures and processes.

First, in recent years, a whole host of low-cost digitally enabled devices—from smart tags and Internet of Things-based sensors to drones and microsatellite constellations—have emerged that allow for the remote collection and transmission of high-resolution data on different socioecological factors of relevance to GC initiatives. The range of these telemetry devices often cross national and regional borders and allow for global-level aggregation of structured data. For example, Saildrone operates a large fleet of wind- and solar-powered drones that collect highly detailed oceanic data, including sea levels, weather, and health of fish stocks. Similarly, radiofrequency identification (RFID)-based smart labels and tags allow for monitoring sustainable practices in the food supply chain (including food waste).

Second, data analytics and blockchain technologies enable more rigorous automated validation of data collected and shared, particularly in places where local norms and work practices inhibit data transparency. For example, spectral fingerprinting, blockchain, and other technologies enhance traceability of food-related data and enable rapid backtracking of food provenance (Montecchi et al. 2019, Misra et al. 2020), thereby ensuring food safety compliance verification. Similarly, machine learning and other AI techniques allow for identifying and isolating questionable data collected from remote locations (Misra et al. 2020). For example, TinyML, when paired with smart sensors, can provide instant validation of data collected remotely on diseases in farm animals (Prakash et al. 2023).

Yet, these technology-based solutions may be in conflict with existing socio-political arrangements for data collection and analysis and raise the broader research question: how can remote and automated data collection, validation, and monitoring systems help bridge gaps in information-provision capabilities and norms between global and local entities in GC initiatives and, at the same time, reduce the information and power asymmetries that exist between them? In what ways can technologies help improve data transparency in lowcost settings in GC initiatives? What policies and guardrails should be implemented to ensure the cooperation of local agencies? How should deployment of boundaryless telemetry devices adapt to regional/national regulatory and security policies and considerations? Beyond these, there are also questions related to quantifying and codify ing complex socioecological systems that are not always amenable to such measurement. Similarly, automated validation may itself be prone to mistakes that reflect the inherent social and cultural biases of the global actors who implement them. How should global entities ensure appropriate mechanisms to correct for such biases? Further, although the eventual goal is compliance checking, decontextualization of data for automated validation may lead global entities to reach conclusions that are not sensitive to dynamic local conditions. Future research may need to bring together ideas and concepts from mul tiple disciplines (e.g., information science, information systems, ecology) to ensure that the capabilities offered by new digital technologies are appropriately used in diverse GC contexts. Theories of information provision, transparency, transactive memory systems, and structural holes provide conceptual grounding to digitally addressing these organizing design problems (e.g., Huvila 2022 and Valdovinos 2022).

## 3.5. Penalties

A key challenge in joint value creation projects is to implement sanctions or penalties for noncompliance, especially if the participants belong to different jurisdictional areas, as is true in many GC initiatives. Two tasks assume significance: application of graduated penalties (i.e., penalties based on the extent of noncompliance) and authentication of data (to enhance the transparency of the sanctioning process). By combining socio (human) and technological (digital) elements to different extents in these tasks, we identify two alternate digital approaches: (a) automated authentication and graduated penalties; and (b) automated authentication and human-imposed graduated penalties.

Blockchain-based smart contracts—traceable, transparent, and irreversible self-executing contracts—can be used to automate the application of penalties for nonperformance in GC initiatives. Such smart contracts are predicated on the availability of (almost) real-time data on relevant socioecological outcomes. Further, in deploying smart contracts, a key enabler is decentralized oracle services (consensus oracles) that verify and authenticate data coming from a wide range of external sources. As discussed previously, with increasing digital codification of socioecological information, automated enforcement of graduated penalties (i.e., penalties based on the extent of noncompliance) become feasible. For example, an initiative launched by Green World is focused on building hybrid smart contracts that use satellite data to automatically dispense rewards and penalties to local communities based on outcome measures related to successful regeneration of designated bodies of land by increasing tree cover, improving soil, and other rehabilitation techniques. However, smart contracts are not devoid of biases, and automated penalty systems could reify existing social and political biases in the GC context, raising important research questions. Who should govern the process of creating the automated contracts, particularly when there is considerable power asymmetry between globa and local entities? What contextual factors shape the choice between code-only and hybrid (text and code) smart contracts in GC initiatives? In what ways can GC initiatives address the potential inability of some participants (nontechnical parties) to understand the smart contract code? How can technologies facilitate graduated penalties and incremental deterrence of nondesired behavior by participants where operations cross national borders? Recent research in IS on blockchain applications and alternative smart contract models (e.g., Rossi et al. 2019 and Qin et al. 2021) may offer a starting point on some of the above issues. The second approach involves bringing together automated data authentication with a human-based graduated penalty decision process, allowing greater transparency and visibility to the sanctioning process, thereby creating a high-trust environment in GC initiatives. In GC initiatives, ensuring such information reliability is critical, as data could originate from different countries/regions, as well as from nonprofit and for-profit entities.

The broader question then is: how should digital and human elements be combined in different GC contexts to achieve a fair and transparent way to execute graduated penalties fo global or local nonperformance? And how should such digital design approaches account for sociocultural differences to enhance perceived distributive and procedural justice? In addressing these and related questions, conceptual frameworks on norm violation and ostracism (Howard et al. 2020); distributive and procedural justice (Luo 2007, Colquitt et al. 2023); and relational governance (Zaheer and Venkataraman 1995) may be informative.

## 3.6. Standards of Conduct

Often, in GC joint value creation initiatives, conflicts related to noncompliance arise that need to be resolved through adjudication, rather than predefined sanctions. Yet, the nature and range of conflict-resolution mechanisms accessible to global and local entities vary significantly, where technologies can play an important role in offering novel conflict-resolution solutions.

Online Dispute Resolution (ODR)—the application of digital technology to the practice of dispute resolution— holds considerable promise as a means to bring together participants from around the world to engage in realtime joint single-text negotiation (Martinez 2020, Rule 2020). Although its origins trace back to dispute resolution in ecommerce marketplaces such as eBay, ODR platforms (e.g., Modria, Smartsettle) are now being deployed in a wide range of noncommercial contexts and are particularly suited to GC initiatives, as they seek to offer an international virtual venue for dispute resolu tion. In recent years, several international and regional organizations, including United Nations Commission on International Trade Law, the EU, and the International Organization for Standardization, have launched initiatives to support ODRs. The role of technology as the “Fourth Partner” in conflict resolution (Katsh and Rifkin 2001)<sup>2</sup> in GC initiatives points to two alternative digital design approaches: (a) informating ODRs and (b) automating ODRs. As we discuss below, these hold different implications for the accompanying socio-political structures and processes.

Diverse technologies, ranging from AI and big data to metaverse, can be applied to enhance the different ODR processes, including diagnosis, negotiation, mediation, evaluation, and appeal (e.g., Alessa 2022 and Watanabe and Rule 2022). Informating ODRs, focused on the front end of the conflict-resolution process, will be aimed at preparing human negotiators by providing them with relevant information on the case. For example, ODRs can search through and identify statutory provisions relevant to a given dispute, enabling human negotiators to rapidly develop a deeper understanding of the context. Similarly, data science techniques allow for verifying and authenticating the data to be used in conflict resolution, thereby hastening the process toward a settlement. The automating ODRs, focused more on the back-end of the resolution process, offer potential “solutions” to disputes. For example, deep learning techniques allow for using data from similar historical cases to develop a nominally optimal solution to the dispute, which could be used as a starting point in negotiation (Cohen et al. 2022). eBay employs such techniques to offer solutions that its customer-service representatives could readily adopt, in effect automating the resolution process (Rule and Schmidtz 2018).

Although the two approaches vary in the extent of human-technology collaboration, both could help address gaps in conflict-resolution capabilities involving global and local entities and raise the broader research question for IS scholars: in what ways would technology’s role as a neutral “fourth partner” impact the dispute-resolution process in GC initiatives? More specifically, how can the different choice architectures of ODR platforms (ranging from informating to automating) impact the tension between the goals of achieving justice/fairness and speed/efficiency in dispute resolution? What are the ethical and moral implications of using “Online Wizards” and other types of ODRs in conflict resolution in GC initiatives (e.g., data privacy and confidentiality; selection of technology platform and self-determinism; one or the other party facing technological disadvantages)? Under what GC initiative contexts would ODRs be preferred—and likely more effective— over existing national/regional dispute resolution mechanisms? How can technologies enhance the visibility of conflict-resolution processes and help enforce guided, ethical decision making? These questions draw from a broad array of theories from negotiation (Bazerman et al. 2000), judgment, and decision making (Tversky and Kahneman 1974), to norms, standard setting, and compliance (Cialdini and Trost 1998).

## 3.7. Autonomy and Task Division

The ability of GC project participants (especially local communities) to exercise autonomy in adapting their policies and practices becomes important in rapidly changing contexts. Recent research (e.g., Hsieh et al. 2018, Lumineau et al. 2021, and Murray et al. 2021) has explored how digital-enabled decentralized networks (distributed ledger technologies) and tokens enable novel forms of governance that involve less centralization and greater local autonomy in both task division and task integration.

The application of these ideas has led to blockchainbased decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) (Wright 2021, Zhao et al. 2022) that encompass structural coordination via machine consensus and governance via social consensus (tokens that grant rights to propose and vote on protocol updates). Although the scope and reach of DAO applications are yet to be determined, the underlying thesis that technology-based decentralized platforms and tokens offer new forms of project governance and fuel collective value creation (Hsieh et al. 2018) does apply to several GC contexts. Illustrative of this potential, experimental DAOs have been constituted in several social impact areas, including climate change, food production, and drug develop ment (e.g., Regen Network, VitaDAO, Big Green DAO). Drawing on the above ideas, we suggest two alterative digital approaches—(a) transparency-focused DAOs and (b) autonomy-focused DAOs—that entail different levels of local autonomy to achieve adaptive governance in GC projects and hold different socio-technical implications.

Transparency-focused DAOs emphasize machine-based automation (via tamper-resistant rules) and transparency in task division and the coordination of participant activi ties, while limiting the governance rights (tokens) to a smal ler set of participants. For example, task division decision making is modularized and distributed; specifically, task division is achieved based on specific capability criteria and automated (via algorithms or machine consensus) in a decentralized manner. Similarly, the underlying distributed ledger technology maintains an immutable record of all activities (transactions) securely and redundantly (across network nodes), allowing for greater task-level transparency in GC projects. Yet, the tokens, which represent the rights to modify the rulebook (the social consensus), may be limited to a few regional and local entities based on some criteria (e.g., expected contribution in the GC project). On the other hand, autonomy-focused DAOs, although maintaining the same level of transparency and coordination mechanisms, may assign such tokens to all project participants, irrespective of their size or resources, thereby providing for greater local autonomy in GC activities.

The above two approaches raise the broader research question: how can human and software (algorithmic) mechanisms be combined in DAOs to allow for greater local autonomy in task division and, at the same time, ensure the alignment of diverse GC actors with shared goals? What should be the criteria for deciding the “voting power” of participants in such DAOs? Or, in what ways should we create different classes of local participants who enjoy different rights in GC project governance? What GC initiative-related criteria should be employed to evaluate the appropriateness of DAOs that offer different levels of autonomy? What processes can be instituted for the gradual evolution of DAO protocols, so as to retain the collective objectives? Conceptual frameworks that might provide useful starting points for these conversations include theories of structure, power, control, and decision making within and across organizations. Further, emergent work on the different aspects of DAOs (e.g., Fan et al. 2023 Goldberg and Scha¨r 2023, and Ellinger et al. 2024) may also be helpful.

3.8. Formalizing and Informalizing Relationships The success of GC initiatives is predicated on being open to adopting novel institutional arrangements (formal and informal), as the requisite capabilities and knowhow are often distributed among diverse private and public/state entities. Digital plug-and-play innovation platforms can help bridge the gap in openness toward such arrangements among global and local actors and contribute to better coordination to achieve GC outcomes. We identify two alternative digital approaches based on the nature and extent of relationships facilitated by such platforms—(a) resource-sharing innovation platforms and (b) collaborative innovation platforms—that call for different socio-technical design elements.

Multisided digital innovation platforms facilitate varied types of interactions and “plug and play” partnerships among diverse entities, thereby enabling exchange of critical resources, as well as collaborative value creation. In the context of GC initiatives, resourcesharing platforms reduce the friction in establishing arms-length relationships and engaging with nontraditional partners (including corporates and advocacy groups) for acquiring critical resources and assets. For example, platforms such as Climate Crowd crowdsource data on climate impacts from communities and local organizations; analyze the data using AI and other technologies; and feed the results back to local communities in ways that could lead to effective solutions. On the other hand, collaborative innovation platforms allow for establishing and maintaining longer-term relationships with varied partners and engaging in joint problem exploration and solving. For example, Rhode Island-based Business Innovation Factory employs such collaborative platforms and digital tools to partner with a diverse array of actors in designing, prototyping, and testing transformative business models for social impact in the areas of healthcare, education, and public services. The modularity and portability of digital assets (e.g., specialized algorithms, simulation tools, prototyping tools) allow for corporates and new ventures to contribute such assets to GC initiatives and participate in both revenue-based and non-revenue-based business models.

Much of the innovation platform literature in IS and strategy has focused on commercial innovation contexts; although many of the underlying theoretical ideas and concepts apply equally well to social impact initiatives, the unique aspects of GC initiatives also imply a broader research question: what are the socio-technical design characteristics of digital innovation platforms that promote more inclusive partnerships and novel formal/informa institutional arrangements in GC initiatives? When and how can innovation platforms help overcome local norms and policies that hinder partnerships with nonstate and market actors in GC initiatives? What types of digital business model innovations would enable leveraging the portability and modifiability of digita assets in collaborative GC initiatives that bring together nonprofits and corporates? How can technologies allow for the participation of disenfranchised actors, especially those in local communities, in GC initiatives (Boast et al. 2007)? Prior studies (e.g., Nambisan 2009 and Temmerman et al. 2021) have shown that digital innovation platforms can serve to address different aspects of the problem-solving process in collaborative social impact initiatives—for example, platforms focused on problem exploration, experimentation, and adoption of solutions. Future research may build on these ideas and examine the orchestration of digital platforms and ecosystems, so as to adapt to handle complexities that arise from the interaction effects among the nature and focus of the GC initiative, the type of participants involved, and the nature of their relationships (e.g., Addo 2022). Similarly, drawing on the ecosystem and business model innovation literature, future research may examine the roles that technologies could play in connecting locally led and globally led GC-focused innovation ecosystems (e.g., Falcke et al. 2023).

## 4. Conclusions

Digital technologies can play an important and transformative role in addressing the organizing design tensions that pervade grand challenge problems and present considerable opportunities for IS scholars to contribute to the broader dialog on GCs. In pursuing the related research agenda, however, a few issues warrant special consideration.

As our discussion illustrates, the effectiveness of the digital approaches will be critically shaped by the challenges and constraints posed by the prevailing sociopolitical order in GC contexts. For example, although the consequences of GCs, such as climate change, affect the world inequitably, with marginalized communities in the Global South bearing the worst, often they have limited voice in shaping the solutions. As such, the implementation of the digital approaches proposed here will necessarily need to incorporate appropriate governance mechanisms to reimagine GC decision structures and processes that ensure more equitable sharing of value from enacted solutions. Further, the concept of joint value creation among global and local actors in GCs will require a shared sense of what value is, for whom, and the relative emphasis on what is valuable. These divergent perspectives among global and local actors require a multidisciplinary approach to how GCs can be addressed. More broadly, IS scholars need to adopt a socio-technical lens in investigating the research questions highlighted here.

The research agenda posed here implies both opportunities and challenges with regard to research methods and access to data. A wide range of research methods, from surveys and comparative case studies to ethnographic fieldwork and design science (action research), have been employed by IS scholars in prior studies of societal challenges (for example, see table 1 in Majchrzak et al. 2016), and similar methodological opportunities exist for examining the digital approaches noted here. At the same time, increasing use of digital sensors, drones, and other low-power autonomous devices in monitoring a wide range of ecological phenomena portend newer possibilities to access and analyze data to evaluate the efficacy of varied digital solutions in addressing GCs. For example, initiatives such as the Ribbit Network seek to offer crowdsourced global data sets on greenhouse-gas emissions that are freely accessible to researchers worldwide. Similar GC-related datacuration initiatives are being launched by other entities (e.g., the Industry Data for Society Partnership). The methodological challenge would be to combine such sensor-based big data with contextual information to develop more nuanced insights on GCs.

In conclusion, we emphasize the promising opportunity for IS scholars to contribute to the broader dialog on GCs by drawing on and integrating existing IS theoretical themes and concepts (e.g., socio-technical affordances, supra-organizational digital governance, socio-technical elements of decentralized digital platforms) with complementary theories from management, social sciences, and beyond. We hope the framework of organizing design tensions and digital approaches that we offer here would fuel a rich and varied stream of studies in IS and in the broader management field that, in turn, would contribute to more effective solutions to grand challenges.
