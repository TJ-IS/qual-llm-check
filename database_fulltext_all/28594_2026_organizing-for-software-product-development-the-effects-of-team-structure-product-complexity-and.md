# Organizing for Software Product Development: The Effects of Team Structure, Product Complexity, and Cross-Team Coordination

Abstract. Despite prior studies on managing the development of software products, it remains unclear which organizational structure is effective in delivering software products to market. In this study, we examine two common approaches to forming software development teams: feature teams that are customer-centric and component teams that are focused on systems architecture. Drawing on the mirroring hypothesis, we propose that feature teams and component teams can mirror the problem space and solution space of the software product, respectively. We investigate which team structure is better suited for building software products with different levels of design space complexity and with varying frequencies of cross-team coordination. Our analysis is enabled by computer simula tion based on the NK fitness landscapes model. The findings highlight the critical role of achieving structural mirroring between the team and the design space. The results also identify specific conditions under which the mirrored team structure is effective, as well as circumstances where mirroring the problem space outperforms mirroring the solution space. We find that mirroring of the problem (solution) space is mostly constrained by the complexity of the problem (solution) space, whereas mirroring of the problem (solution) space is less (more) sensitive to the complexity of the solution (problem) space. We also find that structural mirroring of the problem (solution) space is substituted by frequent coordination and can be enhanced by setting broad focus and responsibility. Interestingly, component teams derive greater benefits from cross-team coordination and maintaining a broad focus because of their sensitivity to complexity in both the problem space and solution space. By integrating the simulation results, we propose a theoretical framework that explains the impact of mirroring different spaces on software development performance.

## 1. Introduction

In today’s fast-paced, hypercompetitive digital landscape, companies strive to deliver high-quality software products quickly to the market. How software teams are organized can profoundly affect software quality and development speed (Cataldo and Herbsleb 2013, <sup>ˇ</sup>Smite et al. 2017, <sup>ˇ</sup>Sa¯blis et al. 2021). The two most widely used structures for software development teams are feature teams and component teams (Larman and Vodde 2008). A feature team implements end-to-end functionality for end users, whereas a component team develops a set of specific technology components needed to implement the customer-centric features. Both team structures have pros and cons. Feature teams provide more flexibility in software development as each team takes ownership of an end-to-end feature and can deliver value to customers faster, but they are more prone to skill redundancy and technical debt (Larman and Vodde 2016, Skelton and Pais 2019). Component teams can ensure architectural integrity and facilitate component reuse, but they may face more coordination challenges and require more extensive cross-team coordination (Larman and Vodde 2016, Fuller and Kruchten 2021). The choice between organizing software development around feature teams or component teams remains under debate among both academic researchers and practitioners (Larman and Vodde 2008, Leffingwell 2010).

The academic research in organizational design and software engineering (Baldwin and Clark 2000, Mac-Cormack et al. 2012, Skelton and Pais 2019, <sup>ˇ</sup>Sa¯blis et al. 2021) offers theoretical prescriptions about how to effectively organize software development that seem to favor component teams. The “mirroring hypothesis” posits that an organizational structure aligned with the structure of technical dependencies of the software product leads to superior development outcomes (Mac-Cormack et al. 2012, Cataldo and Herbsleb 2013, Colfer and Baldwin 2016). The mirroring of organizational and technical structures is supposed to minimize interdependencies among organizational units, thereby reducing the complexity of communication and coordination and ultimately leading to higher-quality outputs and more efficient development. In other words, component teams mirroring the software’s technical interdependencies are expected to outperform feature teams (Skelton and Pais 2019). Paradoxically, although component teams were commonly used in traditional software development (Leffingwell 2010, Masood et al. 2020), component teams have often failed to deliver the system successfully (Standish Group 2001). More recently, the practitioner-oriented literature on agile development and real-world practices tend to favor feature teams over component teams (Larman and Vodde 2008, 2016; Leffingwell 2010; Strode et al. 2022). For example, Spotify, a digital music service company, organizes its software teams called squads and tribes around customercentric end-to-end features for developing large-scale software products (Kniberg and Ivarsson 2012, <sup>ˇ</sup>Smite et al. 2019). The discrepancy between the theoretical prescriptions derived from academic literature on organizational design and the recommendations offered by the agile development literature and practices suggests that our theoretical understanding of optimal team structure is still inadequate, highlighting the need for more robust theorizing on team structure effectiveness (Leite et al. 2021). More specifically, we have yet to fully grasp the conditions under which feature teams and component teams are more or less effective (Kasauli et al. 2021).

Fundamentally, feature and component teams rely on different approaches to abstracting the product into design structures that might be mirrored in organizational structures. Feature teams are organized around the problem space that focuses on business requirements associated with market and customer needs, whereas component teams are organized according to the solution space that relates to technical solutions to satisfy those needs (Czarnecki and Eisenecker 2000). For example, in developing music streaming software, feature teams are organized around specific customer needs, such as listening to personalized recommended music and searching for new music, whereas component teams are responsible for the technical components, such as recommendation engine and streaming service, that support various customer needs. The efficacy of these teams may depend on the complexity of the soft ware product and the frequency of cross-team coordination (e.g., Espinosa et al. 2007a, Hahn and Lee 2021, <sup>ˇ</sup>Sa¯blis et al. 2021). Little is known about the effectiveness of structural mirroring across problem and solution spaces and the boundary conditions under which structural mirroring to either space is more or less effective for software product development. Furthermore, even if an organization chooses the right space to mirror for its team structure, it faces another essential organizational decision regarding the scope of a team’s responsi bilities. That is, the organization may form fewer teams, each with a broader scope of responsibilities, or a greater number of teams, each with a narrower scope.

In this paper, we develop a theoretical model of agile software development based on the NK fitness landscape modeling framework and employ computer simulations to examine the effectiveness of feature and component teams in light of the possibility of mirroring on two different design spaces (i.e., solution and prob lem spaces) to reconcile the discrepancy between the mirroring hypothesis and agile development practice. Informed by prior research (Dikert et al. 2016, Kasauli et al. 2021, <sup>ˇ</sup>Sa¯blis et al. 2021), we investigate how the performance of feature and component teams is affected by the structure and complexity of the problem and solution spaces and synchronization of systems design through cross-team coordination. We also examine the impact of teams’ scope of responsibilities. Our findings offer useful insights for structuring software teams under different technical and organizational conditions.

## 2. Theoretical Background

Software products are inherently complex artifacts because of the large number of interdependencies among their various parts (Brooks 1987, Xia and Lee 2005). The development of software, therefore, requires careful planning and coordination to deal with its inherent complexity (Brooks 1995). The standard approach espoused by classics of problem solving (Alexander 1964, Simon 1981) is to decompose the task into subtasks of manageable size and subsequently integrate them when the subtasks have been completed. These ideas have shaped the foundation for software engineering which prescribes modular decomposition with loose coupling and high cohesion (Parnas 1972).

Modern software products are increasingly more complex with a greater number of features, compo nents, and interdependencies among them. In addition to increased complexity, software organizations must also deal with time-to-market pressures. As a result, modular decomposition alone is insufficient and software organizations must resort to division of labor and develop the software in parallel with multiple teams concurrently working on different parts of the software (Espinosa et al. 2007b). When developing Windows $^ { 7 , }$ for example, Microsoft created 23 teams, each of which had about 40 developers, working on different parts (Schofield 2008). Although software development can proceed in parallel with multiple teams working on different parts, there will inevitably be interdependencies among subparts being worked on by different teams. Consequently, effective coordination among teams is essential to integrate the various parts into a cohesive whole (Malone and Crowston 1994, Espinosa et al. 2007a). The quality and development speed of the software will naturally be affected by how the software is decomposed into subparts, thereby determining the extent of interdependencies among subparts, how the tasks for these subparts are allocated to different teams, and how the software teams coordinate their work.

## 2.1. Team Structure for Software Development

The two canonical approaches to organizing software development are feature teams and component teams (Larman and Vodde 2008). A software feature, from a customer-centric perspective, is defined as a logical unit of behavior specified by a set of functional and nonfunctional requirements (Bosch 2000, Apel and Ka¨stner 2009). For example, in music streaming software like Spotify, software features may include search, personalized radio, offline mode, and social sharing. Each software feature is built upon a set of technical components in various software layers, such as back-end, middletier, and front-end, and the implementation of a feature may require changes across multiple components. A component is an independent executable unit of software that can be assembled into a functioning system (Meyer 2003, Szyperski 2011). A component by itself rarely provides intrinsic value to the user. Instead, it should interact with other software components to deliver value to the user. Components like recommendation engine, metadata service, streaming service, and audio player may interact closely to deliver value to users through the personalized radio feature. Whereas feature teams can deliver valuable and fully integrated “vertical” software functionalities to end users, component teams are focused on developing specific software layers or modules inside these layers.

From a task decomposition perspective, the main difference between feature and component teams is how the work is divided: vertically versus horizontally. Decomposing development tasks by feature teams is analogous to cutting a layer cake vertically, where each slice represents a customer-centric feature and has multiple layers of technological components that implement the feature. On the other hand, decomposing tasks by component teams is similar to cutting the cake horizontally, where each slice represents a system component that supports multiple customer-centric features. Vertical decomposition implies a value-oriented breakdown of the software product where each feature includes technical development tasks from different layers or modules. Horizontal decomposition divides a problem into technical layers or module-based development tasks. In developing a music streaming software program, a feature team responsible for developing the personalized radio feature may work across multiple components, such as the metadata service and recommendation engine, to deliver the complete functionality. In contrast, a component team responsible for the metadata service may support several features, including search, personalized radio, and social sharing, by providing customized metadata across the software. Figure 1 illustrates the difference between feature and component teams in their approach to task decomposition. The items under the product owner represent the customer-centric features that comprise the product backlog. Feature teams complete all development tasks connected to each feature. Because each feature team develops components that only cater to the needs for implementing their feature, they need to synchronize their work with other feature teams to ensure architectural cohesion of the overall soft ware. By contrast, component teams complete development tasks focusing on their respective components, and there is a need to integrate the implemented parts to deliver value to the end user (Larman and Vodde 2016). Therefore, component teams require more frequent crossteam communication and coordination compared with feature teams (Kiani et al. 2013, Bjarnason et al. 2022).

## 2.2. Structural Mirroring

Prior research emphasizes the importance of the fit between the structure of the design space of a problem and the structure of the organization that is tasked to solve that problem (Galbraith 1973, Sanchez and Mahoney 1996, Baldwin and Clark 2000, MacCormack et al. 2012, Querbes and Frenken 2018). Specifically, the structure of the design space captures dependencies both among the business requirements and among technical components that implement them, whereas the organizational structure reflects the division of labor that determines how tasks in the design space are allocated to individuals or teams (Colfer and Baldwin 2016).

The “mirroring hypothesis” posits that mirrored organizational configurations will attain superior development performance. It offers managers and system architects prescriptions on how to change the structure of the organization to align with systems design through improved coordination. The more faithfully the organizational structure mirrors the technical structure of the software product, the less coordination across organizational subunits (i.e., teams) is needed, which implies that the organization will be less likely to face knowledge and communication traps.<sup>1</sup> Sosa et al. (2007) show that organizations that match their engineers interactions with the technical interfaces of their engineering designs avoid painful miscommunications. Empirical and theoretical investigations of the mirroring hypothesis have been conducted under a variety of settings (e.g., Sanchez and Mahoney 1996, Sosa et al. 2004, MacCormack et al. 2012, Querbes and Frenken 2018).

Researchers also argue that building organizational ties upon technical ones is beneficial only for specific types of products and can be ineffective or even harmful in other contexts. With greater complexity, “mirroring” may become less effective (Furlan et al. 2014). Greater product complexity inevitably introduces a greater number of interdependencies, and some of these interdependencies may reside on work responsibilities that cut across teams. For example, Burton and Galvin (2018) argue that mirroring is effective for closed and modular products with simple architectures and stable component technologies. Tee (2019) supports this idea, providing examples of innovations (e.g., the Segway mobility device), which have a more integrated architecture and an intrafirm development focus. Querbes and Frenken (2018) test the mirroring hypothesis via a computer simulation model. They show that the hypothesis holds for products with a small amount of interdependencies between components and for systems with a coreperiphery architecture, but it does not hold for more complex systems with a higher level of coupling. Organizations attempting to enforce modular structures in response to new technical architectures are prone to failure because they may not be aware of the extensive latent technical interdependencies inherent in new technical architectures. Such premature modularization limits opportunities for architectural innovation (Colfer and Baldwin 2016). Thus, organizations may strategically choose partial mirroring or “break the mirror” by setting broader knowledge boundaries and combining integrated and modular structures to stimulate knowledge sharing and achieve better design performance (Colfer and Baldwin 2016, Tee 2019).

Software development is a knowledge-intensive task that requires using multiple abstract spaces by architects and system designers (Kim and Lerch 1997). Software engineers combine and integrate abstractions from the application and technology domains to design and implement a solution (Khatri et al. 2006, Kang et al. 2017). Czarnecki and Eisenecker (2000) propose that there are two primary spaces in software product development: the problem space and the solution space. Whereas the problem space includes domain-specific abstractions or user requirements, the solution space contains implementation-oriented abstractions or system components (Apel and Ka¨stner 2009). The problem space can be conceptualized as a representation of the needs of the system’s users, and the solution space is a representation of the system that is built to satisfy those needs. In our running examples, a customer’s needs, such as listening to personalized recommended music, searching for new music, or listening to music offline, and the interdependencies among these needs lie in the problem space, whereas the components that make up a personalized radio and the interdependencies among those components reside within the solution space.

Taking into consideration the presence of two distinct spaces, the idea of mirroring becomes more nuanced. Prior research has examined the mirroring hypothesis primarily in the context of the solution space, where organizational units are built around technical components (Baldwin and Clark 2000, MacCormack et al. 2012). For simple products, mirroring of the solution space leads to superior performance because the architecture of the system is quite stable and predictable. However, in software product development, developers simply cannot foresee how components should be implemented to satisfy various customer needs, of which some are inevitably interdependent (Cusumano and Selby 1997). That is why building organization structures upon technical constructs may not be effective for tightly coupled complex products (Bick et al. 2017), and mirroring of the problem space rather than of the solution space may be more effective. In this study, we argue that mirroring either the problem space or the solution space does not inherently guarantee better outcomes. Instead, mirroring the appropriate space is crucial for achieving superior performance. In essence, feature teams are designed to capture domain-specific abstractions in the problem space, whereas component teams work with the implementation constructs from the solution space.

## 2.3. Cross-Team Coordination

In software product development, the organization is typically split into smaller teams. Software teams cannot operate in isolation, making frequent communication and coordination essential (Rahy and Bass 2019). Dingsøyr et al. (2018) highlight the importance of crossteam communication and coordination in coping with technical interdependencies across software team boundaries and argue that there is a need to balance interteam cooperation and self-management. <sup>ˇ</sup>Sa¯blis et al. (2021) analyze the patterns of external coordination of feature and component teams and show that the fulfillment of their external communication needs improves development performance. Lee et al. (2013) find that effective coordination of globally distributed teams in highly dynamic environments leads to software development success.

Cross-team coordination is challenging because of the complex interdependencies between software modules, which are often unforeseen (Crowston et al. 2016). Bick et al. (2017) find that cross-team coordination faces severe issues because of a lack of awareness of dependencies. In their literature review of 34 papers studying distributed software development, da Silva et al. (2010) conclude that team coordination is the most important challenge in software development. Importantly, crossteam coordination is more costly and slower than intrateam coordination because of various team boundaries such as geographic distance, time zone, and organizational culture (Espinosa et al. 2006). Therefore, software teams should minimize the need for cross-team coordination while maximizing intrateam coordination (Avritzer et al. 2010, Skelton and Pais 2019).

Cross-team coordination interacts with structural mirroring to influence software development. The mirroring hypothesis is fundamentally about adopting an organizational structure—via task decomposition and division of labor—that “mirrors” the product design structure to enhance development performance. The mechanism behind the performance benefit of mirroring is that structural mirroring minimizes the need for coordination across teams by assigning teams to work with interdependencies within each team’s scope of responsibility while minimizing the interdependencies across teams’ scopes of responsibility. If this is achievable, the work completed by each team can effectively be combined into a cohesive whole. However, as the software product becomes increasingly complex, mirroring may become less effective (Furlan et al. 2014, Burton and Galvin 2018), inevitably leaving a greater number of interdependencies—some of which may span work responsibilities that cut across teams. In other words, the likelihood of cross-team interdependencies will increase and as a result will put a greater burden on effective crossteam coordination. Different levels and structures of product complexity and organizational decisions relating to teams’ scope of responsibilities are therefore important contextual factors that may establish the boundary conditions of the mirroring hypothesis under varying levels of cross-team coordination.

## 3. Conceptual Development

Our conceptual development unfolds in three steps. First, we revisit the mirroring hypothesis within the context of software product development. Second, we investigate the effectiveness of structural mirroring across different levels of software design space complexity and cross-team coordination. Third, we explore partial mirroring by examining how the scope of a team’s responsibilities—whether overfitting or underfitting— affects team performance.

## 3.1. The Mirroring Hypothesis in Software Product Development

While developing a software product, organizations are focused on the exploration of the problem space more than of the solution space, because accurately identify ing customer needs is crucial for the software product’s performance (Cusumano and Selby 1998, Cusumano 2010). The agile development literature suggests that feature teams tend to be favored over component teams in the context of software product development, as they are better designed to deal with abstractions in the problem space. This arises from the idea that feature teams are focused on the development of stand-alone end-toend functionalities to meet customer needs. They have full control over the design elements related to a specific feature and do not need to synchronize their work with other teams so frequently. In contrast, component teams work on modules that serve different features of the product and should constantly synchronize their work to reflect the interdependencies between modules, which can lead to frequent needs for coordination and communication among teams.

## 3.2. Complexity and Cross-Team Coordination

The efficacy of structural mirroring may depend on certain factors. Little is known about the boundary conditions for the effectiveness of structural mirroring. Perfectly modularized structures of the problem or solution space (as shown in Figure 2) are the exception rather than the norm in practice. In more realistic cases of new software product development, there are inevitably multiple interdependencies between components and features. The more interdependencies each of the design elements has with one another, the more complex the software product to be developed is (Xia and Lee 2005). With more complex software products, there is inherently a greater likelihood that interdependencies span across the scopes of responsibilities of different component and feature teams. In other words, a component (feature) team may need to work on configuring design elements whose value may depend on the configuration of a design element under the responsibility of another component (feature) team. Given that teams work independently in parallel, it is necessary to coordinate their efforts (Cataldo and Herbsleb 2013, Dikert et al. 2016, Knauss 2019).

The Scrum methodology has a practice called “scrum of scrums,” a synchronization meeting where one member from each team meets to discuss dependencies on a regular basis (Larman and Vodde 2016). Spotify imple ments similar synchronization meetings on demand, where it identifies and resolves dependencies between squads and keeps track of unresolved dependencies (Kniberg and Ivarsson 2012). 3.3. Partial Mirroring: Overfitting vs. Underfitting Even after an organization chooses the appropriate structure between feature teams and component teams, it faces another essential organizational decision regarding the scope of the teams’ responsibilities. The organization may form either a small number of teams, each with greater responsibilities, or a large number with narrower responsibilities for each team. Skelton and Pais (2019) highlight the importance of setting correct responsibility boundaries for software teams by minimizing unnecessary cross-team dependencies but at the same time avoiding excessive cognitive load. Subramanyam et al. (2011) show that the granularity of software components affects development efficiency and flexibility. Scholars of the mirroring hypothesis also state that for complex environments, there is a need to achieve partial mirroring when a product architecture involves both modular and integrated organizational architectures (Tee 2019). In addition to cross-team coordination that resolves interdependencies across boundaries, another approach is to set knowledge boundaries wider than needed for the current task. In software product development, overfitting refers to setting knowledge borders very tightly around specific parts of the problem $( \mathrm { e . g . , a }$ feature team works only on a narrow set of features connected to a very few use cases; a component team works only on a specific technical component). Conversely, underfitting implies looser decomposition, so a single feature (component) team is responsible for more features (components) and can deal with the interdependencies across features (components) within the team, reducing the need for cross-team coordination.

## 4. Research Method

## 4.1. NK Fitness Landscapes Model

We use a computer simulation model to trace the complex dynamics of the phenomenon of interest. This approach overcomes the limitations of field studies, such as the inability to run isolated experiments in the same problem (solution) space while controlling for parameters. Specifically, we use the NK fitness landscapes model, which is a simulation framework designed to study the adaptive behavior of agents in design problem solving (Kauffman 1993). Software development is fundamentally a design problemsolving process, where teams iteratively identify gaps between the current and desired states of the software, evaluate alternative designs, and refine the software to deliver value to customers (Cerveny et al. 1990). Achieving an optimal software design typically requires multiple cycles of refinement because of the complexity and uncertainty inherent in the process (Larman and Basili 2003). The NK fitness landscapes model enables us to capture this iterative and incremental process by simulating agents’ search process for a technical solution to a business problem. Moreover, the NK fitness landscapes model is particularly well-suited for studying phenomena where a complex problem is decomposed into subproblems, and the task of searching for optimal solutions is distributed among multiple searchers (Baumann et al. 2018). It effectively captures the interdependencies among tasks allocated to different searchers, allowing for an in-depth examination of how the division of labor and task coordination influence the overall problem-solving process. Because of its suitability, the NK model has recently been applied for building theory in software development (e.g., Hahn and Lee 2021).

The fitness landscape represents a design space, which is characterized by N design decisions and K interdependencies between them. All locations on the landscape are represented by an N-element vector d, which represents a design configuration $\mathbf { d } = \langle d _ { 1 } , d _ { 2 } , \ldots , d _ { N } \rangle$ , where $d _ { i }$ can be either zero or one. Every configuration is associated with a fitness value, and the set of all such pairs forms the fitness landscape. Each design decision $d _ { i }$ makes a contribution $c _ { i }$ to the overall fitness of $\mathbf { d } , F ( \mathbf { d } )$ , where the value contribution $c _ { i }$ depends not only on the configuration of decision $d _ { i } ,$ but also on the configurations of K other decisions $d _ { k }$ that influence $d _ { i }$ , that is, $c _ { i } = c _ { i } ( d _ { i } \vert K$ other $d _ { k } { } ^ { \prime } { \mathbf { s } } )$ The overall fitness value $F ( \mathbf { d } )$ is calculated by averaging fitness contributions over the N design decisions (i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { F ( \mathbf { d } ) = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } c _ { i } / N ) } \end{array}$

The shape of the fitness landscape, and consequently the complexity of the search process, is determined by the parameter K. With low K (i.e., few interdependencies between decisions), there are only a few local optima (i.e., design configurations that perform well within a narrow scope but are not globally optimal when considering all possible design configurations), making the fitness landscape “smooth.” Conversely, with high K (i.e., a large number of interdependencies between decisions), the landscape becomes “rugged” with many more local optima. The aim of computational agents is to find the best configuration of the decision string by changing the decisions in vector d. As K increases, however, it becomes less likely that a positive change in a focal decision $d _ { i }$ will improve the overall fitness value $F ( \mathbf { d } )$ ). Even if the change increases its payoff $c _ { i } ,$ it may reduce the fitness contributions of the other K interdependent decisions $d _ { k } ,$ , especially when those decisions are already well configured. As a result, it is harder for an agent to reach the maximum fitness value (i.e., find the best possible configuration) on the landscape. The search process becomes more complicated as incremental search strategies are likely to lead the agent to get stuck at a local optimum. In the NK simulation model, researchers can vary three basic parameters: $K ,$ which controls the ruggedness of the landscape; the behavior of the agents; and the influence matrix (INF). The influence matrix reflects the interdependencies between decisions and thus defines the $D S { \hat { M } } . ^ { 4 }$ In this study, we extend the classical NK model to allow for several decision-making units that can make adaptive changes to different parts of the design decision string.

## 4.2. An Extended NK Model

Although the mirroring hypothesis provides a valuable theoretical framework for interpreting the choice between feature and component teams, it requires significant extensions to account for the nuances in the mirroring of problem and solution spaces, software product complexity, cross-team coordination, and scope of responsibilities to identify the conditions under which feature and component teams are more or less effective. To facilitate our theory development, we extend the classical NK model as follows.

The software product consists of M features that deliver value to customers. Each feature can be characterized by a string consisting of N elements representing the design elements for a prespecified number of components (nComp) of the software product.<sup>5</sup> For simplicity, we assume each component contains an equal number $( \mathrm { i . e . , } ( N / n C o m p ) )$ ) of design elements. A predefined interdependency matrix $I N F _ { C }$ reflects the interde pendencies between design elements of different components in accordance with the product’s solution space. For instance, Figure $2 ( \mathrm { a } )$ shows that the value of each design element depends on another design element (in addition to being dependent on its own configuration). Each feature would contribute a value equal to the fitness value of the N-element string F(d) to the overall value of the software. In our model, fitness value reflects software development performance, specifically representing users’ perceived value of software features. Although different features are built upon the same set of components, the ways these components are utilized may vary according to the specific requirements of each feature. This can be modeled as the M features sharing the same interdependency matrix of design ele ments but exhibiting different fitness contribution values. Specifically, the value contribution of design element $d _ { i }$ for the $j { \cdot } \mathrm { t h }$ feature, $c _ { i j } ,$ is randomly drawn based on the same interdependency matrix of design elements $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , I N F _ { C } )$ . The value of the software is largely determined by the extent of meeting all the user requirements. As such, the overall value of the software is calculated by averaging fitness values over the M features $\begin{array} { r } { ( F ( \mathbf { d } ) = \dot { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } } \sum _ { j } ^ { M } c _ { i j } / ( N \times M ) ) . ^ { 6 } } \end{array}$

As discussed earlier, different features may be interdependent with each other in delivering value to customers. To capture the interdependencies among features, we modify the fitness contribution $c _ { i j }$ of $d _ { i j }$ (i.e., design element $d _ { i }$ for the j-th feature), such that $c _ { i j }$ depends not only on how the i-th element and K other elements in the focal feature are configured, but also C other design elements from other features (Ganco and Agarwal 2009). A separately predefined interdependency matrix $I N F _ { F }$ reflects the interdependencies between features in accordance with the product’s problem space. For instance, Figure 2(b) shows that each design element in the first feature is interdependent with another design element in the second feature. Taken together, we can model the structures of the problem and solution spaces by specifying the pattern and levels of complexity of $I N F _ { C }$ and $I N F _ { F } ,$ , respectively.

We model two types of team structures: feature and component teams. Both types exhibit the same behaviors, such as solution search strategy and coordination processes. The only distinction between the two types is how they decompose the task, either vertically or horizontally. Feature teams decompose it into sets of features equally according to the number of feature teams (nTeams) and the number of features assigned to each team (i.e., (M=nTeams)). Component teams split all the strings of design elements into equal-length substrings that represent components (nComp). For example, suppose a software product has nine features $( \mathrm { i . e . , } M = 9 )$ with nine design elements in each feature $( \mathrm { i . e . , } N = 9 )$ . If three feature teams are responsible for the software development, the feature team setup would decompose it into three sets of features—for example, feature team 1 would work on features $M = \{ 1 , 2 , 3 \}$ , feature team 2 on features $M = \{ 4 , 5 , 6 \}$ , and finally feature team 3 on features $M = \{ 7 , 8 , 9 \}$ . Each set of features includes three features, of which each consists of all $N = 9$ design elements. On the other hand, a component team structure would decompose it into three component teams working on three sets of design elements—for example, component team 1 would work on design elements $\left\{ d _ { 1 } , d _ { 2 } , d _ { 3 } \right\}$ , and so on and so forth. Each component team is responsible for configuring the design elements across all $M = 9$ features.

The search behavior of the development teams is organized as a series of incremental iterations. At each simulated time tick, each feature (component) team randomly changes one design element within its scope of responsibility and checks whether changing it improves the overall fitness value of the software product.<sup>7</sup> The change is accepted by the team only if it increases the overall performance.<sup>8</sup> Importantly, teams make changes independently and do not know about the changes made by the other teams until cross-team synchronization occurs. Prior to synchronization, teams are only aware of the past configurations of the other teams design elements and use them to assess the fitness of their design configurations. During the synchronization process, feature (component) teams share their current configurations of design elements for different features (components) with each other and accept the configura tions as a new synchronized configuration.<sup>9</sup> In our model, synchronization frequency (SF) represents the extent of cross-team coordination. In addition to measuring team performance levels, we assess the speed at which teams achieve high performance, which, in real-world scenarios, translates to satisfying users’ needs more quickly.

## 4.3. Model Validation

An important step in simulation-based research is model validation (Davis et al. 2007). Because of the high level of abstraction of computational simulation models, their validity is assessed mainly in terms of content (face) validity and construct validity (Burton and Obel 1995, Taber and Timpone 1996). Content validity focuses on whether the model makes sense to domain experts. Construct validity concerns whether the computational model’s outcomes are consistent with the outcomes expected in the real world and the findings from prior research.

Prior literature has provided ample support for the content validity of our computational simulation model of agile software development. Agile software develop ment is iterative and incremental because the team rarely finds the final system design in a single iteration (Larman and Basili 2003, Cockburn 2007, Lee and Xia 2010). The design elements in software development are interdependent as the value of a certain design element may depend on other design elements (MacCormack 2001; Xia and Lee 2004, 2005; MacCormack et al. 2006). Hahn and Lee (2021) conducted field interviews with 16 software development managers to validate their NK model of software development. The practitioners confirmed that software development is an incremental and iterative search process and that interdependencies among design elements are what make software devel opment challenging.

To ensure the construct validity of our model, we produced baseline simulation results. We validate our extension of the NK model, namely, the coexistence of the problem and solution spaces, by setting the complexity of one space (i.e., K or C) to zero. We find that our NK model degrades to well-established models and the results are consistent with prior research findings and what is observed in the real world. Specifically, the results show that for a perfectly modular software product, a mirrored team structure increases software product development performance in terms of its business value and development speed, whereas over- and underfitting can lead to poor performance (Ethiraj and Levinthal 2004). The results also suggest that a higher degree of software product complexity in terms of design element interdependence leads to longer development time and lower business value of the software product (Rivkin and Siggelkow 2007, Hahn and Lee 2021). Therefore, we conclude that our NK model demonstrates sound content validity and construct validity and the results based on the model can offer valid theoretical insights.

## 4.4. Experimental Design

The basic setup for all experiments is as follows. The number of features and the number of design elements remain constant $( N = 8 , M = 8 )$ , whereas K and C vary from one (i.e., the minimal complexity settings according to Figure 2) to seven (i.e., the maximal possible complexity settings for $N = 8$ and $M = 8 )$ ).<sup>10</sup> The selection of M and N is influenced by three major considerations: (1) the design space consisting of eight features and eight design elements produces $2 ^ { 6 4 }$ distinct configurations, which is, in practical terms, an almost infinite number of design options; (2) the design elements and features can be divided into parts (modules) in different ways, which gives us flexibility in terms of studying effects of the number of modules (teams) and module size (team size) on software performance; (3) feature teams and component teams can consider and change the same number of design elements within their scope of responsibility in a simulated time tick such that the only difference between them is how they decompose the task. To ensure that the simulation results reflect the underlying structure of the model rather than a particular realization of a stochastic process, for every experimental condition, we randomly generate 100 fitness landscapes according to the specified complexity levels (K and C), with 100 feature teams and 100 component teams seeded onto random initial locations on each landscape.<sup>11</sup>

To validate the mirroring hypothesis in the context of software product development, we run two experiments with block-diagonal (see Figure 3, (a) and (d)) and rearranged block-diagonal interdependency matrices (see Figure 3, (b) and (e)) to investigate how the decomposition of tasks (i.e., mirrored versus unmirrored) affects the performance of different team structures (i.e., feature versus component teams). To investigate the effectiveness of structural mirroring across different levels of software design space complexity and cross-team coordination, we run a series of experiments using interdependency matrices $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Figure 4) with varying levels of K and $C ,$ and also with varying synchronization frequency (SF). A smaller value of $\dot { S } F$ indicates more frequent synchronization. For example, $S F = 1$ if teams synchronize at every simulated time tick and $S F = 5 \mathrm { i f }$ synchronization occurs after every five time ticks. Specifically, we vary the frequency of synchronization across $S F \overset { \cdot } { = } \{ 1 , 5 , 2 5 , 5 0 , 1 0 0 \}$ , representing real-time, daily, weekly, biweekly, and monthly synchronization, respectively.<sup>12</sup> For exploring partial mirroring with different scopes of teams’ responsibilities, we conduct two experiments with different parameters of nTeams (nComp): (1) four teams controlling two design elements (features) each (i.e., overfitted; see Figure 3, (a) and (d)); (2) two teams controlling four design elements (features) each (i.e., underfitted; see Figure 3, (c) and (f)). If we suppose that a team makes around five design changes per day (i.e., five time ticks represent a working day, and $S F = 5$ represents daily synchronization), a simulation run of $1 { , } 3 0 0$ time ticks is equivalent to 260 working days or approximately one year. ## 5.4. Robustness Checks

We conducted various robustness checks to ensure that our results hold under alternative assumptions. First, we varied the number of features, components, and teams and found that our results remain consistent across alternative values of these decision variables (see Online Appendix C.1). Second, it is possible that teams would uncover unforeseen issues requiring additional work and task reallocation in the software development process. We relaxed the assumption that software teams work on a fixed set of features by allowing them to endogenously discover new features (see Online Appendix C.2). Third, we considered the possibility of component reuse in software development (see Online Appendix C.3). Fourth, it is possible for a feature team to be organized into several component teams, each responsible for a specific technical component. Likewise, a component team can be organized into several feature teams, each working on a set of features. We accounted for this possibility in Online Appendix C.4. We find that our conclusions remain qualitatively unchanged across these variations.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions 6.1. Findings and Insights

Software product development is influenced by the inherent interdependencies across various software parts, as well as how the development tasks are decomposed into software teams to manage these interdependencies (Xia and Lee 2005, Espinosa et al. 2007a). The mirroring hypothesis presents a strategic approach to minimizing task interdependencies across teams by arranging the organization structure in accordance with the structure of the product to achieve superior performance. However, organizations may opt for different approaches to abstracting the product into design structures that need to be mirrored into team structures.

We propose that organizations can mirror the software product based on two spaces: a problem space that reflects domain-specific abstractions or user requirements and a solution space that contains implementationoriented abstractions or system components. These spaces are tasked to feature and component teams for exploration, respectively. Our results (F1) validate the mirroring hypothesis in the software product development context by showing that mirroring the correct structure of the design space leads to better performance at a faster rate (Cataldo and Herbsleb 2013, Colfer and Baldwin 2016). The simulation results also offer additional insights. We observe that structural mirroring of the problem space achieves high performance faster than mirroring of the solution space. This suggests that mirrored organization structures may perform differently depending on how the product is abstracted into design structures.

In investigating the impact of software complexity on structural mirroring, we observe results consistent with prior research on feature and component teams (Leffingwell 2010, Kasauli et al. 2021, Ågren et al. 2022). However, our simulation results offer additiona explanatory depth by elucidating the origins of these interdependencies and the extent to which feature and component teams can manage them effectively. Whereas feature (component) teams primarily face the complexity of the problem (solution) space, feature teams are less sensitive to the complexity of the solution space than component teams are to the complexity of the problem space (F2). Moreover, the simulation results also suggest a potential misalignment in how software complexity impacts development performance between the early and late stages (F3). Interdependencies across teams, arising from the complexity of both the problem and solution spaces, make each team’s software part highly interdependent with others, which slows the pace of performance improvement. However, these interdependencies also compel teams to explore alternative software configurations in response to each other’s modifications, helping them achieve higher ultimate performance. As such, for a given software development task, although component teams face the complex ity of both the problem and solution spaces, they may achieve higher performance than feature teams in the later stages of development.

Cross-team coordination (i.e., via synchronization) helps software teams to overcome the interdependencies between each other (Dikert et al. 2016, Bick et al. 2017, Fuller and Kruchten 2021). Our simulation results support the value of cross-team coordination by showing the substitutive relationship between synchronization and structural mirroring (F3). As cross-team coordination increases, the performance advantage of structural mirroring diminishes. With continuous synchronization, there is no difference in performance between feature and component teams. In this case, teams function as one integrated unit, and unresolved interdependencies across teams can be mitigated by fre quent synchronization and coordination. The simulation results offer novel insights by highlighting how the value of synchronization varies with different approaches to structural mirroring. The value of synchronization among feature teams is mostly affected by the complexity of the problem space, whereas the value of synchronization among component teams is impacted by both the complexity of the solution space and the complexity of the problem space. The higher the complexity involved in the software development process for feature and component teams, the more critical cross-team coordination becomes.

The substitutive relationship also influences the potential misalignment between software performance in the early and late stages of development. Frequent synchronization is positively associated with the performance improvement speed of software development. However, with more frequent synchronization, teams operate as a cohesive unit, leaving little room for each team to independently make larger design moves, thus potentially hindering broader exploration of alternative software configurations. In contrast, without synchroni zation, each team’s independent modifications cannot trigger changes in other teams’ respective software parts. As a result, when feature teams and component teams engage in synchronization at a moderate frequency, they may attain higher final performance than those with high synchronization frequency (F3), particularly when there remains a modest level of cross-team interdependencies (e.g., when the complexity of the problem space is moderate or high for feature teams and when the complexity of either the problem space or the solution space is moderate or high for component teams). These results suggest the existence of a potential pitfall in coordination in software development, as indicated by prior research on organizational coordination (Knudsen and Srikanth 2014). Increasing coordination may compel agents to abandon their distinct search trajectories in exchange for a shared yet lower denominator, leading to a decline in performance.

The simulation results on overfitting and underfitting reveal a trade-off between having more specialized teams working on the same software development tasks and setting a broader scope of responsibilities to enhance structural mirroring. For developing simple software products, highly specialized teams develop well-performing products faster (F4). However, the development of more complex systems requires teams with a broader scope of responsibility to resolve interdependencies, especially when frequent cross-team coordination is not feasible (F4). With more frequent synchronization, the advantage of a broader focus in capturing interdependencies diminishes, whereas the disadvantages associated with having fewer teams working simultaneously become more pronounced. Furthermore, the results suggest that the shift from an overfitted setup to an underfitted one is more critical for component teams as they face the complexity of both problem and solution spaces. These findings support the effectiveness of partial mirroring in certain circumstances (Colfer and Baldwin 2016) but also provide a more nuanced understanding of the impact of partial mirroring on performance improvement speed and final performance under varying levels of problem space complexity, solution space complexity, and crossteam coordination. The findings suggest that it is not always advantageous to set broader knowledge boundaries for teams unless these boundary conditions are carefully considered.

## 6.2. Theoretical and Practical Contributions

This study makes several theoretical and practical contributions. Firstly, it contributes to the mirroring hypothesis literature. Scholars have a longstanding tradition of examining the effectiveness of mirroring from a normative perspective (e.g., Colfer and Baldwin 2016, Querbes and Frenken 2018). This study adds to this stream of research by proposing that, in addition to comparing mirrored and unmirrored structures, it is crucial to consider different approaches to abstracting the product into design spaces: problem and solution spaces. This perspective allows us to reconcile the inconsistency between the mirroring hypothesis favoring component teams and modern agile practices advocating for feature teams. We have shown that if the mirroring of the solution space proves ineffective, the approach to decomposing tasks may shift from horizontal to vertical. In such cases, software teams can and should be built around the structure of the problem space. The distinction between the problem and solution spaces also underscores the presence of problem complexity and solution complexity, both of which moderate the mirroring hypothesis. Organizations that mirror one space may face challenges if the complexity of the other becomes the bottleneck. Relatedly, we also contribute to this stream of literature by showing the boundary condition of “partial mirroring.” The previ ous research has proposed that with technologically complex products, firms can (and should) strategically break the mirror by drawing boundaries more broadly (i.e., underfitting) or by building relational contracts to support interdependencies across boundaries (Furlan et al. 2014, Colfer and Baldwin 2016, Burton and Galvin 2018). Our findings suggest that these two approaches— underfitting and coordination—are substitutive. The underfitting approach may be less advantageous when teams synchronize more frequently. Moreover, we do not always need to break the mirror but rather change what it reflects. It is possible to increase performance by reconceptualizing the mirror—that is, from a component (feature) team-based mirroring to a feature (component) team-based mirroring even at the same level of mirror fitting.

Second, this study contributes to the research on agile software development and, more specifically, on feature and component teams. Prior work has stated that feature teams should perform better than component teams in large-scale agile software development (Lar man and Vodde 2016, Skelton and Pais 2019) but also recognized the persistent use of component teams in practice (Leffingwell 2010, Masood et al. 2020). This study adds to the literature by identifying boundary conditions that influence the relative effectiveness of feature and component teams, offering a more nuanced understanding of when each team structure may be most beneficial. We demonstrate that the relative performance of feature teams compared with component teams is contingent upon factors such as problem space complexity, solution space complexity, and effective ness of coordination. In addition, this study also contributes to the research on cross-team coordination. Previous case studies on large-scale software develop ment have emphasized the increasing importance of cross-team coordination as the complexity of the task rises (e.g., Dikert et al. 2016, Fuller and Kruchten 2021). This study adds to this literature by revealing the substitutive relationship between synchronization and structural mirroring on software development performance and the potential misalignment in the benefits of coordination between the early and late stages. Finally, we provide a method to examine the mirroring hypothesis using the NK fitness landscapes model. Whereas prior research focused on how the correspondence between two high-level networks (technical and organizational) affects organizational performance (Querbes and Frenken 2018), we extend the NK fitness landscapes model to study specific real-world organizational forms (i.e., feature versus component teams) used in industry practice and examine how they cope with different structures of software products in the light of the mirroring hypothesis.

Based on our findings, we offer the following managerial recommendations for practitioners. First, our results support a contingency perspective on choosing between feature and component teams. For simple products, feature teams are more effective and can deliver high-quality software systems faster (F1). However, when selecting a team structure, organizations should consider both problem and solution complexity because a mismatch can lower delivery speed and product quality. Organizations should prefer cross-functional feature teams if organizations face a high level of complexity in the solution space, characterized by interdependencies among technical components (F2). Conversely, organizations should form component teams if the software is characterized by intertwined business requirements (F2). When both technical components and business requirements are highly interdependent, feature teams remain the better choice (F2). Second, organizations should selectively invest in cross-team coordination, especially when developing moderately or highly complex software products or when the structure of the design space is not well understood (F3). Coordination is especially valuable for feature teams in a complex problem space and for component teams when either problem or solution space is complex. When the structure of the design space is unclear, frequent cross-team coordination can help mitigate the adverse effect of a mismatched team structure. However, organizations should be aware of the potential myopia associated with excessive coordination. Although frequent crossteam coordination can accelerate software development, it may not always result in maximal long-term performance (F3). A moderate level of synchronization that balances cross-team coordination to resolve interdependencies and independent exploration of different software parts can yield the best outcomes. For example, in the Spotify model, a tribe consists of multiple squads working within a common product domain and facilitates informal gatherings and knowledge-sharing activities, thus enhancing cross-team coordination. Our findings can inform how organizations design and implement tribes more effectively based on the complexity of problem and solution spaces. Finally, organizations should strategically assign responsibilities to development teams (F4). For less complex products, higher specialization of feature and component teams can accelerate development. However, when the problem space is moderately or highly complex, organizations should avoid highly specialized feature teams focused on specific product areas. Similarly, organizations should avoid highly specialized component teams when either the problem or solution space is moderately or highly complex, unless frequent cross-team coordination is feasible and cost-effective. This approach helps to reduce cross-team coordination overhead and should lead to better outcomes.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research

There are several limitations to this study. The computational model that we use is a simplified abstraction of the real world based on certain assumptions about how software development is carried out. Furthermore, the model does not reflect all possible factors that migh affect the behavior of software teams. For example, the model does not take into consideration intrateam communication and coordination, which are an important aspect affecting a team’s overall performance, especially when teams are very large; there is room for future research to explore this area in greater depth. Therefore, caution should be taken when interpreting our simulation results, as the model’s abstraction of the real world defines the results’ contextual boundaries (Rivard 2014). Despite these limitations, our computational model provides an effective approach to uncovering nuanced insights into software product development through extensive randomization and replication. It also opens up exciting new avenues for future research, providing a solid foundation for exploring complex dynamics in software development.

Future research may focus on studying the behavior of the teams when facing rapidly changing customer needs and component technologies. Additional insights can be obtained by incorporating in the model the process of structuring the problem and solution spaces because, at the beginning of the work, teams may not know the dependencies between elements and explore them as they progress in developing software products. Another interesting extension would be to study the gradual reorganization of feature teams into component teams as the market needs and structure become cleare and the architecture of a software product becomes more stable over time. By the same token, it would also be valuable to investigate the potential transition from component teams to feature teams as they gain a deeper understanding of their software’s design element interdependencies over time. Future research could also investigate the long-term effects of different team structures across projects on an organization’s technical capabilities and software development costs, because feature teams are more prone to introducing technical debt, and component teams are more prone to greater integration costs.
