# Win by Hook or Crook? Self-Injecting Favorable Online Reviews to Fight Adjacent Rivals

Abstract. The existing literature has long assumed that unethical behaviors are fueled by competition. Under this premise, rivalry and competition are often treated interchangeably. This study seeks to challenge this prevailing notion by introducing a conceptual distinction between rivalry and competition. Specifically, we explore the multifaceted relationship between rivalry and unethical behaviors in the context of online review selfinjecting behaviors. Drawing on the collective regulatory focus framework and deterrence theory, we posit that rivalry-induced deterrence triggers a shift in mindset from promotion focus to prevention focus, which leads to the formation of mutual forbearance in selfinjecting behaviors. Mutual forbearance collapses when one party unilaterally engages in self-injection, prompting the other party to retaliate by increasing their self-injecting activities. Additionally, we propose that firm reputation plays a crucial role in reinforcing the formation of mutual forbearance while mitigating its collapse. Employing an exhaustive data set from Ctrip.com and Qunar.com that comprises 85,939 observations from 3,996 hotels and spans 2018–2020, we found that the presence of an additional rival leads to a 3.2% decrease in self-injecting intensity. Furthermore, self-injecting intensity increases by 0.095% for every 1% rise in that of the rivals and increases by 0.041% for a 1% rise in that of the nonrival competitors. Crucially, our study identifies that firm reputation acts as a significant factor that strengthens the negative relationship between the number of rivals and self-injecting intensity and weakens the positive relationship between a hotel’s selfinjecting and its rival’s self-injecting intensity. These empirical findings are supported by a myriad of robust examinations, including the use of a set of subsample analyses, alternative measures of independent variables, alternative instrumental variables, alternative observation time windows, and multiple endogeneity tests. This study contributes to the literature by highlighting the pervasive and potent role of rivalry as an understudied inhib itor of self-injecting behaviors. Our empirical evidence offers critical managerial implications for platforms, sellers, and consumers.

## 1. Introduction

Studies have long asserted that competition galvanizes firms to engage in unethical practices, such as cheating, lying, and sabotage (Chui et al. 2021, Vadera and Pathki 2021, Thanassoulis 2023). This assertation has been extensively corroborated in the context of online transactions, as multiple studies have found that hotels tend to self-inject more favorable online reviews in response to heightened competition (Lappas et al. 2016, Luca and Zervas 2016, Nie et al. 2022). The underlying rationale posits that competition not only intensifies incentives for unethical actions but also fosters risk taking behaviors (To et al. 2018). Notably, in these studies, competitors are often viewed as homogeneous economic actors vying for the same scarce resources.

However, not all competitors are alike. In certain instances, competitors, as exemplified by the iconic dynamic between Magic Johnson and Larry Bird<sup>1</sup> in basketball, embody profound relational elements characterized by mutual recognition and respect (Kilduff

2019, Ager et al. 2022). Incorporating this relational view into the realm of competition, a recent body of research has coined the term “rivalry” to denote this unique form of competition (Kilduff 2019, Sgourev and Operti 2019, Sharapov and Ross 2023). Rivalry, as defined by Kilduff et al. (2010), refers to the competitive relationship between a focal firm and a target firm, distinguished by the focal firm’s experience of heightened psychological stakes of competition when competing against the target firm. Importantly, studies advocating a positive link between competition and unethical behaviors have starkly conflated rivalry with competition, leaving us a significant paucity in our understanding of how rivalry specifically influences firms’ self-injecting behaviors. In essence, rivalry is a concept related to but different from competition, as it possesses a myriad of unique idiosyncrasies (Yip et al. 2018, Operti et al. 2021). First, rivalry involves relational factors. Unlike competition, which can take place between unfamiliar or anonymous opponents, rivalry concentrates on a specific, identifiable, and familiar opponent (Kilduff et al. 2010, 2016). Second, the essence of rivalry extends beyond the current state of conflicting goals; it also encompasses historical interactions (Jarzabkowski and Bednarek 2018). Akin to the development of friendship, rivalry typically evolves over time through recurrent interactions. These historical interactions between rivals facilitate the establishment of mutual recognition and respect. Third, while competition may involve unevenly matched contests, where one competitor may potentially be superior to the other(s), rivalry inherently entails evenly matched contests (Pike et al. 2018).

We contend that the presumed positive link between competition and unethical behaviors may not hold in the case of rivalry due to its unique characteristics. On the one hand, as rivalry represents an evenly matched contest and unethical behaviors are more likely to occur in such scenarios (To et al. 2018, Vadera and Pathk 2021), it is plausible to expect that, akin to competition, rivalry would positively predict self-injecting behaviors. On the other hand, because rivalry centers on a specific, identifiable, and familiar opponent, firms are interested in and capable of scrutinizing the unethical behaviors of their rivals, thereby leading to self-restraint in engaging in unethical actions (Sun 2021). Moreover, the presence of mutual recognition and respect in rivalry diminishes one’s inclination to outperform their rivals through unfair means. These inspiring studies imply that, in contrast to competition, rivalry may serve as a deterrent to unethical behaviors. Furthermore, given the considerable heterogeneity among firms in their behaviors involving unethical actions, it is improbable that the influence of rivalry on firms’ online selfinjecting behaviors would be uniformly applied across hotels with heterogeneous attributes. These conflicting views therefore motivate us to advance this line of research and explore the following questions in greater depth.

Research Question 1. Does rivalry, like competition, fuel online self-injecting behaviors or inhibit online selfinjecting behaviors?

Research Question 2. How does the effect of rivalry on firms’ online self-injecting behaviors vary with hotel heterogeneity?

To address the above questions, we first conceptually differentiate rivalry from competition. Based on the theoretical definition of rivalry, we then develop a novel measurement method, including both objective filtration and subjective evaluations, to further discern rivals from competitors. Embracing collective regulatory focus framework and deterrence theory, we seek to explore the relationship between rivalry and self injecting behavior from a dynamic point of view. In organizational settings, firms typically assemble teams to deal with review-related decisions. Through ongoing interaction, review management team members cultivate a shared understanding of the values of the team to regulate or guide their behaviors toward achieving collective goals (Owens and Hekman 2016). The collective regulatory focus theory suggests that teams are motivated by either a promotion focus, aimed at maximizing gains, or a prevention focus, focused on minimizing losses to guide their collective behaviors (Dimotakis et al. 2012, Emich and Vincent 2020). Because self-injecting favorable online reviews entails both gains (i.e., high rating, increased web traf fic) and losses (i.e., reputation damage, potential penal ties), To et al. (2018) illuminate that rivalry promotes a strong desire to outperform and leads to a promotion mindset. Recent studies have provided supporting evidence that a promotion mindset increases risk-taking behaviors, which fuels self-injecting intensity (Ager et al. 2022). Deterrence theory, on the other hand, posits that sanctions or punishments could serve as a deter rence mechanism against unethical behaviors (Yu et al. 2009). Because of the historical and relational nature of rivalry, firms possess the capability to monitor and track the actions and strategic maneuvers of their rivals (Uribe 2020). If a hotel self-injects favorable online reviews, these unethical behaviors are likely to be observed by rivals, who may then report to regulators, thereby increasing the likelihood of punitive measures. Thus, we argue that rivalry-induced deterrence contri butes to the formation of mutual forbearance in acting unethically, which hinders self-injecting intensity. As the number of rivals increases, the deterrence against acting unethically also intensifies. Consequently, two competing conjectures emerge: the number of nearby rivals would either foster or impede self-injecting behaviors.

Further, mutual forbearance signifies an implicit agreement between a focal firm and its rivals, wherein all parties refrain from engaging in the practice of selfinjecting favorable reviews. Additionally, the unilateral betrayal of this agreement manifested through observing a rival’s self-injecting behaviors serves as a signal of the collapse of mutual forbearance in the rivalry. The breakdown of mutual forbearance triggers a retaliatory reaction against the betrayers in a more intensive manner (Ryu et al. 2020). Intuitively, another vividly portrayed conjecture emerges: the self-injecting behaviors of rivals prompt the focal hotel to engage in self-injecting to a greater extent than nonrival competitors. The primary cost of self-injecting behavior lies in the potential loss of reputation when such behaviors are exposed. Insights from organizational studies suggest that reputable firms tend to suffer more from scandals than less reputable counterparts due to negative spillovers resulting from firm misconduct (Knittel and Stango 2014, Johnson et al. 2018, Paruchuri et al. 2021). Given this, it can be argued that rivalry-induced deterrence would be more pronounced for reputable hotels than for less reputable ones. Consequently, we conjecture that the relationship between rivalry and self-injecting intensity would be influenced by the reputation of the hotel.

We empirically examined the aforementioned hypotheses using comprehensive panel data sets from Ctrip. com and Qunar.com, which encompassed 85,939 observations from 3,996 hotels and spanned the period from April 1, 2018, to November 30, 2020. The examination of the impact of rivalry on firms’ online self-injecting behaviors poses challenges vis-a\`-vis potential endogeneity issues. To mitigate this concern, we employed an instrumental variable approach. Furthermore, we conducted a series of supplementary tests to rule out alternative explanations and enhance the robustness of our findings. Our analyses reveal compelling evidence that the presence of nearby rivals acts as a deterrent to a hotel’s self-injecting intensity. More precisely, each additional nearby rival corresponds to a 3.2% decrease in a hotel’s self-injecting intensity. Comparative analyses underscore that a hotel’s online self-injecting intensity is more responsive to the self-injecting behavior of nearby rivals than to that of nearby nonrival competitors. Specifically, self-injecting intensity increases by 0.095% for every 1% rise in the intensity of rivals and by 0.041% for a 1% rise in the intensity of nonrival competitors. In addition, our investigation reveals that hotel reputation plays a pivotal role in strengthening the negative relationship between the number of rivals and a hotel’s self-injecting intensity while weakening the positive relationship between the hotel’s self-injecting intensity and its rival’s self-injecting intensity. These interesting results challenge the long-standing assertion that competition fuels unethical behaviors and shed new light on the multifaceted nature of rivalry in the evolving context of online reviews (Vadera and Pathki 2021, Chang and Sokol 2022, Nie et al. 2022).

This study makes several significant contributions to theory. First, this study ushers in a novel conceptual distinction between rivalry from competition in the accommodation sector in general and the context of hotel online reviews in particular and further advances our understanding of the epistemological roots of online self-injecting behaviors by pinpointing rivalry as a previously unexplored inhibitor of such unethical behavior. Second, this study quantifies the effects of rivalry, both in terms of the number of rivals and the intensity of rivalry, on firms’ online self-injecting intensity. Third, this study uncovers the multifaceted effect of rivalry on unethical behaviors from a dynamic point of view. Fourth, this study sheds new light on how the influence of rivalry on firms’ online self-injecting behaviors differs from that of nonrival competition, thereby providing novel insights into the conditions under which rivalry is generally more or less likely to lead to firms’ self-injecting behaviors. Lastly, this study enriches the literature on rivalry by demonstrating how firm reputation can foster the positive aspects and circumvent the negative consequences of rivalry.

## 2. Related Literature

## 2.1. Self-Injecting Favorable Online Reviews in the Accommodation Sector

The global accommodation market has witnessed increasing dominance by a handful of well-established reservation platforms, such as Booking.com, TripAdvisor, Expedia, and so on (Lappas et al. 2016). When travelers plan a trip, these online reservation platforms play a paramount role as users usually search for hotels on them. Admittedly, the visibility of a hotel in search results on these platforms significantly influences consumer choices. Studies have shown that websites listed on the first page of search engine results receive almost 95% of web traffic, leaving only 5% for those on subsequent pages of search results (Kaye 2013). Although consumers increasingly rely on online reviews to assess the quality of products prior to making purchasing decisions (Feng et al. 2019, Kwark et al. 2021, Fang 2022), the online reservation market remains highly concentrated. As hotels vie for attention on these renowned platforms, securing top-ranking positions becomes crucially impor tant for hotel managers seeking to attract more web traffic. To influence consumer perceptions and promote their rankings, firms often resort to self-injecting favorable consumer reviews of their products on their web sites, review platforms, and e-marketplaces<sup>2</sup> (Mayzlin et al. 2014, Luca and Zervas 2016).

Self-injecting favorable online reviews have been proven to corrode review credibility (Jabr 2022), cause rating bias (Hu et al. 2017), and diminish consumer surplus (Mayzlin et al. 2014). From a pragmatic perspective, e-platforms such as Amazon and Yelp have responded to this challenge by implementing opinion spam algorithms to detect and filter fake reviews. Further, from an epistemological perspective, a wealthy line of studies has examined the relationship between product characteristics and self-injecting behaviors. For example, Hu et al. (2012) suggest that experience products tend to attract more fake reviews than search products on Amazon. Sellers frequently self-post positive online reviews of newly launched products because early consumer reviews of a product are crucial to its success (Park et al. 2021). A large number of empirical studies have shown that existing positive evaluations tend to attract further positive evaluations while existing negative evaluations attract less consumer attention (Goes et al. 2014, Li 2018, Schneider et al. 2021). Park et al. (2021) suggested that a product has a higher average rating and a greater review volume after a positive initial consumer review compared with a negative one. In addition, firm heterogeneity has also been found to be correlated with firms’ self-injecting behaviors. For example, Mayzlin et al. (2014) found that chain hotels are less likely to engage in self-injecting behaviors than nonchain hotels. Further, Luca and Zervas (2016) suggested that firms are more prone to commit online review fraud when their reputation is weak. This might occur when forms have few reviews, have recently received bad reviews, or possess a low average rating.

The economics and psychology literature has long recognized competition as a catalyst for unethical behaviors (Lee et al. 2018, Chui et al. 2021, Vadera and Pathki 2021). Because self-injecting favorable online reviews constitutes a typical form of unethical business practices, a fruitful line of research has positioned competition as one of the primary causes of firms’ self-injecting behaviors. For example, Mayzlin et al. (2014) found that the geographic density of competitors increases the likelihood of firms’ engagement in self-injecting behaviors. Moreover, Lee et al. (2018) showed that the growth of self-injecting behavior might be attributed to escalating competition. Although certain competitors or rivals can instill a motivation to perform beyond an ordinary competitive spirit or the objective stakes of the contest, prior studies have often treated rivalry and competition as interchangeable terms, resulting in a dearth of knowledge about the specific effect of rivalry on firms’ selfinjecting behaviors. Furthermore, prior studies have yet to identify the conditions under which the relationship between competition and self-injecting behavior is more or less likely to manifest. Thus, there is a paucity of understanding vis-a\`-vis whether any rivalry exists among the competitors being studied, which might confound the effects of rivalry and nonrival competition on self-injecting behaviors. This study diverges from prior research by conceptually and empirically differentiating rivalry from nonrival competition and comparing their effects on firms’ online self-injecting behaviors.

## 2.2. Differentiating Rivalry from Competition

Competition is ubiquitous in life, from employees vying for promotions to athletes striving for championships. Existing literature predominantly holds a structural view of competition and views it as a situation in which the outcomes of actors are diametrically opposed (Bennett et al. 2013, Charness et al. 2014). However, competitors are not homogeneous per se. As Kilduff et al. (2010) suggested, “certain competitors, or rivals, can instill a motivation to perform that goes above and beyond an ordinary competitive spirit (p. 943).” For example, two soccer teams in the England Premier League, Manchester City and Manchester United, consider their contests against each other (known as “Local Derby”) as considerably more important than games against other teams, reflecting an obsessive focus on their relative performance.

In prior competition literature, the term “rivalry” has been generally used synonymously with the term “competition”; rivals are simply opponents in competition with one another in a competitive setting (Garcia et al. 2006, Luca and Zervas 2016, Kitchens et al. 2018, Chan and Briers 2019, Chui et al. 2021, Vadera and Pathki 2021). However, recent research (Kilduff et al. 2016, To et al. 2018, Operti et al. 2021, Ager et al. 2022) challenges this perspective and suggests that rivalry encompasses more than a mere current state of conflicting or opposing goals. Rivalry exists when the psychological stakes of competition increase as a result of the existing relationship between competitors, and it is independent of objective stakes or other structural or situational characteristics (Kilduff et al. 2016). In essence, there are two types of competitive influence: one driven by rivals and the other by nonrival competi tors (Kilduff et al. 2016, Connelly et al. 2019). The key difference between rivalry and nonrival competition lies in the historical and relational nature of rivalry.

Importantly, researchers suggest that “rivalry is a relational schema in which the representations of partner, self, and dyad are linked to representations (i.e., memories, and expectations) of shared competitive interactions” (Converse and Reinhard 2016, p. 191). Therefore, rivalry is neither a single competition nor its immediate outcomes; however, it is psychologically embedded within an ongoing competitive narrative with relational components. The conceptualization of rivalry likens to friendship: a relationship that typically evolves from repeated social interaction (Kilduff et al. 2016). In contrast to nonrival competition that can take place between unknown or anonymous opponents, rivalry entails a focus on a specific, identifiable opponent (Kilduff et al. 2010). Conversely, given that rivalry represents a relationship, it is always directed toward a recognized competitor, featuring a historical component built up over time through recurrent interaction. The full scope of rivalry cannot be captured by the current state of conflicting goals. To better illustrate the differences between rivalry and nonrival competition, we present the following example of Hilton and Marriott in the accommodation sector. In their pursuit of being world-leading chain brands, Hilton is one of Marriott’s well-known rivals, engaging in historical, evenly matched competitions. Hotels of these two chain brands have similar price ranges, offer comparable facilities, and are situated in close proximity, competing for each other’s consumers. On the other hand, there are other well-known brand chain hotels such as Holiday Inn, which are considered nonrival competitors to Marriott.

## 3. Theoretical Underpinnings and Hypothesis Development

In the context of online review ratings, we meticulously embrace the collective regulatory focus framework and deterrence theory to formulate specific predictions on how rivalry influences online self-injecting behaviors. In particular, we focus on rivalry-induced deterrence to elucidate why rivalry differs from competition in predicting online self-injecting behaviors. Additionally, we delve into the nuanced aspect of how the influence of rivalry is contingent on the heterogeneity of hotels. ## 3.1. Collective Regulatory Focus Theory and Risk-Taking in Rivalry

In this section, we seek to explore the relationship between rivalry and a hotel’s self-injecting behavior through the lens of collective regulatory focus theory. The practice of self-injecting favorable reviews entails both positive and negative outcomes for hotels. Because hotels typically assemble teams to deal with review-related decisions, the collective regulatory focus theory provides a pertinent lens through which we can comprehend this phenomenon, suggesting that teams employ two distinct means of self-regulation, known as promotion focus and prevention focus, to guide their collective behaviors (Faddegon et al. 2008, Owens and Hekman 2016). Promotion focus emphasizes the pursuit of gains (or the avoidance of nongains), whereas prevention focus focuses on the avoidance of losses (o the pursuit of nonlosses). According to collective regu latory focus theory, teams are driven by either a promo tion focus or a prevention focus. Those exhibiting a promotion focus tend to be more attuned to positive outcomes, harbor stronger nurturance needs, care more about their accomplishments, and pay closer attention to the results of gains or nongains. Conversely, teams with a prevention focus are more attuned to negative outcomes, possess stronger security needs, care more about their responsibilities, pay more attention to the results of loss or nonloss, and utilize avoidance as a strategic means of mitigation (Faddegon et al. 2008, Dimotakis et al. 2012).

Rivalry heightens the psychological stakes of competition and promotes a strong desire to outperform each other (Jarzabkowski and Bednarek 2018). When engaged in rivalry, people are more result-orientated than when competing against nonrival competitors (Kilduff et al. 2016). Thus, rivals propel each other toward higher levels of motivation and performance. Hotel’s review management teams are more concerned about winning rather than the means to achieve victory when they compete against rivals, as rivalry elevates the subjective importance placed on competitive outcomes (Pike et al. 2018, Connelly et al. 2019, Operti et al. 2021). As rivalry increases the psychological stakes of competition, this heightened effort in the pursuit of maximum performance is consistent with a promotion mindset whereby teams seek to maximize gains.

Prior work has suggested that teams with a high promotion focus are more inclined to take risks because a focus on the pursuit of maximizing gains increases tolerance for risk (Bennett et al. 2013). Furthermore, Ma (2023) found that promotion-focused teams employ riskier strategies in pursuit of gains (e.g., pursuing more aggressive performance at the expense of reduced accuracy), whereas prevention-focused participants adopt more cautious strategies that prioritize avoiding losses (e.g., trading slower performance for increased accuracy). Collectively, these studies suggest that a rivalry-induced promotion mindset will lead to greater risk-taking behaviors because people commit themselves to maximizing positive outcomes. Although selfinjecting is generally considered unethical and may incur punishment, we expect that hotels will take a risk to do so to outperform their rivals. As the number of rivals increases, the psychological stakes of competition heightens and the perceived ease of winning decreases (Chui et al. 2021). Previous studies suggest that people are more prone to engage in risk-taking behavior when the competitive outcome is attractive, and the likelihood of winning is low (Vadera and Pathki 2021). Hence, being in a larger (versus smaller) rivalry pool can fuel risk-taking tendencies and thus boost a hotel’s inclination toward self-injecting behaviors. We therefore posit the following hypothesis.

## 3.2. Rivalry-Induced Deterrence and the Formation of Mutual Forbearance

Integrating rivalry-induced deterrence theory into collective regulatory focus theory, this section provides an alternative view regarding the relationship between rivalry and a hotel’s self-injecting intensity. Deterrence theory submits that sanctions or punishments could serve as a deterrence mechanism to deter deviant behavior (Yu et al. 2009). The efforts of sanction or punishment are measured by two dimensions: severity and certainty of punishment (Burns et al. 2023). Severity of punishment refers to the degree of punishment associated with not complying with security policy, and certainty of punishment pertains to the probability of being punished. Sanctions could manifest in either formal or informal (Pate and Hamilton 1992). Forma sanctions involve concrete penalties imposed by society (e.g., fines or imprisonment for criminal acts) or employers (e.g., demotions or employment termination for policy violations), whereas informal sanctions entail the disapproval from oneself, the community, or moral beliefs for a given action (Johnston et al. 2015).

Because of the historical and relational nature of rivalry, firms can track the actions and strategic maneuvers of their rivals, leading to a transparent competition among them (Uribe 2020). In contrast to novel or anonymous competitors, rivals represent counterparts with whom actors have long-standing relationships and engage in intensive interactions. The behaviors of rivals are more prone to observation and notice compared with those of nonrivals. In the event that a hotel acts unethically, such as self-injecting favorable online reviews, these unethical behaviors could soon be detected by rival hotels. Consequently, firms may report the self-injecting behaviors of their rivals to authorities or platforms, escalating the likelihood of punishment for the rivals. Beyond formal sanctions, self-injecting favorable reviews may incur informal sanctions, such as disapproval of self and community. As discussed above, rivalry involves mutual recognition and respect (Ager et al. 2022), which may mitigate the inclination to win through unfair means. Competing against a rival can invoke greater concerns about one’s own sense of self-worth, thereby rendering competition against rivals more threatening due to selfesteem considerations (Kilduff et al. 2016).

The deterrence imposed by a rival’s formal or informal sanctions may shift a hotel’s collective regulatory focus from a promotion focus to a prevention focus. For example, Gino and Margolis (2011) suggested that prevention focus can reduce unethical behavior by induc ing a fear of punishment. Prior regulatory focus studies suggest that a prevention mindset curtails actors’ appetite for risk by causing teams to pursue more familia and conservative strategies as well as eschew nove or riskier strategies that may leave them vulnerable (Chakravarthy et al. 2021). We therefore expect that rivalry-induced deterrence contributes to the formation of mutual forbearance. In essence, mutual forbearance is a form of tacit collusion that alludes to the phenomenon of diminished aggressive actions between firms that compete against each other (Golden and Ma 2003). In such conditions, hotels might not act aggressively because the expected gains from self-injecting favorable reviews may be lower than the potential losses due to retaliatory measures from rivals. As the number of rivals increases, the deterrence from rival’s formal and informal sanctions intensifies. This heightened deterrence leads to rivals viewing the payoff from mutual forbearance to be more attractive than the payoff from self-injecting favorable reviews. We therefore posit the following.

Hypothesis 1b. The number of nearby rivals negatively influences a focal hotel’s online self-injecting intensity.

## 3.3. Collapse of Mutual Forbearance in Self-Injecting

On reservation platforms, the ranking for each hotel is inversely correlated with that of its rivals. The primary objective behind self-injecting favorable reviews is to increase hotel ranking and thus attract more web traffic. Hotel managers increasingly find themselves in a “prisoner’s dilemma” scenario regarding whether to self-inject favorable reviews or not (Gossling et al. 2018). If a focal hotel and its rivals opt to self-inject at the same time and to the same degree, their relative ranking on the reservation platforms would remain unchanged, resulting in a lose-lose situation. Instead, the potential win-win situation for this prisoner’s dilemma scenario is that the hotel and its rivals established a tacit collusion of mutual forbearance, wherein none of them engages in self-injecting favorable reviews. Despite this, some hotels start to self-inject favorable reviews intensively as they anticipate obtaining a huge competitive advantage by unilaterally betraying the mutual forbearance agreement. Notably, in the accommodation sector, the observation of a rival’s self-injecting behaviors serves as a signal of the collapse of mutual forbearance.

Previous studies indicate that the collapse of mutual forbearance can entail one retaliating against the betrayers (Chung and Beamish 2010, Ryu et al. 2020). In the context of online reviews, hotels possess various ways to retaliate against the betrayers, but the quickest, most effective, and most commonly used way of retaliation is to engage in more intensive self-injecting of favorable reviews<sup>3</sup> (Nie et al. 2022). We therefore expect that the self-injecting behavior of the rival will trigger the focal hotel to retaliate in a similar manner. When observers perceive that rivals have gained desired benefits (e.g., higher ranking and more web traffic) or opportunities through self-injecting, negative emotions such as anger, envy, and a sense of injustice are often evoked. Research has consistently shown that these negative emotions are found to correlate with more aggressive retaliations in rivalry than in nonrival competition (Jones and Barnett 2022, Malik and Pichler 2023). Taken together, we propose that the self-injecting behaviors of rivals will lead the focal hotel to engage in more intensive self-injecting behaviors than nonrival competitors. Therefore, we put forth the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 2a. The self-injecting intensity of nearby rival hotels positively influences a focal hotel’s online self-injecting intensity.

Hypothesis 2b. A hotel’s online self-injecting intensity is more responsive to the self-injecting behavior of nearby rivals than to that of nearby nonrival competitors.

## 3.4. Differential Effects of Firm Reputation on the Formation and Collapse of Mutual Forbearance

The primary cost of self-injecting behavior is the risk that the activity will be publicly exposed by its rivals and punished by the platforms or authorities. Admittedly, self-injecting behaviors could improve hotel rankings through numerical ratings and influence consumer decisions through textual persuasion. Government fines, penalties imposed by the review-hosting platform, and reputation damage are among the punishments that deter focal hotels from self-injecting favorable reviews (Mayzlin et al. 2014). However, the cost associated with self-injecting behaviors (i.e., reputational loss) is higher for reputable hotels than for less reputable ones. Organizational studies suggest that reputable firms ensure more severe consequences from scandals than less repu table counterparts due to negative spillovers resulting from firm misconduct (Johnson et al. 2018, Paruchuri et al. 2021). For example, if a brand chain hotel is detected in self-posting favorable reviews, adverse publicity and potential sanctions could extend to all properties unde the brand (Mayzlin et al. 2014). This phenomenon is also exemplified in a recent case, where the Irish hotel Clare Inn Hotel and Suites, part of the Lynch Hotel Group, was issued a “red badge” by TripAdvisor, warning consu mers that the hotel manipulated reviews after it was dis covered that a hotel executive solicited positive reviews. TripAdvisor also removed reviews from other Lynch Hotel Group hotels. In such cases, platforms take action against the entire hotel group, given the common owner ship and management of the hotels. Therefore, we posi that rivalry-induced deterrence will be more prominen for reputable hotels than for less reputable counterparts. Mutual forbearance denotes the situation in which hotel reach a tacit collusion with their rivals. The establishmen and sustainment of mutual forbearance in self-injecting behavior requires a foundation of mutual trust, as firms would get huge advantages if one unilaterally betrays the mutual forbearance (Nie et al. 2022). In this context, we propose that firm reputation plays a vital role in intensify ing the formation of mutual forbearance and strengthen ing the negative relationship between the number of rivals and a firm’s self-injecting intensity. Fundamentally, reputation is an observer’s impression of the actor’s dis position to behave in a certain manner (Clark and Mont gomery 1998). Reputation signifies a firm’s credibility and moral capital, given that reputation involves signifi cant investments and accumulates over time, thereby sug gesting a firm is unlikely to act otherwise (Dal Bo and Tervio 2013, Servaes and Tamayo 2013, Chiles 2021). Pre vious studies affirm that consumers tend to deposit more trust in reputable firms than in those with low reputation (Kim 2019). Hotels, in turn, may believe that reputable rivals are more likely to behave as agreed (not to self inject) than less reputable rivals and mutual forbearance is more likely to be formed among reputable hotels. We expect that firm reputation reinforces the formation of mutual forbearance and posit the following.

Hypothesis 3a. Hotel reputation strengthens the negative relationship between the number of nearby rivals and online self-injecting intensity.

Further, observing a rival’s self-injecting behavior may trigger the focal hotel to retaliate by engaging in more intensive self-injecting practices. We expect that such a positive link would be weakened by the influence of firm reputation. Compared with less reputable hotels, reputable hotels usually have a larger volume of reviews on reservation platforms (Hollenbeck 2017, Chiles 2021). Additionally, on most reservation platforms, the ranking of a hotel depends on its average rating that is computed by the arithmetic mean value of all consumer reviews within specified periods (Ghose et al. 2012, Chambers and Baker 2020, Hunold et al. 2020). Therefore, the ranking of hotels with a larger quantity of consumer reviews is less susceptible to the impact of a certain number of fake reviews than that of hotels with fewer consumer reviews. In support of this argument, Chen and Papanastasiou (2021) suggested that firms tend to frequently self-inject positive reviews for products with few consumer reviews. Consumers often peruse previous reviews before making a purchase, and the persuasiveness of a self-posted favorable review on consumer decisions would be less impactful when many consumer reviews are available. Therefore, the effectiveness of self-injecting as a retaliatory strategy against a rival’s self-injecting behavior is lower for reputable hotels than for less reputable counterparts. Moreover, the reputation loss incurred from unethical behaviors is higher for reputable hotels than for less reputable ones (Mayzlin et al. 2014, Luca and Zervas 2016). Because the effectiveness of self-injecting as a retaliation to a rival’s betrayal of mutual forbearance is lower and the risk is higher for reputable hotels than for less reputable ones, we expect that hotel reputation weakens the positive relationship between self-injecting behaviors of rivals and the focal hotel’s self-injecting intensity.

Hypothesis 3b. Hotel reputation weakens the positive relationship between the self-injection of rivals and online self-injecting intensity.

## 4. Data

## 4.1. Sample

To empirically validate the aforementioned hypotheses, we collected hotel reviews from two leading reservation platforms in China, namely Ctrip.com and Qunar.com. We chose to focus on the hotel category on Ctrip.com and Qunar.com to construct our data set for three reasons. First, competition, especially rival competition, is ubiquitous in the accommodation sector, making it an ideal research context (Chiles 2021). Second, because a hotel is a typical experience product, consumer reviews play a crucial role in shaping travelers’ decisions (Ghose et al. 2012, Lappas et al. 2016). Moreover, prior research has indicated that review manipulation is more prevalent in experience products than in search products, making the hotel category a compelling context for examining the relationship between rivalry and review manipulation (Hu et al. 2011). Third, Ctrip.com and Qunar.com stand out as leading reservation platforms in China, and they are well recognized for their extensive collection of hotel reviews (Chen et al. 2019). These online platforms provide a natural field for experimentation to identify review manipulations due to their distinct review policies. Ctrip.com is a “closed” reservation platform, meaning that only consumers who have stayed at a hotel can post a review for that specific hotel. On the other hand, Qunar.com is an “open” accommodation forum in which anyone can publish a review of any hotel. This means that publishing self-injecting positive reviews on Qunar.com is considerably less costly for a hotel than publishing positive ratings on Ctrip.com. When hotels seek to self-post favorable consumer reviews, a rational choice is to do so on platforms with an “open” review policy, such as Qunar.com. Empirical work by Mayzlin et al. (2014) has confirmed this conjecture. In line with the existing literature, we quantified firms’ self-injecting intensity by comparing the differences in the distributions of positive reviews for the same hotel on Qunar.com and Ctrip.com (Mayzlin et al. 2014, Zhuang et al. 2018, Nie et al. 2022).

Given the extensive volume of online hotel reviews spanning different travel destinations, we chose the city of Shanghai as our focal point: a major metropoli tan area and a popular destination for both business and leisure travelers in China. Unlike smaller cities or resort locales, a major metropolitan city such as Shang hai has a wide variety of hotels catering to various traveler preferences. In addition, a cosmopolitan hub such as Shanghai encompasses different competing modes, including both rival and nonrival competitions, which are the central focus of the current study. We devel oped a Python-based crawler for data collection. The crawler first retrieved the data of all available hotels in Shanghai. The names of the hotels as well as their contact phone numbers, locations, and postal code were collected. This process resulted in 12,975 hotels listed on Ctrip.com and 10,582 hotels listed on Qunar.com in Shanghai, respectively. We intended to compare the review distributions of the same hotel listed on both Ctrip.com and Qunar.com. Because some hotels were not listed on either site, we developed a matching algo rithm to identify the hotels that were listed on both sites to enable a robust comparison. The algorithm matched hotels based on postal code, name, and address. We then manually verified the matched list to ensure accuracy in the matching process. Through this combined matching approach, we identified 8,633 hotels located in 135 postal code areas available on both sites in ou sample. Because we are interested in the distribution of consumer reviews, 427 hotels that had no consumer reviews on either Ctrip.com or Qunar.com throughout our observation period were excluded from our sam ple. We also dropped 1,051 hotels that commenced operations after the beginning of our database in Apri 2018. We further abandoned 174 hotels as they had been in business for less than six months. After these initial sample refinement processes, our sample com prised 6,981 hotels for further analysis. To enhance data accuracy, we then conducted telephone interviews with the managers of these 6,981 hotels one by one. After three rounds of telephone interviews, the final sample contained 3,996 hotels.<sup>4</sup> Heckman’s two-stage models were employed to ascertain that there is no selfselection bias in our sample, following the methodology proposed by Clougherty et al. (2016).<sup>5</sup>

For each of these 3,996 hotels in our study, we collected all the historical reviews, including the identity of the reviewer (as displayed by the site), review date, review ratings (from one to five), review content, the presence of management response to the review, and the publication date of the management response on both Qunar.com and Ctrip.com. To capture the temporal dynamics of hotel ratings in different periods, we used one month as the time window and calculated the monthwise average hotel rating. We also collected hotel-specific characteristics, such as hotel star ratings, hotel locations, hotel ages, and a series of hotel service dummies (e.g., fitness, restaurant, and swimming pool). Overall, we collected monthly data on the hotels from Qunar.com and Ctrip.com for 32 months, from April 1, 2018, to November 30, 2020. Our final unbalanced hotel-level data set included 85,939 observations across 3,996 hotels.

To augment our data set, we sought supplementary information from external sources. First, we searched the hotel location on map.baidu.com to acquire latitude and longitude coordinates for each hotel. We then used the coordinates to calculate the geographic distances between hotels. Subsequently, we accessed Anjuke. com—a leading real estate application in China—to obtain the historical commercial real estate and leasing prices for each postal code area. These variables were used as the exogenous instrumental variables for causal identification.

Deterrence theory suggests that a firm’s decision to engage in unethical behaviors is determined by the possibility and severity of the sanctions for those unethical behaviors (Trinkle et al. 2021). The risks of self-injecting behavior being caught and the severity of the sanctions for self-injecting may influence a firm’s self-injecting intensity. These factors, however, are unobserved. To account for the endogeneity generated from omitted variables, we developed an instrumental variable (IV) identification technique. ## 6. Discussion and Implications 6.1. Discussion and Key Findings

Our study aimed to answer a set of research questions with progressive relationships: Does rivalry fuel or impede online self-injecting behaviors? How does the effect of rivalry vary with hotel heterogeneity? To answer these questions, we develop a set of theoretically driven hypotheses and rigorously test them by leveraging an exhaustive data set from multiple sources. Contrary to previous studies that assert that competition fuels unethical behaviors, we discovered robust evidence that rivalry inhibits, rather than fuels, online self-injecting behavio (supporting Hypothesis 1b). This striking finding survived numerous robustness tests, including a set of subsample analyses, the use of alternative measures of dependent and instrumental variables, the use of alternative observation time windows, and several endogeneity tests. This seemingly counterintuitive conclusion suggests that rivalry-induced deterrence outweighs the gains from risk-taking behaviors. The new finding proves the necessity of differentiating rivalry from competition and underscores that rivalry and competition could play distinct roles in predicting unethical behaviors.

We further uncovered a causal relationship between a hotel’s self-injecting intensity and its rival’s self-injecting intensity, thus supporting Hypothesis 2a. This finding extended the prevalent peer influence phenomenon from ethical to unethical behaviors. The underlying mechanisms of peer influence on ethical behaviors might differ from those on unethical behaviors. For ethical behaviors, the influence of peers typically manifests through a variety of mechanisms, such as observational learning, social norms, imitation, and homophily (Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Beer et al. 2021, Li et al. 2021, Cu et al. 2024). A more compelling explanation for “peer influence” in the context of unethical behaviors (selfinjecting favorable reviews) is to minimize losses. The ranking of a focal hotel is negatively correlated with that of its rivals. Seeing rival self-injecting favorable reviews, hotels may engage in self-injecting to offset the disad vantages caused by the self-injecting behaviors of rivals.

We confirmed that a hotel’s online self-injecting intensity is more responsive to the self-injecting behavior of nearby rivals than to that of nearby nonrival competitors (supporting Hypothesis 2b). We show that self-injecting intensity increases by 0.095% for every 1% rise in that of its rivals, which is more than twice the corresponding increase observed in that of its nonrival competitors (0.041%). The result implies that observing a rival’s self-injecting behavior causes the focal hotel to self-inject in a more intensive way as a retaliatory response than observing nonrival’s self-injecting behaviors. Although previous studies suggested that rivalry fosters greater motivations and efforts than nonrival competition (Kilduff 2014, Pike et al. 2018), our findings present a nuanced perspective by showing rivalry is a “double-edged sword.” The confirmation of these hypotheses suggests that unethical behaviors are positive responses to the unethical behaviors of rivals but exhibit a negative response to the mere presence of rivals.

As expected, we uncovered divergent moderating roles of reputation on the relationship between rival and self-injecting intensity. We show that hotel reputation plays a crucial role in strengthening the negative relationship between the number of rivals and self-injecting behaviors (supporting Hypothesis 3a). Economic literature has proved that collusion, collaboration, and contracts are more likely to be formed among reputable firms than less reputable firms due to a high level of mutual trust (Cai and Obara 2009). Our results affirm this line of work by showing that mutual forbearance, functioning as a tacit form of collusion, is more prone to be established among reputable rival hotels than less reputable counterparts. We also found that hotel reputation weakens the positive relationship between rival injecting and self-injecting (Hypothesis 3b). This finding corroborates the important role of firm reputation in alleviating unethical behaviors (Hollenbeck 2018, Kokkodis and Lappas 2020, Yang et al. 2021).

Taken together, our empirical findings unveil three compelling insights that have profound theoretical and practical implications for platforms, marketers, and consumers. First, we find that rivalry stimulates the formation of mutual forbearance in acting unethically. Second, the delicate balance of mutual forbearance collapses when one party starts to unilaterally self-inject, triggering retaliatory self-injection from the other party. Third, we establish that the reputation of a firm emerges as a pivotal factor, strengthening the formation of mutual forbearance and weakening its collapse concurrently. In the following sections, we will delve into these results to unravel their implications for research and practice.

## 6.2. Theoretical Contributions

This study advances theory in several significant ways. First, this study enriches the literature on online review manipulation by identifying rivalry as a hitherto undiscovered inhibitor of online self-injecting behaviors, thus helping scholars better understand when and why firms behave unethically and offering insights on how to curtail such unethical behaviors. Previous studies have generally concurred that competition fosters self-injecting behaviors (Mayzlin et al. 2014, Luca and Zervas 2016, Gutt et al. 2019, Park et al. 2021). However, these studies often treated rivalry and nonrival competition interchangeably, leaving us starkly uninformed about the specific relationship between rivalry and a firm’s selfinjecting behaviors. Diverging from the previous studies situated in the context of competition, this study is grounded in rivalry, which is a related but theoretically different concept. By conceptually differentiating rivalry from nonrival competition, we provide concrete evidence that rivalry and nonrival competition play distinct roles in predicting a firm’s self-injecting behaviors. Drawing on the collective regulatory focus framework and deterrence theory, we show that rivalry-induced deterrence of acting unethically shifts review management team’s mindset from a promotion focus (maximize the gains of self-injection) to a prevention focus (minimize the risks/costs of self-injection) that dampens hotel’s self-injecting intensity. Our discovery of the negative relationship between rivalry and self-injecting intensity challenges the long-held belief in previous competition literature and opens up a new direction of review manipulation governance: the cultivation of transparent rivalry. Thus, our work further extends the existing paradigm on review manipulation and enriches the understanding of the competitive determinants influencing online review manipulation. Further, this study provides an opportunity to quantify the effect of rivalry on unethical behaviors such as self-injecting intensity studied in this paper, thereby offering a valu able contribution to the empirical exploration of these phenomena.

Second, this study substantially extends the literature on self-injecting behaviors by investigating how the number of rivals can affect self-injecting behaviors: an unpretentious yet important contextual attribute of rivalry that has received surprisingly limited attention in previous research. Extant studies on competitive contexts and unethical behaviors have primarily focused on the effects of the individual attributes of competitors (Tzini and Jain 2018), the relationship between competitors (Kilduff et al. 2016), and the competitive intensity between competitors (Yip et al. 2018, Feltovich 2019). The number of rivals or competitors is often a readily accessible cue in competitive contexts. For example, e-commerce platforms explicitly display the number of available sellers under certain search criteria. However, the effect of rivalry pool size in predicting self-injecting intensity has yet to be examined in previous literature. This study addresses this notable gap by showing that self-injecting intensity is indeed responsive to the presence of nearby rivals and that an increase in the numbe of nearby rivals reduces hotel’s self-injecting intensity. In doing so, our research sheds light on an unexplored facet of rivalry dynamics, offering valuable insights into how the sheer number of rivals can shape and influence self-injecting behaviors in competitive contexts.

Third, in contrast to prevailing literature that generally holds a unitary perspective on rivalry (Ager et al. 2022, Sharapov and Ross 2023, Akerlof et al. 2024), this study extends the rivalry discourse by uncovering the multifaceted nature of rivalry. Although past research has criticized the role of rivalry in stimulating unethical behaviors (Kilduff et al. 2016, Ager et al. 2022), our conceptual exploration of the rise and fall of mutual forbearance in rivalry offers a dynamic viewport, revealing the double-edged sword effect of rivalry. We contribute by discovering that rivalry-induced deterrence leads to the formation of mutual forbearance in acting unethically, which ultimately attenuates selfinjecting intensity. We also show that hotels will retaliate against their rivals in a more aggressive way than against nonrival competitors once the tacit mutual forbearance crumbles. These results underscore that rivalry incorporates not only confrontation but also collaboration and retaliation. Understanding the multifaceted nature of rivalry delivers great theoretical values to inform policymakers to fully utilize rivalry to reduce online review manipulations. We further advance this stream of research by proposing hotel reputation as an effective way to foster the advantages and circumvent the disadvantages of rivalry. This initial attempt has opened up a strategic window for future studies to explore other catalytic factors of rivalry.

Fourth, we enrich the literature by extending the prevalent “peer influence” phenomenon beyond ethical behaviors to encompass unethical behaviors. More importantly, we enrich theory by providing the collapse of rivalry-induced deterrence as an alternative mechanism for peer influence in unethical behaviors. Regarding ethical behaviors, the influence of peers takes effect through a variety of mechanisms, such as observational learning, social norms, imitation, and homophily (Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Li and Wu 2018, Beer et al. 2021, Li et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2022a). These mechanisms may not be directly applicable to unethical behaviors. Our study proposes a more compelling explanation for the phenomenon of peer influence in self-injecting behaviors by emphasizing the collapse of rivalry-induced deterrence. Analogous to the old saying “A tainted person is less likely to expose another tainted person,” when hotels witness their rivals engaging in self-injecting favorable reviews, the deterrence associated with the expectation that those rivals would expose such unethical behaviors diminishes significantly. Consequently, hotels become more inclined to follow the unethical behavior of their rivals as the rivalry-induced deterrence collapses. By presenting this viewpoint, we gain a deeper comprehension of the processes that propel peer influence in the realm of unethical actions, thereby providing significant contributions to the knowledge of how online self-injecting behaviors are impacted by peer actions.

## 6.3. Managerial Implications

Our findings suggest a range of practical implications for platforms, sellers, and consumers. We found that rivalry-induced deterrence is a critical force that inhibits hotel’s self-injecting behaviors. It is imperative for platforms to emphasize the rivalry-induced deterrence of self-injecting behaviors. Platforms, as key stakeholders, are advised to upgrade their fake review filter systems regularly to detect self-injecting behaviors swiftly and publicize hotels that act unethically. Then, platforms are suggested to increase the possibility, severity, and promptness of the punishment for hotel’s self-injecting behaviors. Meanwhile, hotels that do not engage in self-injecting behaviors should be recognized and rewarded. Moreover, platforms can foster a culture of mutual regulation among hotels and encourage whistle-blowing mechanisms for reporting unethical behaviors. The establishment of transparent channels for reporting such behaviors is essential. Given the apparent role of rivalry in reducing self-injecting behaviors, platforms should strategically consider how system designs and policies can cultivate firms’ feelings of rivalry and avoid nonrival competition. For instance, platforms may aim to design hotel reservation systems to encourage the antecedents of rivalry, such as fostering repeated offline interactions and promoting peer recognition among hotels. In the current online review systems, reservation platforms recommend hotels based on the evaluations of their previous consumers. We strongly recommend platforms consider including evaluations from peer hotels as an alternative index for recommending hotels to potential consumers. The ranking of hotels on the reservation platforms could be determined by the ratings of both previous consumers and peer hotels. Introducing peer evaluations to the current online review systems could attenuate the bias of consumer ratings and facilitate the formation of rivalry, which hinders self-injecting behaviors.

Hotels on reservation platforms are suggested to foster mutual forbearance and enable long-standing reciprocal relationships between rivals to persist. Hotels are also encouraged to scrutinize the behaviors of their rivals regularly to maintain a healthy competitive environment. Our research reveals that the collapse of mutual forbearance among rivals can lead to retaliatory selfinjecting behaviors of the other party, resulting in a lose-lose situation. Instead, we strongly suggest hotels remain rational and address rival’s unethical behaviors by reporting them to platforms and relevant authorities. Hotel managers and owners that face intense rival competition must strengthen the monitoring and governance mechanisms to ensure their legal compliance if they want to mitigate the risk of government sanctions and reputation damage. It is important for hotel managers to strike a balance, avoiding an excessive focus on outperforming rivals that may prioritize profits over ethical business practices. Hotel executives may want to take explicit measures to prevent rival’s unethical behaviors from influencing their moral judgments and strategic decisions, such as desensitizing data from rivals and relying on objective decision criteria.

Our findings indicate that hotels tend to engage in self-injecting behaviors when their rivals have done the same. When consumers become aware of a focal hotel self-injecting favorable reviews, they normally turn to the rivals of the focal hotel. In such instances, consumers are suggested to discount or at least be skeptical about the credibility of the favorable online reviews of rivals. This cautious approach enhances consumer discernment and encourages a more informed decisionmaking process when evaluating hotels in a competitive online landscape.

## 7. Concluding Remarks and Future Directions

## 7.1. Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations that provide fruitfu avenues for future research. First, this study focuses on the effect of rivalry on self-injecting favorable online reviews. However, review manipulation also takes place in other forms, such as deleting or hiding unfavorable reviews<sup>9</sup> and adding unfavorable reviews to others (Lappas et al. 2016, Luca and Zervas 2016, Nie et al. 2022). The costs and benefits of review manipulation vary according to the specific ways of review manipulation. For example, adding unfavorable reviews to other firms’ sites and deleting unfavorable reviews are considered more unethical and riskier than self-injecting favorable reviews. Although it has been discovered that adding unfavorable reviews to potential competitors is more efficient in outperforming competitors (Lappas et al. 2016), deleting unfavorable online reviews is more effective in predicting product sales than self-injecting favorable online reviews because deleting negative reviews is more discrete and less likely to be noticed by consumers (Zhuang et al. 2018). Do firms establish mutual forbearance in other forms of review manipulation? Do the effects of rivalry on self-injecting favorable reviews differ from those of adding unfavorable reviews to others? Future studies should further explore the antecedents and consequences of different forms of review manipulation to illuminate similarities and differences. Second, although this study proposes the shift of collective regulatory focus and rivalry-induced deterrence as the theoretical underpinnings to explain the negative relationship between rivalry and self-injecting, these psychological underpinnings remain untested. Firms are encouraged to harness the benefits of rivalry and simultaneously avoid its downsides. Understanding the psychological underpinnings of rivalry in predicting unethical behaviors provides operable implications for reducing review manipulation (George et al. 2021, Kokkodis 2021, Yang et al. 2021, Naumzik et al. 2022). Therefore, exploring the conditions in which rivalry may be beneficial or harmful constitutes an essential next step. Third, we have examined the influence of rivalry on self-injecting favorable online reviews in the context of symmetrical rivalry, yet whether our findings can apply to asymmetric rivalry warrants further investigation. Rivalry is a subjective notion, such that one firm conceives the other as a rival; however, this perception may not be reciprocated (Kilduff et al. 2016, To et al. 2018).

Rivalry could be either asymmetric or symmetric. Future research can employ both observational and subjective data to identify asymmetric rivals and compare the effects of symmetric and asymmetric rivalry on firms’ self-injecting behaviors. Fourth, it is worth noting that our measurement of rivals might not fully capture the dynamic nature of rivalry over time. Future research endeavors could focus on developing a more dynamic measurement approach to better capture the temporal variation in rivalry.

## 7.2. Conclusion

When consumers peruse a particular hotel on online reservation platforms, the platforms generally recommend a set of nearby alternatives. This recommendation system fuels the production of rivalry. Our theorydriven study suggests that the prevalence of online self-injecting behavior is notably lower in hotels experiencing stifling rivalry. This study suggests that while the presence of more rivals inhibits self-injecting, the self-injecting intensity among rivals stimulates selfinjecting. These empirical findings portray rivalry as a multifaceted force, holding significant implications for policymakers, organizations, and e-platforms alike. It is expected that this study will spark additional research on rivalry vis-a\`-vis online review manipulation.

<sup>2</sup> Self-injecting behavior generally takes three forms: self-injecting positive reviews, deleting or hiding negative reviews, and posting negative reviews for competitors. This study focuses exclusively on self-injected positive reviews for two reasons. First, self-injected positive reviews are more ubiquitous than other forms of selfinjecting. Most e-platforms do not allow sellers to hide or delete negative reviews about themselves. Posting negative reviews to competitors is less efficient in promoting a focal hotel’s ranking. Hotels may not post negative reviews to all the competitors. Instead, they have to identify their major rivals (usually there is more than one) and post negative reviews for them. In case of some e-platforms, consumer can post a review only after they have purchased a product/service and made a payment. Unlike self-injected positive reviews—for which sellers hire people to buy their pro ducts with your own money (usually, the “hired” consumers just place an order and sellers do not ship the corresponding products) to post negative reviews to competitors—sellers have to hire peopl to buy the products of their competitors and make real payments. Therefore, posting negative reviews to competitors costs more than self-injected positive reviews, and the high cost makes fake negative reviews less ubiquitous. Second, self-inject positive reviews is more identifiable than the other two forms of self-injecting. Because sellers may post negative reviews for one or more competitors, we do not know who exactly the negative reviews were given to, which makes the act of posting negative reviews for competitors unmeasurable.
