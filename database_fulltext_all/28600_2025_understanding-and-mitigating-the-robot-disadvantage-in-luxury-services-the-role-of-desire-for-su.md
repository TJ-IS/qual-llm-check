# Understanding and Mitigating the Robot Disadvantage in Luxury Services: The Role of Desire for Superiority

Abstract. Whereas service firms have started to deploy service robots on the service front line, it remains less understood whether all types of service firms gain a similar benefit from this advance in information technologies. With one secondary data analysis of review content from a leading travel website, one field study conducted at a hotel, and three randomized controlled experiments, the current research addresses this important managerial issue. We propose that replacing human service providers with service robots dampen customers’ feelings of superior social status over service providers, which drives a lower relative preference for service robots over human service providers in luxury services than in mainstream services. Supporting this mechanism, we show that the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on customers’ relative preference for robots over humans is mediated by the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers. Furthermore, the robot disadvantage in luxury services can be mitigated when a luxury service firm adopts a social equality–oriented positioning and when the use of service robots is endowed with superior status.

## 1. Introduction

Across lodging, dining, and retail sectors, the use of service robots in frontline service encounters has become drastically more prevalent in recent years (Mende et al. 2019). The rapidly growing global service robot market is expected to expand from \$19.08 billion in 2023 to \$62.35 billion in 2030 (Fortune Business Insights 2023). Service robots contribute to service operations in multiple ways, such as reducing operation costs, improving service efficiency, enabling 24-hour service support, and ensuring consistent service quality (Mende et al. 2019, Belanche et al. 2021, Dixon et al. 2021, Wirtz et al. 2023). Nevertheless, can service firms equally benefit from service robot deployment across all service settings? This is a crucial managerial issue given that identifying contextual features that influence customers’ responses to automated agents can lead to more effective deployment of these agents, thereby improving business performance (Adam et al. 2023).

The current research addresses this issue through an overlooked social status–focused perspective and examines how consumers’ acceptance of service robots differs between luxury services and mainstream services. One key motive driving customers’ pursuit of luxury brands is to feel superior in social status (Han et al. 2010). Different from the consumption of luxury goods in which customers acquire superior social status primarily from product ownership, the consump tion of luxury services is process-oriented, in which interpersonal interactions with service providers play an important role in defining luxury service experiences (Zeithaml et al. 1985). Specifically, when customers seek luxury service experiences, we expect that the superiority-seeking motive will manifest as the desire to be treated as a customer of higher status by service providers. Given that the respect and recognition of other humans in social relationships serve as an important foundation for feelings of superiority (Halevy et al. 2012), service robots—as nonhuman service providers— cannot make customers feel superior to the same extent as human service providers. Consequently, customers relative preference for service robots over human service providers is lower in luxury services than in mainstream services. We call it the robot disadvantage in luxury services (relative to mainstream services).

Adopting a multimethod approach, one secondary data analysis of review content, one field study, and three randomized controlled experiments demonstrate the robot disadvantage in luxury services and, more importantly, offer managerially actionable solutions to the robot disadvantage. Supporting our proposed mechanism, the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on relative preference for service robots over human service providers is mediated by the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers. Moreover, we provide two interventions—adopting a social equality–oriented positioning and imbuing the use of service robots with superior status—to effectively mitigate the robot disadvantage in luxury services.

The current work contributes to multiple streams of research. First, complementing the information systems (IS) literature that emphasizes design features (e.g., human-like attributes) of automated agents (Qiu and Benbasat 2009, Yuan and Dennis 2019, Gao et al. 2023, Han et al. 2023, Gnewuch et al. 2024), recent IS research has started to examine how decision contexts shape customers’ preference for automated versus human agents (Adam et al. 2023). We extend this emerging endeavor by revealing that customers’ preference for robotic versus human service providers shift across two common service contexts (luxury versus mainstream). Our focus on service robots—sophisticated automated agents in a physical form—broadens the scope of IS research primarily focusing on virtual automated agents (e.g., chatbots; Schanke et al. 2021, Chandra et al. 2022, Seymour et al. 2024).

Second, existing IS research on human–computer interaction distinguishes between customers’ responses to automated agents and those to human agents from the perspectives of identity threats (Mende et al. 2019, Han et al. 2023), lay beliefs (Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni et al. 2019, Luo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022, Adam et al. 2023), and communication styles (Hill et al. 2015, Gao et al. 2023, Gnewuch et al. 2024). Extending extant research, our work examines customers’ responses to automated agents from a novel social status–focused perspective. We identify service robots’ inability to satisfy one’s desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers as another mechanism that underlies resistance to service robots in the specific con text of luxury services.

Third, the current research contributes to the recent research on the role of information technology (IT) in luxury branding. Some new information technologies, such as user-design crowdsourcing platforms (Fuchs et al. 2013) and self-customization toolkits (Moreau et al. 2020), may not benefit luxury brands to the same extent as they benefit mainstream brands because these crowd-based technological advances dilute the essences of luxury brands contributed by professional designers (not by ordinary users). Adding to these findings with a novel social status–focused perspective, we show that service robots, as another advance in information technologies, are also less beneficial fo luxury services than for mainstream services. Focusing on luxury services, our research extends the emerging IS literature on artificial intelligence (AI)–based service automation that has primarily focused on mainstream services (Saldanha et al. 2022, Gnewuch et al. 2024).

Importantly, our findings provide implications for how a service firm should manage service robots according to its price image. Deploying robots may benefit mainstream services but ultimately backfire in luxury services, resulting in lower customer satisfaction. Thus, we suggest that luxury service firms should be more cautious about deploying service robots on the front line. Furthermore, we offer effective remedies for luxury service firms—highlighting the concept of social equality in communications or imbuing the use of service robots with elite social status—to mitigate customers’ resistance to service robots in luxury services. The managerial implications of our findings are applicable not only to large service chains that manage both luxury and mainstream service facilities (e.g., major hotel groups) but also to individual service firms that aim to customize service robot deployment based on the price that customers pay for a service.

## 2. Theoretical Background and Hypothesis Development 2.1. Service Robots

Service robots are increasingly deployed on the service front line to supplement human labor and interact directly with customers. Whereas most research on robots is conducted within the robotics field, focusing on industrial robots, behavioral research on custome responses to service robots has been scant until recently (Mende et al. 2019, Yam et al. 2021). Nonetheless, we can glean some relevant insights from several burgeoning streams of behavioral research on automation, machines, chatbots, algorithms, and AI given that service robots represent a specific application of automation and, in essence, are machines empowered by sophisticated algorithms and AI to facilitate customer–robot interactions (Choi and Wan 2021, Puntoni et al. 2021).

One general conclusion from the emerging literature is that customers may prefer human agents to automated agents for several reasons, which are related to either customers’ identities or their lay beliefs. From an identity perspective, customers may feel threatened by an automated agent’s ability to excel at or even outperform humans in task domains that define human identity (Mende et al. 2019). Such identity threats are aversive and may deter customers from interacting with service robots that impose identity threats. From a cognitive perspective, customers’ lay beliefs about automated agents may discourage their acceptance of these agents in certain decision contexts. For instance, many customers believe that AI-enabled automated agents cannot sufficiently attend to unique aspects of individual humans because AI is built on summary statistics of the entire population or specific customer segments (Longoni et al. 2019, Ge et al. 2021, Schanke et al. 2021, Fu¨ gener et al. 2022). Moreover, customers believe that automated agents lack the ability to process affective, experiential, and sensory inputs from the outside world (Castelo et al. 2019, Longoni and Cian 2022, Han et al. 2023) and are less knowledgeable and empathetic than humans (Luo et al. 2019, Gao et al. 2023, Gnewuch et al. 2024).

Through the lenses of identity threats and lay beliefs, the findings of emerging research on automated agents provide insightful implications for understanding customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers. The current research aims to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of this crucial issue by introducing a fresh theoretical perspective that considers the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers as a key determinant of customers’ differential responses to service robots and human service providers. Based on this new perspective, we predict that customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers will be lower in luxury services than in mainstream services. More importantly, we draw on the proposed mechanism to further provide remedies for overcoming the robot disadvantage in luxury services. We detail our discussions in the following sections.

## 2.2. Robots vs. Humans in Luxury Services

One important factor that makes customers willing to pay a price premium for luxury market offerings is the motive to feel superior (Rucker and Galinsky 2008, Han et al. 2010). Across different stages of the production-toconsumption chain, feelings of superiority could be driven by different sources (see Figure 1 for an illustration and Table 1 for a summary).

In the production stage, high production cost may contribute to customers’ feelings of superiority through the perception of possessing abundant resources given that the Latin origin of the word “luxury” means abundance or even waste of resources (Bellezza 2023). In the provision stage, the supply of luxury market offerings is limited and exclusive (Wang 2022). The exclusive access to luxury market offerings can boost customers feelings of superiority because exclusivity represents one’s superior status over other customers who have no such access. In the consumption stage, owning or using luxury market offerings has symbolic value because luxury market offerings serve as a status signal in front of others and can convey one’s taste to others (Han et al. 2010, Wang and Griskevicius 2014, Geva et al. 2017, Dubois et al. 2021). In contrast to the above existing perspectives, we examine an under-researched source of feelings of superiority by focusing on the service process of market offerings in this research.

Whereas most research focuses on luxury market offerings that have a physical form (i.e., luxury goods; Han et al. 2010, Wang and Griskevicius 2014, Moreau et al. 2020, Dubois et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2021), relatively scant attention is paid to luxury service offerings as well as differences between luxury goods and luxury services (Dion and Borraz 2017, Desmichel et al. 2020, Wirtz et al. 2020). Unlike physical goods, services are performances—complex processes in which service providers interact with customers at multiple touchpoints (Zeithaml et al. 1985, Ba et al. 2010). Compared with the consumption of physical goods, the consumption of services revolves around interpersonal interactions and, thus, is more relational (Saldanha et al. 2022). As such, customers’ feelings of superiority in luxury services result not only from high production costs, exclusivity, and symbolic value examined in extant research (summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1) but also from the interactive experiences of being served by service providers, which have not been systematically discussed and empirically tested in the literature on luxury market offerings.

In luxury service encounters, one practice to enhance customer satisfaction is to create the perceived status disparity between customers and service employees (Dion and Borraz 2017). In some situations, service employees may even put themselves into subordinate positions to elevate customers’ perception of privilege and entitlement (Sherman 2005). Thus, in luxury services, one important way to fulfill customers’ desire for superiority is to make them feel that they are indeed being served as a prestigious and esteemed customer of higher social status than service providers.

As summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1, our theoretical perspective based on being treated as a customer of superior status by service providers is different from several other existing perspectives in the literature on both luxury market offerings and automated agents in terms of the scope and focus. For instance, symbolic value as a driver of feelings of superiority is about expressing something related to oneself to other people (Granulo et al. 2021), which is oriented at shap ing others’ perceptions of oneself. In our theorization, being treated as a customer of superior status by service providers emphasizes one’s own feelings about oneself. As another driver, exclusivity focuses on the limited provision of market offerings and contributes to feelings of superiority through social comparison with other customers who have no access to the offerings (Cheema and Kaikati 2010), whereas being treated as a customer of superior status by service providers focuses on the service process and contributes to feelings of superiority through social comparison with service providers who put themselves in a lower position. Furthermore, whereas high cost, symbolic value, and exclusivity as sources of feelings of superiority are more relevant to the possession of physical goods, being treated as a customer of superior status by service providers, which this research examines, is uniquely applicable to service interactions and, thus, serves as a more relevant source of feelings of superiority in luxury services.

Because feelings of being treated as a customer of superior status are essentially rooted in social interactions with other humans who provide respect and recognition (Halevy et al. 2012), we expect that removing the human factor from service interactions impedes one’s feelings of being treated as a customer of supe rior status. For this reason, we propose that being served by nonhuman robots in service interactions is not able to make customers perceive themselves as being treated as a customer of superior status to the same extent as being served by humans. Given that the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers is an important driver of the pursuit of luxury service experiences, we predict that customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers is lower when they pay a higher price to acquire luxury service experiences than when they pay a lower price to purchase mainstream service experiences. Formally, we propose the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1. Price image (luxury versus mainstream) has a negative effect on customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers.

Hypothesis 2. The desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers mediates the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers.

## 2.3. Theoretically Motivated Interventions for Mitigating the Robot Disadvantage in Luxury Services

To further provide practical implications, we draw on our proposed mechanism to offer two theoretically motivated interventions for mitigating the robot disadvantage in luxury services. The desire to be in a superior position relative to other people reflects a belief in social hierarchy. Based on prior research showing that one’s belief in social hierarchy versus social equality is malleable and can be primed by situational cues (Gao et al. 2016), we expect that exposure to a social equality–oriented positioning instills the concept of social equality or makes this concept more accessible in customers’ minds. Furthermore, we propose that, when luxury service firms highlight a social equality–oriented positioning, the salience of social equality in one’s mind should suppress one’s desire to feel superior to human service providers and consequently increase customers relative preference for service robots over human service providers in luxury services.

Hypothesis 3. Adoption of a social equality–oriented positioning increases customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers in luxury services.

As summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1, among four sources of feelings of superiority, being treated as a customer of superior status by service providers is most relevant to the service process and, therefore, by default, plays the most crucial role in shaping customers’ preference for service robots versus human service providers. Nevertheless, if other sources of feelings of superiority are made salient and relevant to the focal service provision, they may potentially override the impact of being treated as a customer of superior status by service providers. Accordingly, directly associating the use of service robots with superior social status through other routes should mitigate the robot disadvantage in luxury services. In line with another type of exclusivity-based superiority as previously summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1, membership in an elite social group that has exclusive privileges can also make customers feel superior (Han et al. 2010). Thus, we predict that customers’ preference for service robots in luxury services increases if the use of service robots is framed as a privilege exclusive to the most elite group of customers. This is because the endowed membership in the elite customer group associated with using service robots can also boost feelings of superiority through the exclusivity-based route.

Hypothesis 4. Endowment of using service robots with superior status increases customers’ relative preference for ser vice robots over human service providers in luxury services.

## 3. Research Methodology

In a multimethod investigation (Venkatesh et al. 2013), one secondary data analysis, one field study, and three randomized controlled experiments support our central proposition that customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers decreases as the price image (luxury versus mainstream) of a service increases. Note that the baseline of this preference may depend on other factors, such as the nature of a service and customers’ general acceptance of new tech nologies in a specific geographical market. In any given circumstance, these factors together yield one of three outcomes: an absolute preference for service robots over human service providers, an indifference about them, or an absolute preference for human service providers over service robots.

Because of the aforementioned complexities, the focus of this research is not on absolute preference for service robots over human service providers under a specific circumstance. Instead, we are interested in how relative preference for service robots over human service providers shifts across services with different price images. If customers have an absolute preference for robots over humans in mainstream service encoun ters, we expect a decrease in the strength of or even a reversal of this preference in luxury service encounters, representing a decrease in relative preference for service robots over human service providers. If customers instead have an absolute preference for human service providers over service robots in mainstream service encounters, we expect this disliking of service robots to become even stronger in luxury service encounters, again yielding a decrease in relative preference for service robots over human service providers. Moreover, we examine the key dependent variable (i.e., relative preference for service robots over human service providers) with two complementary operationalizations across different studies: satisfaction with the service performed by either a robot or a human at the postconsumption stage (Study 1) and choice between a robot and a human at the decision stage (Studies 2–5; see Figure 2 for the conceptual framework and empirical package).

In Study 1, we retrieved data from a leading travel website in China. We matched hotels through propensity score matching (PSM) and conducted a differencein-differences (DiD) analysis of hotel-level guest ratings as a proxy for service satisfaction. The study shows that the effect of service robot deployment on service satisfaction becomes more negative as price image becomes more luxurious such that service robot deployment increases customer satisfaction with three-star hotels but decreases customer satisfaction with five-star hotels. Study 2 is a field study conducted at a hotel, which provides further real-world evidence for our proposed effect by demonstrating that hotel guests’ real choice of a service robot over a human service provider decreases as the price image of the guests’ room increases from a mainstream image to a more luxurious image.

Study 3 provides causal evidence for our proposition by manipulating price image in a randomized controlled experiment. Moreover, Study 3 provides mediating evidence for the proposed mechanism. This study shows that the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on relative preference for service robots over human service providers is mediated by the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers, controlling for other factors related to the cost, exclusivity, and symbolic value of the focal service.

Based on our proposed mechanism, the last two studies are aimed at developing interventions for mitigating the robot disadvantage and increasing customers’ preference for service robots in luxury services. Focusing on decreasing customers’ desire to be treated as a customer of superior status in luxury services, Study 4 demonstrates that, when a luxury service firm adopts social equality–oriented positioning, the robot disadvantage in luxury services can be attenuated. Focusing on enabling service robots to boost customers’ feelings of superiority in luxury services, Study 5 shows that endowing the use of service robots with superior status through associating users of service robots with an exclusive elite customer group can increase customers’ preference for service robots in luxury services.

## 4. Study 1: Preliminary Evidence from a Leading Travel Website in China

Study 1 aims to provide preliminary real-world evidence for our proposition that customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers decreases as the price image of a service increases. We retrieved data from Ctrip (the Chinese site of the Trip. com Group), which is the largest comprehensive travel website in China. In this study, we focus on its hotel booking service, in which customers can book hotel rooms, post review comments, and rate hotels after completing their stays.

## 4.1. Data

Following prior IS research that utilizes data from the same website (Chen et al. 2019), we developed a crawler using Python to retrieve data about hotels located in 10 major cities (e.g., Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, etc.) in China from Ctrip. We collected customer reviews (including guests’ rating, review text, and posting date) and other information (including review volume, hotel type, and hotel age) about all three-, four-, and five-star hotels from these cities. This hotel classification was based on the hotel star rating scheme developed by and shown on Ctrip. We operationalized price image as hotel star rating such that a higher star rating represents a more luxurious price image (i.e., five-star hotels are more luxurious than four- and three-star hotels). Ou data set contains 74,016 observations for 4,112 properties that were in operation continuously between July 2018 and December 2019 (18 months). Among these properties, 73.44% were three-star hotels, 18.10% were four-star hotels, and 8.46% were five-star hotels.

## 4.4. Complementary, Cross-Sectional Analysis

In our DiD analysis, service robot deployment was identified based on review comments. It is possible that the actual time at which the robot deployment started at a hotel and the time at which the robots were first mentioned in the review comments were not perfectly aligned. Relatedly, we could not exclude the possibility that a hotel deployed but stopped using service robots later. To address these issues, we conducted another cross-sectional data analysis, in which we employed the service robot tag currently available on Ctrip to operationalize service robot adoption (see Online Appendix D for the detail of this cross-sectional analysis). This analysis also showed that the impact of service robot deployment on average guest rating as a proxy for service satisfaction became less positive as the hotel’s price image became more luxurious.

Of note, several limitations may exist in this complementary, cross-sectional data analysis. First, this analysis relies on the assumption that service robots have been available during the entire data collection period, whereas the service robot tag only indicates the availability of service robots at the moment. Second, the update of the service robot tag may not be completely real time. Third, we cannot exclude the possibility that hotels deployed service robots without adding them to the list of their facilities. Despite each having its own limitations, this cross-sectional analysis and our main DiD analysis complement each other and provide convergent evidence for our core proposition.

## 4.5. Discussion

Analyzing data from a leading travel website in China, we find that the association between a hotel’s service robot deployment and its overall guest rating as a proxy for service satisfaction became more negative as the hotel’s price image became more luxurious (Hypothesis 1). Although consistent with our central proposition, the empirical evidence from Study 1 should be interpreted as preliminary given several limitations. First, the models, tasks, and potential quality differences related to service robots were not reflected in the review comments or revealed by the service robot tag. Second, we operationalized service satisfaction as guest ratings for a hotel. This operationalization only serves as a proxy given that not all guests submitting review ratings might have actually used service robots and that guest ratings can also be influenced by other aspects of service provision beyond service robots. To address these limitations, we conducted a field study at a hotel to directly measure hotel guests preference between a service robot and a human service provider.

## 5. Study 2: Field Study on Hotel Guests’ Choice

Study 2 is a field study in which we collaborated with a hotel to provide direct real-world evidence for the core effect. This study operationalizes customers’ preference for service providers as a choice between a robot and a human and examines how the choice varies with a hotel room’s price image.

## 5.1. Method

We conducted the field study at a hotel in Beijing, China. On Ctrip, focused on in Study 1, the hotel is classified as a four-star hotel, which offers different types of rooms for different prices. At this hotel, basic rooms provide mainstream services for a standard price, whereas a selection of higher end rooms that offer more space and various design themes are available for higher prices. Given this hotel’s room and pricing system, guests who book a more expensive guest room are also more likely to consider their purchased service as more luxurious. Thus, the unique setting of this four-star hotel provides a feasible and conservative ground for our hypothesis testing.

At the time of the study, the hotel had one service robot available to deliver small items (e.g., disposable toiletry kits, packages that guests scheduled with couriers, food sealed in takeout boxes that guests ordered from restaurants outside the hotel) to guest rooms upon request. At this hotel, the staff normally decided whether the service robot or an employee would deliver small items to guest rooms, depending on the availability of the robot. During our three-week study period, however, the hotel staff offered guests a choice between the service robot and a human server for each delivery request. ## 6. Study 3: The Mediating Role of Desire to Be Treated as a Customer of Superior Status

The purpose of Study 3 is threefold. First, complementing Studies 1 and 2 that provide initial real-world evidence for our proposed effect, Study 3 aims to establish the causality of the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers in a randomized controlled experiment.

Second, Study 3 aims to provide process evidence for our proposed mechanism via a mediation approach. We predict that customers have a stronger desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers in luxury service encounters than in mainstream service encounters, and this desire, in turn, decreases customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers (Hypothesis 2).

Third, another purpose of Study 3 is to show that our proposed mechanism based on the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers adequately explains the observed effect in a parallel mediation analysis even when other drivers of feelings of superiority, including cost, exclusivity, and symbolic value as previously summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1, are controlled for.

## 6.1. Method

This study adopted a single-factor, two-cell (price image: luxury versus mainstream) between-subjects design. Sim et al. (2022) recommended that an adequate sample size for a mediation model with two parallel mediators is around 393, and a larger sample size is required when more paths need to be estimated. Because we examined four parallel mediators (doubling the number of two parallel mediators), we aimed to recruit 800 U.S.-based participants from Prolific and obtained 802 responses (480 females; $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 3 8 . 9 8 ,$ $S D = 1 3 . 5 2 )$ . We did not exclude any data.

6.1.1. Manipulation. At the beginning of the study, participants worked on a writing task that manipulated price image (see Online Appendix E for the manipulation). In the mainstream (luxury) condition, participants imagined that they were on vacation and had just checked into a budget (luxury) hotel. They were asked to write about their expectation about how much a room would cost per night and how staff would treat them at a budget (luxury) hotel. A pretest for the manipulation check (N � 50) using an independent sample from the same participant population as in the main study confirmed that participants indeed perceived the hotel as more luxurious in the luxury condition than in the mainstream condition $( M _ { \mathrm { l u x u r y } } =$ 5.84, SD � 0.85 versus $M _ { \mathrm { m a i n s t r e a m } } = 2 . 5 2$ , SD � 0.96; F(1, 48) � 167.01, p < 0.001; 1 � not luxurious at all, 7 � very much luxurious).

6.1.2. Dependent Measure. After the writing task, participants learned that advanced robot servers are available to deliver items to guest rooms. Next, participants imagined that they needed some extra bottled water. Then, they chose between a robot server and a human server for the delivery. This binary choice served as the dependent measure of this study. We expected that participants’ relative preference for the robot server over the human server would be lower in the luxury condition than in the mainstream condition (Hypothesis 1).

6.1.3. Measure of the Mediator of Focal Interest. After indicating their choice between a robot server and a human server, participants completed a few additional measures. Specifically, we measured participants’ desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers with two items on a seven-point scale (1 � not at all, 7 � very much): To what extent do you desire to be served by hotel staff who act respectfully as if you have a higher status than them? To what extent do you desire to be served by hotel staff who behave respectfully in a subordinate manner toward you? These were averaged and served as the mediator of focal interest (α � 0.75).

6.1.4. Measures of Other Possible Accounts. To test our proposed explanation against several other drivers of feelings of superiority as previously summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1, we further measured participants’ considerations about the exclusivity (To what extent do you perceive your guest experiences as “exclusive,” “novel,” and “unique?;” α � 0.94), symbolic value (To what extent do you perceive that your stay provides symbolic value?; Granulo et al. 2021), and cost reduction (To what extent do you perceive that the hotel cuts its cost when serving you?; Castelo et al. 2023), regarding the hotel about which they were asked to write on a seven-point scale (1 � not at all, 7 � very much).

6.1.5. Measures of Individual Differences. At the end of the survey, we measured participants’ prior experience with service robots (How familiar are you with service robots in general? How frequently have you encountered service robots in your daily life? How much experience do you have in being served by service robots?) on a seven-point scale (1 � not at all, 7 � very much; α � 0.88). Finally, we recorded participants’ demographic information (gender, age, and income). We expected the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers to explain the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on relative preference for robots over humans even when all the other possible accounts and individual difference variables mentioned above are controlled for (Hypothesis 2).

## 6.2. Results

6.2.1. Relative Preference. In a logistic regression with prior experience, gender, age, and income included as covariates, the choice share of the robot server for bottled water delivery was lower in the luxury condition (52.5%) than in the mainstream condition (70.7%; b � �0.80, Wald $\chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 2 8 . 7 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , again supporting Hypothesis 1.

6.2.2. Mediation Analysis. We conducted a bootstrapbased mediation analysis (5,000 resamples) using the PROCESS macro (Model 4; Hayes 2022). In this model, relative preference (0 � human, 1 � robot) was the binary dependent variable, and price image (0 � mainstream, 1 � luxury) was the independent variable. The PROCESS macro automatically adopted a logistic regression model when processing a binary choice dependent measure.

We included the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers (our focal mediator) as well as considerations of cost reduction, exclusivity, and symbolic value as four parallel mediators in the same mediation model (see Table F1 in Online Appendix F for the cell means of all process variables). We also included prior experience, gender, age, and income as covariates.

The parallel mediation analysis revealed that, among the four potential process variables, only desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers (our focal mediator; $b = - 0 . 0 4 , \enspace \dot { S E } = 0 . 0 2 ;$ 95% confidence interval (CI): �0.0944, �0.0045) mediated the effect of price image on relative preference for the robot server (see Figure 3 for path coefficients). None of the considerations of cost reduction (95% CI: �0.1826, 0.0791), exclusivity (95% CI: �0.1766, 0.4435), and symbolic value (95% CI: –0.2124, 0.1256) produced a significant mediating effect (see Figure 3 for path coefficients).

Central to our core proposition and consistent with our expectation, desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers was stronger in the luxury condition than in the mainstream condition $( M _ { \mathrm { { l u x u r y } } } { = } 3 . 7 5 , S D { = } 1 . 8 6$ versus $M _ { \mathrm { m a i n s t r e a m } } = 3 . 3 8 ,$ $S D = 1 . { \dot { 7 } } 3 ; b = 0 . 3 1 , S E = 0 . 1 3 , t = 2 . 4 5 , p = 0 . 0 1 4 )$ . Furthermore, this stronger desire reduced relative preference for the robot server $( b = - 0 . 1 3 , S E = 0 . 0 5 , z = - 2 . 8 2 , p =$ 0.005), explaining why the relative preference for the robot server was lower in the luxury condition than in the mainstream condition. Taken together, these results support Hypothesis 2.

## 6.3. Discussion

In a randomized controlled experiment, Study 3 demonstrates the causal effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers. Importantly, with a mediation approach, Study 3 shows that one’s desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers explained a lower relative preference for service robots over human service providers in luxury (versus mainstream) service encounters, importantly, even when other possible mechanisms and various individual difference variables were controlled for. The novel mechanism demonstrated in Study 3 provides us with a fresh perspective on the effective management of service robots. Based on the proposed mechanism, the two final studies further inform practitioners of how they could effectively mitigate the robot disadvantage and improve customers’ acceptance of service robots in luxury services.

## 7. Study 4: Adopting a Social Equality–Oriented Positioning

Study 3 shows that customers have a lower relative preference for service robots over human service providers in luxury service encounters because customers have a desire to be treated as a customer of superior status, which can only be satisfied by human service providers who put themselves in a subordinate position but cannot be satisfied by robots. Following this logic, the robot disadvantage in luxury services should be mitigated if service firms could make the concept of social equality accessible in customers’ minds to mitigate their resistance to service robots in luxury service encounters. Study 4 explores this possibility by focusing on messages that a service firm can adopt and emphasize in communications to customers.

Many luxury hotels have joined the diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives and communicated their commitment to DEI to key stakeholders (Myles 2022). For instance, Rosewood, a global luxury hotel chain, emphasizes its “belief in equal access to opportunity” on its official website. Against this backdrop, we examine a potential consequence of exposure to social equality–related messages on customers’ adoption of service robots. We expect that exposure to a social equality–oriented positioning will increase preference for service robots over human service staff in luxury services (Hypothesis 3).

## 7.1. Method

This study employed a single-factor, three-cell (price image: mainstream, luxury, versus luxury with a social equality–oriented positioning) between-subjects design. For this study design, G\*Power (Faul et al. 2009) suggested a minimal sample size of 248 (power � 0.95, effect size � 0.025). We did not exclude any data. We adopted the same price image manipulation and choice measure as in Study 3. In addition to the mainstream and luxury conditions, we included a third condition, which was identical to the luxury condition with one exception: participants were exposed to a statement of the hotel’s social equality–oriented positioning (“Social equality defines human society. We provide accommodation services tailored to your unique position in an equal world”—see Online Appendix E.) Providing convergent support for our proposed mechanism, Study 4 shows that suppressing the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by exposing customers to a social equality–oriented positioning statement increases customers’ relative preference fo service robots over human service providers in luxury services. ## 8. Study 5: Endowing the Use of Service Robots with Superior Status

Study 5 has two main purposes. First, Study 5 aims to test the effectiveness of another theoretically motivated intervention that can mitigate the robot disadvantage in luxury services. If we endow the use of service robots with superior status, we should be able to reduce customers’ relatively negative reaction to service robots in luxury service contexts (Hypothesis 4). Given that feelings of superiority can be instilled through membership in an elite group that has exclusive privileges (Han et al. 2010), we expect that framing the use of service robots as a privilege exclusive to an elite customer group should improve relative preference for service robots in luxury services.

Second, Studies 2–4 focus on the delivery service for small items, which is simple and standardized. Study 5 further generalizes our proposed effects to another service scenario that involves a higher level of complexity and requires a higher level of expertise. Specifically, Study 5 examines massage robots, which have recently appeared in the hospitality industries (Cairns 2021). Because of a higher level of complexity involved and expertise required regarding the focal massage service, we expect that the absolute preference for service robots will be much lower compared with those in our other studies. Even so, we should still observe that customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers decreases when we switch from the mainstream service context to the luxury service context (Hypothesis 1) and that imbuing the use of a service robot with superior status can increase relative preference for service robots in luxury services (Hypothesis 4).

## 8.3. Discussion

By examining massage robots as another type of service robot, Study 5 provides further evidence for the proposed effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers. Because the complimentary nature of the massage session highlighted in the study instruction indicated that there was no extra charge for the service, our findings cannot be explained by differences in participants’ expectations about charges for the service robot. Importantly, this study demonstrates another simple strategy—framing the use of service robots as a privilege of an elite customer group—which can increase preference for service robots in luxury services.

Taken together, our findings reveal a full picture of how different sources of feelings of superiority influence customers’ acceptance of service robots. Specifically, when the access to service robots is not exclusive, customers’ preference for service robots in luxury services is lower because service robots cannot make customers feel as if they are being treated as a customer of superior status by service providers (as the default mechanism that is most relevant to service interactions; Studies 1 � 4). In contrast, when the exclusive access to service robots is made salient and, thus, overrides the default mechanism, customers’ preference for service robots in luxury services is boosted (Study 5).

## 9. General Discussion

One secondary data analysis (Study 1), one field study (Study 2), and three randomized controlled experiments (Studies 3–5) collectively demonstrate that customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers is lower in luxury services than in mainstream services. Our findings are robust across both services that are simple and standardized (i.e., small item delivery in Study 2 and bottled water delivery in Studies 3 and 4) and services that involve higher levels of complexity (i.e., massage in Study 5). Furthermore, convergent evidence supports our proposed mechanism. Specifically, the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on relative preference for service robots over human service providers is mediated by the stronger desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers in luxury services (Study 3). Moreover, when a luxury service firm adopts a social equality–oriented positioning (Study 4) and when the use of service robots is endowed with superior status (Study 5), customers relative preference for service robots in luxury service encounters can be enhanced.

## 9.1. Contributions to Research

Automated agents have attracted a lot of attention from IS researchers. Our research broadens the IS literature on automated agents in several ways. First, whereas prior IS research primarily focuses on virtual automated agents, such as chatbots (Verhagen et al. 2014, Yuan and Dennis 2019, Schanke et al. 2021, Adam et al. 2023, Gao et al. 2023, Han et al. 2023, Gnewuch et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2024), scant IS research has been conducted on service robots, which have a technologically sophisticated physical form and are increasingly deployed on the service front line. Our research adds to the IS literature on automated agents by examining the deployment of service robots, which other related business disciplines, such as marketing (Mende et al. 2019) and management (Yam et al. 2021, Tang et al. 2022), have recently started to investigate.

Second, extant IS research has put emphasis on design features of automated agents, for instance, how human-like automated agents should be, whether automated agents should express emotions, or whether the information related to AI augmentation should be disclosed to users (Qiu and Benbasat 2009, Verhagen et al. 2014, Araujo 2018, Luo et al. 2019, Yuan and Dennis 2019, Choi and Wan 2021, Schanke et al. 2021, Gao et al. 2023, Han et al. 2023, Gnewuch et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2024). To complement existing research and facilitate the deployment of automated agents, an emerging stream of IS research has begun to examine how deci sion contexts affect customers’ reactions to automated agents. For instance, Adam et al. (2023) demonstrate that, compared with human sales agents, automated sales agents are more preferred in the contact information provision stage than in the initial interest indication stage. Relatedly, Schanke et al. (2021) suggest that automated agents with human-like features could be more effective when the focal sales task is to lead to purchase conversion than when the focal task is to negotiate prices with customers. We join this emerging decision context–focused stream of IS research on automated agents and examine how two common decision contexts (luxury versus mainstream services) influence customers’ preference for service robots over human service providers.

Third, by focusing on the unique setting of luxury services, the current research broadens the emerging IS literature on mechanisms underlying customers responses to automated agents during human–AI interactions. We propose and find evidence for a new mechanism that underlies a lower preference for service robots in luxury services than in mainstream services. We theorize that the human identity of a service provider is an important source of superiority that customers can attain in luxury services. Thus, replacing human service providers with nonhuman service robots makes it more difficult for customers to feel superior to service providers in their pursuit of luxury service experiences. Moreover, we demonstrate that our proposed mechanism has unique predictive power in explaining our proposed effect. In Study 3, we directly measured participants’ desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers as well as their other considerations. A parallel mediation model showed that our proposed account well explained the focal effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on relative preference for robots over humans even when controlling for other possible accounts documented in the literature and a set of individual difference variables. With these findings, the current work complements research on responses to automated agents by identifying the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers as a novel factor that explains aversion to robots relative to humans in service settings. Relatedly, our findings also contribute to the broad literature on luxury market offerings by gaining further insights into the sources of feelings of superiority. Our findings emphasize the superior feelings that customers derive from their interactions with service providers in the service process, which has been under-researched in literature that primarily focuses on the production, provision, and consumption of luxury market offerings.

With a specific focus on luxury services, our research also contributes to the emerging literature on the role of IT in service encounters (Saldanha et al. 2022, Gnewuch et al. 2024). The current research taps into an important distinction between luxury market offerings and mainstream market offerings. Specifically, our findings are consistent with a notion in recent research that luxury firms may not benefit (or may even suffer losses) from adopting trendy advances in information technologies, such as user-design crowdsourcing platforms (Fuchs et al. 2013) and self-customization toolkits (Moreau et al. 2020), which hurt the essence of a luxury brand defined by professional designers even though these technologies benefit mainstream market offerings. Whereas this stream of research focuses on the design and creation of market offerings, our research sheds light on another drawback of new information technologies for luxury market offerings from a novel service process perspective. Our research shows that replacing human service employees with nonhuman service robots on the service front line demolishes an important source of customers’ feelings of superiority in luxury service encounters.

## 9.2. Implications for Practice

Service robotics is renovating service industries and benefiting various stakeholders in numerous ways. Nevertheless, roboticization is not a panacea for all service firms, especially when robots are deployed on the front line of luxury services. In our empirical analysis, we found that customers’ relative preference for service robots over human service providers is lower in luxury (versus mainstream) service contexts, and service robot deployment may even decrease customer satisfaction with luxury services. These findings offer important cautions for luxury service firms that are tempted to supplement their frontline service staff with a robotic labor force. Particularly, large service chains that manage both luxury and mainstream service facilities (e.g., major hotel groups operating both luxury and mainstream properties) should strategically deploy service robots according to the price image of the service provided.

Moreover, some firms may offer a large selection of basic services and a small selection of luxury services at the same time for different customers. We advise these firms to adopt a fine-grained perspective on service robot deployment, ideally customizing the type of service providers according to the price level of the service offerings. Especially for customers who pay a price premium for luxury service experiences, firms should try to assign human service providers rather than service robots to enhance the experience of being treated as a customer of superior status.

More importantly, our research offers remedies that can make service robots more palatable even on the front line of luxury service encounters. First, our Study 4 sheds light on the type of message that can be put in communications to customers to enhance their acceptance of service robots in luxury services. Specifically, luxury service firms could emphasize values related to social equality, such as adopting a social equality–oriented positioning, to alleviate pushbacks from customers against service robots in luxury service encounters. Second, our findings suggest that luxury service firms can encourage customers to use service robots by framing the use of service robots as a privilege for an elite customer group. This framing bestows superiority on customers who embrace the services performed by the robots. We tested one implementation of this strategy in Study 5 and expect that other viable methods for endowing the use of service robots with superior status should similarly increase customers’ acceptance of service robots in luxury services.

## 9.3. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

The current research offers an initial demonstration of the effect of price image (luxury versus mainstream) on customers’ preference for service robots versus their human counterparts. Several limitations of our research suggest directions for future research.

First, although our studies focus on hospitality industries, we expect that our findings are applicable to other service domains, such as healthcare and finance, in which customers who pay a price premium also have a similar need for superiority in their pursuit of upscale service experiences. In addition, the current research did not distinguish different types of luxury (e.g., ultimate luxury versus mass luxury), in which customers may have different expectations, and accordingly, the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers may take more specific forms. Future research can further empirically generalize our findings to other types of luxury service encounters.

Second, in this research, we generalized our findings across delivery robots (Studies 2 � 4) and massage robots (Study 5), which differ in the level of the complexity of the service provided. Whether our findings can be extended to other specific roles of service robots awaits further empirical investigation. Future research may examine the full range of service robots (e.g., robots for concierge services or interactive entertainment) by employing a more nuanced approach.

Third, whereas the empirical evidence documented in our studies supports the proposed mechanism based on the desire to be treated as a customer of superior status by service providers, we acknowledge that feelings of superiority can be achieved through multiple routes as previously summarized in Table 1 and Figure 1 at the beginning of this paper. We further show that activating the link between service robots and exclusivity as one driver of feelings of superiority can effectively mitigate the robot disadvantage in luxury services in Study 5. Future research can attempt to design other interventions to enhance acceptance of service robots in luxury services.
