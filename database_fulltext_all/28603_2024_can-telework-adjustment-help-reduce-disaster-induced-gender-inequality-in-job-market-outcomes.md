# Can Telework Adjustment Help Reduce Disaster-Induced Gender Inequality in Job Market Outcomes?

Abstract. Disasters usually induce sudden changes to the work and home environment with significant consequences for workers’ labor market outcomes (i.e., unemployment, work absence, and layoff). Prior literature suggests that female workers tend to be more affected by such changes (e.g., the limited availability of childcare and/or domestic services) as female workers often (or are expected to) shoulder more childcare and/or household responsibilities. As a result, disasters tend to affect female workers more because of their higher needs/preferences for flexibility and time to cope with the changes induced by disasters, leading to increased gender inequality during disasters. In such a case, telework adjustment has emerged as a silver lining by providing workers with more flexibility and helping them meet their needs/preferences. This paper investigates if there is any gender difference in telework adjustment as a response to the disaster and whether and to what extent telework adjustment can reduce the gender inequality induced by disasters, taking the COVID-19 disaster as an example. Our analysis shows that (1) comparing workers in the same industry and holding the same occupation, we find that female workers’ telework adjustment rate is more responsive to external constraints and is 7% higher than that of male workers. (2) Telework adjustment helps reduce gender inequality in labor market outcomes via two means: (i) the higher telework adjustment rate among female workers (which reduces gender inequality by 25.48%) and (ii) the stronger marginal effect of telework adjustment on female workers (which reduces gender inequality by 31.94%). (3) Better digital infrastructure can enhance the mitigating effect of telework adjustment. Our findings are robust to alternative measures of constraints or telework, which suggests the generalizability of our results to other disasters or disruptions that induce changes and increase workers’ needs/preferences for flexibility and time. Our study advances the literature on how information technologies can be leveraged to mitigate disaster-induced gender inequality in the labor market.

## 1. Introduction

The COVID-19 disaster significantly changed the way people live and work. Since the unexpected breakout of the COVID-19 disaster in March 2020, millions of Americans have been asked by the government to do what would have been unthinkable before: don’t go to the workplace, don’t go to school, don’t leave the house unless they have to.

Whereas such mobility restriction policies were effective in slowing down the spread of the COVID-19 virus, they engendered a tidal wave of changes in people’s lives and their work environment. On the one hand, COVID-19 imposed acute external constraints on people because of the limited availability of childcare and food services, which significantly increased people’s needs or preferences for flexibility and time. The burden falls disproportionately on the shoulders of female workers, who, on average, spend 15 hours per week more on unpaid domestic work than their male counterparts.<sup>1</sup> As a result, recent studies suggest that the COVID-19 disaster has a stronger negative impact on female workers’ working hours and employment (Collins et al. 2021, Adams-Prassl et al. 2022, Couch et al. 2022). Prior disaster studies show a similar pattern in which females, as a result of their caregiving role and accordingly more stringent family-related needs/preferences during disasters, are at greater risk in many disaster situations (Fothergill 1996).

On the other hand, COVID-19 led to the closure of many business premises and forced many previously off-line tasks to be performed remotely, which leads to a massive trend of telework. Here, telework refers to a work flexibility arrangement under which an employee performs job duties and responsibilities from an approved worksite other than the traditional workplace.<sup>2</sup> Based on the May 2020 current population survey (CPS), about 35% of employed individuals teleworked for pay because of COVID-19. Apart from avoiding virus transmission in the workplace, telework can help workers maintain their productivity and keep their jobs, adapting to the new changes brought by COVID-19 to their work environment. This is also supported by recent studies reporting that occupations wherein workers can do a large share of tasks remotely are significantly associated with less disasterinduced unemployment (Adams-Prassl et al. 2022, Papanikolaou and Schmidt 2022).

However, despite the great potential for telework to help workers adapt to the new work environment reshaped by disasters, to what degree telework can meet their needs or preferences for flexibility and time is still an open question. More importantly, although there is considerable agreement on the gender difference in needs or preferences in the disaster literature, there is a void in the literature on whether telework can better support female workers’ needs or preferences and, thus, reduce disaster-induced gender inequality.

We draw on the theory of work adjustment (TWA) to theorize the gender difference in needs or preferences during a disaster and the effect of telework on disaster-induced gender inequality (e.g., Lofquist and Dawis 1978, Dawis 1994). We conceptualize disaster-induced telework as a form of information technology (IT)–enabled work adjustment and work adjustment as an evolving interplay between individuals and their work environment, which underscores the continuous adjustment required in response to significant environmental shifts, such as those introduced by disasters. Unlike prior studies, such as Couch et al. (2022), which view teleworkability as an occupational characteristic and link the gender employment gap to occupational gender segregation, we focus on telework adjustment. This involves analyzing the impact of the actual adaptation to telework during disasters, controlling for occupational gender segregation by matching female and male workers in the same occupation and industry.

Based on TWA, workers’ needs/preferences largely affect their adjustment decisions and benefit from the adjustment. Following this, because of the gender difference in needs/preferences for flexibility and time, telework adjustment may not hold the same appeal for female and male workers, and its effect is also likely to depend on their genders. Specifically, if telework adjustment can better serve female workers’ needs or preferences, it should be more appealing to female workers than male workers, especially when there is an acute increase in their needs or preferences caused by disasterinduced external constraints (e.g., the closure of childcare centers or restaurants). Furthermore, if telework adjustment can more adequately meet female workers needs or preferences, its effect in mitigating the negative impact of disasters on labor market outcomes should be stronger for female workers. This suggests that telework adjustment can reduce disaster-induced gender employment inequality. In addition, because the feasibility of teleworking is highly dependent on the digital infrastructure in their homes (Dettling 2017), the local digital infrastructure level could potentially moderate the mitigation effect of telework adjustment. Accordingly, the following research questions are raised:

1. (Needs-/preferences-based telework adjustment) Are females more likely to make a telework adjustment during the COVID-19 disaster? How do external constraints, such as childcare closure, affect the difference in telework adjustment across genders?

2. (Differential effect of telework adjustment across genders) Is the effect of telework adjustment in mitigating the negative impact of disasters stronger for female workers?

3. (Differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on digital infrastructure) To what extent does digital infrastructure play a role in strengthening the mitigation effect of telework adjustment?

To answer our research questions, we compile a rich data set, including individual demographic and employmentrelated data from the CPS and various supplementary data sets. Given our focus on reducing disaster-induced gender inequality, we further ensure the comparability between female and male workers by employing the coarsened exact matching (CEM) approach to match female and male workers based on various characteristics (i.e., occupation, industry, education, part-/full-time employment status, family income group, age group, marital status, childcare constraint, year of the individual’s first survey, and whether the first survey is after the outbreak of COVID-19).

Based on the individual-level telework adjustment data, we first examine if there is a gender difference in the adjustment rate and whether such a gender difference is related to external constraints. Answers to these questions provide insights on the potential differential needs/preferences for time and flexibility across genders and why/how telework adjustment may help address disaster-induced gender inequality. Moreover, we lever age the variation in telework adjustment rate across occupations and genders and employ a difference-indifferences (DID) model to estimate the effect of telework adjustment in mitigating the disaster-induced unemployment problem. We then use a difference-in-difference-in-differences (DDD) model to investigate if there is a differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on gender or digital infrastructure.

This study provides novel insights regarding the effect of telework adjustment on disaster management and gender inequality. First, we find that, even after matching occupation, industry, residence area, etc., female workers are more likely to take up telework adjustment because of the COVID-19 disaster than their male counterparts. Moreover, female workers are more likely to make a telework adjustment when they face higher external constraints (proxied by the childcare closure rate). These results provide support for the increased needs/preference for time and flexibility by female workers,<sup>3</sup> resonating with Goldin’s (2014) advocacy for enhanced flexibility. Second, we find that telework adjustment not only can mitigate the unemployment problem during the COVID-19 disaster, but also is more effective in mitigating female unemployment than male unemployment. Specifically, the mitigating effect of telework adjustment is 1.4 times stronger for female workers than male workers in reducing unemployment, 1.6 times stronger in reducing work absence, and 1.4 times stronger in reducing layoffs. Third, the mitigation effect of telework adjustment is stronger in geographic areas with better digital infrastructure. In addition, we find that females from low-income families are hit harder by the COVID-19 disaster, and the mitigation effect of telework adjustment is stronger for them than for workers in other gender or income groups, suggesting that it contributes to social well-being. Furthermore, we find that telework adjustment can help reduce gender inequality in labor market outcomes not only because of the higher telework adjustment rate among female workers (which reduces gender inequality by 25.48%), but also because of the stronger marginal effect of telework adjustment on female workers (which reduces gender inequality by 31.94%).

We find highly consistent results when we use other measures of external constraints as the proxy for the negative shock of disasters, which suggests that our results are potentially generalizable to other disasters or disruptions that may pose constraints and increase workers needs or preferences for flexibility and time. For example, there is anecdotal evidence suggesting that some workers have made a telework adjustment when facing other types of disasters, including man-made disasters (e.g., the 9/11 attacks)<sup>4</sup> and natural disasters (e.g., earthquakes) (Donnelly and Proctor-Thomson 2015).

We make several contributions to the literature. First, our study contributes to the disaster management literature by proposing a theory-driven framework to better understand the gender differences in disaster vulnerability and coping strategies. Specifically, to answer Fothergill’s (1996) call for a theoretical perspective to help explain the gender differences in disaster situations, we systematically theorize and validate that both the gender difference in telework adjustment and the differential effect of telework adjustment are rooted in the gender difference in needs or preferences for flexibility and time. As female workers’ needs or preferences are disproportionately affected by disaster-induced external constraints, such constraints can significantly increase female workers’ telework adjustment and strengthen the mitigation effect of telework adjustment on them. Overall, our study calls for additional attention to the needs or preferences of female workers in the disaster response and recovery phases.

Second, we contribute to the telework literature by underscoring the impact of actual telework adjustment in reducing disaster-induced gender inequality. Recen research demonstrates the positive effect of teleworkabil ity on the resilience of business operations in the face of catastrophic events (e.g., Bai et al. 2021, Adams-Prassl et al. 2022) as well as the role of occupational gender segregation in employment (Couch et al. 2022). However, findings based upon teleworkability as an occupation characteristic can only suggest that different labor out comes are observed/expected for different occupations, as in Couch et al. (2022), but cannot separate the effect of actual telework from occupation characteristics. Addi tionally, high teleworkability of a job does not imply that everyone holding the same job teleworks to the same extent. Our study is among the first studies to establish and quantify the effect of actual telework adjustment in reducing the disaster-induced gender employment inequality, accounting for occupational teleworkability and occupational gender segregation. Moreover, our study reveals the dual roles of telework adjustment with decomposition analysis: (i) the differential endowment effect because of the higher telework adjustment rate among female workers and (ii) the heterogeneous marginal effect because of the stronger effect of telework adjustment on female workers. This finding yields important insights for public policy aimed at addressing gender inequality.

Third, our study adds to the literature on the value of IT by drawing attention to the importance of digital infrastructure, which is critical to enhancing the mitigation effect of telework adjustment during catastrophic events. Extending the prior literature on the value of IT in improving productivity (Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1994, Chan 2000) and stock returns (Bai et al. 2021), we show that good digital infrastructure can strengthen the effect of telework adjustment in reducing disaster-induced gender inequality. Our finding has significant policy implications for IT infrastructure investment.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Disasters and Gender

Disasters pose a big threat to work continuity and job stability. When a disaster strikes, many jobs are at risk for being lost or interrupted. Taking COVID-19 as an example, recent studies find that the COVID-19 disaster, along with mobility restriction policies, has a profound effect on the economy and labor market at large as well as remarkable unequal impacts among workers. The welfare loss because of the nonworking days under mobility restrictions amounts to \$7 trillion per year (Mulligan 2021). Along with the massive shutdown, the mobility restriction policies are found to lead to a plummet in job vacancies (Forsythe et al. 2020) and a jump in the unemployment rate (Gupta et al. 2023).

Worse still, there is increasing evidence suggesting that COVID-19 and mobility restriction policies are likely to induce (especially female) workers’ acute needs/ preferences for flexibility and time to fulfill their domestic responsibilities, which subsequently widen employment and income inequality across genders. For example, several studies show that mobility restrictions may have a disproportionally negative impact on female workers employment and working hours during the COVID-19 disaster (Collins et al. 2021, Adams-Prassl et al. 2022, Couch et al. 2022), suggesting a wider gender gap in earnings. Apart from the gender inequality in employment and earnings, Cui et al. (2022) find that the COVID-19 disaster also led to gender inequality in research productivity.

Although the potential unequal effect of the COVID-19 disaster on employment and earnings across genders is well-documented in the literature (listed in Online Appendix B), how to reduce such disaster-induced gender inequality is still an open question. To fill in this gap, we propose that telework adjustment could be an equalizer and focus on investigating whether and to what extent telework adjustment reduces gender inequality in disaster-induced unemployment, which has direct implications for gender inequality.

## 2.2. Telework

Telework is a common practice of workplace flexibility enabled by the modern digital infrastructure,<sup>5</sup> and it allows workers to have the ability to decide where to perform work-related tasks. According to Bloom et al. (2015), telework can lead to a 13% increase in workers performance by reducing sick days and improving the convenience of the work environment.

There is a stream of literature focusing on the role of telework in different types of disaster situations. For example, Donnelly and Proctor-Thomson (2015) find that many public sector workers in New Zealand shifted to telework during a series of earthquakes between 2010 and 2012 to better achieve a work–family balance. Moreover, recent COVID-related research points out that telework can improve the resilience of business operations to catastrophic events (e.g., Bai et al. 2021). In addition, several studies suggest that occupations with higher teleworkability (Adams-Prassl et al. 2022, Papanikolaou and Schmidt 2022) experienced a smaller increase in unemployment during the COVID-19 disaster. Couch et al.

(2022) reveal that female workers are more likely to work in occupations with higher teleworkability. All of these studies use the teleworkability measure (Dingel and Nei man 2020, Papanikolaou and Schmidt 2022) derived from pre-COVID Occupational Information Network (O\*NET) surveys, which measure whether tasks in an occupation could feasibly be done at home. To disentangle the actual impact of telework from teleworkability as a characteristic of occupations on disaster response, we concentrate on telework adjustment, which captures the extent to which workers in an occupation have actually teleworked during COVID-19, which is constructed on our own based on the CPS COVID-19 supplemental data.<sup>6</sup>

In addition, the prior studies focus on the main effect of telework in improving productivity or reducing disaster-induced unemployment, and none of them considers its potential differential effect depending on workers’ genders or needs/preferences. Meanwhile, given the relatively flexible work arrangements in telework, workers with different needs/preferences may not only sort into occupations with teleworkability differently, but may also have different tendencies to make telework adjustment. Unfortunately, the latter aspect is mostly overlooked in the literature. Our study extends related literature by drawing attention to the gender difference in telework adjustment because of the gender difference in needs/preferences and revealing the differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on gender and digital infrastructure.

## 3. Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses Development

Drawing on the TWA (e.g., Lofquist and Dawis 1978, Dawis 1994), we conceptualize telework behavior as a form of work adjustment to cope with disaster-induced changes in needs/preferences and study the impact of such adjustment (i.e., telework adjustment). We first introduce TWA in Section 3.1. Based on TWA, we propose hypotheses regarding the impact of needs/preferences on telework adjustment and the differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on gender and digital infrastructure in Section 3.2. Further, we present our research framework in Section 3.3.

## 3.1. Theoretical Underpinning: TWA

TWA is a well-known theory in the psychology and management literature (Judge 1994, Raghuram et al. 2001), and it is mainly used to describe and explain the dynamic process of workers interacting with their work environment. In TWA, each person (P) is described as having needs and obtaining abilities to perform tasks, whereas the work environment (E) can supply tasks that require certain abilities and meet certain needs (e.g., Lofquist and Dawis 1978, Dawis 1994). When E adequately meets P’s needs and P capably performs tasks in E, P is satisfied with the job and P’s performance is satisfactory, which, in turn, leads to career success and positive labor market outcomes.

In this study, in line with Raghuram et al. (2001) and Carillo et al. (2021), we apply the lens of TWA to describe disaster-induced telework adjustment as a form of IT-enabled work adjustment triggered by disasters and explain how it is related to P’s needs or preferences and work E.

## 3.2. Hypotheses Development

## 3.2.1. Gender Difference in Telework Adjustment.

Following TWA, whether workers can make a telework adjustment depends not only on whether their jobs are teleworkable (which depends on job characteristics and is measured by teleworkability), but also on their own needs or preferences, which are likely to be genderspecific. Female workers are known to have higher needs or preferences for flexibility (Goldin 2014) and a short commute (Le Barbanchon et al. 2021) because they often (or are expected to) bear higher domestic responsibilities. The gender difference in needs/preferences becomes more prominent when there are disaster-induced external constraints on the operations of childcare centers and restaurants. Specifically, the extant literature (Collins et al. 2021) consistently reveals that, during the COVID-19 disaster, compared with their male counterparts, female workers bear a heavier burden of childcare and are more likely to reduce their working hours. Del Boca et al. (2020) further find that the COVID-19 disaster leads to a larger increase in female workers’ time on childcare and housework, and those working female workers with children under five tend to have more difficulties in work–life balance. In addition, given that childcare and some housework, such as cooking, are usually timeinflexible, the larger increase in such domestic demands on female workers suggests they need more time and flexibility to achieve work–life balance during the COVID-19 disaster. As the traditional work E (i.e., office) often cannot meet female workers’ high needs/preferences for time and flexibility, the P–E discorrespondence tends to occur more frequently among female workers during the disaster.

In this case, telework adjustment, which allows workers to save commuting time and have relatively flexible work schedules, is more appealing to female workers, especially when there are external constraints on the operations of childcare centers and restaurants. Therefore, we propose the following two hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1a. Ceteris paribus, female workers are more likely to make a telework adjustment than their male counterparts.

Hypothesis 1b. Ceteris paribus, female workers’ telework adjustment is more responsive to external constraints than that of their male counterparts.

3.2.2. Impact of Gender Difference in Needs or Preferences. Furthermore, because of the gender difference in needs or preferences, telework adjustment is likely to be more essential and effective for female workers to reduce the P–E discorrespondence, suggesting the differential effect of telework adjustment across genders.

In light of TWA, we argue that external constraints on the operations of childcare centers and restaurants during the COVID-19 disaster are likely to cause a surge in time-inflexible domestic demands (e.g., childcare, cooking), especially for female workers. The larger increase in domestic demands impinges more on female workers paid work, suggesting a higher level of P–E discorrespondence. Telework adjustment can, thus, help meet female workers’ higher time needs by saving commuting time and meet their higher needs or preferences for flexibility by offering flexible work schedules and arrangements, which help reduce the potential P–E discorrespondence for them. As a result, telework adjustment can help female workers better juggle the increased domestic demands and paid work. Meanwhile, the commuting time saved by telework adjustment can be reallocated to childcare and housework, thereby better meeting their time needs. Therefore, we expect that telework adjustment is more beneficial to female workers who usually have higher needs or preferences for flexibility and time, suggesting that the differential effect of telework adjustment is contingent on gender. Bearing this in mind, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 2. Ceteris paribus, the effect of telework ad justment in mitigating the negative impact of disasters is stronger for female workers than their male counterparts.

3.2.3. Impact of Digital Infrastructure. In addition, the success of the conversion from traditional office-based work E to home-based work E, depends on the digital infrastructure (e.g., affordable network, high-speed network) at home. If the digital infrastructure is not suffi cient to meet the needs of workers, this could reduce their work efficiency and productivity and, thus, limit the mitigating effect of telework adjustment. As suggested by Dettling (2017), telework adjustment may become less feasible and face technical glitches without high-speed internet access. For instance, workers living in areas with low-speed broadband connections are more likely to experience a delay when searching and processing information online and communicating with supervisors or customers. Accordingly, we expect that good digital infrastructure can strengthen the effect of telework adjustment in mitigating disaster-induced work absence, layoff, or unemployment and propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. Ceteris paribus, the local digital infrastructure level enhances the effect of telework adjustment.

## 3.3. Research Framework

Based on TWA, we argue that disasters lead to increased gender differences in needs/preferences for flexibility and time in order to cope with additional constraints posed by disasters (such as childcare/school closure) and propose the following research framework (Figure 1). In this framework, we suggest that the female workers higher needs/preferences for time and flexibility lead to a higher telework adjustment rate among female workers (Hypothesis 1a) after controlling for the occupation-level difference in teleworkability, especially when there are disaster-induced external constraints on the operations of childcare centers (Hypothesis 1b). Taking this into account, we compare the change in female versus male workers’ labor market outcomes before and during the disaster to test our hypotheses regarding the differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on gender (Hypothesis 2) and digital infrastructure (Hypothesis 3).

Furthermore, when examining the differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on gender, we aim to control for the gender difference in job sorting<sup>7</sup> (denoted by C1 in Figure 1) and the gender difference in telework adjustment because of job sorting (denoted by C2 in Figure 1) by matching male workers with female workers who are in the same work E (i.e., industry and occupation) and are similar in other aspects (i.e., age, education, and childcare constraints). This allows us to make an applesto-apples comparison instead of comparing female and male workers in different work E. By doing so, our study focuses on the differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on gender (and digital infrastructure) instead of the main effect of telework adjustment in mitigating the negative impact of disasters.

We caution that the estimated difference between the disaster-induced unemployment among those individuals in occupations with teleworkability and that among those individuals in occupations without teleworkability may not have a causal interpretation. To avoid cross-occupation comparison, as done by Couch et al. (2022), in our gender-related analyses, we use the CEM approach to match female and male workers based on industry, occupation, education, age, and various other observable characteristics. Comparing female and male workers in the same occupation (therefore, subject to the same teleworkability) and with similar socioeconomic status (e.g., income and education) allows us to understand the impact of actual telework adjustment.

## 4. Data and Empirical Approach 4.1. Data

To answer our research questions, we compile a rich data set from multiple data sources. First and foremost, we compile an individual-month level data set as our main data source, including individuals’ demographics (e.g., age, gender, education, having child(ren), occupation, industry), their labor market outcomes, and their COVID-driven telework status from CPS conducted by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. In CPS, each household is interviewed at most eight times in total (once a month for four consecutive months one year and again during the same months the following year).<sup>8</sup> Our individuallevel panel includes all the individuals who are aged between 15 and 64 (i.e., the working-age population) and have ever answered the CPS monthly survey between January 2019 and December 2020. For the sake of data quality, we remove the observations of individuals who report inconsistent demographic information (i.e., gender and race) during the observation window.

We focus on three measures of individuals’ labor market outcomes:<sup>9</sup> Unemployed indicates whether the individual was unemployed during the previous week, WorkAbsent denotes whether the individual had a job from which the individual was temporarily absent because of any reasons during the previous week, Laid-Off denotes whether the individual had a job from which the individual was temporarily absent because of layoff during the previous week.<sup>10</sup> Whereas being laid off or absent from work captures a temporary cessation of work, being unemployed captures the steady state of not having a job currently.

## 4.2. Model-free Results

We first provide model-free evidence that shows gender inequality increases with the exogenous shock of the COVID-19 disaster. Specifically, Figure 2 visualizes how the COVID-19 disaster influences male and female workers differently. We find that male and female workers exhibited similar trends before the disaster struck. However, in April 2020, the disaster had a noticeably stronger impact on female workers, and this inequality lasted until May 2020, which coincides with the time when most states started the reopening plan.<sup>16</sup>

In Online Appendix F, we show that disasterinduced gender inequality mainly exists in April and May 2020, that is, the disaster response phase (which is in line with our theoretical arguments regarding the gender difference in needs or preferences), and telework adjustment helps mitigate the gender inequality during that phase.

After showing the increased gender inequality during that phase, we further visualize whether telework adjustment can help reduce this disaster-induced gender inequality in Figure 3. To this end, we classify occupations into two groups (i.e., high versus low telework) based on whether their telework adjustment rates are higher than the average of the whole population.<sup>17</sup> Further, we divide individuals into four groups based on their occupation-level telework adjustment rates and genders: (1) MaleLowTelework, (2) FemaleLowTelework, (3) MaleHighTelework, (4) FemaleHighTelework. Figure 3 visualizes the impact of the disaster on labor market outcomes in these four groups before and after the outbreak of COVID-19. The figure shows that all four groups exhibit rather parallel trends before the disaster struck. However, the high and low telework groups diverged remarkably in April and May 2020, and the increase in unemployment (absence or layoff) is noticeably smaller in high telework groups than in low telework groups. Most importantly, among the two low telework groups, the FemaleLowTelework group suffered more than the MaleLowTelework group, whereas there are no substantial gender differences among the two high telework groups (i.e., MaleHighTelework and FemaleHighTelework). In Online Appendix G, we report the results of a relative time model as a formal parallel trend check.

## 4.3. Identification Strategy

To account for the gender difference in job sorting, in our gender-related analyses (Hypotheses 1 and 2), we employ the CEM method to match female workers with male workers based on industry, occupation, part-/fulltime employment status, and other observable characteristics (i.e., age, education, marital status, and young childcare constraints).<sup>18</sup>

Based on the matched sample, we first examine whether there is a gender difference in telework adjust ment (Hypothesis 1a) and how the difference is related to disaster-induced external constraints (Hypothesis 1b) with the model specification introduced in Section 4.3.1. 4.3.2. Differential Effect of Telework Adjustment by Gender. To examine if there exists a differential effect of telework adjustment by gender, we estimate the following DDD model as shown in Equation (2). The model leverages the exogenous COVID-19 outbreak $( A f t e r _ { t } )$ to disentangle the differential impacts of telework adjustment on male and female workers’ labor market outcomes during the disaster, respectively:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} Y _ {i t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {After} _ {t} + \beta_ {2} \text {Telework} _ {\mathrm{og} (i t)} + \beta_ {3} \text {After} _ {t} \times \text {Female} _ {i} \\ & + \beta_ {4} \text {Telework} _ {\mathrm{og} (i t)} \times \text {Female} _ {i} + \beta_ {5} \text {After} _ {t} \times \text {Telework} _ {\mathrm{og} (i t)} \\ & + \beta_ {6} \text {After} _ {t} \times \text {Telework} _ {\mathrm{og} (i t)} \times \text {Female} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\alpha} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\mathsf {M o n t h}} _ {t} \\ & + \boldsymbol {\mathsf {C o n t r o l s}} _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array} \tag {2}
$$

In this model, $Y _ { i t }$ denotes the three labor market outcome measures for individual i in time t, namely, Unemploye $\boldsymbol { l } _ { i t } ,$ WorkAbsen $\dot { \mathbf { \rho } } _ { i t } , L a i d O f f _ { i t } .$ . The binary variable $A f t e r _ { t }$ indicates whether time t is after the outbreak of the COVID-19 disaster. The term $T e l e w o r k _ { o g ( i t ) }$ is the COVID-driven telework adjustment for each individual with $o g$ as the index for occupation–gender pairs. The vector $\pmb { \alpha } _ { i }$ denotes individual-level fixed effects, which account for the timeinvariant aspects for each worker (e.g., work–life ideologies, education, skill, and personal traits). Here, Month captures the month-of-year effect, which is used to adjust for seasonality and $C o n t r o l s _ { i t }$ includes the number of monthly job postings in individual i’s residence state and industry and occupation fixed effects. We cluster the error term $\left( \varepsilon _ { i t } \right)$ at the county and occupation levels.

In Equation $( 2 ) , \beta _ { 1 }$ captures the impact of the COVID-19 disaster on male workers’ employment status in those occupations wherein telework is infeasible, $\beta _ { 3 }$ captures to what extent the disaster affects female and male workers differently in those occupations wherein telework is infeasible, $\beta _ { 5 }$ captures the mitigation effect of COVIDinduced telework adjustment on male labor market outcomes, and $\beta _ { 6 }$ captures the female–male difference in the mitigation effect of telework adjustment. In addition, we also consider alternative telework measures proposed by prior studies (del Rio-Chanona et al. 2020, Papanikolaou and Schmidt 2022) in Section 6 and find consistent results.

## 6.3. To What Extent Can Telework Adjustment Help Disadvantaged Female Workers?

Females in disadvantaged positions (e.g., in low-income households, in low-skill occupations) tend to have limited resources. Based on the TWA, we expect the mitigating effect of telework adjustment to be stronger on them than on other subpopulations. To confirm this expectation, we classify workers into four groups based on their annual family income<sup>26</sup> and their genders (Female\_HighEarn, Female\_LowEarn, Male\_HighEarn, Male\_ LowEarn) with Male\_LowEarn as the baseline. ## 6.4. Decomposition: Understanding the Sources of Effects

Considering our findings that female workers are more likely to make telework adjustment than male workers (Hypothesis 1a) and the mitigation effect of telework adjustment is also stronger for female workers than male workers (Hypothesis 2), we need to take both effects into account when quantifying to what extent telework adjustment can reduce the disaster-induced gender inequality. First, based on the results reported in Section 5.1, we find that, even among the matched sample wherein female and male workers are exactly matched on their occupations, industries, education, etc., female workers still are significantly more likely to make a telework adjustment because of the COVID-19 disaster (7% higher in the probability of teleworking) than their male counterparts. The higher telework adjustment rate among female workers than male workers also lends support to the endowment effect of telework adjustment. Second, based on the DDD results reported in Section 5.2, we find that telework adjustment does have a stronger positive effect on female workers than on male workers, which verifies the coefficient effect of telework adjustment.

Bearing these in mind, we apply a decomposition model to estimate to what extent the endowment and coefficient effects of telework adjustment can reduce gender inequality in labor market outcomes during the COVID-19 disaster.

6.4.1. Decomposition Model. To estimate how and to what extent telework adjustment can help to narrow gender inequality in the impact of the disaster on labor market outcomes, we follow the prior literature on the Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition (Kitagawa 1955, Blinder 1973, Oaxaca 1973) and use a linear regression model to represent the relationship between the labor market outcome Y and the covariate telework for two gender groups, F (female) and M (male), during the COVID-19 disaster. ε is the error term.

$$
Y ^ {G} = T e l e w o r k ^ {G} \beta_ {T e l e w o r k} ^ {G} + \varepsilon^ {G}, E (\varepsilon^ {G}) = 0 \mathrm{and} G \in [ F, M ],\tag{4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \Delta Y = E (Y ^ {F}) - E (Y ^ {M}) = E (T e l e w o r k ^ {F}) \beta_ {T e l e w o r k} ^ {F} \\ - E (T e l e w o r k ^ {M}) \beta_ {T e l e w o r k} ^ {M} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

We can further decompose $\Delta Y$ into the endowment, coefficient, and interaction effects.

$$
\Delta Y = E + C + I\tag{6}
$$

The endowment effect $( E = ( E ( T e l e w o r k ^ { F } ) - E ( T e l e w o r k ^ { M } ) )$ $\beta _ { T e l e w o r k } ^ { M } )$ denotes the part of the gender difference in labor market outcomes that is due to differences in telework adjustment between female workers and their male counterparts. The coefficient effect $( C = E ( T e l e w o r k ^ { M } )$ $( \beta _ { T e l e w o r k } ^ { F } - \beta _ { T e l e w o r k } ^ { M } ) )$ denotes the gender difference in labor market outcomes that is due to differences in the marginal effect of telework adjustment on female workers’ labor market outcomes than on those of male workers. The interaction effect $( I = ( E ( T e l e w o r k ^ { F } ) -$ $E ( T e l e w o r k ^ { M } ) ) ( \beta _ { T e l e w o r k } ^ { F } - \beta _ { T e l e w o r k } ^ { M } ) )$ denotes the gender difference because of the interaction of the gender groups’ different telework adjustment rates and coefficients. Scholars tend to primarily focus on the endowment and coefficient effects because the interactions between different covari ates are usually difficult to interpret and have limited implications. Specifically, the endowment effect in our model captures the gender difference in actual telework adjustment induced by the COVID-19 disaster in the face of similar job characteristics (i.e., teleworkability), whereas the coefficient effect captures the gender difference in the marginal effect of telework adjustment, distinguishing our study from prior literature $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ Couch et al. 2022, which examines both effects of job characteristics). Based on this decomposition framework, we estimate the proportion of each effect in reducing gender inequality in labor market outcomes during the disaster.

6.4.2. Decomposition Results. Detailed results are reported in Table 7. Based on the results from the full sam-$\mathrm { p l e } , ^ { 2 8 }$ the endowment and coefficient effects of telework adjustment can reduce the gender difference by 25.48% and 31.94%, respectively, whereas the remaining unexplained gender difference is 42.58%. The results vis-a\`-vis work absence and layoff show a similar decomposition pattern, but telework adjustment is less effective in reducing the gender difference in work absence. To summarize, our results show that telework adjustment can help reduce gender inequality in labor market outcomes not only because of the higher telework adjustment rate among female workers, but also because of the stronger marginal effect of telework adjustment on female workers.

## 8. Discussion and Conclusion

Recent studies suggest that the catastrophic advent of the COVID-19 disaster has stronger negative effects on female workers than on male workers (e.g., Heggeness 2020, Col lins et al. 2021) primarily because of increased domestic responsibilities induced by disasters on female workers. Increased domestic responsibilities for female workers suggest that they have stronger needs/preferences for time and flexibility, and telework has the potential to help address such increased needs/preferences triggered by disasters. An important goal of this research is to better understand whether and to what extent telework adjust ment can reduce disaster-induced gender inequality. To this end, we use TWA to propose and explain the gender difference in telework adiustment and its marginal effect based on a needs-/preferences-based theoretical frame work. We find that female workers have a higher tele work adjustment rate than their male counterparts (i.e., the endowment effect) and telework adjustment is highe with stronger external constraints that directly influence the needs/preference for time and flexibility. Moreover, telework adjustment is more effective in mitigating the negative impact of COVID-19 on female labor market outcomes (i.e., the coefficient effect). We find that the endowment and coefficient effects of telework adjustment can reduce gender inequality by 25.48% and 31.94%, respec tively. We further reveal that female workers’ labor mar ket outcomes are more negatively affected by domestic constraints (e.g., childcare constraints) and external con straints (e.g., childcare closure, restaurant closure, and the stringency of mobility restriction policies), providing evidence for our needs-/preferences-based theoretica framework. More importantly, the mitigation effect of telework adjustment becomes stronger as domestic and external constraints increase, which underscores the criti cal role of telework adjustment in reducing gender in equality. Additionally, the consistent results based on alternative measures of constraints or telework suggest the generalizability of our finding to any disaster or event that increases workers’ needs/preferences for flexibilit and time. In addition, good digital infrastructure can fur ther enhance the mitigation effect of telework adjustment.

Our study has several key contributions. First, we contribute to the disaster management literature by underscoring the role of telework adjustment and digital infrastructure in supporting the economy and narrowing gender inequality. Based on a needs-/preferences-based theoretical framework, our study provides a systematic explanation for the disaster-induced gender inequality and implications for how to mitigate such inequality in the disaster response phase. Second, our study contributes to the growing telework literature by highlighting the importance of telework adjustment when facing catastrophic events or disruptions that are associated with higher needs or preferences for flexibility and time. Our research extends this stream of research by revealing the differential effect of telework adjustment contingent on gender and digital infrastructure and quantifying to what extent telework adjustment can reduce the disasterinduced gender employment gap. Third, our study contributes to the growing literature that attempts to reduce gender inequality by highlighting the critical importance of the telework option for workers with high needs for flexibility and time. We find that telework adjustment helps female workers more when there are more constraints. Furthermore, we find that telework adjustment helps disadvantaged female workers (e.g., those in lowincome households, in low-skill occupations, and with low education) even more. Our research suggests that telework adjustment could be an equalizer and benefit those disadvantaged female workers the most.

Our study has important implications for policymakers, business leaders, and workers. First, our study suggests that telework adjustment can reduce the negative disasterinduced shock on the labor market, especially for female workers who are hit harder by disaster-induced external constraints. Government policymakers and business leaders are advised to provide more workers with the telework option and more guidance on how to telework efficiently. Moreover, for workers, because telework adjustment is more important to female workers with childcare constraints, they should acquire more telework soft skills and improve home technologies (e.g., internet, computers) to maximize their telework productivity. Additionally, local employment and training offices should provide more training regarding various information and communications technology skills (e.g., Word use, Excel use, email use) for unemployed workers to better prepare them to find teleworkable jobs.

In addition, our study demonstrates that the effect of telework adjustment also depends on digital infrastructure. Therefore, policymakers should take action or develop policies to encourage the development of better digital infrastructures. For example, government policymakers can collaborate with internet service providers to provide more free Wi-Fi coverage during disasters. It would also be valuable for policymakers to provide more federal or state funds to provide high-quality internet services for the underserved population (e.g., females with young children, low-income females, and less-educated females) given that telework is shown to help address some gender-based inequities during serious labor market disruptions. Finally, business leaders are also suggested to incentivize workers to upgrade their internet service and home technologies.

This study has several limitations. First, although we conducted a series of analyses regarding the impact of telework adjustment on different labor market outcomes (e.g., unemployment, work absence, layoff, labor force participation), data limitations regarding workers’ actual productivity, long-term career development, and mental wellbeing prevent us from quantifying the impact of telework adjustment on those social welfare measures in this paper. We call for future studies to investigate the gender difference in stress, burnout,<sup>32</sup> and productivity in the short and long term and how such a gender difference could be affected by telework adjustment. Second, our study primarily focuses on individual agility in making a telework adjustment depending on their needs or preferences. We acknowledge that the actual realization of telework adjustwhich is not observed here. Future research can further examine the impact of organizational flexibility in telework arrangements on employee turnover, employee satisfaction, and revenue. Finally, our study mainly examines the effect of telework adjustment in reducing gender inequality during the disaster response phase. We advise future studies to investigate the potential differential effect of hybrid work by gender in the post-COVID era. Overall, we hope that our study sparks discussion about how information tech nologies can be leveraged in disaster management to mitigate disaster-induced gender inequality.
