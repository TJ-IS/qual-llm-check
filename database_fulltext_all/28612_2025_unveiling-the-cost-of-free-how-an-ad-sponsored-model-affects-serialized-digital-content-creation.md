# Unveiling the Cost of Free: How an Ad-Sponsored Model Affects Serialized Digital Content Creation

Abstract. The selection of a business model significantly impacts the success of digital content platforms, influencing both consumers and creators. Within the field of serialized digital content, establishing incentives that align with creators’ interests poses a notable challenge. Web novel platforms, which allow writers to produce and market their work online, have traditionally adopted a pay-per-view model, where readers pay for access to content. However, a recent trend has seen the introduction of an ad-sponsored model, aimed at attracting readers with free content. Although the ad-sponsored model expand reader accessibility, its effects on writers remain uncertain. Our study investigates the impacts of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ content creation efforts, leveraging a policy change within a leading web novel platform as a natural experiment. Our results indicate a general decrease in writers’ productivity under the ad-sponsored model. To uncover the underlying mechanism, we focus on the role of reader engagement and conduct a media tion analysis. Our results demonstrate that the ad-sponsored model is associated with less reader engagement, which leads to a subsequent decrease in writers’ content creation efforts. Further, we conduct a reader-level analysis and find a significant decline in engagement with writers among those who consume ad-sponsored content, providing additional evidence supporting our main findings. These results substantiate our hypothesis that diminished reader engagement stems from a perception of reduced “sunk costs” associated with free, ad-sponsored content compared with paid books. From a psychological standpoint, the sunk costs inherent in paid content motivate continued reader engagement and interaction with writers, whereas ad-sponsored reading removes such costs, thereby decreasing reader engagement. This study holds profound implications for digital content platforms, underscoring the necessity for platform managers to carefully evaluate the impacts of implementing the ad-sponsored model on both creators and consumers of serialized digital content.

## 1. Introduction

The global market for digital content creation is experiencing rapid growth, fueled by advancements in information technologies. In 2022, the market was valued at \$25.6 billion, and it is projected to expand at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 13.5% from 2023 to 2030 (Grand View Research 2023). Digital content creation involves the generation and contribution of digital information, including activities such as blogging, writing, video production, music streaming, and social media management. Its growth and popularity are evidenced by the estimated 207 million content creators worldwide in 2023 (Balkhi 2023). As more individuals engage in digital content creation, monetizing this content has become a mutual interest and key focus for both creators and platform practitioners. In terms of revenue generation, in addition to paid plans launched by the platforms themselves, advertising sharing is becoming an important source (Jain and Qian 2021). A survey of content creators found that 70.7% of respondents earn money from advertising (Carufel 2023). For digital content platforms, the choice of the business model is vital as it directly influences the incentives, productivity, and sustainability of content creators. Furthermore, rapid technological innovation and evolving audience preferences associated with different business models have profoundly affected creators in content production. Thus, understanding how these business models impact creator motivation and output appears to be essential for better managing the workforce in the digital content industry.

## 1.1. Motivations

The prevalence of online content in the digital age has profoundly influenced people’s thinking and behavior. Serialized digital content, defined by its regular, sequential release, is experiencing significant growth across various digital platforms. For instance, Webtoon and Tapas are known for publishing comics, whereas Patreon has become a hub for serialized podcasts and videos. Web novels, a specific type of serialized content, are published online by individual writers, typically one chapter at a time on a set schedule (Wu and Zhu 2022). These writers can earn income by producing content and contracting with web novel platforms as digital content creators. Web novel platforms, which connect writers and readers, are witnessing rapid global growth and attracting many writers who seek to serialize their novels and engage with their audiences. According to a recent report (Nan 2022), the Chinese web novel market was valued at over \$4.2 billion in 2021, with more than 502 million users, representing 48.6% of the total number of internet users in China. Web novels have also gained considerable readership in the Americas. For example, a web novel featuring a secret love story has garnered over 400,000 subscribers and 50 million views since its serialization in 2019 on Radish Media, a United States-based web novel platform (Kim 2022).

The web novel industry has employed the pay-perview business model for many years (Wei 2018), where readers have to pay to access books. In practice, web novel platforms set a fixed unit price for all book chapters across genres (e.g., \$0.01 per 1,000 words for each reader) (Wu and Zhu 2022). However, some web novel platforms have recently started using the ad-sponsored model to provide free content and attract more readers. In the ad-sponsored model, readers are not obligated to make payments for accessing content. Rather, they engage with advertisements to continue reading, circumventing the need for direct payment to access chapters. The revenue model hinges on the volume of users viewing the content page containing ads, with writers earning a share of the generated revenue (Sun and Zhu 2013, Ren 2023). For a comprehensive elucidation of the revenue generation and distribution process within the ad-sponsored model, we furnish a detailed calculation in Online Appendix A. Although introducing ad-sponsored reading provides readers with an additional option for accessing online content, its impact on writers remains uncertain. Currently, there is limited empirical evidence available on the impact of the ad-sponsored model on content creation. Therefore, our study aims to address this research gap.

## 1.2. Research Questions and Contributions

Previous literature has shown the effects of business model shifts on companies and users in various contexts (Pauwels and Weiss 2008. Kammer et al. 2015 Arora et al. 2017, Kumar and Qiu 2022). In addition to exploring the impact of business models of digital content on platforms and consumers, understanding con tent creators’ motivations and productivity in each business model is also crucial. The products and services content creators offer are the foundation of digital content platforms and can determine a platform’s success and future (Jain and Qian 2021). However, extant studies do not provide a clear understanding of the impact of business models on content creators. As noted above, some web novel platforms have shifted from the pay-per-view business model to offering ad-sponsored reading options, allowing readers to read content for free. Although the ad-sponsored model attracts readers, the impact on writers remains underexplored. On the one hand, the effects could be positive. The ad-sponsored model removes the financial barrier for readers, allowing free access to books. Therefore, by reducing the cost of accessing web novel content, this model may lead to greater exposure for writers’ works, potentially expanding their readership and enhancing reader engagement. Prior literature sug gests that content creators are motivated extrinsically by economic incentives (Poch and Martin 2015). In our context, writers derive their earnings from the performance of their work, and readership serves as a key indicator of their economic incentives (Sun and Zhu 2013). Consequently, the ad-sponsored model has the potential to enhance readership, which may translate into higher revenue for writers compared with the pay per-view model. This uptick in revenue could serve as a motivating factor for writers, encouraging them to intensify their efforts in content creation.

However, on the other hand, the ad-sponsored model could negatively affect writers’ content creation. Because web novels are serialized, readers typically consume and read them chapter by chapter following the writers schedule. As readers consume chapters progressively, the upfront fees in previous chapters comprise sunk costs for them. Although these fees have already been incurred and should no longer be relevant to future decisions, they typically motivate readers to continue reading (Arkes and Blumer 1985). The upfront fees keep readers more engaged because they pose a psychological cost that incentivizes them to take action to amortize the cost (Iyengar et al. 2022). Thus, under the pay-per-view model, readers exhibit a greater tendency toward continuing to read novels and engage with writers, as they already paid at an earlier stage. However, in the ad-sponsored model, readers no longer need to pay to read; therefore, they perceive reduced sunk costs, and their engagement is likely to decrease. If readers show less engagement with a book, writers may be negatively influenced by reduced reader feedback and decrease their efforts accordingly. This motivates us to empirically investigate the impact of introducing the ad-sponsored business model on serialized digital content creators efforts. Therefore, we pose our first research question: (1) What is the effect of the ad-sponsored model on the efforts in serialized digital content creation?

We examine this question using a data set from a leading Chinese web novel platform. We leverage this platform’s launch of a new policy in June 2020 as a natural experiment that exogenously allows writers to choose the ad-sponsored model. As writers are sequentially involved in the ad-sponsored model, we adopt a staggered difference-in-differences (DID) estimate to examine their efforts in content creation in the new model. We find that after writers are involved in the ad-sponsored business model, they decrease their efforts in content creation in general. Thus, the results indicate a potential side effect of the ad-sponsored business model on web novel platforms. Our findings have important implications for web novel platforms and writers. They could provide a warning for web nove platform managers regarding changes in writers’ productivity when platforms adopt the ad-sponsored business model, reminding them to pay close attention to writers’ motivation and productivity. The findings also provide insights for writers who have or plan to produce content on web novel platforms under the ad-sponsored business model.

To further explore the underlying reasons why the ad-sponsored model negatively affects writers, we focus on changes in reader engagement, evidenced by reader comments and social gifts. As denoted by prior literature (Poch and Martin 2015), content creators are motivated extrinsically by both economic incentives and social benefits. We measure economic incentives through reader comments, which reflect reader consumption and directly correlate with writers’ revenue (Tatar et al. 2014, Mousavi and Gu 2019, Kim et al. 2020). In addition to compensation, content creators remain motivated by positive feedback from readers, which can be considered social benefits (Poch and Martin 2015). To measure social benefits, we leverage social gifts from readers to writers as expressions of appreciation, which are prevalent on web novel platforms. For writers, social gifts not only represent support but also signify recognition of their content by readers (Skageby 2010). Thus, social gifts can be considered social benefits for writers in our context. Given that the impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts may be explained by changes in reader engagement (measured by reader comments and social gifts), we pose our second research question: (2) What underlying mechanisms contribute to the reduction in effort by serialized digital content creators under the ad-sponsored model?

By examining this research question, we can gain insights into the underlying mechanism of the negative effects of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ content creation efforts. As writers are motivated by reader engagement, writers who receive less engagement from readers could potentially produce less content. A recent study suggests that upfront payment in a program increases customer engagement because customers experience the sunk cost effect (Iyengar et al. 2022). The sunk cost effect is manifested in a greater tendency to continue an endeavor once an investment in money, effort, or time has been made (Arkes and Blumer 1985). In our context, the money and time invested in the early stages of reading serialized content constitute sunk costs for readers. Compared with the pay-per-view model, the sunk costs essentially decrease in ad-sponsored reading, because readers do not need to pay for chapters. Thus, we hypothesize one potential mechanism causing readers to engage less with writers in ad-sponsored reading is that accessing free content rather than paying for books prevents readers from perceiving sunk costs.

To examine the role of reader engagement in the relationship between the ad-sponsored business model and writers’ decreased efforts, we conduct a mediation analysis. This analysis focuses on the mediating role of reader engagement, which we measure through two key indicators: reader consumption and support, as evidenced by the number of comments and social gifts received. Our empirical results show that both reader comments and social gifts mediate the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ efforts in content creation. Specifically, in ad-sponsored reading, readers post fewer comments and give fewer socia gifts, which leads writers to decrease their content creation efforts. In addition, our hypothesis that readers perceive lower sunk costs in the ad-sponsored model prompts further exploration of reader-level activities in ad-sponsored reading. Our reader-level analysis demonstrates that, after reading ad-sponsored books, although readers read more books, their engagement with writers significantly decreases. Therefore, our hypothesis is supported. The results of the mediation and reader-level analyses demonstrate the importance of reader engagement and provide essential implications for platform managers and writers when they are involved in the ad-sponsored business model.

Next, to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanism, we propose that the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ efforts in content creation is moderated by writers’ writing styles and the content creation stage. In web novel content creation, writers have their distinct writing styles, such as story length and genre. The sunk costs readers perceive differ between short and long novels; thus, distinct writing styles may impact the negative effects writers experience from the ad-sponsored business model. Specifically, readers experience greater sunk costs during the reading of long novels than when reading short ones. As reader engagement could decrease if readers do not perceive sunk costs when reading, the ad-sponsored model may have a smaller influence on writers who write short novels compared with those who write long ones. Therefore, we propose our third research question: (3) Does the writing style of serialized digital content creators moderate the association between the ad-sponsored model and their efforts in content creation? We find that writers’ writing styles, in terms of novel lengths, have a moderating effect on the impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts. Specifically, short-novel writers decrease their efforts in content creation less compared with long-novel writers when providing ad-sponsored reading. Our results contribute to a better understanding of the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ efforts in content creation. This finding offers platform managers insights into strategies for managing writers with different writing styles in the ad-sponsored model while emphasizing the importance of writing style in helping writers make informed decisions about joining the ad-sponsored business model.

Although reader engagement can affect writers’ content creation efforts, it takes time for readers to discover books, begin reading, and engage with writers. To better understand writers’ motivation in the serialized content creation process, we pose our fourth research question: (4) Do serialized digital content creators vary their efforts during the content creation process under the ad-sponsored business model? We consider the first three months after providing ad-sponsored reading as the early stage of ad-sponsored content creation and the subsequent months as the late stage. Our results show that writers exhibit reduced efforts in the later stages of ad-sponsored content creation compared with the earlier stages. This variation in effort levels may be attributed to changes in the intensity of writers’ motivation. It is plausible that writers, attuned to fluctuations in reader engagement throughout the content creation process, adapt their effort levels accordingly. The results indicate a fluctuation in incentives after writers are involved in the ad-sponsored option, which reminds platform managers to tailor their strategies to support writers at various stages of ad-sponsored content creation.

This study explores the impact of introducing an ad-sponsored (free) business model on digital content creators’ efforts, considering the existence of a pay-perview (paid) model. It shows that writers providing ad-sponsored content decrease their content creation efforts compared with those who do not, influenced by reduced reader engagement. We suggest that this decline in engagement is due to readers perceiving lower sunk costs when shifting from paid to free reading, as evidenced by fewer reader comments and social gifts. Our reader-level analysis supports our proposed mechanism, demonstrating that readers consume more books under the ad-sponsored channel but engage less with individual writers. Additionally, the study uncovers that the negative impact on writers’ efforts varies by their writing styles and content creation stages. Specifically, writers of shorter novels are less affected than those writing longer ones, and writers tend to reduce their efforts in the later stages of content creation.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first empirical examination of the impact of the ad-sponsored business model on digital content creators’ efforts. Our study considers the reactions of both writers and readers to the ad-sponsored model on digital content platforms. Our findings contribute to the broad literature on digital content creation and platform business models. Although content creation efforts are vital in the digital content industry, research on labor supply management in this field is largely limited to theoretical models (Jain and Qian 2021, Ren 2023). Despite exterior shocks (Wu and Zhu 2022), platforms’ operating strategies, such as prod uct business models, significantly affect content creators motivations and productivity. Our study addresses this research gap by empirically investigating the impact of an additional business model (i.e., ad-sponsored) on digi tal content creators’ efforts. Additionally, our work extends the application of sunk cost effects to serialized digital content consumption, providing new insights into consumer behavior.

## 2. Literature Review

In this section, we review three relevant streams of literature: digital content creation, platform business models, and sunk cost effect. We compare our research with previous studies to highlight our contributions in each stream.

## 2.1. Digital Content Creation

Our research contributes to the field of digital content creation, a topic explored in prior literature spanning online books, music, and videos (Susarla et al. 2012, Cho et al. 2016, Datta et al. 2018, Mei et al. 2022). In light of the increasing prominence of serialized digital content, scholars have observed a proclivity among users to engage excessively (Schweidel and Moe 2016, Zhang et al. 2022). The burgeoning popularity of serialized digital content has prompted recent studies to examine the design and management strategies for this form. We have integrated these findings and conducted a thorough comparison with our study, as detailed in Online Appendix B. For example, Zhao et al. (2023) compare platforms’ release strategies for serialized digital content, finding that sequential release generates higher revenue compared with simultaneous release.

Although success in the serialized digital content industry hinges on content creation, the literature on motivating and managing labor supply in this field is predominantly theoretical. Jain and Qian (2021) analytically demonstrate that heightened creator competition can lead to increased compensation, content quality, and creator profits. Ren (2023) investigates how a platform’s advertising policy and various market factors influence content creation on digital platforms using a game-theoretical model. In the empirical realm, Wu and Zhu (2022) find that intensified competition accelerates content production, with varying effects on book novelty. Beyond potential shifts in external landscapes, the operational strategies of platforms, including their product business models, wield a substantial influence on the motivations and productivity of content creators. Our study addresses this research gap by empirically examining the impact of an additional business model, namely ad-sponsored, on digital content creators’ efforts.

## 2.2. Platform Business Models

Our study also relates to the literature on business models in digital content platforms, which has extensively explored the differences among various models. For instance, Prasad et al. (2003) analytically compare the business models of pay-per-view and advertising in electronic media and suggest marketing optimality regarding the number of options, subscription price, and amount of advertising that should be offered to consumers. Our study significantly differs from them by empirically examining the impact of business model change on content creators who have no control over the underlying business models. In addition, existing literature has examined how business strategies and consumer engagement levels affect content quality, revenue generation, and the competitive dynamics of content consumption and contribution (Casadesus-Masanell and Zhu 2010, Sun and Zhu 2013, Huotari and Ritala 2021). For instance, Ren (2023) reveals that content quality is influenced by the level of advertising, viewers’ satiation rates, and their distaste for ads using a game-theoretical model. Our study enhances these discussions by exploring the impact of two types of business models (i.e., pay-per-view and ad-sponsored models) on content creators. To provide a comprehensive understanding of prevalent business models in the digital content industry, we also compare these models in detail and discuss the generalizations of our findings in Online Appendix N.

In the context of serialized digital content, recent studies have examined several aspects of the business model efficiency, including pricing schemes and commission plans. Choi et al. (2023) investigate the effects of waitfor-free (WFF) pricing schemes on the monetization of serialized digital content and find that WFF pricing increases the sales of the content, generating new demand from customers who would otherwise forgo participation in the market. Similarly, Choi et al. (2022) indicate that WFF promotion can boost paid readership and accelerate users’ consumption of future content. Although these studies provide important insights for platform strategists, research focusing on the perspective of content creators for serialized digital content remains scant.

In addition, many studies have examined changes between paid and free business models, but most focus on a shift from free to fee, which is a freemium strategy (Pauwels and Weiss 2008, Kammer et al. 2015, Oh et al. 2016, Lambrecht and Misra 2017). More recently, Filip pas et al. (2023) investigate the impact of centralized versus decentralized pricing on workers in the sharing economy. Although our study relates to them in demonstrating the adverse outcomes of platform pricing strategies on workers, we distinctly focus on digital platforms and services, particularly examining the content creation efforts of writers who lack control over service pricing. Transitions from fee-based to free models, especially in the digital content area, remain insufficiently understood. In the mobile app market, Liu et al. (2014) and Arora et al. (2017) examine how a mobile app’s free version affects sales of its paid version and the association between them. To the best of our knowl edge, our work is the first to empirically study the impact of the ad-sponsored model on content creators within the area of digital serialized content, considering the existence of the pay-per-view model.

## 2.3. Sunk Cost Effect

Our study aligns with the extensive literature that comprehends the impact of sunk costs on decision making. The sunk cost effect suggests that past investments in terms of money, effort, or time can influence continued commitment to an endeavor (Arkes and Blumer 1985). This phenomenon is prevalent across various domains, such as job promotions and real estate services (Black and Vance 2021, Ratnadiwakara and Yerramilli 2022).

In the realm of consumer behavior, the sunk cost effect is a prominent and well-documented phenomenon. Many studies have illustrated how initial payment can shape future consumption patterns and engagement levels for consumers. Loyalty and membership programs, which require upfront payments, are effective business models for building customer loyalty and promoting repeat purchases. The access fee (membership fee) acts as a sunk cost, stimulating consumers to continue utilizing program benefits (Ailawadi et al. 2018). In addition, these sunk costs lead consumers to form a positive attitude toward their chosen retailer, escalating their long-term commitment (Dick and Lord 1998, Ashley et al. 2016). Recent studies have further investigated the role of the sunk cost effect in customer engagement. Goli et al. (2022) find that the mere act of paying for a course at the beginning can increase user subsequent engagement with its content on online learning platforms. Iyengar et al. (2022) find that the subscription program, through the upfront fees, keeps members more engaged in terms of spending, frequency, and variety of their purchases. Similarly, Guo and Liu (2023) demonstrate that membership fees in membership-based free shipping programs, representing a sunk cost, affect customers’ purchase behaviors. Our study extends these findings to serialized digital content consumption, exploring how removing sunk costs impacts user engagement. This provides new insights into consumer behavior in the digital content area.

## 3. Research Context and Data Description

In this section, we delve into the context of our research and provide a detailed description of the data collected for our study, ensuring a clear understanding of the foundation upon which our analysis is built.

## 3.1. Research Context

We obtain data from a leading web novel platform in China that connects writers and readers online. Before introducing the ad-sponsored model, the platform has adopted the pay-per-view business model for the past few years. With the pay-per-view model, readers are charged on a chapter basis, with fees contingent upon the total word count of those chapters. According to the platform rules, reading paid content incurs a cost of 0.05 CNY (Chinese dollars) for every thousand words. Under this model, writers earn revenue share based on the consumption of their content. In June 2020, the platform updated its policy and provided writers with the option to choose the ad-sponsored model. This new model allows readers to read books for free, viewing advertisements instead of paying per chapter (Xiao et al. 2022). Writers receive a share of the revenue generated from these advertisements based on reader viewership. According to our interviews with writers, the revenue per view under the ad-sponsored model is generally comparable to that of the pay-per-view model. Further details are provided in Online Appendix D.

Prior to this policy change, writers were limited to the pay-per-view model. With the introduction of the ad-sponsored business model, writers gain the flexibility to choose between the two models for each new book they start. This selection is book specific, and once made, it becomes immutable. In addition, the writers choices of the business model are indicated through labels on the books after the first content update, making it unlikely for readers to infer their selection before the book’s release. However, once the books are pub lished and readers begin to explore individual book pages, they can readily discern the chosen business model associated with each book based on the labels. According to the policy introducing the ad-sponsored model, the revenue share between writers and the platform is fixed and set at an equitable 50-50 split, mirroring the existing pay-per-view model’s structure. This arrangement is a standard practice in the web novel industry (Zhao 2017, Suh 2023). We also confirmed this revenue share percentage with several writers during our interviews. Notably, although ad rates and bidding mechanisms determine ad revenues, these are negotiated exclusively between the ad sponsors and the platform. Writers are not involved in these negotiations and are only informed of their revenue share through their contracts, without insights into the ad revenue generation process. Therefore, the details of ad operations would not influence writers’ decisions regarding their choices of business models or their efforts in content creation. We also depict and discuss the readership patterns and content creation trends on the platform in Online Appendix C.

## 3.2. Data Description

Our data include both writer-level and reader-level information. For writers, we obtain information on all their books and personal details, such as gender and level as a writer, on the platform. For readers, we collect information regarding their reading activities and engagement with writers.

3.2.1. Writer Level. To investigate the impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts, we begin by collecting full data on books, including chapter-level information such as update times, word counts, reader comments, and social gifts. Then, we identify the writer of each book and capture personal information through their user pages. Our analysis focused specifically on writers with published books both before and after the ad-sponsored model was introduced on the platform during our observation window. Although we observed that some writers offered both paid and free content simultaneously post-policy introduction, these cases represented a minor fraction (0.93%) of our data set. To ensure a more accurate estimation of the treatment effects, we excluded writers who participated in both the pay-per-view and ad-sponsored models postpolicy introduction from our empirical analysis. Consequently, our treatment group comprises writers who initially write books under the pay-per-view model and then switch to offering only ad-sponsored content after the new model’s introduction. In contrast, our control group includes writers who consistently provide paid content throughout the observation window.

Our final data set includes 2,020 writers who wrote books throughout the policy change. These writers publish 4,862 books in total during our observation window, which is from May 2019 to May 2021. Using writer and book information, we construct panel data at the writer-month level for our main analysis. Our primary focus is on understanding the impact of introducing the ad-sponsored model on writers’ content creation efforts. Although writers’ revenue is not directly tied to the word count of their content under the ad-sponsored model, readers still tend to invest more time in longer chapters, thereby maintaining a connection to writers’ economic incentives in content creation. Therefore, word count effectively serves as a representative measure of a writer’s content creation effort, applicable to both the pay-per-view and ad-sponsored models. Additionally, the number of chapter views plays a direct and pivotal role in revenue generation for both models. As a result, the consistent production of chapters serves as an indicator of writers’ efforts in content creation. To comprehensively gauge a writer’s content creation efforts, our study employs two dependent variables: the number of chapters and the number of words produced in a month. In our panel data, we focus on periods where writers have ongoing books and exclude any periods during which writers cease writing or leave the platform. Consequently, the zero values in our dependent variables represent instances where a writer, despite having ongoing books, exhibits no content creation effort within a given month. The key independent variable (treatment) in our study is whether the writer has been involved in the ad-sponsored model in a given month. Table 1 provides the variable definitions, and Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics.

3.2.2. Reader Level. To examine how readers react to reading books in the ad-sponsored channel, we also collect reader information and track their reading and social activities within the same observation window as our writer-level analysis (i.e., from May 2019 to May 2021). First, we randomly collect information on 100,000 readers. To empirically evaluate reader activi ties and engagement, we then identify a subset of readers (16,350 readers), who have each read at least one book and posted at least one comment both before and after the ad-sponsored model was introduced. In the reader-level study, we are interested in understanding the impact of the ad-sponsored model on reading activities and engagement with writers. Our outcomes of interest in the reader-level analysis are (i) the number of books a reader reads in a month, (ii) the total number of comments a reader posts in a month, and (iii) the average number of comments per book a reader posts in a month. The key independent variable in the reader-level analysis is whether a reader has started reading ad-sponsored books in a month. The details are presented in Tables 1 and 2.

## 4. Empirical Analysis

In this section, we perform our empirical analysis and present the results. We first examine the impact of the ad-sponsored business model on writers’ efforts in content creation. In addition, we perform various supplementary tests to ensure our results are robust. To reveal the underlying mechanism, we conduct a mediation analysis and show that reader engagement mediates the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ efforts in content creation. Next, we find that writers’ writing styles and the content creation stage have moderating effects on the impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts. Lastly, we conduct a reader-level analysis to understand the impact of ad-sponsored reading on readers’ activities, which corroborates our proposed hypotheses.

We begin this section by examining our first research question to analyze the impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts on web novel platforms. We use panel data from May 2019 to May 2021, encompassing one-year pre- and post-launch periods from the web novel platform. Following prior research (Wu and Zhu 2022), we employ the following regression specifications to examine changes in the total number of chapters and words writers produce after the involvement of the ad-sponsored model:

4.2.2. Relative Time Model (RTM). To alleviate the concern that time-variant unobserved confounders may result in significant heterogeneity in pretreatment trends, we verify whether writers’ efforts evolved in parallel before the involvement of the ad-sponsored business model. Following prior studies (Chen et al. 2022, Han et al. 2022), we test the parallel trend assumption by expanding Equation (1) and including indicators for the first to sixth months before and after the treatment. Specifically, we replace the indicator AdSponsored with the dummy variable $M o n t h _ { i , i } ^ { \tau }$ in Equation (2), where $\tau \in \{ - 6 , ~ \ldots , ~ - 2 , ~ 0 , ~ \ldots , ~ 6 \}$ . The value of $M o n t h _ { i , t } ^ { \tau }$ indicates whether month t is the τth month from the involvement of the ad-sponsored model for writer i. The parameter τ takes negative, zero, or positive values depending on whether the month t is before, at, or after the involvement of the ad-sponsored model for writer i. For the treatment group, $M o n t h _ { i , t } ^ { \tau }$ takes one when month t is the τth month from the treatment and is zero in all other cases. For our control group, $M o n t h _ { i , t } ^ { \tau }$ remains zero across all months. The one-month pretreatment period (τ � �1) is considered as the benchmark group.

$$
\log {(y _ {i, t})} = c + \sum \beta_ {\tau} M o n t h _ {i, t} ^ {\tau} + X _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}\tag{2}
$$

The coefficients of $\beta _ { - 2 } \ t o \ \beta _ { - 6 }$ identify month-by-month changes in pretreatment trends. Following Autor (2003) and Alyakoob and Rahman (2022), we provide a visual representation of the lead and lagged coefficients along with their estimated confidence intervals in Figure 2. The lead coefficients are close to zero, suggesting the parallel trend assumption holds in our case. In addition, we also perform the RTM on our matched samples from Section 4.2.1 and the results are consistent. The details of applying RTM on matched writers are presented in Online Appendix F. Therefore, we rule out the possibility that the impact of pretreatment trends is a confounding factor affecting writers’ decisions to provide ad-sponsored content.

4.2.3. Generalized Synthetic Control Method (GSCM). To further address the concern of unobservable timevarying confounders, such as the unobservable incentives across writers, we turn to counterfactual analysis and employ the GSCM to generate counterfactual prediction for the treated writers. The GSCM extends the synthetic control method proposed by Abadie et al. (2010) by constructing a weighted combination of control units based on the pretreatment outcome variables of the treated units (Xu 2017). This method enables us to predict the counterfactual outcomes, which would have occurred without the treatment, for treated writers in the post-treatment periods. Next, it estimates the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) by computing the average difference between observed outcomes and predicted (counterfactual) outcomes of the treated units. It is important to note that by modeling the outcome variable trends, the GSCM can naturally account for the effects of both observable and unobser vable time-varying confounders (such as the unobser vable writers’ incentives) (Wang et al. 2023). Thus, we can further alleviate selection bias associated with the time-varying unobservable incentives by using the GSCM. We implement the GSCM using the “fect” package in R (Liu et al. 2024). The results are presented in columns (3) and (4) of Table 5 and are consistent with our main results. Thus, our findings are robust against unobservable time-varying confounders.

4.2.4. Hazard Rate Test. Next, we conduct an additional test of the choice exogeneity issue at the writermonth level. In our setting, various factors can affect the decision to provide ad-sponsored content. If writers are involved in the ad-sponsored model (our treat ment) based on their productivity (our outcome of interest), it can cause endogeneity issues and reverse causality regarding our analysis. To alleviate this concern and rule out the possibility of reverse causality, we perform a hazard rate test following Gu et al. (2022), predicting the choice of the ad-sponsored model as a function of writers’ productivity and other writer characteristics. If the choice of providing ad-sponsored content cannot be predicted well by writers’ productivity (our outcome of interest), then the reverse causality issue should not be severe. In the model, survival time is calculated as the number of months since the introduction of the ad-sponsored business model on the platform. The coefficients of log(ChapNum) and log(WordNum) represent the possibility of reverse causality. The results are reported in Table $^ { 6 , }$ which shows that the coefficients of log(ChapNum) and log(WordNum) are both statistically insignificant, effectively ruling out the possibility of reverse causality.

## 4.3. Mediation Analysis

In this section, we conduct a mediation analysis using our data to investigate the potential mediating role of reader engagement based on Baron and Kenny (1986) and Zhao et al. (2010). Within the mediation framework, an independent variable affects the dependent variable through mediators. In our context, we suggest that the ad-sponsored model affects writers’ content creation efforts via reader engagement. Figure 3 illustrates our conceptual model for the mediation effects.

In our study, we focus on two crucial aspects of reader engagement as mediating factors, reader consumption and support. These factors are measured through comments and social gifts, which respectively correspond to economic incentives and social benefits for content creators (Poch and Martin 2015). In the context of web novel platforms, economic incentives primarily refer to revenue provided to writers tied closely to readership (Sun and Zhu 2013). To measure this aspect, we employ reader comments, given their direct correlation with view counts, to reflect content consumption behaviors (Tatar et al. 2014, Mousavi and Gu 2019, Kim et al. 2020). On the other hand, social benefits refer to the positive feedback that writers receive from others (Poch and Martin 2015). In our context, social gifts are digital gifts, which are images of items that readers send to writers on web novel platforms. These social gifts primarily show appreciation and support for the writers. For writers, receiving social gifts is a form of support that indicates their content is being recognized by readers (Skageby 2010). Moreover, writers who receive social gifts perceive a sense of individual worth through experiencing reciprocity, pleasure, sup port, and encouragement (Kim et al. 2018). Therefore, we use the number of social gifts from readers as a mea sure of writers’ perceived social benefits. Note that these social gifts possess monetary value, albeit contributing only a small proportion to the writers’ overall revenue. In our data, for writers who receive social gifts, we calculate the average monthly income from social gifts as 37.2 CNY (approximately U.S. \$5.31). However, according to the Chinese web novel annual report in 2019, the average monthly salary of web novel writers is 5,133.7 CNY (approximately U.S. \$766) (Zhang 2020). This calculation indicates that the revenue from social gifts represents just about 0.72% of the average monthly revenue. Thus, although they provide some economic incentive, the primary value of social gifts lies in their representation of social benefits to writers.

Mediation procedures have been utilized in the information systems literature (Zhou and Duan 2016, Bai et al. 2020) under the panel data structure. Based on these prior studies, our estimation procedure comprises two steps. First, we evaluate the effect of the ad-sponsored model on the mediators, including reader comments and social gifts (path a in Figure 3). Second, we examine the effects of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts while controlling for both mediators (path b in Figure 3). The regression equations for the two steps are as follows:

Step 1:

This aligns with our sunk cost hypothesis, suggesting that readers perceive fewer sunk costs in ad-sponsored reading, leading to decreased consumption and support. For the second stage of our mediation analysis (path b), our results reveal a positive correlation between reader engagement and writers’ content creation efforts. ## 4.4. Moderation Analyses

In this section, we examine the two moderating roles in the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ efforts in content creation. We find that writers writing styles and the content creation stage both have moderating effects on the impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts.

4.4.1. Writing Styles. We first examine how writing styles, in terms of novel length, affect the impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ content creation efforts. Specifically, we examine whether writers who produce short novels are less affected by the ad-sponsored model. We hypothesize that writers of shorter novels may be less impacted by the ad-sponsored model compared with those who write longer novels. This is based on the premise that within a paid book context, readers experience varying degrees of sunk costs depending on the total length of the book. A longer novel incurs a higher cost for completion compared with a shorter one, assuming a consistent word count per chapter. Consequently, in an ad-sponsored model where content charges are removed, readers may perceive substantially fewer sunk costs, potentially affecting their engagement with the content and, by extension, the revenue of writers.

To examine this hypothesis, we use the “short” tag assigned to books on web novel platforms. This tag serves as a direct indicator of whether a book is catego rized as a short story. Importantly, these tags are assigned by writers at the commencement of serialization and are documented in the contract with the platform, outlining the categorization of the book. Consequently, the category of the book is a predetermined choice unaffected by the impact of the ad-sponsored model. Writers typically maintain a consistent writing style, as affirmed during our own interviews with writers. Most writers specialize in specific novel types, including length, because of the distinct skill sets required (Wu and Zhu 2022). To categorize writers’ writing styles, we identify the tags of their books both before and after the policy change. If a writer writes short novels consistently, we consider the corresponding writer as a short-novel type; otherwise, the writer is considered a long-novel type. As previously discussed, short- and long-novel writers may experience different effects from the ad-sponsored model because of varying sunk costs perceived by readers. We include the writer’s writing style as a moderating variable and estimate the regression models as follows:

As a result, when the sunk cost is reduced in ad-sponsored reading, the effect on reader engagement may be less pro nounced for short novels. Consequently, the negative impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers is likely to be smaller for those who primarily write short novels as compared with those who mainly write long novels.

4.4.2. Content Creation Stage. We further investigate the moderating role of writers’ content creation stage on the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ content creation efforts on web novel platforms. Our mediation analysis demonstrates that writers’ efforts are significantly influenced by reader engagement, which inherently changes over time. Given the serialized format, reader engagement can fluctuate, with readers potentially becoming more involved or choosing to disengage over time. This temporal aspect of reader engagement suggests that the writer’s efforts may vary across different stages of content creation. Early stages may witness different levels of writers’ efforts compared with later stages, as the reader engagement with the content develops or wanes. Therefore, we aim to explore how writers’ efforts in later stages of content creation contrast with those observed in earlier stages. To examine this, we consider months following the first three months of a writer’s involvement in the ad-sponsored model as the “later stage” of ad-sponsored content creation. Our regression model to estimate the moderating role of a later stage is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \log (y _ {i, t}) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} A d S p o n s o r e d _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} A d S p o n s o r e d _ {i, t} \\ \times L a t e r _ {i, t} + X _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

where $L a t e r _ { i , t }$ indicates whether the current month is after the first three months since the writer i began providing ad-sponsored content. It is important to note that this later stage of ad-sponsored content creation is applicable only to writers in our treatment group who might start ad-sponsored books at different time periods. For writers in our control group, who have never participated in the ad-sponsored model, the $L a t e r _ { i , t }$ value remains at zero throughout our observation window. When estimating the moderating effect, $A d S p o n s o r e d _ { i , t }$ measures the overall impact of the ad-sponsored model on the content creation efforts across both treatment and control groups whereas the interaction between $A d S p o n s o r e d _ { i , t }$ and $L a t e r _ { i , t }$ <sub>t</sub> specifically assesses how the later stages of ad-sponsored con tent creation affect writers’ efforts.

The significantly negative coefficients for the interaction terms between $A d S p o n s o r e d _ { i , t }$ and $\boldsymbol { L a t e r } _ { i , t }$ suggest that writers who provide ad-sponsored content exhibit reduced efforts in their later stages of content creation after being involved in the ad-sponsored model. As discussed in Section 4.3, reader engagement plays an important role in motivating writers to create content by providing both economic incentives and social benefits. However, for serialized web novels, the intensity of these motivations may vary across different stages of the content creation. Specifically, in later stages, writers experience reduced reader engagement, which in turn decreases their content creation efforts.

To address potential concerns that this pattern simply reflects a natural creation cycle of new books, we analyze the average word count at different stages of the writing process, utilizing books written by the writers in our data set. Contrary to the idea that writers efforts decrease over time, our findings indicate a slight increase in efforts in a later stage of the book-writing process (detailed in Online Appendix J). Additionally, we conduct a subsample analysis focusing on writers who have completed books before the policy change and begun new books post-policy change. Essentially, these writers did not have ongoing books during the introduction of the policy, ensuring comparability in content creation stages between our treatment and control groups. Results from this analysis support our main findings, showing a decrease in writers’ efforts following the introduction of the ad-sponsored model, as detailed in Online Appendix K.

## 4.5. Reader-Level Analysis

The results indicate that the ad-sponsored model leads to a decline in writers’ content creation efforts. To understand the underlying mechanism, we conduct a mediation analysis in the previous section. In the first step, we hypothesize that readers are less affected by the sunk cost effect when switching from fee to free for reading web novels; thus, they reduce their engagement with writers under the ad-sponsored model. In this section, to further investigate the impact of the ad-sponsored model on readers and provide additional evidence for our underlying mechanism, we use reader-level data from May 2019 to May 2021 to examine whether reader behavior differs for ad-sponsored content compared with pay-per-view content. To evaluate reader engagement, we identify a subset of readers (16,350 readers), who have each read at least one book and posted at least one comment both before and after the ad-sponsored model was introduced. ## 5. Discussion and Conclusions

Although adopting appropriate and effective business models is crucial for operating digital content platforms (Casadesus-Masanell and Zhu 2010, Cho et al. 2020), little is known about how these models affect content creators’ productivity. Our study empirically examines how introducing the ad-sponsored (free) business model affects content creators’ efforts, considering the existence of the pay-per-view (paid) model, leveraging a unique data set collected from a leading web novel platform. We find that, with the ad-sponsored business model option, writers who provide ad-sponsored free reading decrease their content creation efforts compared with those who do not consider this option.

To identify the underlying mechanisms of this reduction in writers’ efforts, we conduct a mediation analysis and focus on reader engagement, as indicated by reader comments and social gifts. Our empirical evidence suggests that readers engage less in ad-sponsored reading, leading to decreased writer efforts in content creation. We employ a theoretical framework based on the decreased sunk costs readers perceive when transitioning from paid to free reading as just one potential mechanism to explain the decline in reader engagement under the ad-sponsored model. To further explore the potential mechanism regarding decreased sunk costs, we examine the impact of the ad-sponsored business model on reader behaviors. Our reader-level analysis reveals that, although readers read more books after accessing ad-sponsored content, they generally decrease their engagement with writers, particularly on individual books. To further understand the underlying mechanism, we consider writers’ writing styles and the content creation stage as moderators in the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ efforts in content creation. We find that writers who write short novels experience a smaller negative impact from the ad-sponsored model compared with those who write long novels. In addition, writers exhibit reduced efforts at a later stage after providing ad-sponsored free reading compared with an earlier stage. This decline correlates with decreased reader engagement, which in turn affects both the economic incentives and the social benefits for writers.

Our findings demonstrate a negative impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers, prompting us to investi gate their switching behaviors on the platform. In the short term, writers select the business model at the start of each book, and this choice cannot be changed later during the writing process. Therefore, even if writers are dissatisfied with the model’s performance, they are unable to switch it for the rest of the content. Additionally, writers are generally hesitant to switch to a new book prematurely because of the potential impact on their reputation and reader feedback. Abruptly halting updates for a current book can alienate existing readers, damage the writer’s reputation on the platform, and make it more challenging to attract readers to future books. Consequently, even if the ad-sponsored model negatively affects the performance of a current book, writers typically choose to finish their books before starting a new one. To investigate the long-term switching behaviors of writers involved in the ad-sponsored model, we expand our data set to cover the two years following the introduction of the ad-sponsored model, focusing on writers who are ever involved in this model. In our data set, 58.21% of writers who used the ad-sponsored model and wrote additional books subsequently switched to the pay-per-view model. This trend suggests that after experiencing the ad-sponsored model, many writers opt to return to the pay-per-view model, indicating potential drawbacks associated with the ad-sponsored model.

Our study demonstrates broad generalizability within the realm of digital content creation, encompassing platforms such as Netflix for TV shows, Audible for audiobooks, Spotify for music streaming, and Vimeo for web series and films. As monetization strategies evolve, these platforms are increasingly offering diverse business models, enabling creators to select the most suitable options for monetizing their work. For instance, YouTube Originals introduced an ad-supported free option for customers in 2018 that coexisted with their paid model, giving creators flexible monetization choices through advertisements, subscriptions, or direct payments (Spangler 2018, Aurora 2024). Particularly, our findings hold significant relevance for platforms featuring serialized digital content. There is notable growth in platforms for digital comics such as Webtoon and Tapas, as well as in platforms for serialized online podcasts and videos like Patreon and online courses like Udemy. Given the burgeoning popularity of serialized formats, the consumption patterns of web novel readers observed in our study can be generalized to these other types of content. Therefore, our findings have wideranging implications across various digital content plat forms, enhancing our understanding of how different business models impact content creation and distribution.

## 5.1. Theoretical and Managerial Implications

Our study has important implications for both research and practice. From a theoretical perspective, our study makes contributions to the literature on the digital content creation. Although prior studies provide valuable insights into the labor supply of the digital content creation (Jain and Qian 2021, Wu and Zhu 2022, Ren 2023), little is known about the impact of business models on content creators’ productivity. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to empirically examine how introducing the ad-sponsored business model affects content creators’ efforts on web novel platforms. Our results indicate that the ad-sponsored business model leads to a decrease in writers’ efforts in content creation, with reader engagement mediating the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and changes in writers’ efforts. Specifically, the ad-sponsored model negatively affects reader engagement, and writers efforts are positively associated with the reactions they receive from readers. Thus, writers decrease their content creation efforts in the ad-sponsored business model. Furthermore, we find empirical evidence that readers may perceive lower sunk costs after accessing free ad-sponsored content, leading to decreased engagement with writers. These results demonstrate the importance of using a comprehensive data set to achieve a complete understanding of the relationship between the ad-sponsored model and writers’ efforts on digital content platforms.

Our study also contributes to the understanding of business models on digital content platforms. Although prior studies have investigated the effects of business model transitions on platforms or users (Pauwels and Weiss 2008, Lambrecht and Misra 2017), they have mainly focused on shifts from “free to fee” (Kammer et al. 2015, Oh et al. 2016), with less attention provided for “fee to free” (Liu et al. 2014). Moreover, we focus on business model changes in the context of web novel creation and find a negative effect of the ad-sponsored (free) model on content creators’ efforts compared with those of writers who retain the pay-per-view (fee) model.

From a managerial perspective, our study offers important implications for managers of digital content communities including web novel platforms. Whereas existing studies primarily focus on policy or environmental changes external to the platforms (Wu and Zhu 2022), our study enhances the understanding of internal models. In the web novel industry, although the ad-sponsored model is introduced with expectations of benefiting writers through increased readership and potential revenue from free access to content, our study reveals a negative impact of the ad-sponsored model on writers’ efforts in content creation. This suggests that the anticipated benefits may not fully account for other key factors that affect the success of this model. Notably, we find that the perceived sunk costs of readers (costs incurred during the consumption of serialized content) play a crucial role in reader engagement.

Our empirical analysis suggests that reader engagement with web novels is negatively influenced by these sunk costs under the ad-sponsored model, potentially leading to a decreased readership and reduced writer revenue. These findings provide crucial insights for platform operators and creators of serialized digital content, emphasizing the need for careful consideration for both creator and user dynamics when implementing the ad-sponsored model. To mitigate the negative impacts on writers, platforms could offer incentives such as additional benefits or support to those partici pating in the ad-sponsored model.

In addition, our results show that writers experience different effects from the ad-sponsored model depending on their writing styles. Because short and long novels differently impact readers when transitioning from feebased to free reading, managers can offer tailored incentives to effectively promote the ad-sponsored model. For writers accustomed to writing long novels, platforms could provide extra promotion for their books to generate increased reader interest and encourage more feedback for writers. Our results also reveal that writers exhibit reduced efforts in the later stage after they provide ad-sponsored reading than in the earlier stage. We suggest that this reduction in efforts is associated with decreased reader engagement, which affects both economic incentives and social benefits for writers. Therefore, managers could provide support even at a later stage of ad-sponsored content creation to incentivize content creation. Finally, our study provides useful guidance for writers and readers on web novel platforms. Readers may consider engaging more with writers by posting comments and sending social gifts to express their appreciation in ad-sponsored free reading. Writers should be aware of decreased reader engagement under the ad-sponsored model and adjust their motivations and efforts accordingly during content creation.

## 5.2. Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our study has several limitations. First, our empirical results relate to a specific context within the digital content industry. Nevertheless, web novels are becoming increasingly popular, and the platform we examine is among the most prominent web novel platforms glob ally. Although some web novel platforms have introduced the ad-sponsored business model, the majority continue to rely on the single pay-per-view business model. Our results are valuable for platforms that have already or are planning to introduce the ad-sponsored model to attract readers and promote web novels. Second, our empirical study faces data constraints. We lack direct measures of writers’ revenue, including data on readership and sales performance of their books. Writers’ revenue is a crucial economic incentive closely tied to their content creation efforts. Thus, this limitation prevents us from fully analyzing the economic impacts and comprehensively examining the effect of the ad-sponsored model on writers. Moreover, our data set does not allow for tracking readers’ progress or engagement in terms of reading behavior, limiting our understanding of readers’ consumption patterns. These limitations underscore the need for future research with more comprehensive data, enabling a deeper exploration of the economic outcomes for writers and reader engagement patterns in the digital content industry.

Our work can be extended in several directions. First, although our focus has been on a web novel platform that incorporates an ad-sponsored model alongside the traditional pay-per-view model, the digital content industry is evolving with diverse business models. Future research could delve into the dynamics of these emerging models, expanding our understanding of their impacts and effectiveness. Second, our main analysis of the impact of the ad-sponsored business model focuses on the writer’s perspective. If detailed purchasing transactions can be accessed, the research could examine reader consumption patterns. Finally, as we identify the negative effects of introducing the ad-sponsored model, platforms with the ad-sponsored model may eventually recognize the adverse impact and revoke the new model to stimulate writers’ productivity in content creation. It would be interesting to investigate how rescinding the ad-sponsored model affects writers and readers and whether the negative effects of the ad-sponsored model can be reversed.
