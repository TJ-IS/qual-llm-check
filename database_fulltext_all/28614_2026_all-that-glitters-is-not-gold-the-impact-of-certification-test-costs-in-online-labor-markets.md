# All That Glitters Is Not Gold: The Impact of Certification Test Costs in Online Labor Markets

Abstract. Third-party skill certification is widely employed to address the ubiquitous information asymmetry between workers and employers. Offering certification tests at zero cost could be a natural strategy to encourage workers to certify their skills; however, neither theoret ical nor empirical studies have examined how employers react to such a policy. This research fills that gap. First, we develop a stylized model to theorize the effects of free certification tests on employers’ recruitment decisions and the transaction amounts (i.e., contract prices) with workers at both the job and platform levels. Second, we empirically test these hypotheses using a natural experiment in which one of the largest online labor markets unexpectedly eliminated certification test fees. The policy change caused a surge in certification attempts and an increase in the number of certified workers, enabling employers to make faster hiring decisions. However, contrary to the platform’s expectations, the zero-cost certification policy diluted the signaling value of the certifications. Specifically, employers became less likely to prefer certified workers in terms of both hiring probability and contract price. Crucially, this change appeared to be economically justified because workers certified under the new policy were less likely to deliver high-quality work. Moreover, the decline in signaling value disproportionately affected inexperienced workers, who rely heavily on certifications to compensate for their lack of employer feedback. Finally, we demonstrate that by improving the accessibility of certification test information, platforms can better preserve the value of certifications while minimizing dilution effects. Our findings strongly support the study’s theoretical propositions and provide important insights for online labor market platforms and practitioners.

## 1. Introduction

Labor markets are vulnerable to the challenges of asymmetric information (Akerlof 1970) because it is difficult to assess a job candidate’s technical skill and work ethic during the limited time of interviews (Spence 1973). A bad hire can be much more damaging and costly to remediate than buying the wrong product. If these concerns are left unaddressed, employers will be reluctant to hire, leading to market failure.

Such challenges have now extended to the increasingly popular online labor markets, which have emerged and expanded tremendously in the past couple of decades, boasting global reach and the convenience of virtual work for both employers and workers.

Examples include Freelancer, Upwork, and Fiverr. These platforms profit primarily by taking a percentage of the payment that employers make to hired workers. Addressing the issue of information asymmetry and, therefore, enabling transactions to take place is critical for these platforms. Paradoxically, the features of global reach and virtual work not only enable these platforms emergence but also create additional challenges in establishing trust between workers and employers. Therefore, the platforms have devised a repertoire of mechanisms, including ratings, guarantees, arbitrations, escrows, and so on, to ensure that the platforms can operate smoothly. Given the success of third-party certi fications such as Square Trade and TRUSTe (Hui et al.

2007, O<sup>¨</sup> zpolat et al. 2013) in online product markets, it appears natural for online labor markets to adopt certifications. These typically take the form of objective, standardized assessments of workers’ technical skills.

Although its implementations can vary among different platforms, skill certifications are typically provided by specialized third parties integrated into each online labor market. Workers take the tests voluntarily, and there is a plethora of different subjects for them to choose from. Typically, workers must pay a fee to take the tests, similarly to other well-known skill tests such as CompTIA A+ or Microsoft certifications, and this fee is not refundable even if the attempt is unsuccessful. Given the prevalence of fee-based tests in many contexts, this appears to be a natural approach. Under this arrangement, workers take a calculated risk based on self-evaluation and attempt the test only if they are sufficiently confident.

In recent years, a notable development is that several online labor platforms have begun offering certification tests at no cost. Although there is inevitably a cost for the platform to do so, there appear to be significant potential benefits to the platform that may outweigh such costs. First, if the tests are free, then skilled workers have no reason to avoid taking them and becoming certified. This means that employers could use the presence (or absence) of certification to infer the technical capabilities of the worker. Uncertified workers are either not confident enough to try or not competent enough to pass. This provides a simple heuristic for employers to screen job applicants. Second, offering certification tests at no cost should trigger an information-unraveling process. Workers with the highest skill levels will be most confident and will attempt the tests first, becoming certified before others. As a result, the second tier of workers (in terms of technical skills) will also seek certification to differentiate themselves from the rest. This process continues until all workers sufficiently skilled to pass have taken the test and become certified. In the extreme (ideal) case where multiple workers bid on a project, the employer could ignore uncertified candidates and use the certification test scores to easily compare certified workers against each other. The market will, therefore, become more transparent, and employers should be more confident in posting jobs and hiring workers. This outcome will increase the volume and value of projects on the platform and, ultimately, its profit, thereby justifying the cost of making the tests free.

Given the potential upside to making certification tests free, it becomes imperative to empirically evaluate the consequences of such a policy when a platform implements it. Specifically, the assumed benefit of the platform hinges on how employers react to such policy changes, especially the role of certifications in the employers’ hiring decisions. Although arguments in the previous paragraph suggest that employers should react positively to the policy change and be more willing to hire workers, they may also grow skeptical of the value of certifications. This raises a key question: Is elimi nating certification test fees a beneficial strategy for onlin labor markets?

Specifically, offering certification tests at no cost could prompt negative employer responses because it removes the financial risk associated with failing a test. Certifications are considered economic signals (Spence 1973). The financial cost of taking a test (and the risk of losing money when one is not sufficiently skilled) is an essential element in the signal’s value. In other words, the financial barrier also serves as a crude filter against those who merely want to “try their luck” with the tests. Prior to the policy change, the certification’s selectivity may actually improve its legitimacy (Polidoro Jr 2013), whereas the free-of-charge model may lead to “legitimacy dilution” of certification as a signal (Desai 2018). When the financial barrier is removed, some lowquality workers may become certified by mere chance. This will reduce the scarcity and perceived value of certifications. As a result, employers may give certifications less credence in terms of both the likelihood of hiring certified workers and the amount they are willing to pay for their work. Such an effect will be especially pronounced if the certification status is binary (c.f. CPAs where clients cannot see their scores on the tests) because all certified workers are “pooled” together. In contrast, if certification test scores are accessible and employers use them, those certified with higher scores will still be able to stand out.

Given these conflicting arguments, we first develop a stylized model to analyze the potential consequences of offering free certification tests in an online labor market. When the platform reduces the cost of taking certification tests to zero, our model yields several interesting results. Although the number of certified workers increases (as intended), it is accompanied by a decrease in the likelihood of winning contracts for both certified and uncertified workers. The gap between these two probabilities (i.e., the advantage of certified workers over uncertified workers) narrows under the zero-cost policy. Accepted bid prices will also decrease, regardless of whether the hired worker is certified. In fact, the price difference between these two groups will also diminish. From the platform’s perspective, our model predicts that even though the platform has more certified workers, the overall transaction volume—the basis of the transaction fees that the platform would charge—will actually decrease. In some respects, these predictions contradict the platform’s intention. Because the model is theoretical, it is essential to test these predictions empirically.

A major online labor platform unexpectedly implemented a zero-cost test policy for its certification tests.

We obtained data from this platform to test our theoretical predictions and document additional empirical findings, providing guidelines for similar platforms considering similar policies. Like many other platforms, this one collaborates with a third-party test provider, allowing workers to take tests directly on the platform. Originally, each test attempt cost \$50, which was significantly higher than the median first-project earning of \$31 for new workers. Moreover, this \$50 was nonrefundable, even if the worker failed the test. The policy change eliminated this fee entirely. All other platform features, including the certification process, remained unchanged. Specifically, if the worker scored at least 60 out of 100 points on a test, a prominent icon (with an HTML tooltip that says the worker is a certified expert) automatically appeared next to all of their future bids. Importantly, detailed information about each certification (subject, date, score) was available to employers, but only with extra effort. An employer had to click the “certified” icon or the worker’s username to open the profile page and then click a link for each test, requiring two to three additional clicks per test. When multiple certified workers bid on a project, the employer would need to visit each of their profiles to gather such information. Furthermore, because such information was embedded into the profile, workers could not turn off the icon, hide the certification details, or selectively disclose it to certain employers. However, unsuccessful certification test attempts did not appear on worker profiles and thus were not visible to employers. Our data set, in contrast, captured the time and results of all attempts, including those that were unsuccessful.

Our analyses of this data set confirm our model’s predictions and yield many useful additional findings. Once the certification tests were free, there was indeed a spike in the number of attempts from workers and the number of workers who became certified. For employers who hire workers, their time-to-hire (the number of days between posting a job and hiring someone) decreased. Thus, the implementation of the zero-cost policy did yield some benefits.

However, this is not the complete picture. As our theoretical model predicted, having more certified workers led to a lower overall chance of certified workers winning contracts. Contract prices were also lower because of the competition among certified workers. The gaps between certified and uncertified workers on these dimensions also narrowed. The value of certifications decreased even more for new workers who always faced the “cold start” challenge. In fact, overall, the platform transaction volume also declined. Remarkably, when we examined the perceived quality of work that the workers delivered, it appeared that the employers actions were economically justifiable. The average quality of work of certified workers was indeed lower after the policy. These observations are consistent with our model’s assumption that the binary “certified” icon leads employers to adopt a binary view of certification, forming expectations about a certified worker’s quality both before and after the policy.

Importantly, the binary indicator did not include all information about a worker’s certification test performance. Unlike some certifications (such as the CPA exam) that do not disclose scores, this platform publi cized the actual scores workers received upon passing their certification tests. As is typical in any theoretical model of certifications (ours included), the scores that the workers achieved on their certification tests should have been good predictors of their unobserved quality. In addition, workers who were certified prior to the zero-cost policy demonstrated not only technical capa bilities but also significant confidence in their abilities to pass the test despite the financial risk at that time. Such confidence should have been a strong predictor of performance (Coate and Loury 1993). These two conjectures could point to potential mitigating mechanisms for the dilution effect documented earlier, and we explore this further.

We empirically tested and verified these conjectures. We found that even after the zero-cost test policy was implemented, workers with higher test scores, and those certified before the policy, were still better at completing their jobs. Had employers looked beyond the binary certified/uncertified indicator and considered test scores and timing, they could have hired better workers, resulting in greater satisfaction and improved market efficiency.

This raises the question of why employers did not do so. One possibility is the sheer number of certified work ers; another is that the platform’s design made it difficult for employers to access test scores and certification date information. Understanding the contribution of each of these factors is important for devising ways to mitigate the negative consequences documented earlier. To that end, we expanded our theoretical model to include a new parameter representing the “cost” an employer must incur to process detailed certification information. The expanded model confirms that lowering the cost parameter could indeed mitigate the negative effect of the policy.

To demonstrate how platforms can mitigate these negative effects, we conducted an experiment to see whether hiring outcomes improve when the platform design makes score information easily accessible. Our experiment suggests that keeping all other design fea tures the same but automatically displaying certification scores (without extra clicks or navigation) reduces the dilution effect of the zero-cost policy. In other words, a better design would have preserved the transparency and efficiency benefits of having more certified workers while making it easier for employers to identify and hire the candidates most likely to complete the job successfully.

Our research contributes to the growing literature on platform resource allocation. Whereas prior studies have explored the initial introduction of platform resource provisioning and its impact on market dynamics (see, e.g., Karhu et al. 2018, Hukal et al. 2020, Li et al. 2021, and Li and Wang 2025), we examine the consequences of altering resource provisions by removing certification test costs. Our research also contributes to the rich literature on quality assurance mechanisms, with certification being a key component. Throughout history, quality assurance has been crucial to commerce; it is even more crucial in the digital marketplaces (Elfenbein et al. 2015) such as eBay (Hui et al. 2007, Lewis 2011), Yelp (Jin and Leslie 2003, Luca and Smith 2015), and Amazon (Pavlou and Gefen 2004, Gefen and Carmel 2008). We investigate the role of certification in online labor markets, focusing on employers’ short-term responses to a zero-cost certification test policy, an area previously unexplored. Our research also contributes to the growing literature on online labor markets, which has garnered interest from scholars across various disciplines, including economics, information systems, and marketing. Although researchers have studied reputation (Yoganarasimhan 2013, Moreno and Terwiesch 2014, Lin et al. 2018), third-party certification has received limited attention beyond serving as a control variable. We contribute to this literature by focusing on employers’ responses to certification tests’ cost change and proposing platform design recommendations to maximize the benefit of certifications in promoting trust on these platforms.

## 2. Related Literature

The online labor platform’s main challenge is to solve a multisided information asymmetry problem. This issue not only exists between employers and workers but also involves the platform because the platform has very limited information about both parties. The platform’s best approach is to design features that incentivize truthful information disclosure and enable differentiation between high- and low-quality participants. Implementing these features requires significant resources, and employers’ responses to them have critical implications for all stakeholders. Consequently, we review the literature on platform resource provision, mechanisms for mitigating information asymmetry in the online labor market, and employers’ responses to these mechanisms.

## 2.1. Platform Design and Platform Resource Allocations

The literature on digital platforms highlights the pivotal role of platform resource allocation design in driving platform growth, with a particular emphasis on the interplay between resource allocation strategies and governance mechanisms. A recurring theme is the importance of balancing cost-effective resource provisioning with the maintenance of service quality to sustain platform growth. For instance, studies by Li et al. (2021) and Guo et al. (2019) have demonstrated how efficient resource allocation can enhance platform performance and user satisfaction. Similarly, research on boundary resources—such as software tools and regulatory frameworks—has explored their role in mediating tensions between platform openness and control (see, e.g., Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2010 and Eaton et al. 2015). These studies underscore the need for platforms to strategically design and allocate resources to balance participa tion incentives with quality assurance.

Whereas many studies have highlighted the positive impact of well-intentioned platform designs on growth, others have revealed unintended consequences. For example, Luo et al. (2021) and Li and Wang (2025) examined how policies like commission fee caps and protection insurance can disrupt platform dynamics, illustrating the complexities of platform governance. Building on these insights, our study investigates how a resource allocation policy in online labor markets affects employers and uncovers unintended consequences. Specifically, we find that certain platform design choices, such as requiring employers to click on certification icons to view scores or test passing dates, can inadvertently obscure critical information, leading to suboptimal hiring decisions. Our findings suggest that making detailed certification information more transparent could counteract the negative effects of removing certification costs, offering actionable recommendations for platform designers and policymakers.

A key objective of platform resource allocation is to design effective signaling mechanisms that address information asymmetry and guide user behavior in online contexts (see, e.g., Karhu et al. 2018, Hukal et al. 2020, and Chen et al. 2022). In the following section, we will review how these mechanisms are explored in the context of online labor markets.

## 2.2. Information Asymmetry and Online Labor Market Designs

The rapid expansion of the online labor market has attracted huge attention from academia. Some have studied how macro-environmental factors, such as high unemployment levels (Laitenberger et al. 2023) and low foreign currency exchange rates (Gong et al. 2018), influence the supply of workers in this emerging market. But many other studies have focused on mechanisms to reduce information asymmetry between workers and employers and aid employers’ hiring decisions.

Employers in traditional labor markets rely on mechanisms to infer worker productivity (Kirmani and Rao 2000). These mechanisms include education (Spence 1973), occupational licensing (Blair and Chung 2025), promotion (Waldman 1984), and credit (Dobbie et al. 2020), and even observed characteristics such as race (Autor and Scarborough 2008) or gender (Azmat and Petrongolo 2014).

Online labor markets resemble traditional labor markets in many ways but also have unique characteristics such as global reach, lack of face-to-face interactions, and typically shorter durations of work. These unique features make it more challenging to foster trust (Gu and Zhu 2021, Kokkodis and Ipeirotis 2021) and hold “bad actors” accountable (Hong et al. 2016, Liang et al. 2022). Therefore, online labor platforms have adapted existing mechanisms from traditional markets to combat information asymmetry. These features include information provided by workers, such as their reputation (Yoganarasimhan 2013, Moreno and Terwiesch 2014, Lin et al. 2018, Benson et al. 2020), their affiliation with outsourcing agencies (Stanton and Thomas 2015), their working capacities (Horton 2019), and their direct messaging to employers (Hong et al. 2021). These features also include information provided by platforms such as workers’ verified experience (Agrawal et al. 2013, Leung 2018), prior hiring (Gefen and Carme 2008), monitoring (Liang et al. 2022), platform guarantee (Barach et al. 2020), and recommendation systems (Kokkodis and Ipeirotis 2023). Other information, such as workers’ appearance in profile photos (Troncoso and Luo 2023) and genders (Chan and Wang 2017), also plays a role in employer decisions.

We contribute to this line of research by studying the consequences of third-party certification tests and their costs, which directly affect platform design and the information environment of online transactions.

## 2.3. Employers’ Responses to Platform Designs

The above-mentioned mechanisms cannot function without fully considering how the employers would react to them. Studies have shown that employers respond both rationally and irrationally to platform design features. For example, the studies cited in the previous section show that employers are (correctly) more likely to hire workers who have better reputations, are affiliated with outsourcing agencies, have verified or prior experience, are guaranteed or recommended by the platform, have high working capabilities, and send direct messages to employers. Yet employers sometimes respond irrationally to certain features. For example, Stanton and Thomas (2015) found that employers pay less attention to workers’ affiliation with outsourcing agencies when other signals exist (i.e., workers’ public feedback). Employers also exhibit behavioral biases regarding worker characteristics. For example, Troncoso and Luo (2023) demonstrated that employers favor freelancers who “look the part” of the job, even though there is no strong correlation between looks and job performance.

In contrast with other mechanisms, research on thirdparty skill certifications in the online labor market is much more limited. Most studies have treated certification as a moderator or a control variable and have reported mixed results, with some finding that certifica tion helps workers (Moreno and Terwiesch 2014) and others suggesting that workers with more certifications are less likely to win contracts (Chan and Wang 2017). Ka¨ssi and Lehdonvirta (2022) found that skill certification increases workers’ earnings. However, their study context differs from ours in that the disclosure of certification is voluntary rather than mandatory. More recently, several platforms such as Upwork and 99Designs have introduced free certification tests. It seems intuitive that the presence of more “certified” workers should boost employer confidence in hiring and ultimately lead to a thriving marketplace. However, for those benefits to materialize, it is not sufficient for more workers to seek and be certified. Such a best-case scenario assumes that employers react to certification the same way they would when tests are not free and the workers’ expected quality is the same under these two regimes. If there is a signifi cantly larger number of certified workers under the free test scheme, including low-quality workers who would not have sought certification if they had to pay a fee to take the tests, then the certifications’ informational value may decrease, and employers may react to certifications differently. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no clear theoretical or empirical work in this regard. Therefore, we conduct theoretical and empirical evalua tions to assess the impact of zero-cost certification tests. We first develop a theoretical model to hypothesize the effect of this policy on hiring decisions and job outcomes (i.e., contract price) and then empirically test our hypothe ses using field data.

## 3. A Model of the Impact of Certification Costs

Upon passing the certification tests, prominent icons will be displayed to show their certified status when they bid for jobs. Workers cannot hide these icons and related information (e.g., passing scores). Employers make hiring decisions by considering the price and the expected service quality that workers offer, as informed by their certification status. ## 3.2. Equilibrium Results and Testable Hypotheses

Because of space constraints, we solve the model in Section 3.1, summarizing our key findings from the model as propositions to be tested empirically in the following section. The proofs of all propositions are available in Online Appendix A.2.

Proposition 1. After removing certification test costs $( i . e . ,$ $c _ { t } = 0 )$ , in equilibrium, the number of certified workers increases.

Proposition 2. After removing certification test costs $( i . e . ,$ $c _ { t } = 0 )$ , in equilibrium, the likelihood of hiring both certified workers and uncertified workers decreases. The difference between these two likelihoods also decreases.

Proposition 3. After removing the certification test cost $( i . e . , c _ { t } = 0 )$ , in equilibrium, the average price of hiring both certified and uncertified workers decreases. The difference between them also decreases.

Proposition 4. After removing the certification test cost $( i . e . , c _ { t } = 0 )$ , the platform profit decreases.

The intuitions behind these propositions are as follows. Eliminating the certification test costs encourages previously uncertified workers to become certified, leading to an influx of certified workers. This creates a quality dilution effect. As lower-quality workers obtain certification, the average quality of certified workers decreases. Meanwhile, higher-quality workers who previously avoided certification now enter the certified group, which further lowers the expected quality of uncertified workers. This shift decreases employer utility and reduces the signal value of certification, ultimately lowering hiring probabilities for both certified and uncertified workers, with the gap between them narrowing. Consequently, both groups lower their prices to attract employers, leading to a decrease in equilibrium prices as well as the price gap. Although the number of certified workers increases, platform profits are more likely to decline because of the reduced prices and hiring probabilities.

We next translated the above propositions into the following empirically testable hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1. After the removal of the certification test cost, the probabilities of hiring both certified and uncertified workers decrease. The difference between these two probabilities also decreases.

Hypothesis 2. After the removal of the certification test cost, the average prices paid to both certified and uncertified workers decrease. The difference between these two prices also decreases.

Hypothesis 3. After the removal of the certification test cost, the platform’s profit decreases.

We next provide detailed descriptions of our data and methods to test these hypotheses.

## 4. Data and Variables 4.1. Data

We obtained our data from one of the largest and earli est online labor markets, headquartered in the United States. This platform typically provides short-term projects and shares many operational features with other platforms such as Freelancer, Guru, and Upwork. A typical transaction on the studied platform starts with a reverse auction where an employer posts detailed requirements of a job, including deliverables, specifications, and deadlines. Workers who meet the requirements and have verified their identities can place bids. Bids are sealed, so workers cannot see the details of other bids. Only the employer can view the list of bids, including the timing and amount of each bid, as well as information about the workers who placed those bids, such as their rating, certification status, and location. The employer selects the preferred worker from that pool, and the lowest bid does not guarantee a win. The employer must then deposit the winning bid amount into an escrow account hosted by the platform, establishing a contract with the selected worker. The worker then starts working on the job and can communicate with the employer if necessary. Once the worker finishes the job and the employer is satisfied, the escrowed funds are released to the worker after a service fee is deducted, and the job is marked as complete. The employer and the worker can then rate each other. The cumulative ratings received by a worker, weighted by each job’s dollar value, are displayed on the worker’s profile and future bids.

Recognizing the importance of certifications in such a global market, the platform had partnered with a thirdparty certification provider for a long time. Previously, workers were required to pay a nonrefundable \$50 fee for every certification test they attempted, regardless of whether they passed. This scenario was comparable to other professional certification processes, such as ISO certification, bar tests, and CPA tests. The platform then unexpectedly announced the zero-cost certification test policy. All other features of the certification tests remained the same; certification logos continued to appear on workers’ profiles after they successfully passed certification tests on specific subjects, with links to details of the tests. Workers responded enthusiastically to the policy. The number of tests taken, passed tests, and firsttime testers all increased drastically (Figure 1). This aligns with the prediction made by our theoretical model in Proposition 1. Online Appendix Figure B also shows that the percentage of jobs receiving bids from certified workers and the percentage of bids per job from certified workers (Online Appendix Figure B.1(a)) also increased. Meanwhile, the test-passing percentage dropped dramatically after the policy change (Online Appendix Figure B.1(b)). Importantly, the number of newly registered workers and posted jobs over time do not show significant changes in response to the policy (Online Appendix Figure B.1, (c) and (d)).

To study the short-term effect of this policy on employers, we constructed a comprehensive data set that included information about all requests for bids (jobs) posted within six months before and after the policy change. We had access to detailed information on all transactions on these auctions (e.g., descriptions and job types), employers who posted the auctions (e.g., tenure on the platform and location), workers who bid on these auctions (e.g., certification status, ratings, and locations), and bidding information (e.g., bid amounts and timestamp for all bids). Most importantly, we have information about all attempted certification tests, successful or not, such as worker identifier, subject of the test attempted, timestamps of each attempt, and the scores of the tests. In all, 29,503 employers created 151,037 auctions in this period. A total of 43,387 workers placed 1,085,795 bids on these auctions; 11,822 of them won 104,723 projects; 2,396 certified workers placed 96,676 bids, and 1,389 of them won 14,830 bids. During our study period, no other major changes occurred on this platform.

## 5. Employer Response to Zero-Cost Certification Test Policy: Hiring Decisions

We now empirically test the first hypothesis specified in Section 3.3, that is, the impact of zero-cost testing on the relationship between certification and the probability of the worker being hired.

We first used all bids for jobs posted six months before and after policy implementation to explore the effects of the policy on hiring probabilities. Online Appendix C Figure C.1(a) provides model-free evidence that the percentage of bids from certified workers who won contracts decreased after the policy. The decrease was greater for workers who obtained their certification under the zerocost scheme than for workers who were already certified before the policy change. Bids from certified workers had higher contract-winning rates than bids from uncertified workers both before and after the policy. However, the difference shrank after the policy. The percentage of contracts won by uncertified workers also dropped slightly after the change. Online Appendix Figure C.1(b) shows that the percentage of jobs entering contracts gradually declined after the policy was implemented.

## 5.1. Main Analyses

We now conduct detailed tests for the hypotheses. We first create three separate subsamples of jobs and related bids during three different periods: the month before policy implementation, the first month after the policy, and the third month after. We then analyzed each subsample to observe the immediate effects of certification on employer hiring decisions before and after the policy change. The unit of analysis in all models was each bid placed on posted jobs. The dependent variable was Awarded, a binary variable indicating whether a bid was selected by the employer (1) or not (0). The main independent variable, Certified, was a binary variable that signified whether workers were certified when bidding.

Bids on a given job are naturally correlated; they were competing for the same job, forming the choice set from which the employer would pick a winner. An employer chose one bid out of this group to maximize expected profit. Therefore, our focus was on the effects of choicespecific attributes (i.e., worker and bid attributes) rather than on the employer or job characteristics. Thus, we implemented a conditional fixed-effects logistic regression (Chamberlain 1980). The probability that bid j was chosen as the winning bid in job $i ( \mathrm { i . e . , } P ( Y _ { i j } = 1 ) )$ ), when the total number of bids for the job i was J, can be written in the following conditional logistic expression<sup>4</sup> (Greene 2008) with job fixed effects:

$$
P (Y _ {i j} = 1 | X _ {i j}, C e r t i f i e d _ {i j}) = \frac {e x p (\gamma X _ {i j} + \beta C e r t i f i e d _ {i j})}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} e x p (\gamma X _ {i j} + \beta C e r t i f i e d _ {i j})},\tag{7}
$$

where we let $j = 1 , 2 , 3 , \ldots$ denote the j-th bid for job i;

$X _ { i j }$ was a vector of control variables described earlier. The coefficient $\beta$ for Certified was our main interest; a value greater than 0 and significant suggested that having certification improved the chances that a worker behind bid j would win the job i. We further clustered errors at the job level in our models, with the conditional fixed-effects logit model focusing on the variation across choices within each job.

We combined these subsamples to construct two separate data sets to examine the changes in employers views on certification due to the zero-cost certification test policy. The first data set covered the period of one month before policy implementation to one month after, using a narrow time frame to eliminate potential alternative explanations such as time-varying changes in workers’ unobservable characteristics (e.g., personalities and capabilities) and employer preferences. The second set covered one month before policy implementation and the third month after implementation, pro viding a check on the robustness of the findings from the first set. Both sets included the information listed in Table 1.

For each data set, we estimated policy effects on hiring by comparing employers’ decisions before and after the policy change using a matching approach. Specifi cally, we first grouped projects based on their job categories and value ranges and then matched bids posted before and after the policy change within each group, using propensity scores derived from worker, employer, and job characteristics (one-to-one nearest neighbor matching without replacement). We ensured that in the matched samples, the number and the proportion of certified workers were comparable for those jobs.<sup>5</sup> Table C.1 in Online Appendix C shows the validity of the matching results.

Using these samples, we compare whether or how similar employers, who posted similar jobs, received bids from similar workers, and received comparable numbers and portions of bids from certified workers, responded to certification before and after policy implementation. We estimated logit models using each set of newly constructed samples, with each bid as the unit of analysis Awarded as the dependent variable. The main independent variable was the interaction term between Certified and AfterPolicy (i.e., whether the job was posted after the policy change; 1 if yes, 0 otherwise). We estimated the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} P \left(Y _ {i} = 1 \mid X _ {i}, \text {Certified} _ {i}, \text {AfterPolicy} _ {t}, \text {Certified} _ {i} \times \text {AfterPolicy} _ {t}\right) \\ = \gamma X _ {i} + \beta * \text {Certified} _ {i} + \delta * \text {AfterPolicy} _ {t} + \theta * \text {Certified} _ {i} \\ \times \text {AfterPolicy} _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i}, \end{array} \tag {8}
$$

where $X _ { i }$ was a vector of covariates presenting worker, bid, worker’s message, and employer and job character istics specified in Table 1, and parameter θ captured the impact of the zero-cost certification test policy on employers’ decisions. We used robust standard errors.

Columns 1 � 3 in Table 2 show the results. The coefficient of Certified in Column 1 was positive and statistically significant, showing that before policy implementation, employers were more likely to hire certified workers than uncertified workers. This result was consistent with Ka¨ssi and Lehdonvirta (2022). The coefficients of Certified in Columns 2 and 3 in Table 2 were positive but statistically insignificant, showing that the positive effects of certification on employers’ hiring decisions became insignificant after the policy change when the percentage of bids from certified workers for a job jumped. Moreover, the magnitudes of the differences between certified and uncertified workers were greatly reduced after policy implementation. The results shown in Columns 4 and 5 in Table 2 confirm this finding. The coefficients of AfterPolicy × Certified all were negative and statistically significant at the 0.01 level, showing that the zero-cost certification test policy reduced the contract-winning probabilities of certified workers.

Table 3 reports the decomposed marginal effects of the interaction terms. Columns 1, 2, 4, and 5 show that hiring rates dropped for both certified and uncertified workers after the policy. The economic effects were also significant. For example, the contract rates of certified workers dropped from 0.191 (p < 0.001) before the policy change to 0.052 (p < 0.001) after the policy change, and the contract rates of uncertified workers dropped from 0.108 (p < 0.001) to 0.048 (p < 0.001) after the policy. The difference in contracting rates between certified and uncertified workers decreased from 0.083 (p < 0.001) before the policy change to 0.004 (p � 0.545) after the policy change, supporting Hypothesis 1.

## 5.2. Alternative Explanations, Robustness Tests, and Post Hoc Heterogeneity Analysis

We conducted a series of analyses to rule out alternative explanations and test the robustness of our findings, summarized in Table 4 and detailed in Online Appendices C.3 and C.4.

One potential issue is unobservable variables that may have also affected the employers’ decisions. We implemented four different approaches to address this concern. In the first approach, we exploited the fact that the platform sent the zero-cost announcement directly to workers but not the employers, such that some employers who also had worked as workers before the policy change would have received such notification, but those who are employers would not. This awareness differential provided us with a unique opportunity to verify that our findings were the result of the policy that made certification tests free. In the second approach, we exploit the fact that some employers posted jobs both before and after the policy change. These employers were likely to be aware of the increase in the number of bids from certified workers. If there was a quality dilution effect on employers’ perceived quality, then the dilution effect should be strongest for them. Our results support these conjectures and lend support to our findings.

In the third approach, we conducted an experiment to verify our findings by recruiting 154 working adults residing in the United States via a U.K.-based survey response panel (Prolific) as potential employers. We used a between-group design to manipulate job applicants’ attributes in terms of their certificate cost (\$0 versus \$50). After viewing six different bids with different attributes (e.g., certification and rating) placed by different workers in a randomly assigned case, the participants ranked the bids according to their hiring preferences from highest (most likely that they would hire) to lowest (less likely). We found that participants were more likely to hire certified applicants but, compared with those in the \$50 certification test case, those in the \$0 certification test case were less likely to rank certified applicants highly. We also directly asked participants to rank factors (including certification) that affected their hiring decisions, which further supports our finding that when the certification test cost is zero, the signaling value of certification decreases.

In the final approach, we conducted a series of falsification tests. If a factor rather than zero-certification test cost leads to our finding, this factor likely leads to similar findings for periods with hypothetical policy shocks. The results consistently show that these hypothetical policies had no effect on employers’ hiring decisions regarding certification.

Another concern regarding the matching method is that it may not be able to fully control for worker and employer heterogeneity. The results of our tests find that inexperienced workers had lower chances of winning contracts, but certification mitigated this “cold start” problem only when it required a fee. Under a zero-cost test policy, certification no longer benefited inexperienced workers. Interestingly, inexperienced employers were more likely to hire certified workers, a trend that strengthened after the policy change, which increased certification visibility.

For robustness, we analyzed bids from workers active before and after the policy change using worker panel analysis. We also followed the same procedure as in Section 5.1, using full samples and samples matched on employer and job characteristics (excluding bid-level data on certified workers). We further confirmed our findings by incorporating the percentage of bids from certified workers in the analysis. All results consistently supported our findings and demonstrated the persistence of these effects.

Our tests show that, contrary to platform expectations, the increase in the number of certified workers due to the zero-cost certification test policy reduced employers’ preferences for certified workers, thereby supporting our Hypotheses 1. There is clear evidence that the value of certification for workers was diluted in this process. In fact, our post hoc analyses show that these negative effects were even stronger for new workers, exacerbating the “cold start” problem for new entrants. This further negatively affected the competitive landscape on the worker side.

There is, however, one benefit of the policy for employers. In unreported analyses, we find that as a result of the zero-cost policy, employers’ time-to-hiring decisions are reduced.<sup>6</sup> That is, there is at least some evidence that employers are able to make hiring decisions faster.

Despite this silver lining, it seems that employers reacted negatively to the policy change. A natural question to ask next is how the policy change affected the actual outcome of the jobs on the platform. Does it benefit the platform in the form of higher revenues? Does it benefit employers by helping them find the right candidate and therefore become more satisfied with thei experience on the platform? We next turn to other outcome variables of concern, starting with the price that employers pay for work on the platform, which is the basis for the platform’s overall revenue, and the quality of work that workers deliver. Our goal is to investigate how these metrics respond to the policy change.

## 7. Mitigating Negative Effects of Zero-Cost Test Policy

A likely reason for the certification’s value to be diluted is that the number of certified workers increased significantly after the zero-cost test policy. In our data set, before the policy change and because of the cost of tests, only about 2% of workers on the platforms had certifications, so it was easy for them to stand out because employers could readily use the binary indicator (the certification icon) as a heuristic and hire them when they show up in an auction. After the policy change, about 9% of workers carried certifications. Although the increase in the number of certified workers is generally good for the market, it also increases the employers challenge of deciding which one to hire.

Although the binary certification icon is the most prominent indicator of certification, employers can click on it to access additional details, including the test scores and timestamps. Test scores should serve as strong predictors of a worker’s unobserved quality, aligning with theoretical models (Coate and Loury 1993). Furthermore, workers who obtained certification before the zero-cost policy demonstrated not only technical competence but also confidence in their abilities because they were willing to take on financial risk. This additional signaling may differentiate them from workers certified under the free-test policy. If employers do not factor in these variations when making hiring decisions, despite having access to this information, they may be overlooking valuable signals of worker quality, potentially “leaving money on the table.” These insights also raise important considerations for platform design, which we explore next.

In other words, by ignoring the time at which (before or after policy change) the workers received certification, employers were “leaving money on the table.”

## 7.2. Certification Test Scores

In the second approach, we explored workers’ certification test scores because employers can indeed check their certification test scores before deciding whom to hire. If workers with higher passing scores perform better on jobs than those certified with lower scores, but employers do not show hiring preference toward those with higher scores even though the scores are available to them, it would suggest that employers did not fully take scores into account in their decisions.

We run four analyses to examine such a possibility. In the first two analyses, we study how employers react to certification scores during hiring. The unit analysis is each bid. The dependent variables are Awarded. These two analyses examine how employers view test scores differently before and after policy change, respectively, using data from these two periods (the first one includes all bids placed by certified workers within three months before the policy change, and the second one uses bids placed by certified workers within three months after). The last two analyses compare the relationships between test scores and workers’ perceived performance before and after the policy, respectively. The unit of analysis is each winning bid from certified workers. The dependent variables are WorkerRating. We also replace dependent variables with Rehire for robustness. The main independent variables in all analyses are Test\_Score. Control variables in these four analyses are similar to earlier models reported in the paper.

Table 10 reports the results. The coefficients of test scores in columns 1 and 2 are positive but only statistically significant at 0.01 level in column 1. These suggest that employers were more likely to hire certified workers with higher than lower test scores before the policy (column 1) but paid less attention to test scores after the policy (column 2). The coefficients of test scores in other columns are all positive and statistically significant at 0.01 levels. These results show that test scores are good indicators of workers’ perceived quality of work both before (for workers certified before policy) (columns 3 and 4) and after policy (for workers certified after policy) (columns 5 and 6).

Overall, we find that although workers’ test-passing scores are consistently good indicators of workers’ perceived work quality both before and after policy, employers did not fully take advantage of this important certification information after the zero-cost policy was implemented.

One potential concern for this analysis is that workers could attempt the same test repeatedly. The platform only displays their most recent score if they pass, although we have information about every attempt. We verified that even if the workers repeatedly attempted the same subject, there is no evidence that they were able to improve their score significantly, at least during the study period (Online Appendix E, Figure E.1(b)). This is also unsurprising because a worker’s technical capabilities are unlikely to change quickly.

## 7.3. Potential Platform Design Changes to Facilitate Easy Access to Score Information

Results in Section 7.2 suggest that after the policy change, employers were unable to fully utilize the valuable information embedded in certification test scores. This may stem from the sharp rise in certified workers; among projects with certified bids, the average increased from 1 before the policy to 4.5 after. Another possible factor is the cost of accessing certification details, because employers must click through multiple pages to view test scores and certification dates.

To better understand these mechanisms, we expanded our theoretical model to incorporate a new parameter capturing the cost that employers incur when processing certification information (details in Online Appendix E.2). The model confirms that reducing this cost could mitigate the negative effects of the policy by enabling employers to better leverage certification data. In other words, although the rise in certified workers is an expected outcome when the platform incentivizes testtaking behavior, the benefits of a transparent market are not automatically realized if employers face barriers to digesting relevant score information. Even in a system where everyone is certified, the market may not function optimally if accessing and interpreting certification details remains cumbersome.

To test this conjecture, we conducted an additional online experiment. We used ${ \tt a } 2 \times 2$ factorial design to manipulate two factors. The first factor is the ease with which platform users (employers) would be able to access score information of certification tests (Easy Access versus Difficult Access) and the supply of certified workers (Low Supply versus High Supply). Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions (Easy Access & Low Supply, Easy Access & High Supply, Difficult Access & Low Supply, and Difficult Access & High Supply) and asked to make hiring decisions. We validated the manipulations and analyzed how participants considered certification and test-passing scores in their decisions. Details of this experiment are in Online Appendix E.3. Importantly, we found that both factors discourage employers from considering passing scores in their hiring decisions. Furthermore, the difference in hiring probability between easy and difficult access (0.596, $p <$ 0.01) is larger than the difference between low and high supply of certified workers (0.483, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ ). These findings underscore the importance of information transparency in mitigating the negative effects of an oversupply of certified workers.

## 8. Discussions and Implications

Perhaps because of its success in traditional labor markets, third-party certification has emerged as a popular online mechanism intended to help workers stand out from competitors. Yet the cost of certification tests, typically nonrefundable in the case of unsuccessful attempts, could create financial barriers that exclude workers who are otherwise technically qualified. This, in turn, will reduce the amount of human capital available in online labor markets and reduce its supply-side competition. One of the most interesting and noteworthy efforts in this regard was to remove the cost of the certification test entirely. Although such policies will unsurprisingly encourage workers to attempt certifications, its impact on the overall market (and, therefore, platform revenue and welfare) will remain ambiguous unless we have a clear under standing of how employers react to the policy, especially in terms of how they hire workers, and whether thei reactions are rational. Our study fills these gaps.

We first developed a theoretical model to hypothesize how employers respond to this policy regarding hiring and job outcomes in terms of transactions. We showed that the dramatic increase in the number of certified workers causes perceived worker quality dilution and reduces the credibility of third-party certification among employers. Therefore, we hypothesized that under the zero-cost policy, employers will be less likely to hire workers and offer lower contract prices. We test our hypotheses by exploring a unique opportunity from one of the largest online labor platforms, from which the certification test cost was suddenly removed.

We found that contrary to platforms’ expectations, this policy reduced workers’ odds of winning contracts and decreased their contract prices, even if they won. The adverse effects of the policy change occurred for both certified and uncertified workers. We also found that the policy change reduced the differences between certified and uncertified workers in terms of both contract winning and contract price. The economic effects are nontrivial. For example, using data from jobs posted one month before and one month after the policy, we find that the contract price of certified workers dropped from 86.263 $( p < 0 . { \bar { 0 } } 0 1 )$ before the policy change to 76.358 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) after the policy change. This leads to, on average, an 11.48% decrease in earnings for each contracted job. The contract price of uncertified workers also dropped from 60.427 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ to 59.215 (p < 0.001) after the policy. We also found that the policy change reduced the overall transaction amount at the platform level. We ruled out alternative explanations using various methods and strategies, supporting the hypotheses derived from our theoretical model.

Among workers on the platform, the most fragile are perhaps the new workers faced with the “cold start” problem because they have yet to establish a reputation. We found that the zero-cost policy was especially harmful to them. Although they were able to take the certification tests to stand out before the policy change, they were not able to do so after.

Most importantly, however, we found that employers failed to consider the full information about certification after the policy change. We identified observable heterogeneities among certified workers that correlated with better job performance; however, they were not captured by the conspicuous “certified” icon attached to bids placed by certified workers, which was only binary. Specifically, we found that among certified workers, those certified prior to the policy change, as well as those who achieved higher scores on the certification tests, are more likely to deliver satisfactory work if hired. Employers, on the other hand, seemed to have treated these higher-quality workers the same way as other certified workers during the hiring process. In other words, it appeared that employers did not look beyond the binary indicators to examine the timing and scoring of these higher-quality certified workers, even though the timestamp of each test as well as the scores that the workers had received to earn their certification were publicly available to the employers both before and after the policy change. In fact, as our experiment in Section 7.3 shows, a small improvement in the ease with which employers could access score information would have increased the chances that workers with higher scores would be hired, thereby at least partially mitigating the dilution effect of the zero-cost policy.

Our study makes several contributions. From a research perspective, this study contributes to the platform resource provision literature (see, e.g., Karhu et al. 2018, Hukal et al. 2020, and Li and Wang 2025) by highlighting the unintended consequences of resource allocation policies (e.g., implementing a zero-cost certification test policy) and suggesting that complementary platform design choices (making certification scores more directly visible) can counteract such negative effects. Our paper also enriches the disclosure literature (see, e.g., Grossman 1981 and Dranove and Jin 2010) by examining the implications of certification test costs, particularly the novel case of reducing those costs to zero. We further contribute to a fast-growing literature stream that focuses on online labor markets (see, e.g., Agrawal et al. 2013, Moreno and Terwiesch 2014, Hong et al. 2016, and Chan and Wang 2017). Our paper is among the first to examine platform policy regarding third-party certification in this market, especially with regard to the cost of certifications. Although workers may enthusiastically respond to such a change, we systematically documented the more subtle responses of employers to such a policy change. This is an important contribution because employers’ responses determine whether platforms can achieve the goals of a well-intentioned policy.

Our study also offers important practical implications. First, our study highlights the value of a more systematic and cautious approach to platform design changes. Making certification tests free is no trivial matter. Despite the policy’s noble intentions, our analysis reveals some surprising outcomes that partially defied its goals and even negatively affected some of the platform’s most long-standing members (those certified before the policy change). This is not only relevant for the platform we study but also serves as a cautionary tale for similar initiatives on other platforms. Platforms should conduct thorough evaluations or small-scale trials before enacting a policy that may lead to unintended negative outcomes.

Second, our study has direct platform design implications. Simply making scores available does not mean that employers will use them. And when employers did not fully utilize such information, the platform was also unable to fully realize the benefit of a more transparent market. In other words, removing certification costs may have fared better if it had been complemented by design changes that made detailed certification information more accessible (as our experiment showed). Furthermore, it may be worthwhile to explore whether a low (but nonzero) certification test fee could mitigate some of the negative consequences we observed.

Third, our results indicate that although the binary certification badge is convenient, employers leave money on the table if they ignore other available data (e.g., test scores and certification timing). Hiring the wrong person for a job incurs not only the direct costs of hiring but also additional costs, such as wasted time if the project fails. Employers, and indeed all platform users, should be aware of their own potential biases and strive to take full advantage of available data, even if platform designs make it difficult to do so.

Beyond online labor markets, our study at least serves as a valuable reference for policymakers and other stakeholders when considering similar policies related to third-party certifications (e.g., product markets and ecommerce). Our findings highlight the unintended consequences of offering free certification tests, including decreased credibility. For example, although low-cost college degree programs or initiatives have many benefits, educators and administrators should also carefully consider whether unintended, negative impacts from potential employers may occur.

This study lays the groundwork for future research. Potential avenues include examining the long-term effects of certification and its costs on skill development. Design science researchers can also study how to optimize the design of certification information presentation so as to improve market efficiency while reducing the likelihood of certification value dilution. Further insights could also be gained by exploring various outcomes derived or assumed in our theoretical models under suitable conditions.
