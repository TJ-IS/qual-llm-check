# Gains from Product Variety: Evidence from a Large Digital Platform

Erik Brynjolfsson,<sup>a</sup> Long Chen,<sup>b</sup> Xijie Gao<sup>c,</sup>\*

Abstract. Digital platforms can increase product variety and consumer choice by facilitating the discovery and availability of new products. In this paper, we document the massive growth of new products on the largest digital platform in China and quantify the welfare implications for consumers. Using sales data on three categories of books from 2015 and 2019, we find that the number of product titles almost doubles, whereas prices fall somewhat. Most of the new products are niche products sold to consumers with unusual tastes. We estimate that demand for these niche products is more than 20% more inelastic than that of mass products. Embedding the estimates of demand elasticity into a two-segment constant elasticity of substitution framework, we find the gain from increased variety is more than the total expenditure on books in 2019 and about 40 times larger than the gain from lower prices. This welfare gain is 30% higher compared with the existing approach that does not distinguish mass and niche products. Our results highlight both the massive increase in product variety and the importance of accounting for differences in price elasticity across products. We also examine the geographic variation in gains from variety and find that consumers in rural and low-income regions enjoy larger gains from the digital platform.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet and platform technologies have facilitated coordination among large sets of buyers and sellers, transforming the retail market. As a result, an increasing fraction of retail trade has shifted online. According to Euromonitor, in 2020, e-commerce sales as a percentage of total retail sales exceeded 27% in China and 20% in the United States. By integrating markets and reducing the cost of distribution, online platforms provide a large and growing set of product choices to consumers, potentially increasing consumer welfare significantly.

Although the gains from new products in the economy have garnered increasing attention since the seminal work by Bresnahan and Gordon (1996), there is little recent empirical evidence on the rapidly growing role of niche products. In this paper, we use data from the largest e-commerce platform in China to document large increases in product variety in recent years. We find that most of the new products are niche products, which are qualitatively different from mass products, resulting in disproportionate welfare gains for consumers.

We obtain detailed transaction data for three categories of books from the e-commerce platform and find that the number of distinct book titles increases by 98% from 2015 to 2019. Despite the large increase in product variety, the market share of the top products remains largely unchanged. Specifically, we define the top 1,000 book titles as mass products and find that they account for just under 60% of the market share in both years. However, they only account for 0.6% of the total book titles available in 2015 and 0.3% of those available in 2019, implying that most of the increase in product variety is in niche products.

To capture the niche nature of new product variety, we propose a two-segment constant elasticity of substitution (CES) framework, which allows for heterogeneity in the demand elasticity of mass and niche products. The advantage of CES demand is that we can infer the surplus generated by new products from the expenditure shares on those products. Leveraging detailed county-level transaction data, we estimate demand elasticity for mass and niche products separately using an exogenous variation of prices and quantities sold. Our estimates indicate that the demand for niche products is more than 20% less elastic than the demand for mass products.

Embedding the elasticity estimates into the twosegment CES framework, we find that the gain from increased variety is about 120% of the total expenditure on books in 2019, which is about 40 times larger than the gain derived from the price effect alone. To put the number into perspective, for the three categories of books in our sample, the gain from variety is about 1.45 billion yuan. This welfare gain is about 30% higher compared with the existing approach that does not distinguish mass and niche products. We further explore geographic heterogeneity by estimating gains from variety at the county level. Accounting for the heterogeneous demand elasticities reveals that consumers in rural and lowincome regions enjoy larger gains from variety. This may be because rural consumers have limited access to product variety via traditional brick-and-mortar stores. Thus, they benefit more from the rise of online channels.

This paper contributes to the literature in several ways. First, we empirically document a massive growth of new products and find that most of the new products are niche products, which exhibit less elastic demand compared with mass products. Accounting for the niche nature of these new products generates higher gains from variety than the existing estimates (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Quan and Williams 2018).

Second, adopting a two-segment CES framework to allow for the different demand elasticities of mass and niche products is a theoretical innovation compared with the existing literature (Feenstra 1994, Broda and Weinstein 2006). This paper highlights that using the average demand elasticity to calculate welfare gains can significantly underestimate the gains from new products. Our two-segment CES model is a flexible framework that can account for heterogeneity in the demand elasticities of mass and niche products and recover the value of new products to consumers.

Third, although e-commerce seems to integrate the segmented retail markets and levels the product choices across regions, there is little empirical evidence showing that e-commerce can be an inclusive force that delivers larger welfare gains to people in less developed regions, especially in a country where the traditional retail infrastructure is still underdeveloped. This paper not only estimates the gains from variety for an average household but also illustrates the inclusive nature of ecommerce.

Finally, as China has the most rapid development of e-commerce globally and an underdeveloped traditional retail market, this paper quantifies the welfare gains from the explosion of new products by leveraging the detailed data from the largest e-commerce platform in China. In particular, compared with the existing literature, the detailed e-commerce transaction data allow us to better identify new product variety, explore the nature of new products, estimate the heterogeneous demand elasticity of mass and niche products, and gauge the welfare gains for consumers from different regions.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 presents the related literature. Section 3 discusses the data and documents the pattern of rising product variety. Section 4 develops a framework to measure consumer welfare gains from increased product variety. Section 5 estimates the demand elasticities for mass and niche products. Section 6 reports the gains from product variety by applying the framework to the data. Section 7 presents our conclusions and some ideas for future research.

## 2. Related Literature

This paper is related to the literature that evaluates the impact of new information technologies on the proliferation of niche products (Anderson 2004, Brynjolfsson et al. 2006, Cachon et al. 2008, Tucker and Zhang 2011, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan 2012, Yang 2013). Following the studies on the categorization of product popularity (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, 2010b; Anderson 2004; Zhou and Duan 2012; Tan et al. 2017), we classify products into mass and niche segments using product sales rank and find that there is an explosion of new product variety on the largest e-commerce platform in China over time and most of new products are niche products with low market shares.

Our theoretical framework follows the trade literature and uses a CES demand structure to study the gains from variety. Krugman (1979) pioneers the use of loveof-variety models to study how countries gain from trade through the import of new varieties. Feenstra (1994) derives the exact formula to account for the impact of new varieties on prices in a CES model, which has been widely used in the following literature (Hummels and Klenow 2005, Broda and Weinstein 2006, Arkolakis et al. 2008, Feenstra and Kee 2008, Li 2021). Those existing studies focus on one-segment CES framework for each industry, which fails to account for the niche nature of new varieties. In contrast, we emphasize the importance of incorporating the differences in the demand elasticities of mass and niche products and adopt a two-segment CES framework.

We document that the gain from variety is 40 times larger than the gain derived from the competitive price effect. This is much larger than the 7–10 times documented in Brynjolfsson et al. (2003). This difference is driven by three factors related to the research methodology, research question, and context. First, our methodology extends the representative consumer framework in Brynjolfsson et al. (2003) and shows that neglecting the low demand elasticity of niche products can significantly underestimate the welfare gain. Second, although previous studies focus on the gains from niche products on online platforms, our setting examines gains from new varieties in both mass and niche product segments. Lastly, in our context, the price effect reflects a reduction in prices over time, which is only half of the price effect reported by Brynjolfsson et al. (2003).

This paper also highlights the inclusive nature of e-commerce platforms in delivering larger welfare gains to consumers in rural and low-income regions. This contrasts with the findings of Dolfen et al. (2023), who show that the earlier waves of e-commerce disproportionately benefited American consumers in more densely populated areas. The difference reflects variations in the penetration of e-commerce—we examine a country, time period, and product category where e-commerce is more developed.

Finally, this paper is related to a strand of the industrial organization literature studying product design and differentiation (Johnson and Myatt 2006, Bar-Isaac et al. 2012, Menzio 2023). Our estimates of demand elasticity suggest a relatively modest level of price competition on the e-commerce platform, reflecting the large scope of product differentiation even within a seemingly standardized book market. A seller could use advertising, marketing, and product design decisions to influence consumers’ valuations and soften price competition.

## 3. Data

We obtain weekly transaction data for the book category from the largest Chinese e-commerce platform in 2015 and 2019.<sup>1</sup> In particular, data from three subcategories of books are extracted: (1) foreign languages and linguistics, (2) reference books and encyclopedias, and (3) philosophy and religion.<sup>2</sup> We focus on these three categories because the way the platform catalogues the books evolves over time, but due to the nature of the products in these three categories, there have been no changes to the cataloguing rules over the sample period, which makes it possible to more directly study the gains from product variety.

A product is defined by an item ID, which is listing specific. For each item, we gather transaction records at the item-week-county level. We collect data on both sales revenue and quantity sold. The revenue information contains the amount consumers actually pay, which includes the shipping fee. We compute the weekly average price as the ratio of revenue to quantity sold.

In addition to the transaction information, we also observe the listing information of items, including the item title, seller’s information, and most importantly, the International Standard Book Number (ISBN) of each product. ISBN is a numeric commercial book identifier that uniquely identifies each edition and variation of a book—for instance, the paperback, e-book, and hardcover editions of the same book title will have different ISBNs. Thus, the ISBN helps us to identify the same book titles across different items.

Panel A of Table 1 reports the number of product variety in 2015 and 2019. When measured by distinct ISBNs, the number of products increases by 98%, from 167,116 in 2015 to 330,597 in 2019. The number of new ISBNs in 2019 is about three times that of the surviving ISBNs. In addition, there are typically multiple items selling the same ISBN, reflecting different sellers with different ways of positioning the same book. In 2015, there are about 5.7 different items for each ISBN. The number of items per ISBN declines over time. As a result, when measured by the number of distinct items, the increase in product variety is not as great as the increase in ISBNs. Finally, compared with surviving products, new products tend to be niche products. The average market share of a new ISBN is only about 29% that of a surviving ISBN. There are also fewer items selling new ISBNs; on average, there are 2.8 items for a new ISBN and 7.2 items for a surviving ISBN.<sup>3</sup>

## 4. Estimating Gains from Variety

We follow the trade literature to estimate the gains from product variety by using a CES specification for consumer demand. To highlight the difference between mass and niche products, we extend the existing onesegment CES framework by adopting a two-segment CES utility function:

$$
U _ {t} = \left[ C _ {M, t} ^ {\frac {\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} + C _ {N, t} ^ {\frac {\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} \right] ^ {\frac {\sigma}{\sigma - 1}}\tag{1}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{l} C _ {M, t} = \left[ \sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {M, t}} \left(\phi_ {i} C _ {i, t}\right) ^ {\frac {\sigma_ {M} - 1}{\sigma_ {M}}} \right] ^ {\frac {\sigma_ {M}}{\sigma_ {M} - 1}}, \\ C _ {N, t} = \left[ \sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {N, t}} \left(\phi_ {i} C _ {i, t}\right) ^ {\frac {\sigma_ {N} - 1}{\sigma_ {N}}} \right] ^ {\frac {\sigma_ {N}}{\sigma_ {N} - 1}}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The aggregate consumption (utility) $U _ { t }$ of the representative consumers is given by a CES combination of category consumption goods $\dot { C } _ { M , t }$ and $C _ { N , t }$ , which in turn are produced by combining individual products within the mass and niche product categories; $C _ { i , t }$ is the consumption of product i at time $t ; \phi _ { i }$ denotes the perceived quality of the product; and $\Omega _ { M , i }$ and $\Omega _ { N , \mathrm { ~  ~ } }$ <sub>t</sub> are the sets of available mass and niche products at time t, respectively. We allow the demand elasticities to differ between mass and niche products. Product i refers to an item in our data, as we observe a sizable product differentiation even within an ISBN.<sup>6</sup>

Under the CES preferences, the unit cost function for the two category consumption goods $C _ { M , t }$ and $C _ { N , t }$ are

$$
\begin{array}{l} P _ {M, t} = \left[ \sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {M, t}} \left(\frac {P _ {i , t}}{\phi_ {i}}\right) ^ {1 - \sigma_ {M}} \right] ^ {\frac {1}{1 - \sigma_ {M}}}, \\ P _ {N, t} = \left[ \sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {N, t}} \left(\frac {P _ {i , t}}{\phi_ {i}}\right) ^ {1 - \sigma_ {N}} \right] ^ {\frac {1}{1 - \sigma_ {N}}}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

For a given product i within a product category $j \in \{ M , \bar { N } \}$ , the utility maximization problem of consumers entails that the expenditure share of the product within the product category depends on the price and perceived quality of the product:

$$
s _ {i, j, t} = \frac {\left(\frac {P _ {i , t}}{\phi_ {i}}\right) ^ {1 - \sigma_ {j}}}{\sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {j , t}} \left(\frac {P _ {i , t}}{\phi_ {i}}\right) ^ {1 - \sigma_ {j}}} = \frac {\left(\frac {P _ {i , t}}{\phi_ {i}}\right) ^ {1 - \sigma_ {j}}}{P _ {j , t} ^ {1 - \sigma_ {j}}},\tag{4}
$$

where $P _ { i , t }$ <sub>t</sub> is the price of the product at time t. With an elastic consumer demand $( \sigma _ { j } > 1 )$ , Equation (4) shows that products with lower prices and higher perceived quality have larger market shares.

In a dynamic market, product prices change over time, and the set of available products churns as new varieties enter the market and old varieties exit the mar ket. We first look at the impact of product market dynamics on the unit cost of category consumption goods. Let $\Omega _ { j , t , t + 1 } ^ { \star }$ denote the set of common products in category $j$ that have transactions in both periods t and t + 1. Summing expenditures across these common varieties, we obtain the following expression for the aggregate share of common varieties in period t:

$$
\lambda_ {j, t} = \frac {\sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {j , t , t + 1} ^ {\star}} P _ {i , t} C _ {i , t}}{\sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {j , t}} P _ {i , t} C _ {i , t}} = \frac {\sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {j , t , t + 1} ^ {\star}} \left(\frac {P _ {i , t}}{\phi_ {i}}\right) ^ {1 - \sigma_ {j}}}{\sum_ {i \in \Omega_ {j , t}} \left(\frac {P _ {i , t}}{\phi_ {i}}\right) ^ {1 - \sigma_ {j}}} = \frac {P _ {j , t} ^ {\star 1 - \sigma_ {j}}}{P _ {j , t} ^ {1 - \sigma_ {j}}},\tag{5}
$$

where $P _ { j , t } ^ { \star }$ is the cost function of the common varieties in product category j. The expenditure share of an individual variety in Equation (4) can be then rewritten as its expenditure share on common varieties $( s _ { i , j , t } ^ { \star } )$ times the aggregate share of common varieties in the category:

$$
s _ {i, j, t} = s _ {i, j, t} ^ {\star} \lambda_ {j, t}.\tag{6}
$$

Using Equations (4), (5), and (6), we can derive the change in the unit cost of category consumption goods over time:

$$
\frac {P _ {j , t + 1}}{P _ {j , t}} = \Pi_ {i \in \Omega_ {j, t, t + 1} ^ {*}} \left(\frac {P _ {i , t + 1}}{P _ {i , t}}\right) ^ {\omega_ {i, j, t, t + 1}} \cdot \left(\frac {\lambda_ {j , t + 1}}{\lambda_ {j , t}}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{\sigma_ {j} - 1}},\tag{7}
$$

where $\omega _ { i , j , t , t + 1 }$ is the Sato-Vartia weight of the product<sup>7</sup>:

$$
\omega_ {i, j, t, t + 1} = \frac {\frac {s _ {i , j , t + 1} ^ {\star} - s _ {i , j , t} ^ {\star}}{l n (s _ {i , j , t + 1} ^ {\star}) - l n (s _ {i , j , t} ^ {\star})}}{\sum_ {k \in \Omega_ {j , t , t + 1} ^ {\star}} \frac {s _ {k , j , t + 1} ^ {\star} - s _ {k , j , t} ^ {\star}}{l n (s _ {k , j , t + 1} ^ {\star}) - l n (s _ {k , j , t} ^ {\star})}}.\tag{8}
$$

Equation (7) shows that a reduction in the unit cost of category consumption goods can be driven by two channels.<sup>8</sup> First, declines in the prices of common varieties can reduce the cost of category consumption goods. Second, an increase in product variety that reduces the expenditure share of common varieties can also lower the cost of category consumption goods. This variety channel becomes stronger when products are less substitutable, that is, when $\sigma _ { j }$ is low.

The expression in Equation (7) has been applied extensively in the trade literature to estimate the gains from import variety (Feenstra 1994, Broda and Weinstein 2006). In our context of online platforms, we find there is an explosion of new product varieties over time, and most of them are niche products with low market shares. To highlight the niche nature of the new varieties, we extend the conventional one-segment CES framework to capture the potential differences in the demand elasticity between mass and niche products.

The demand elasticities $\sigma _ { M }$ and $\sigma _ { N }$ can differ for many reasons. First, as the market size is different for products with different levels of popularity, the demand elasticities of mass and niche products tend to be different. For example, a large market size may attract more sellers to the market, which increases the competition and demand elasticity. Additionally, there is a potential salience effect. Mass products are more likely to enter the consideration set of consumers, making their demand more sensitive to price changes. If we neglect the elasticity difference between mass and niche products, we will most likely underestimate the gains from variety by mistakenly treating niche products as just as substitutable as existing products.

Finally, for the aggregate consumption, the price index is again a weighted average of unit cost changes of the category consumption goods:

$$
\frac {P _ {t + 1}}{P _ {t}} = \left(\frac {P _ {M , t + 1}}{P _ {M , t}}\right) ^ {\omega_ {M, t, t + 1}} \left(\frac {P _ {N , t + 1}}{P _ {N , t}}\right) ^ {\omega_ {N, t, t + 1}},\tag{9}
$$

where $\omega _ { M , t , t + 1 }$ and $\omega _ { N , t , t + 1 }$ are the Sato-Vartia weights at the category level. To convert the price index into welfare gains, we ask the following question: if there are no product market dynamics with the price index staying at period t level, how much more do consumers have to spend to achieve the same level of utility at period $t + 1 2$ The solution to this question is given by the difference in the consumer’s expenditure function:

$$
\Delta C S = E (U _ {t + 1}, P _ {t}) - E _ {t + 1} = \left(\frac {P _ {t}}{P _ {t + 1}} - 1\right) E _ {t + 1}.\tag{10}
$$

Under the CES specification, the impact of product market dynamics on consumer welfare can be fully characterized by the change in the aggregate price index, which in turn depends on the product-level prices, expenditure shares, and demand elasticities of mass and niche products. In the following sections, we estimate the demand elasticities for the two product categories separately and apply Equation (10) to estimate the gains from product variety over time.

## 5. Demand Elasticity Estimation

We use a log-linear demand system to estimate the ownprice elasticity and omit the cross-price elasticity.<sup>9</sup> We denote the quantities as $q$ and prices as $p .$ For an item i sold to county l at week t in quarter q, we estimate log linear regressions as follows:

$$
l o g (q _ {i t l}) = \alpha_ {i t} + \beta_ {j} l o g (p _ {i t l}) + \gamma_ {q l} + \epsilon_ {i t l},\tag{11}
$$

where $\alpha _ { i t }$ are item-week fixed effects, and $\gamma _ { q l }$ are quarter-county fixed effects. The coefficient on prices, $\beta _ { j } ,$ measures the price elasticities separately for products in different categories (mass or niche). The high-dimensional fixed effects at the item-week level guarantee that the demand response we study comes from the exact same product of the same seller.

The direct ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of Equation (11) may suffer from standard endogeneity concerns: sellers could adjust prices in response to county-level demand shocks. On the e-commerce platform, although the listing price may be the same across consumers, sellers could still discriminate by issuing coupons that target particular markets and consumers.

If sellers tend to increase the price when there is a positive demand shock, we would expect the OLS estimates to be biased toward zero. To minimize this endogeneity concern, we apply an instrumental variable (IV) approach. The instrument we use is the shipping fee that consumers pay on the transaction. The shipping fee is included in product sales and is a part of prices. The identifying assumption is that sellers are unlikely to change shipping fees in response to a temporary countylevel demand shock.

There are two reasons behind the choice of the instrument. First, on the platform, each seller sells a large number of products every day. The average number of items listed per seller on a particular day is more than 8,000.<sup>10</sup> Thus, it is unlikely that sellers would change individual shipping fees in response to a county-level demand shock. Second, the way that shipping fees are set by sellers also mitigates the endogeneity concern. Each seller only has a few shipping templates, and each template specifies how the shipping fee is calculated based on shipping destination and logistics service providers. Each item is associated with one shipping template, and the shipping fee is calculated automatically once a transaction takes place. The fact that one shipping template is shared by a large number of items makes the identifying assumption likely to hold. Furthermore, according to several sellers we interviewed, in cases when they do change the shipping fee of an item, it usually involves switching between two templates rather than adjusting a shipping template in response to a temporary countylevel demand shock.

It is worth noting that, for identification purposes, we do not assume that shipping fees are completely exogenous to demand shocks. If sellers adjust shipping fees in response to demand shocks at the item-week level or quarter-county level, this does not jeopardize the identification of demand elasticity. After controlling for fixed effects in the regression, we use the residual variation of shipping fees across counties to identify demand elasticity. 11

Table 2 shows the estimation results of the demand elasticity. Panel A of Table 2 reports the first-stage regression between shipping fees and prices. As expected, shipping fees strongly correlate with product prices, and the amount of price variation that can be explained by shipping fees is large, about 19%. Panel B of Table 2 shows our estimates of demand elasticities using weekly transaction data from 2015. The first three columns show the results using OLS regressions. The last three columns show the IV regressions where we instrument prices using shipping fees. Several observations can be made. First, the demand elasticity is low even in the standardized and competitive book market. On average, a 1% increase in price translates into a 1.8% decrease in quantity sold. One explanation for the low demand elasticity is the large scope of product differentiation on the e-commerce platform. Second, the demand for mass products is more elastic. A 1% price increase corresponds to a 1.9% and a 1.5% decrease in sales for mass and niche products, respectively. In addition, we find that the IV estimates are larger for each specification than the OLS estimates. Sellers seem to be more likely to increase prices when there is a positive demand shock, generating a standard attenuation bias in the OLS estimates. By instrumenting prices using the shipping fees, we address this bias and recover the estimates of demand elasticities for the two product categories.<sup>12</sup>

## 6. Quantitative Results 6.1. Gains from Variety

In our CES framework laid out in Section 4, the welfare implications of product market dynamics can be summarized by their impact on the CES price index. Table 3 reports the change in the price index and consumer surplus over time by leveraging the transaction data on the e-commerce platform.

In a dynamic market, falling prices of common products can lower the cost of living and increase consumer surplus. The first row in Panel A of Table 3 reports the change in the price index for common products between 2015 and 2019. The Common Products Price Index is a geometric average of product-level price changes of the common products.<sup>13</sup> Over the four years, the Common Products Price Index declined by 3.3%, which implies a 3% increase in consumer welfare.

The Common Products Price Index only evaluates the price changes for surviving products, which fails to account for the product turnover in the market. With our CES framework, we can use the expenditure shares of common varieties over time to infer the impact of product turnover on the price index. In the second row of Panel A, we find a much sharper decline in the price index after accounting for product turnover: the price in 2019 is 56% lower than in 2015. Given the large increase in product variety, consumers reallocate their expenditure toward new products, which results in a significantly lower cost of living. The large price decline translates into a dramatic increase in consumer surplus, which is about 128% of the total expenditure on books in 2019.

The Common Products Price Index is an average of the category-level price index for common varieties, which in turn is computed as a weighted average of the product-level price changes of the common varieties.

As consumers gain from both the price reduction in common varieties and the emergence of new varieties, we isolate the gain from increased variety alone by treating the Common Product Price Index as unity. In the last column of the second row, we show that the variety channel alone generates welfare gains of about 120% of the total expenditure on books in 2019, which is about 40 times larger than the gain derived from the price effect on common varieties.

To put the number into perspective, for the three categories of books in our sample, the total expenditure in 2019 $( E _ { 2 0 1 9 } )$ is about 1.2 billion yuan, implying that the gains from the increased variety are about 1.45 billion yuan. For all categories of books on the platform, $E _ { 2 0 1 9 }$ is about 40 billion yuan.<sup>14</sup> If there were a similar entry pattern, given the estimates of demand elasticity, the gains would be 48 billion yuan for books overall. At current exchange rates, that is about 6.7 billion U.S. dollars.

The third and fourth rows in Panel A of Table 3 show the gains from variety for different values of $\sigma _ { M } .$ Even when the demand for mass products becomes much more elastic, that is, $\sigma _ { M } = 2 . 5$ , the gains from variety are still large, given the low elasticity of niche products. This highlights that the value of niche products is largely underestimated if we do not correctly identify their demand elasticity.

We also compare our estimates with the traditional approach that features the one-segment CES demand. The second row in Panel B of Table 3 shows the gains from variety using the conventional approach. With an average elasticity of $\sigma = 1 . 8 ,$ , the price index declines by 50% between 2015 and 2019, implying a gain in consumer welfare of $0 . 9 4 E _ { 2 0 1 9 }$ . The traditional approach assumes that demand elasticities for mass and niche products are identical. However, given the observation that there is a sharp increase of niche products over time and niche products tend to have more inelastic demand, this approach underestimates gains from product variety by about 30%.

One concern is that our approach may generate spurious product entries and exits that could potentially bias the welfare estimates upward. If an item changes its category from one year to another, we treat it as exiting one category and entering another category. To gauge the magnitude of such bias, the fifth row in Panel A of Table 3 reports the case when $\sigma _ { M }$ and $\sigma _ { N }$ are equal to the average elasticity 1.8. The gain from variety is slightly smal ler than that calculated in the one-segment CES approach, where the latter has no concern for such bias. These results show that the large gains from variety do not come from spurious entries and exits. If anything, the spurious entry pattern slightly reduces the estimated gains.

Our findings indicate that the gains from variety can be 40 times larger than the gains from price effects, which is substantially higher than the estimates found in Brynjolfsson et al. (2003).<sup>15</sup> This difference is driven by three factors related to the research methodology, research question, and context. First, our methodology extends the representative consumer framework used in the previous studies and shows that failing to account for the low demand elasticity of niche products can significantly underestimate the welfare gains. Using the average demand elasticity, $\sigma _ { M } = \sigma _ { N } = 1 . 8 $ , would lower the gains by approximately 30%, from $1 . 2 1 E _ { 2 0 1 9 }$ to $0 . 8 6 \bar { E } _ { 2 0 1 9 }$

Second, this paper addresses a different research question. Although previous studies primarily focus on the gains from niche products on online platforms, this paper examines the gains from new varieties in both mass and niche product segments over time. The last row in Panel A of Table 3 shows the effect of excluding new variety gains in the mass product segment. When focusing solely on niche products, the gains drop by an additional 50% approximately, from $0 . 8 6 E _ { 2 0 1 9 }$ to $0 . 4 0 E _ { 2 0 1 9 }$ . This suggests that the gains from niche product variety alone are about 13 times larger than those derived from price effects on common varieties.

Using a demand elasticity of 1.79, Brynjolfsson et al. (2003) show that gains from niche product variety online are about seven times larger than the gains from lower prices online. This remaining gap between their results and ours can be attributed to differences in research context. In their study, they estimate online prices to be about 6% lower than traditional channels. In contrast, in our study, the price effect reflects the reduction in prices over time, which amounts to only about 3%. Once this difference in price effects is accounted for, the remaining gap is closed.

## 6.2. Heterogeneity Across Markets

The impacts of digital marketplaces may differ across markets. Consumers in rural regions have less access to new varieties via traditional brick-and-mortar stores and thus may benefit more from the online channel. In this section, we explore geographic heterogeneity and estimate gains from variety at the county level.

We examine how the gains from variety vary across counties. Figure 1(a) plots the gains against population density at the county level and confirms a strong negative correlation between the two.<sup>16</sup> The result is consistent with our expectation that the development of online marketplaces provides rural counties with muchneeded access to variety, delivering them larger benefits. Table 4 reports the regression output when we regress gains from variety on the level of urbanization, income per capita, and distance to Hangzhou.<sup>17</sup> In addition to the urbanization level, which is measured by population density, counties with higher incomes tend to derive lower gains from variety in online marketplaces, whereas remote counties, measured by the distance to the e-commerce hub Hangzhou, enjoy larger benefits from variety.

One concern of the results above is that we impose the assumption that all counties have the same demand elasticity. Given the large heterogeneity across markets, the demand elasticity can vary for many reasons. In particular, rural consumers have more limited choices offline and therefore they rely more on the online channel. This is likely to translate into a lower demand elasticity. In addition, there can be taste heterogeneity, as rural consumers tend to be poorer and demand fewer niche products. We explore these possibilities by dividing the sample into four groups based on population density, and estimate demand elasticity separately for each group.

Figure 1(b) reports the estimates of demand elasticity for different groups of counties in 2015, with Group 1 as the least populated counties and Group 4 as the most populated counties. For mass products, the demand elasticity increases strongly with urbanization. This is consistent with the idea that more populated regions have many competing channels for mass products, thus demand is very elastic. Rural consumers are more reliant on online markets for mass products, resulting in a less elastic demand. For niche products, demand elasticity is similar across counties, which can be driven by both taste heterogeneity and alternative shopping channels. 18 The results suggest that gains from variety for rural consumers would be even higher if we considered the heterogeneity of demand elasticities across counties.<sup>19</sup>

Our results on the inclusive nature of e-commerce contrast with the findings of Dolfen et al. (2023). They find that earlier waves of e-commerce disproportionately benefited American consumers in more densely populated areas. This contrast reflects differences in the penetration of e-commerce in these two countries. We examine a country, time period, and product category in which e-commerce is more developed.

## 7. Concluding Remarks

One of the biggest potential benefits of online platforms is that they facilitate the entry of new products, particularly those addressing niche needs that would otherwise go unaddressed. This paper studies the magnitude and nature of new product variety on the largest online platform in China, and quantifies their welfare implications for consumers. Using sales data for three categories of books, we find that the amount of product variety, measured by the number of distinct ISBNs, almost doubles between 2015 and 2019. At the same time, the sales shares of the top 1,000 ISBNs remain largely unchanged. This suggests that the new products tend to be niche products and individually account for small market shares, although they are collectively important.

We develop a two-segment CES framework and find that the demand for niche products tends to be less elastic than that of mass products. Embedding the estimates of demand elasticities into the framework, we find that the gain from product variety is very large. In fact, consumers would have needed to spend 120% more in order to achieve the same level of utility in 2019 if there had been no increase in product variety. Our results highlight not only the explosion of new products, but also the importance of accounting for the fact that most new products are niche products with relatively inelastic demand. The welfare gain in our analysis is about 30% higher than in the traditional approach that does not distinguish mass and niche products.

For platform owners, increasing the supply of niche products delivers greater consumer value than their sales numbers might suggest, making this an effective approach to creating consumer value. Our results suggest that increasing product variety may be more effective than price reductions in generating consumer gains in certain categories. On the policy side, the paper highlights that rural and lowincome consumers benefit greatly from the increased variety available through e-commerce, indicating that policymakers should support the development of e-commerce in underserved regions to enhance consumer welfare.

Many related research questions remain for future research. For example, we need to better understand the competition between sellers and the nature of product creation. We find that despite the multiple listings within an ISBN, demand for even mass products is still not very elastic. This implies that even in a standardized book market, sellers differentiate via marketing and product positioning. Furthermore, our framework does not explicitly model the search and information frictions in the process of consumer decision making. In a dynamic setting where these types of frictions are incorporated, product differentiation could also be a source of obfuscation that increases search costs and reduces consumer welfare. Finally, this paper uses data from a single dominant platform. However, the competitive landscape of e-commerce platforms has changed greatly since 2019. The rise of new platforms with distinctive competition strategies, such as group-selling modes, will have implications for the welfare gains of consumers in the e-commerce ecosystem. We believe these issues are valuable topics for future studies.
