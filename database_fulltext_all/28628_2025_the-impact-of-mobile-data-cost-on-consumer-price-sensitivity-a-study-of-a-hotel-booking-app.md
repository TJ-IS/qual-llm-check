# The Impact of Mobile Data Cost on Consumer Price Sensitivity: A Study of a Hotel Booking App

Abstract. The rapid advancement of mobile internet technologies, such as 4G and 5 G, has greatly enhanced the accessibility and convenience of mobile shopping. However, along with these benefits, mobile data usage has introduced a novel form of search cost in consumers’ purchasing decisions compared with Wi-Fi. This arises from the perception that mobile data are more costly because of additional charges incurred when exceeding the fixed data quota, which contributes to the increase of mental cost during the search process. To examine the influence of mobile data cost on consumer price sensitivity, we conduct empirical research utilizing hotel search data obtained from a prominent hotel booking app. Our findings reveal that consumers exhibit approximately twice the level of price sensitivity when utilizing mobile data instead of Wi-Fi, resulting in an average US\$17 less in revenue per hotel booking. To assess the causal relations between mobile data cost and consumer price sensitivity, we utilize an approach similar to difference-in-differences and observe that this impact is more pronounced when consumers book hotels at the end of the month (versus the beginning of the month). We further find that consumer search effort decreases when using mobile data (versus Wi-Fi) across decision stages of the search process, supporting the mechanism that mobile data cost drives consumers into the satisficing rather than maximizing search mode. Our research offers practical implications for businesses operating in mobile shopping environments and contributes to policy development in this field.

## 1. Introduction

Mobile shopping has become a crucial component of e-commerce in recent years. Since 2015, mobile shopping has experienced steady growth and is anticipated to account for 44% of total e-commerce sales by 2025 (Szaniawska-Schiavo 2022). The evolution of mobile technologies, including 4G and 5 G, has made mobile shopping increasingly accessible and convenient. However, these technologies have also introduced a novel form of search cost in consumers’ shopping processes: mobile data cost. When consumers opt to use mobile data instead of Wi-Fi, they often perceive it as incurring additional expenses (Xu et al. 2019). This perception arises from the fact that mobile data usage is typically associated with charges when surpassing data quota, whereas Wi-Fi usage does not (Fogg 2018). Specifically, when using mobile data, consumers seek to stay within their data limits to avoid potential additional costs in the future (Sahota 2014, Seitz 2024). This forwardlooking behavior when managing their data consumption (Xu et al. 2019) leads to the perceived cost of using mobile data. Such cost is more likely to be perceived internally as a mental cost rather than the actual cost from an external aspect (Smith et al. 1999, Ngwe et al. 2019) as using mobile data does not necessarily result in extra charges if data quotas are not exceeded.

Despite the extensive literature on search cost, there has been limited attention given to the cost difference between using mobile data and Wi-Fi. Previous studies have extensively examined how the adoption of new technologies affects search cost and subsequently influences consumer behavior. For example, scholars find that internet adoption plays a crucial role in reducing search cost for consumers, leading to a compelling difference between consumer behavior in online and off-line channels in terms of price sensitivity, price dispersion, and product variety (Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000, Lynch and Ariely 2000, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Granados et al. 2012). With the emergence of mobile shopping, how mobile channels influence search cost and shape consumer behavior has also drawn substantial scholarly attention. In comparison with PC internet users, mobile channel consumers face higher search cost (Bang et al. 2013), thus leading to different behavioral patterns, such as a preference for higher ranked or habitual products (Ghose et al. 2013, Wang et al. 2015), a tendency to narrow the diversity of purchased products (Park et al. 2020), and a reduction in purchase decision accuracy (Fink and Papismedov 2023).

When it comes to the evolution of mobile technologies, there is a scarcity of research that examines consumer behavior differences between using mobile data and Wi-Fi. To our knowledge, only a limited number of studies address this issue. For instance, Walelgne et al. (2021) find that Finnish mobile users consume more data through mobile data than Wi-Fi, perhaps because of the affordability of mobile data plans in Finland. Li et al. (2015) show that data-intensive apps, such as video or music apps, primarily consume data from Wi-Fi instead of mobile data. Wu et al. (2022) examine users app usage patterns and find that users tend to use mobile apps less frequently with mobile data than with Wi-Fi. However, no studies analyze the differences from a search cost perspective, nor do they address consumers’ purchase behavior in mobile shopping. More specifically, previous literature (Kapteyn et al. 1979, Simonson and Tversky 1992, Hanley et al. 2001) indicates that the mental cost associated with cognitive limitations could alter consumers’ approaches to obtaining information and making choices, switching with two major modes: maximizing and satisficing (Schwartz et al. 2002, Ma and Roese 2014). In our context, it becomes intriguing to examine whether consumers switch from the maximizing to the satisficing search mode when using mobile data compared with Wi-Fi.

Given the prevalence of mobile shopping, even a subtle behavioral difference arising from using mobile data or Wi-Fi could lead to a significant impact on the mobile shopping market. Mobile data usage is commonly perceived by consumers as incurring additional expenses (Xu et al. 2019). This perception may cause consumers to constrain their search efforts and ultimately make fewer purchases when using mobile data as opposed to Wi-Fi, leading to a reduction in online retailers’ sales. To mitigate this concern, mobile data subsidies have been employed as a common marketing tool in recent years. However, ongoing debates surround the provision of mobile data subsidies. Advocates argue that reducing the cost of mobile data enhances customer engagement and retention for firms (Kra¨mer and Peitz 2018). Conversely, some assert that the mobile internet connectivity type has no bearing on transitioning between different apps (Wu et al. 2022), implying that mobile data subsidies may have a limited effect on consumer behavior. Research on the impact of mobile data cost can shed light on these debates and provide insights for researchers and practitioners in the fields of information systems and marketing.

Our proposed study aims to investigate the behavioral differences associated with using mobile data versus Wi-Fi and their impact on consumer purchase behavior. As a key factor in assessing consumers’ willingness to pay for products or services, price sensitivity can significantly reflect changes in purchasing behavior (Wakefield and Inman 2003). Therefore, we seek to address two research questions: (1) What is the influence of using mobile data (versus Wi-Fi) on consumers purchase behavior with specific attention given to their price sensitivity? (2) What are the underlying mechanisms that explain this effect? To answer these questions, we collaborate with a prominent online travel agency in China and obtain a comprehensive individual-level data set of hotel bookings, including consumers’ search and transaction details conducted within a mobile app.

To address the potential issue of selection bias that may arise from self-selection on whether to use mobile data or Wi-Fi, we apply coarsened exact matching (CEM) techniques to ensure comparability between sessions that use mobile data and those that use Wi-Fi. We then employ a hierarchical multinomial discrete choice model to examine the differences in consumers decision-making processes between these two groups of sessions. Our findings show that consumers exhibit approximately twice the level of price sensitivity when utilizing mobile data instead of Wi-Fi. This change in price sensitivity can be translated into an average of US\$17 less in revenue per hotel booking.<sup>1</sup> These results are consistent in various robustness checks. In addition, we leverage the unique mobile data service scheme in our study to assess the causal relations between mobile data cost and consumer price sensitivity using a difference-in-differences (DID)-style approach. In our context, the mobile data usage cycle resets on the first day of each month, providing consumers with adequate data at the beginning of the month. However, toward the end of the month, consumers may reach or exceed their data limit, resulting in a perception of higher mobile data cost. In line with our expectations, we observe a greater impact of mobile data cost on price sensitivity at the end of the month than at the beginning. These results not only provide empirical evidence to support our findings, but also strengthen our causal claim.

We propose an underlying mechanism of the mobile data cost to explain these findings. Because consumers have a fixed mobile data quota and are charged extra fees when exceeding it, they perceive a higher search cost for mobile data compared with Wi-Fi (Walelgne et al. 2021, Wu et al. 2022). This mobile data cost drives consumers into the satisficing rather than the maximizing search mode (Levav et al. 2012, Ma and Roese 2014). Consumers in the satisficing search mode reduce search effort in acquiring information, particularly decreasing the acquisition of product information. As the unavailable product information would not be assigned with importance weights during the search process, consumers rely more on the price information and become more price sensitive when using mobile data compared with Wi-Fi (Degeratu et al. 2000, Lynch and Ariely 2000).

To validate our proposed mechanism, we systematically examine the differences in search effort between mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions across decision stages of the search process in the online hotel booking context. Specifically, we segment the search process into four stages (Kim et al. 2010, Dellaert and Ha¨ubl 2012, Chen and Yao 2017, Ursu 2018, Ghose et al. 2019): conducting search, forming consideration set, acquiring product details, and confirming order. During the conductingsearch stage, consumers specify fewer queries and use refinement tools and query words less frequently when utilizing mobile data in contrast to when using Wi-Fi. Moving to the forming-consideration-set stage, mobile data users view fewer hotels than Wi-Fi users. In the acquiring-product-details stage, mobile data sessions involve reading a lower number of reviews and utilizing filters to read reviews less frequently than Wi-Fi sessions. As for the confirming-order stage, mobile data users are less likely than Wi-Fi users to experience regret and dissatisfaction and, thus, less inclined to abandon their orders during this final confirmation step in favor of exploring other options. For the entire hotel booking journey, consumers spend less time searching when using mobile data compared with those using Wi-Fi. These findings are in line with the behavioral differences between the satisficing and maximizing search modes, supporting the argument that mobile data cost drives consumers toward the satisficing search mode.

Our contributions are threefold. First, our study contributes to the literature by expanding the scope of search cost to include mobile data cost. Previous studies on search cost and mobile shopping overlook the role of connectivity type on consumer purchase behavior, let alone consider the impact of different search costs that arise when using mobile data and Wi-Fi. By examining consumer purchase behavior when using mobile data and Wi-Fi, our research reveals that consumers using mobile data exhibit higher price sensitivity than those using Wi-Fi, and this behavioral difference can be attributed to the impact of a novel form of search cost: mobile data cost. To our knowledge, this research is the first attempt in the fields of information systems and marketing to explore how using mobile data (versus Wi-Fi) affects consumer price sensitivity. Our research deepens the understanding of mobile data cost, arguing that it is inherently a mental cost rather than an actual cost. Our study also sheds light on the important role of connectivity type in mobile shopping, in which even subtle behavioral differences arising from using mobile data or Wi-Fi alter consumers’ search and purchase decisions. Furthermore, our research reinforces that the role of mobile data cost in mobile shopping needs to be taken seriously by researchers and practitioners.

Second, we enrich the literature by proposing an underlying mechanism that explains how mobile data and Wi-Fi induce different behaviors. Whereas previous literature on connectivity type investigates different usage patterns when using mobile data and Wi-Fi, it has not explored how these differences impact user pur chase behavior. Our study examines the underlying mechanism that drives the impact of mobile data cost on consumer purchase behavior. We propose that mobile data cost drives consumers into the satisficing rather than the maximizing search mode, and consu mers in the satisficing search mode constrain their search effort on information acquisition. To investigate how consumers’ search behavior changes under mobile data or Wi-Fi, we conduct a systematic examination of consumers’ search process by exploring numerous search effort variables. Notably, the filed data used in this study is unique and finely granular, which allows for the systematic examination of consumers’ search process consisting of multiple stages. By examining differences in search effort between using mobile data and Wi-Fi across decision stages, our research provides a theoretical framework to explain how mobile data cost impacts consumer price sensitivity, which is comple mentary to current related research. With a detailed examination of the mechanism revealing the empirical results, our findings can also be generalized to most mobile shopping contexts with similar consumer shopping journeys.

Finally, our study offers empirical evidence for the relationship between product information search and consumer price sensitivity. Previous research typically examines this effect by manipulating the amount of product information acquired during the search process using laboratory experiments (Lynch and Ariely 2000, Li et al. 2014). In contrast, our research utilizes search effort variations between mobile data and Wi-Fi, especially focusing on the search effort exerted in obtaining product information, to trigger different search costs for acquiring product information based on a large-scale observational data set from a real-life mobile shopping context. We demonstrate that using mobile data or

Wi-Fi leads to different search costs, which drive consumers into different search modes and, consequently, affect search effort and the amount of product information acquired. In this sense, our study offers unique field empirical evidence to support the theoretical relationship that less product information leads to a higher focus on price information, which increases price sensitivity (Degeratu et al. 2000).

## 2. Literature Review

Three streams of research in information systems and marketing areas are related to our study. We begin by reviewing the literature on the relationship between search costs and consumer purchase behavior, followed by an examination of search cost categories, and end with an exploration of research on connectivity types and consumer behavior. As the role of connectivity types receives scant attention in existing research, we identify gaps in the literature and highlight the need to investigate how mobile data usage affects search cost and ultimately influences consumers’ purchase behavior.

## 2.1. Search Cost and Consumer Purchase Behavior

The relationship between lower search cost and consumer purchase behavior is one of the most frequently discussed research topics since the rise of e-commerce as consumers acquire price and product offering information much more easily in online channels than they could in off-line channels (Brynjolfsson et al. 2009). A stream of research employs internet adoption as a proxy for the reduction of search cost, demonstrating that online shopping offers lower search cost for consumers seeking market information (Bakos 1997, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011). This stream of research provides evidence that lower search cost leads to an impact on purchase behavior, such as price sensitivity (Lynch and Ariely 2000, Granados et al. 2012), price dispersion (Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000), and product variety (Brynjolfsson et al. 2010, 2011).

Research on price sensitivity is highly relevant to our study. Previous studies examine how the reduction of search cost in online channels influences the information gathered during the search process and ultimately affects consumer price sensitivity (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000, Lynch and Ariely 2000, Granados et al. 2012). E-commerce marketplaces provide both price and product information to facilitate product comparison (Bakos 1997), whereas the effect of reducing search cost for price information and product information on price sensitivity is the opposite direction. On one hand, facilitated by easier price information search in online markets, the ease of comparing prices for similar product offerings leads to higher price sensitivity compared with traditional off-line markets (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000, Li et al. 2014). On the other hand, when more product information is available and acquired, consumers become less price sensitive if they pay greater attention to product details to identify those that better fit their needs (e.g., Lynch and Ariely 2000, Granados et al. 2012, Li et al. 2014). In this sense, the relationship between search cost and consumer price sensitivity depends on whether the effect of search cost on acquiring product information outweighs that on acquiring price information.

The emergence of mobile internet changes the behavior and shopping habits of consumers, including their perception of search cost (Bang et al. 2013). Prior research suggests that mobile shopping imposes higher search cost for consumers compared with PC internet usage because of constraints such as smaller screen sizes and lower mobile internet speed (Bang et al. 2013). Because of higher search cost, consumers who shop using mobile internet tend to prefer better ranked products that appear at the top of the screen (Ghose et al. 2013), purchase habitual goods that require minimal information before purchasing (Wang et al. 2015), buy more “head” products that do not necessitate extensive scrolling through product listings (Park et al. 2020), and exhibit lower accuracy in purchase decisions (Fink and Papismedov 2023).

Despite the considerable research on the relationship between search cost and consumer behavior in mobile and online shopping, few studies examine the differences in search cost when using mobile data versus Wi-Fi and their impact on consumer price sensitivity. This gap in the literature highlights an empirical question: how does using mobile data (versus using Wi-Fi) influence consumers’ search process for price and product information and ultimately price sensitivity? Answering this question provides empirical evidence for whether the effect of mobile data cost for product information outweighs that for price information in mobile shopping, which has important implications for both researchers and practitioners.

## 2.2. Mobile Data Cost and Mental Cost

Mobile data cost is a novel form of search cost. In consumer search literature, search cost is the core concept that is conceived as the physical or mental effort, time, and money required to locate potential alternatives and make a purchase decision (Bakos 1997, Bell et al. 1998). According to the source and nature of search costs, prior research has comprehensively divided search costs into external costs and internal costs (Smith et al. 1999, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011). External costs are related to external factors and resources, involving the actual costs or the physical effort invested in information acquisition, such as the time and money spent facilitating search activities, and the physical effort to locate desired products (Smith et al. 1999). Internal costs mainly emerge from the mental efforts involved in processing information, such as organizing incoming information, accessing stored information, and facilitating searches for new information, thereby emphasizing the usage of cognitive resources (Smith et al. 1999, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011).

In the context of using mobile data, consumers are aware that the data billing scheme involves fees for a fixed data quota and additional charges for extra data usage. They also understand that they will not incur additional costs if their usage does not exceed the data limit. Therefore, they aim to stay within their data limits to avoid potential additional costs (Sahota 2014, Seitz 2024). During this process, the cost of using mobile data is perceived before any additional charges are incurred as consumers typically manage their data consumption by incorporating anticipated future charges for extra data usage into their current usage (Xu et al. 2019). Consequently, the perception that mobile data are more costly than Wi-Fi gives rise to mobile data cost. However, using mobile data does not necessarily result in additional costs because consumers have already paid for their fixed mobile data quota and are only charged if they exceed that limit. In this sense, mobile data cost is the mental cost perceived internally rather than actual costs, such as money, time, and physical effort from an external aspect (Smith et al. 1999).

Most previous literature (e.g., Kapteyn et al. 1979, Simonson and Tversky 1992, Hanley et al. 2001) analyzes the mental cost associated with cognitive limitations and indicates that this mental cost could alter consumers’ approaches to obtaining information and making choices. More relevantly, a few surveys and studies examine the mental cost associated with broadband data caps or mobile data quotas. For example, a 2014 survey illustrates that the fear of exceeding data limits and incurring overage charges influences respon dents’ decisions, with 82% avoiding specific apps because of this fear (Sahota 2014). A decade later, this fear persists, prompting consumers to adopt proactive strategies to stay within their limits and avoid overage charges (Seitz 2024). Furthermore, limited numbers of studies find that consumers reduce broadband usage when approaching their broadband data limits and manage their mobile data consumption to follow the decreasing trend of the remaining data quota (Nevo et al. 2016, Xu et al. 2019), highlighting the role of mental costs in influencing user behavior.

However, there is a current gap in research regarding the mental cost associated with mobile data usage and its influence on consumer purchasing behavior, not to mention the lack of understanding of the underlying mechanism. The identified gaps underscore the significance of our study, which centers on the mental cost in the context of mobile data usage and aims to explore the underlying mechanism through how this mental cost impacts consumer purchase behavior.

## 2.3. Mobile Internet Connectivity Type and Consumer Behavio

Because consumers perceive using mobile data with extra cost as opposed to using Wi-Fi (Walelgne et al. 2021, Wu et al. 2022), consumers’ decisions to use mobile data or Wi-Fi is possible to alter their mobile shopping behavior. However, among the rich literature that well-documents consumer behavior in the mobile channel, research on connectivity type is still limited in the fields of information systems and marketing.

To our knowledge, only a limited number of studies address this issue. For instance, Walelgne et al. (2021) investigate the internet usage behavior of Finnish mobile users and find that users consume more data through mobile data than Wi-Fi, potentially because of the affordability of mobile data plans in Finland. Li et al. (2015) investigate the differences in mobile app usage behavior between mobile data and WiFi, finding that more than 80% of data consumption comes from Wi-Fi usage for data-intensive apps, such as video or music apps, whereas nearly 40% of data consumption for communication apps comes from mobile data usage. Wu et al. (2022) examine the impact of contextual factors, such as connectivity type, on mobile app usage behavior and demonstrate that mobile apps are used less frequently with mobile data than with Wi-Fi as using mobile data increases usage cost.

These aforementioned studies primarily focus on exploring usage patterns and summarizing differences in usage behavior between mobile data and Wi-Fi. However, there is a lack of research examining how connectivity type impacts consumers’ purchase behavior, particularly in terms of price sensitivity. These gaps in the literature highlight the importance of our study in further understanding the differences in consumer purchase behavior when using mobile data and Wi-Fi.

## 3. Data and Model 3.1. Data

We collect data from a prominent online travel agency that holds a substantial share of the online hotel booking market in China. From the mobile app operated by this agency, we obtain an individual-level hotel booking data set. The data set consists of search data, clickstream data, and transaction data ranging from July 2016 to March 2017. Search data shows consumers’ search details, including connectivity type, query words that consumers use to search, and consumers’ locations when searching. Based on such data, we obtain the dummy variable that indicates whether consumers use mobile data (versus Wi-Fi). Clickstream data contains information on consumers’ click-throughs and purchases. Search effort variables that depict consumers’ search effort in decision stages are constructed based on this clickstream data, which is carefully discussed in Section 4. Transaction data consists of information including transaction time, hotel room price, hotel reviews, and check-in and check-out dates. By integrating these data, we construct variables to model consumers’ utilities, including the daily room price of each hotel that consumers click on during their search process, the total number of reviews of each hotel, the review rating of each hotel, and the distance between hotel location and users’ query words.<sup>2</sup> We also construct variables about consumer characteristics using historical transaction data. Detailed definitions and summary statistics of the variables are presented in Table 1.

In order to model consumers’ utility in the choice process and account for their consideration sets, data are generated at the session–hotel level. Each session comprises a sequence of hotel search and purchase activities (Chen and Yao 2017).<sup>3</sup> To capture the differences between using mobile data and Wi-Fi, our data set comprises sessions exclusively utilizing either mobile data or Wi-Fi throughout the entire search process.<sup>4</sup> Consequently, this session–hotel–level data set consists of 434,710 observations, comprising 77,169 overall sessions with an average of 5.6 hotels viewed per session. Of these sessions, 42,369 sessions use mobile data (55%), whereas 34,800 sessions use Wi-Fi (45%).

## 3.2. Coarsened Exact Matching

The primary identification challenge to examine the impact of mobile data cost is the decision that consumers choose mobile data or Wi-Fi may not be exogenous, simply comparing consumer behaviors using mobile data and Wi-Fi may be misleading. For example, consumers’ decisions to use mobile data could be driven by urgency, in which people may argue that the behavior differences between using mobile data and Wi-Fi are due to urgency but not connectivity type. To alleviate this concern about selection bias, we employ a matching technique to ensure that we are comparing similar sessions using mobile data and using Wi-Fi.

We identify factors that may influence consumer decisions between using mobile data and Wi-Fi as matching variables. Previous literature suggests urgency reduces search and decision times, prompting the need for faster service and leading to a preference for mobile data for its speed and convenience, especially when immediate accommodation is needed (Chen and Yao 2017). We, thus, measure urgency by the days between booking and check-in dates. The economic cost associated with mobile data usage also influences consumers’ decisions on using mobile data or Wi-Fi (Li et al. 2015, Wu et al. 2022), pushing consumers who seek extensive information toward Wi-Fi because of cost concerns, especially when viewing high-resolution content for informed decision. In the context of hotel booking, consumer information search patterns vary based on trip length, destination familiarity, economic background, payment method, and city-specific factors. To capture the effect of economic cost, we use the following variables: days between check-in/check-out dates, a dummy variable indicating whether consumers book hotels in the same city in which they reside, device prices,<sup>5</sup> a binary variable indicating whether consumers choose to pay upon arrival or pay online, and a categorical variable for destination city tier. The accessibility of mobile data and Wi-Fi also affects consumers’ choices to use mobile data. We include a categorical variable for the telecom carrier to represent the quality of mobile data service. Meanwhile, the closer users are positioned to the downtown, the more likely they are to have access to Wi-Fi as it is easier to connect to Wi-Fi in urban downtown areas where there are more cafes, shopping malls, and office buildings than in suburban areas. We collect GPS data when consumers search and book hotels and calculate the distance from the user’s granular location to the nearest city downtown as a proxy of accessibility of Wi-Fi. Moreover, consumer characteristics, such as price preference and purchase intensity, could also play a role in the decision to use mobile data or Wi-Fi. To describe these consumer characteristics, we include two variables from historical transaction data: the average price of historical orders and the number of historical orders. ## 3.3. Model

To examine the price sensitivity differences between mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions, we employ a hierarchical multinomial discrete choice model to model consumers’ hotel booking decisions (Erdem et al. 2002, Ko¨k and Xu 2011, Wang and Van Der Lans 2018). This model includes two layers. The first layer describes consumers’ utility associated with hotel attributes and price, whereas the second layer examines the variation in consumer price sensitivity when using mobile data versus Wi-Fi. It is worth noting that the mobile data cost referred to in our context is not an actual cost but rather a mental cost. Therefore, the mobile data cost serves as a contextual stimulus rather than a tangible attribute in the decision-making process. We examine how the role of price in the purchase decision-making process differs when consumers are using mobile data compared with Wi-Fi. To achieve this, we do not include the mobile data cost in the first layer of the utility function, incorporating it instead into the price coefficient function as a factor affecting price sensitivity. ## 3.4. Different Price Sensitivity at Month Beginning vs. Month End

To strengthen the causal claim of our findings, we use the unique billing scheme in our context and employ a DID-style approach to examine the role of mobile data cost on consumer price sensitivity. As we describe above, during our observation window, the mobile data usage typically resets on the first day of each month, which marks the beginning of a new billing cycle and a new allotment of mobile data. In contrast, the last day of each month indicates the end of the billing cycle. In a billing circle, the telecom carriers provide a limited amount of monthly mobile data for a fixed fee. Once the allotment is exceeded, additional fees are charged to consumers for excess mobile data usage. As such, consumers tend to have sufficient mobile data at the beginning of each month, whereas they may come close to reaching their mobile data limit toward the end of the month.

Previous research shows that people exhibit forwardlooking behavior when using mobile data by reducing their usage to follow the diminishing trend of the remaining data quota (Xu et al. 2019). As a result, consumers are likely to perceive higher search cost when using mobile data at the end of the month compared with the beginning of the month. We, thus, expect that the price sensitivity differences between using mobile data and Wi-Fi are larger at the end of the month than that at the beginning of the month.

To test this conjecture, we construct two subsamples, respectively, containing sessions that occurred at the beginning and the end of the month.<sup>7</sup> We estimate the model presented in Section 3.3 for these two subsamples separately, and the results are shown in Table 3. As column (1) shows, the coefficient for MobileData is negative but insignificant $( \beta = - 0 . 2 0 1 , p = 0 . 1 8 )$ , suggesting that there are no significant price-sensitivity differences between consumers when using mobile data and those using Wi-Fi at the beginning of the month. The coefficient for MobileData $( \beta = - 0 . 7 9 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ in column (2) is negative and significant, indicating that consumers become more price sensitive when using mobile data compared with Wi-Fi at the end of the month. Taken together, these findings suggest that the variation in price sensitivity between using mobile data and Wi-Fi arises merely toward the end of the month when consumers have heightened mobile data cost. Moreover, the lack of significant differences in price sensitivity at the beginning of the month allays concerns about selection bias in selecting between mobile data and Wi-Fi. If the observed price sensitivity differences are due to selection bias instead of mobile data cost, we would expect to see such differences at the beginning of the month as well.

We further assess whether the differences in the impact of mobile data cost on price sensitivity between the beginning and the end of the month are statistically significant. Specifically, we estimate the choice model by including an interaction term between MobileData and MonthEnd into Equation (2), in which MonthEnd is the dummy variable indicating whether the session occurred at the end of the month. The coefficient for the interaction term MobileData × MonthEnd $( \beta = - 0 . 5 4 3 ,$ p < 0.05) in column (3) is negative and significant, revealing that the price-sensitivity differences are significantly greater at the end of the month than that at the beginning of the month. These results support our arguments that the impact of mobile data cost on consumer price sensitivity is more pronounced at the end of the month and reinforce the credibility of our causal claims presented in Section 3.

## 4. Consumer Search Mode Under Mobile Data and Wi-Fi

## 4.1. Mechanism

Our primary result suggests that consumers exhibit greater price sensitivity when using mobile data (versus Wi-Fi). In this section, we propose a mechanism with supporting empirical evidence. A prevalent strategy in consumer search involves continuous sequential searching until the marginal cost equals the marginal expected benefit of an additional search (Weitzman 1979). Given the constraints of fixed data quotas and potential extra charges for additional data usage, the perception that mobile data are more costly than Wi-Fi contributes to the increase of mental cost. This mental cost is found to lead mobile data users to exhibit forward-looking beha viors by reducing data usage to follow the diminishing trend of the remaining data quota (Xu et al. 2019). Prior research indicates that the mental cost associated with cognitive limitations changes consumer search mode (Dellaert and Ha¨ubl 2012). In our context, we propose that the mental cost stemming from mobile data usage could drive consumers toward the satisficing search mode rather than the maximizing search mode (Schwartz et al. 2002, Ma and Roese 2014). This change in consumer search mode leads to changes in acquiring product information as well as price information

Maximizing and satisficing search modes yield different decision strategies (Schwartz et al. 2002, Iyengar et al. 2006, Ma and Roese 2014). As consumers are adaptable decision makers who can shift their decision strategies based on the features of the decision contexts, the differing contexts of using Wi-Fi and mobile data can serve as a contextual factor that drives consumers into the maximizing or the satisficing search mode (Dellaert and Ha¨ubl 2012, Ma and Roese 2014). Specifically, when using Wi-Fi, consumers engage in the maximizing search mode with the goal of maximizing net benefit, investing considerable effort to thoroughly compare alternatives and identify the optimal option (Ma and Roese 2014). Conversely, when using mobile data, consumers switch toward the satisficing search mode. Their objective shifts to finding a satisfactory option that does not require extensive search effort (Iyengar et al. 2006). Consequently, consumers in the satisficing search mode exert less search effort in gathering product information, obtain fewer product details and may settle for a less optimal choice compared with those in the maximizing search mode. Because the product information that is not available would not be placed with weights during the search process, consumers in the satisficing search mode rely more on price information to make decisions and, therefore, become more sensitive to price (Lynch and Ariely 2000, Granados et al. 2012). In the following sections, we provide empirical evidence to support this mechanism.

## 4.2. Search Effort Differences Between Mobile Data and Wi-Fi Sessions

Our mechanism posits that the differences in price sensitivity between using mobile data and Wi-Fi stem from consumers being in different search modes and exerting varying levels of search effort in information acquisition. In line with the proposed mechanism, we anticipate significant differences in search effort between mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions. To test this conjecture and follow previous literature (Kim et al. 2010, Dellaert and Ha¨ubl 2012, Chen and Yao 2017, Ursu 2018, Ghose et al. 2019), we segment the shopping journey on a hotel booking app into four decision stages: conducting search, forming consideration set, acquiring product details, and confirming order. For each stage, we construct variables to represent consumers’ search effort and analyze the search effort differences between mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions.<sup>8</sup> Table 4 presents the mean values of the search effort variables in mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions in columns (1) and (2), and column (3) illustrates the differences between them.

4.2.1. Conducting-Search Stage. In the conductingsearch stage, consumers specify the hotel of interest, including the location and check-in and check-out dates, and may employ refinement tools, such as filters, sorting, and query words, to improve search results. Three variables are constructed to represent the search effort in this stage: the number of queries, the percentage of queries using refinement tools, and the percentage using query words. Results for this stage in Table 4 show that mobile data users specify fewer queries and use refinement tools and query words less frequently compared with Wi-Fi users. To further examine differences in consumer behavior when searching for various types of product information, we construct four additional search effort variables: the percentage of queries using price-related refinement tools, the percentage using product-related refinement tools, the percentage employing hotel-related query words, and the percentage using location-related query words. We then analyze the differences in search effort between mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions in terms of these variables. The results show no significant difference in the usage of price-related refinement tools between mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions. However, mobile data users use product-related refinement tools and both types of query words less frequently than Wi-Fi users. Notably, the difference is more pronounced with location-related query words than with hotel-related query words as location-related query words are associated with a strong need for product details to evaluate hotels near specific locations.<sup>9</sup>

4.2.2. Forming-Consideration-Set Stage. In the formingconsideration-set stage, consumers click on certain hotels to obtain more information from a list of hotels in response to a query. This forms their consideration sets. We, thus, use the number of hotels viewed as a measure of search effort exerted on forming the consideration set. Results for this stage in Table 4 show that the number of hotels viewed when using mobile data are significantly less than that when using Wi-Fi. This observation is congruent with the satisficing search mode, which leads consumers to inspect fewer options and form a smaller consideration set as consumers settle for satisfactory options (Dellaert and Ha¨ubl 2012, Ma and Roese 2014).

4.2.3. Acquiring-Product-Details Stage. Upon selecting a hotel at the prior stage, consumers in the stage of acquiring product details are directed to a dedicated subpage displaying product details, including reviews from previous customers. In this stage, we construct two variables representing consumers’ efforts in obtaining detailed hotel information: the number of reviews read and the percentage of hotels for which filtered reviews are read. Results for this stage in Table 4 demonstrate that mobile data sessions involve reading a lower number of reviews and utilizing filters to read reviews less frequently than Wi-Fi sessions, indicating that mobile data users exert less effort in researching hotel reviews compared with Wi-Fi users. This finding is in line with the satisficing search mode’s emphasis on seeking a sufficiently good option without conducting thorough product evaluation (Schwartz et al. 2002).

4.2.4. Confirming-Order Stage. When it comes to the confirming-order stage, confirming the order information represents the final step before completing the pur chase. In this stage, consumers must decide whether to confirm the order information and proceed with the purchase or abandon the order at this final confirmation step in search of other options. The tendency to abandon the purchase at this crucial moment reflects consumers regret about their current choices, a typical negative affective response resulting from the maximizing search mode (Schwartz et al. 2002, Ma and Roese 2014). We measure this through the percentage of orders abandoned in the final confirmation step. Results for this stage in Table 4 present that mobile data users are less likely than Wi-Fi users to abandon their orders in the final confirmation step in favor of exploring other options. This behavior aligns with the satisficing search mode in which consumers are less likely to experience regret and dissatisfaction (Iyengar et al. 2006, Ma and Roese 2014).

4.2.5. Overall Effort. For the entire search process, we quantify the overall search effort by measuring the total time spent throughout the process. Results in the last row of Table 4 show that mobile data users spend less time in total than Wi-Fi users, consistent with the features of the satisficing search mode in which less search effort is invested in searching (Schwartz et al. 2002).

In general, the negative and significant differences for all search effort variables suggest that consumers tend to exert less search effort when using mobile data as compared with Wi-Fi. These findings support our argument that mobile data cost drives consumers toward the satisficing search mode, thus limiting consumers’ search effort in acquiring product information. Such reduced search effort could result in less optimal decisions. To further investigate whether this leads to booking lower quality hotels, we compare hotels booked through mobile data and Wi-Fi. We observe that hotels booked via mobile data have lower review ratings and are located further from the destination than those booked via Wi-Fi. These findings are also consistent with the characteristics of consumers in the satisficing search mode who tend to settle for a less optimal choice compared with those in the maximizing search mode (Schwartz et al. 2002, Ma and Roese 2014). ## 4.3. Additional Analyses Relate to Mechanism

To provide additional support for our proposed mechanism, we conduct three additional analyses. First, we analyze sessions in which consumers switch from mobile data to Wi-Fi. Using mobile data sessions as the benchmark group, we examine how the search effort and price sensitivity change when consumers switch from mobile data to Wi-Fi. The findings indicate an increase in search effort and a reduction in price sensitivity when consumers switch from mobile data to Wi-Fi, revealing that reducing mobile data cost facilitates a shift from the satisficing to the maximizing search mode among consumers. These results further strengthen our mechanism. The details of this subsample analysis for sessions that experience switches are presented in Online Appendix H.

Second, we conduct heterogeneity analyses regarding payment type, booking time, income level, and mobile data types. This approach is adopted because certain decision environments can trigger higher mental costs, leading consumers in those environments to be more likely driven into the satisficing search mode. Thus, analyses based on various decision environments can be used to support our mechanism. The results of heterogeneity analyses suggest that the impact of mobile data cost on consumer price sensitivity is more pronounced when (1) booking hotels at night (versus during the day); (2) booking hotels using the pay-online method (versus the payupon-arrival method); (3) booking hotels by low-income consumers; and (4) booking hotels using roaming data, providing further evidence for the primary findings and the proposed mechanism. Details of these heterogeneity analyses are included in Online Appendix I.

Third, we analyze the relationship between search effort and firm revenue. Based on our primary results and proposed mechanism, using mobile data limits consumer search effort in acquiring product information, therefore increasing consumer price sensitivity and reducing online retailers’ revenue. To strengthen this argument linking lower search effort and reduced revenues, we conduct a linear regression between search effort and booking prices. The results reveal a positive correlation between search effort and booking prices, suggesting that reducing search effort is associated with decreased firm revenue, thereby supporting our arguments. Further details of this analysis are provided in Online Appendix J.

## 5. Conclusion

Given that using mobile data are perceived as more costly than using Wi-Fi, the decision to use either mobile data or Wi-Fi significantly impacts consumers’ purchasing decisions. By examining the effect of using mobile data (versus Wi-Fi) on consumer price sensitivity and exploring the mechanism regarding mobile data cost, our research finds that consumers exhibit approximately twice the level of price sensitivity when using mobile data as opposed to Wi-Fi. This effect can be translated into an average US\$17 less in revenue per hotel booking. Our research demonstrates that this effect is primarily because of the perceived cost of using mobile data compared with Wi-Fi. Using mobile data does not necessarily incur extra charges but contributes to the increase of mental cost during the search process and drives consumers toward the satisficing search mode. Consumers in the satisficing search mode tend to reduce the search effort dedicated to product information acquisition. With less accessible product information, consumers place more weight on the available price information during the purchase process, which enhances their price sensitivity. To assess causal relations between mobile data cost and price sensitivity, we use a DID-style approach and find that the impact of mobile data cost on consumer price sensitivity is more pronounced when consumers book hotels at the end (versus the beginning) of the month. To examine consumer search mode when using mobile data and Wi-Fi, we systematically analyze search effort differences between mobile data and Wi-Fi sessions and find that consumers’ search effort decreases with mobile data usage, indicating that mobile data cost drives consumers into the satisficing search mode rather than the maximizing search mode. These results further support our primary result and the proposed mechanism.

Although this study focuses on the context of online hotel booking in China, our findings can also be generalized to situations in which consumers perceive mobile data cost in mobile shopping. On the one hand, the perception of mobile data cost represents a prevalent scenario when shopping in the mobile channel. Using mobile data remains perceived as more costly than using Wi-Fi because of the prevalence of fixed data quotas.<sup>10</sup> Despite the availability of unlimited data plans in some developed countries, a significant portion of mobile users in both developed and developing countries do not subscribe to these services. A recent report shows that only four out of the 21 primary countries in the world have more than 30% unlimited data plan coverage for mobile users, and even in countries with high gross domestic products, such as Germany, the adoption rate for unlimited plans is only 6% (Zandt 2021). Furthermore, unlimited data plans typically feature caps on high-speed 4G/5 G data, which reduces to 3G/2G speeds once the data cap is reached.<sup>11</sup> This significant transition alters the user experience upon exceeding the high-speed data caps, leading to the perception of a fixed data quota when utilizing mobile data. On the other hand, the mechanism associated with mobile data cost can be applied in other mobile shopping contexts. In this research, we propose a mechanism supported by empirical evidence, suggesting that mobile data cost drives consumers into the satisficing search mode, reducing their search effort in acquiring product information. This leads to placing more weight on price information in decision making and results in higher price sensitivity. The shopping journey analyzed in our hotel booking context mirrors those in broader mobile shipping contexts as most mobile shopping platforms are designed with a sequential search strategy. Therefore, our proposed mechanism can be applied in various mobile shopping contexts, such as purchasing groceries and household items through mobile shopping channels, when they follow a similar shopping journey. In general, the impact of mobile data cost on consumer purchase behavior in mobile shopping represents a global and industry-wide issue that requires attention from both researchers and practitioners.

## 5.1. Implications for Managers and Policy Regulators

Based on the finding that consumers become more price sensitive when using mobile data than when using

Wi-Fi, the following managerial implications can be concluded for businesses operating and policy development in mobile shopping environments, particularly in countries or regions where mobile data fees are relatively expensive.

First, firms should incorporate the mobile internet connectivity types used by consumers as a crucial factor in customizing their marketing strategies to encourage consumers to exert more search effort, especially to encourage more search effort among those using mobile data. Our research indicates that the perceived higher search cost associated with using mobile data drives consumers toward the satisficing search mode, which limits their search effort in acquiring product information. This increases consumer price sensitivity and reduces online retailers’ revenue. Modern digital platforms can detect whether consumers are using mobile data or Wi-Fi in real time, enabling them to anticipate that potential revenues from mobile data users are likely lower than those from Wi-Fi users before a purchase is made. As our findings suggest that increased search effort correlates with higher booking prices, understanding the connectivity types that consumers use could serve as a key indicator for developing targeted marketing strategies that aim to encourage more thorough product information searches and mitigate revenue loss among mobile data users.

Second, firms can offer mobile data subsidies to consumers as a means of reducing mobile data cost and increasing revenues in the mobile shopping market. Our research speaks to the debate about whether firms should subsidize mobile data for consumers and demonstrates the necessity of mobile data subsidies by showing that the heightened price sensitivity stemming from mobile data cost can lead to decreased firm reven ues and the purchase of less optimal items. The mobile data subsidies lower the mobile data cost, encouraging consumers to switch to the maximizing search mode and exert more search effort on finding products that best fit their needs. As a result, consumers are not only more likely to choose options of superior quality with higher total prices, but also prefer to use the firms mobile platforms or branded mobile apps for product searches, thus increasing firm revenues. For example, some leading internet companies in China collaborate with telecom carriers to provide special mobile data plans that offer unlimited data for using their branded apps exclusively (Li et al. 2015). As a result, consumers who adopt these plans are more likely to use the branded apps of these companies and are more willing to make purchases from them.

Third, firms should invest more resources in develop ing functions for consumers when using mobile data, such as thoughtfully developing recommendation strategies with enhanced customization. Compared with those on Wi-Fi, mobile data users in the satisficing search mode are more likely to follow recommendations when making purchases. On one hand, these recommendations are better aligned with the consumers’ needs and outperform the less optimal options chosen under the satisficing search mode. On the other hand, consumers in the satisficing search mode can reduce search effort to find a sufficiently good option with recommendations as the recommended alternatives are already more suitable for them (Dellaert and Ha¨ubl 2012, Li et al. 2022, Wan et al. 2024). Thus, providing customized recommendations for mobile data users is more effective in improving their experience than for those using Wi-Fi. Additionally, firms can boost revenues by including high-quality products with higher prices in the recommendations for mobile data users as these products are more likely to be purchased by these mobile data users.

Fourth, firms can provide different versions of a mobile app for consumers using mobile data and those using Wi-Fi. Because consumers using mobile data or Wi-Fi operate in different search modes, having distinct and well-designed app versions tailored to their varying information acquisition needs can enhance their experience. For consumers using Wi-Fi, an elaborate app version that offers detailed product information and a variety of search tools can facilitate their search activities in the maximizing search mode, allowing them to thoroughly compare alternatives to find the best option. When firms identify that consumers are using mobile data, opting for a simplified app version that displays essential product descriptions and key search tools to aid in obtaining crucial information can assist in their search activities in the satisficing search mode, focusing on selecting satisfactory products with minimal search effort. Overall, creating different versions of a mobile app based on consumers’ connectivity types can effectively respond to their information acquisition needs and personalize their search experience, ultimately helping firms enhance customer satisfaction and retain consumers.

Last, policymakers need to consider the potential drawbacks of price discrimination in mobile shopping. Two types of price discrimination can occur. The first type imposes higher charges on consumers when using Wi-Fi because they are likely to be less price sensitive than those using mobile data. The second type imposes higher charges on consumers when using mobile data with subsidies as these consumers exhibit lower price sensitivity compared with those solely using mobile data. Although price discrimination is a common selling strategy, the second type may lead to unfair competition and reduced market efficiency. For example, large firms offering mobile data subsidies may attract more consumers than small firms, leading to the latter being pushed out of the market. With fewer suppliers, consumers may face higher prices for the same products than they would under competitive conditions. Furthermore, consumers receiving mobile data subsidies may pay premiums for their purchases as the subsidy cost is passed on to them. Therefore, policymakers should regulate and monito potential market distortions caused by subsidy strategies to protect consumer interests effectively.

## 5.2. Future Research Directions

Our study suggests potential avenues for future research in mobile shopping. One direction is to conduct laboratory experiments manipulating the amount of mobile data used during the search process, examining the accurate trade-off point between search benefits and mobile data cost to support data subsidy strategies. Another future research direction is to explore how to design recommen dations and app versions more effectively based on connectivity type. Consumers’ information needs vary as they use different connectivity types; thus, how to conduct the recommendations, display information and search tools on the app according to consumer connectivity types presents a challenge to be further explored. A final suggestion is to simulate the effects of different mobile subsidy strategies to aid policymakers in maintaining a fair market environment. Because firms that can provide high levels of subsidies may unfairly compete against firms that cannot, whether subsidies reduce market efficiency and what is the optimal subsidy strategy can be further explored.
