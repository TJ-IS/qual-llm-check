# Two-Sided Impacts of Service Provider’s Identity Disclosure in e-Customer Service Platforms: Evidence from Two Field Experiments

Abstract. Digital advancements have revolutionized customer service by enabling customers to easily express dissatisfaction and request resolutions through online platforms. Customer complaint management platforms are especially unique in facilitating providercustomer interactions, but researchers overlook implications from service provider ano nymity. In this article, two randomized field experiments are conducted to examine a customer complaint management platform to identify how disclosure of service provider identity affects service performance, customer satisfaction, and biases in customers. Study 1, a large-scale randomized field experiment involving 75,041 customers and 1,280 service providers across 672 companies, finds that identity disclosure improves provider performance. This improvement is achieved by removing the provider’s dissociative anonymity, mitigating deindividuation, instilling self-awareness, and motivating personal responsibility. This effect is stronger for inexperienced service providers who work with many colleagues and have more discretion in handling complaints. Customers who receive identity details about providers benefit from better service and perceive higher satisfaction with complaint resolution. Study 2, a field experiment involving 2,710 customers, shows that customers report more satisfaction when customers identify that providers belong to the majority ethnic group compared with when they belong to an ethnic minority. Intriguingly, minority customers showed lower satisfaction with same-ethnicity providers, indicating that ethnic cues and identity matching significantly influence customer satisfaction. Four follow-up studies involving 1,211 participants identify the underlying mechanisms that influence customer and provider behaviors. The article concludes with practical impli cations for firms and platforms dedicated to customer service.

## 1. Introduction

Digital technology channels have drastically transformed customer services by empowering customers to easily voice complaints and concerns about products and services (McKinsey 2022, Kearl 2024). Consequently, companies have dedicated significant resources to customer complaint management (CCM) digital services that can enhance interactions between customers and service providers (Morgan 2019, Sharma et al. 2019).

In all social interactions, including exchanges between customers and service providers, shared personal information helps establish trust and strengthen bonds (Gro¨nroos and Voima 2013). In traditional offline contexts, customers usually have identifying information about service providers, such as names and job titles (Collins and Miller 1994). However, complaint processes have moved to online venues, such as virtual chats and social networking services, where service interactions commonly occur under company or brand names. In such cases, the interactions are asymmetric; customer identities are generally transparent, but service provider identities are opaque (e.g., Kao et al. 2022, Li et al. 2023).

Because of the distinct characteristics of online contexts, a growing body of research has examined the effects of identity disclosure (see a summary of the literature in Table A.1 in Online Appendix A). Ethnic bias is particularly prevalent in the service industry as highlighted by corporate initiatives, like Starbucks racial bias training (Feldberg and Kim 2018), and studies on discrimination triggered by racially suggestive names highlight this ongoing issue (Edelman et al. 2017). Disclosing ethnic backgrounds can enhance cultural sensitivity and inclusivity, but it also introduces risks of discrimination, stereotyping, and privacy concerns that may hinder customer engagement. Despite the pervasiveness of online CCM services, few studies have examined the effects of identity disclosure in online service settings. Moreover, those that have explored this topic have provided inconsistent conclusions (e.g., Gunarathne et al. 2022, Babar et al. 2023), leaving limited understandings about consequences linked with disclosure of service provider identities. Our study bridges the research gap and provides significant practical insights by examining effects on CCM processes when customers receive details about service provider identities, such as names, job titles, and ethnic backgrounds.

In considering the effects of identity disclosure, we asked three specific research questions (RQs). (RQ1) How does service provider identity disclosure affect service performance? (RQ2) Does identity disclosure increase customer satisfaction? (RQ3) Does providing ethnic backgrounds as additional identity cues lead to biases in customers?

In contrast with conventional online community platforms, identity disclosure is more likely to affect online complaint management platforms because they involve provider-customer interactions. To examine RQ1 regarding the effects of identity disclosure on service performance, we considered deindividuation theory, which posits that service providers are more likely to display uninhibited negative behaviors when they act anonymously (Lowry et al. 2016, Chan et al. 2023). When their identity is known, they are more likely to feel that they will be held responsible for handling complaints efficiently (Mero et al. 2007, 2014; Brees and Martinko 2015; Vance et al. 2015). Knowing that their identity is detectable and monitored, however, they may experience heightened stress and privacy concerns, potentially causing disengagement and poor job performance (Johlke and Duhan 2000, Hochwarter et al. 2007).

To examine RQ2 regarding whether identity disclosure affects customer satisfaction, we considered the social presence perspective, which suggests that good communications require a strong sense of presence (e.g., Pu et al. 2020, Gao et al. 2023). Customers who know the provider’s identity are likely to perceive that their interaction is more intimate, trustworthy, and satisfying (Hoffman et al. 1996, 1999). However, if the provider fails to meet expectations, customers who know the provider’s identity can vent dissatisfaction more personally and intensively (Fu et al. 2021, Han et al. 2023).

To answer RQ3 regarding whether disclosing ethnic backgrounds leads to biased reactions among customers, we investigated social identity theory (e.g., Gong et al. 2021), which explains that social in-group and out-group categorizations, particularly regarding ethnic backgrounds, may cause customers to favor or reject service providers. We thus contend that service contexts should be particularly cautious about disclosing identity cues beyond names and job titles because ethnic background cues can generate biased customer perceptions and interactions.

To examine the consequences of identity disclosures, we collaborated with one of the largest CCM platforms in Europe to conduct two large-scale field experiments. CCM platforms facilitate efficient customer-company communications by allowing customers to voice complaints and concerns while enabling companies to identify and address issues (Mei et al. 2019). The CCM platform that we used enabled us to concurrently infer how information disclosure broadly affects actual service performance and customer satisfaction.

To address both RQ1 and RQ2, the first large-scale field experiment involved a month-long observation of services conducted by 1,280 service providers from 672 companies in handling 78,237 complaints from 75,041 unique customers. Service providers were randomly assigned at the company level to one of three treatment conditions: customerInform, bothInform, and baseline (con trol). In the customerInform condition, providers thought that they were anonymous and were unaware that customers knew their identity details. In the bothInform condition, providers no longer had dissociative anonymity because they knew that customers had information about provider identity. This experiment was designed to contrast identity disclosure effects with and without provider awareness. Specifically, in the customerInform condition, service providers were unaware of identity disclosure. Only in the bothInform condition, where dissociative anonymity was eliminated, could we discern how identity disclosure impacted service performance. This condition demonstrates an additive effect on customer satisfaction because of changes in service performance compared with the customerInform condition.

The study showed that identity disclosure notably improved provider performance and customer satisfaction. Specifically, in the bothInform condition, where providers knew that their identities were disclosed, they showed a 6.6% improvement in complaint answer rates and an 11.2% decrease in the time taken to resolve complaints. Interestingly, the enhancements were especially observed for relatively inexperienced service providers or those who worked in large groups. Considering contextual factors, identity disclosure (bothInform) had more positive impacts on service performance when complaints involved simpler issues or cases, including customer experience and financial concerns where the provider had more discretion, resulting in sector differences. Furthermore, customers in the customerInform condition showed a 5.7% increase in satisfaction with their postcomplaint experiences. When identity disclosure was accompanied by improved service provider performance, in the bothInform condition, customer satisfaction increased by 10.2%.

We conducted a second field experiment with the CCM platform to address RQ3. Does providing ethnic backgrounds as additional identity cues lead to biases in customers? We specifically focused on whether a certain ethnicity is preferred or if the alignment of ethnic backgrounds among customers and providers affects customer satisfaction with complaint resolution.

Considering that our partner platform operates in a diverse European context, we focused on ethnicity within Turkey, where the ethnic majority has a unique dynamic relationship with the significant ethnic minority given that the two groups have perceptible differences in appearance. We used photographs of service providers to signify ethnicity and conducted a monthlong observation with 10 Turkish companies that had agreed to participate in the experiment on the platform. Overall, 2,710 customers were randomly allocated to one of four treatment conditions: a control group with no disclosure of service provider information (baseline), a treatment group revealing only names and positions, and two additional groups where we provided a photo of a majority or minority ethnic individual beside the name and position. Our experiment was designed to explore how customer reactions vary in response to disclosing service providers’ ethnicities while maintaining consistent service performance across all groups. To minimize confounding factors, such as age, attractiveness, and potential privacy issues, we devised synthetic data by using generative artificial intelligence (AI) to create images in which ethnicity was the only differing attribute as verified in a pretest. In this study, customers generally expressed greater satisfaction with service providers from the majority ethnicity. Interestingly, instead of showing a preference for minority service providers, minority group customers reported lower satisfaction when the service provider’s ethnicity matched their own. This counterintuitive result suggests that derogation among minorities may contribute to the overall bias, with social categorization processes playing a significant role in shaping customer satisfaction with the service. The findings indicate that identity matching serves as a crucial boundary condition; ethnic cues influence customer satisfaction.

Although the two field experiments enhanced the ecological validity of our findings, they were limited in identifying the underlying mechanisms. To further explore how information disclosure affects both customers and service providers, we conducted four followup studies using 1,211 participants. To understand service provider behavior, in Study 3, we recruited actual front-line workers to participate in complainthandling scenarios in which their identity was disclosed (removing dissociative anonymity) or withheld (allowing dissociative anonymity) to compare how providers perceived accountability and work motivation. Study 4 involved a survey of service providers who had participated in the first field experiment. The survey measured how the removal of dissociative anonymity impacted job performance. Results indicated that when service providers no longer experienced dissociative anonymity, they felt less role ambiguity, increased their efforts, and performed better.

To comprehend customer behavior, Study 5 investigated how identity disclosure affects ex post customer satisfaction and found that even minimal information disclosure increases customer trust and satisfaction during complaint-handling processes. Finally, Study 6 built on the second field experiment by showing that ethnic cues regarding service provider identity influ ence customer perceptions of provider competence. Combined, the findings show that service perceptions have a complex relationship with identity disclosure, ethnic cues, and consumer biases, and they show that platforms and firms should understand these dynamics if they are to benefit from identity disclosure. Figure 1 depicts our overall research framework.

Our research makes several theoretical contribu tions. First, we enrich the literature on identity disclosure (e.g., Huang et al. 2017, Pu et al. 2020, Gao et al. 2023) by investigating interactions among stakeholders involved in two-sided platforms where identity disclosure may (1) influence observer behaviors (customers in our study) or (2) change the behavior of individuals whose identity is disclosed (service providers in our study). That is, actual disclosure and awareness of disclosure affect responses. In the bothInform condition, we removed dissociative anonymity, which has been rarely studied in conventional online communities, although it is a critical differentiation for two-sided platforms (e.g., Forman et al. 2008, Lowry et al. 2016, Chan et al. 2023). Researchers have focused on one side only in broadly assessing aggregated benefits and drawbacks associated with disclosure effects (e.g., Burtch et al. 2015, Pu et al. 2020). Instead, our more nuanced analysis observes the effects of identity disclosure on both the provider and customer sides.

Second, we deepen understandings of potential biases associated with online identity disclosure (Gunarathne et al. 2022, Babar et al. 2023). Researchers have mainly focused on the identity biases inherent in static identity cues, such as minority race or gender (Younkin and Kuppuswamy 2018, Gunarathne et al. 2022). We examine why a preference for the majority ethnicity occurs and show that internal biases within minority groups might be one of the possible mechanisms of this bias in customer service experiences on two-sided platforms.

Third, we contribute to the identity disclosure literature by focusing on the unique e-customer service context. Customer service and complaint management interactions involve two-parties: providers and customers (Gro¨nroos and Voima 2013). Social media-based customer service frequently utilizes brand accounts, which limit the level of identity disclosure and, therefore, fail to fully capture the greater complexity and interdependency associated with shared objectives (Gao et al. 2023). We venture beyond traditional customer service paradigms by using CCM platforms to leverage data across various firms. We also investigate boundary conditions traditionally associated with customer service, such as service provider tenure and the size of the customer service department. Additionally, we introduce innovative contextual factors, such as the complexity and type of complaint, and sector-specific differences that significantly influence the effects of identity disclosure on service performance in CCM platforms.

We provide practical insights for managers of firms and platforms regarding consequences of information disclosure in service settings. We show that even minimal information disclosure motivates service providers to perform better (Brynjolfsson et al. 2023), to be more satisfied with their work, and to be more loyal to the firm, whereas customers form greater trust (Bolton et al. 2013, 2018), especially toward online channels where they feel more certain that a real person will handle their complaints. Strategic information sharing clarifies provider roles, enhances complaint management efforts, increases trust and empathy, and enhances customer relationships (Crosby et al. 1990). Our findings also show that businesses should strategically utilize identity disclosure because social categorization biases might impact customer experiences.

## 2. Related Literature and Theoretica Background

## 2.1. Related Literature

To provide comprehensive understanding of how identity disclosure influences both customers and service providers, we build on research examining (1) consequences of identity disclosure in online platforms, (2) potential biases stemming from identity disclosure, and (3) sustainability for two-sided markets. In this section, we briefly review the literature and highlight our novel contributions. Table A.1 in Online Appendix A shows where our study is placed within the literature on identity disclosure.<sup>1</sup>

First, we extend the literature examining effects of identifiability and deindividuation in online platforms. We build on work that examined various types of online platforms to show how name revelation (e.g., Forman et al. 2008, Burtch et al. 2015, Pu et al. 2020) affects perceptions (Huang et al. 2017), social behavior (Jiang et al. 2013), information provision (Forman et al. 2008), and moral disengagement (Vance et al. 2013, 2015; Chan et al. 2023). Moreover, we help resolve inconclusive debates arguing that identity disclosure mitigates negative behaviors, such as cyberbullying (e.g., Lowry et al. 2016, Chan et al. 2023) and emotional negativity (e.g., Huang et al. 2017), but may also disengage users (e.g., Pu et al. 2020) and reduce crowdfunding participation (e.g., Burtch et al. 2015) because of privacy concerns. Service providers may show increased accountability (Vance et al. 2013, 2015), but this may form privacy concerns (Awad and Krishnan 2006, Jiang et al. 2013). Although informative, that research largely overlooked e-customer service settings where two parties interact to resolve problems. We fill the void by investigating how provider identity disclosure shapes perceptions and behaviors of both providers and customers.

Second, we extend studies that examined biases associated with online identity (e.g., Gunarathne et al. 2022, Babar et al. 2023). For example, a study showed that unconscious bias toward ethnic minorities, such as African Americans, reduces success in crowdfunding efforts (Pope and Sydnor 2011, Younkin and Kuppuswamy 2018), lowers acceptance rates from Airbnb hosts (Edelman et al. 2017), and increases wait durations and cancellations in ride-sharing platforms (Ge et al. 2020). In contrast, businesses that were labeled as owned by Black individuals and women received more positive perceptions and purchase intentions among more liberal customers (Babar et al. 2023). These empirical studies focused on statistical discrimination in online contexts, showing biases toward certain minorities, rather than exploring potential taste-based discrimination through experimental design (e.g., Bertrand and Mullainathan 2004). In a more directly relevant context, a recent study of corporate social media responses revealed that White customers rather than African American customers are more likely to receive replies (Gunarathne et al. 2022). Extending prior research, we aim to explore whether majority favoritism is dominant and whether two interacting parties have biases toward each other because of matching or mismatching identities in two-sided platforms. Our novel approach is to examine how ethnic identity matching affects customer satisfaction, considering that identity cues may trigger unintentional biases.

Finally, we extend studies on two-sided markets where user interaction and matchmaking are important for sustainability (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005), although platforms find it difficult to understand the complex preferences of users on each side (Kanoria and Saban 2021). The literature review revealed that two viewpoints dominate studies of the effects of personal information disclosure in two-sided platforms. One is that personal information disclosure on digital platforms improves matching efficiency (Huang et al. 2022, Johari et al. 2022) and that strategic information disclosure optimizes search and welfare market outcomes (Ostrovsky and Schwarz 2010). The second view contrasts how disclosure and anonymity impact user behaviors and matching outcomes (Bapna et al. 2016) and examines how platform regulations affect privacy concerns and information sharing (Gopal et al. 2018). Although those studies offer valuable insights, we fill a gap by investigating postmatching impacts of information disclosure on the behavior of both service providers and customers.

## 2.2. Theoretical Background

Comprehensive considerations across various theoretical frameworks are essential for understanding identity disclosure effects within two-sided e-customer service platforms. It is necessary to go beyond a single theoretical lens to better understand the complexity of platforms where service providers and customers directly and continually interact.

First, social presence theory suggests that conveying a sense of social presence leads to greater psychological intimacy and connectedness, enabling more profound interaction between parties within the communication media (Short et al. 1976). According to the theory, identity disclosure in online communities (Pu et al. 2020) and social networking services (Gao et al. 2023) can enhance the salience of others, facilitating intimate and immediate social interactions. Thus, two-sided plat forms might foster a sense of intimacy and satisfaction by revealing service provider names and job positions (Hoffman et al. 1996, 1999).

Second, deindividuation theory explains that anonymity enables individuals to avoid self-evaluation and disregard social norms in group settings (Festinger et al. 1952, Zimbardo 1969). When individuals know that they can be identified, they lose the sense of indi viduation (Valkenburg and Peter 2011, Wong et al. 2021, Chan et al. 2023). If identity disclosure prevents dissociative anonymity, providers are likely to feel accountable for their performance, but they might also form privacy concerns and fear negative evaluations (Johlke and Duhan 2000, Hochwarter et al. 2007). In the CCM platform, the group refers to service providers from the same customer service department working alongside the focal service provider. Deindividuation theory complements social presence theory in showing that service providers might perform better if platforms remove their anonymity, whereas social pres ence theory shows that customers might feel greater psychological presence, intimacy, and immediacy if provider anonymity is removed.

Importantly, social presence does not inherently involve a loss of individual identity or self-awareness. It enhances connection, empathy, and positive behaviors by maintaining individual identity and engagement, even in mediated environments. In contrast, deindividuation leads to a loss of self-awareness and personal identity, often resulting in behavior driven by group dynamics rather than personal norms, especially in anonymous or group settings. Although social presence supports positive social interactions, deindividuation can lead to both positive and negative outcomes depending on the context. Given the distinct impacts of social presence and deindividuation on behavior and identity in social interactions, our study provides a more comprehensive analysis of identity disclosure on a two-sided platform by differentiating social presence for customers and deindividuation for providers. Online Appendix A.2 provides a detailed comparison of the two concepts.

Last, social identity theory explains that individuals categorize themselves and others as belonging to in-groups and out-groups within group contexts and that the categories influence attitudes and behaviors in all parties involved (Tajfel and Turner 1979, Gong et al. 2021). This theory helps us understand potential customer biases based on ethnic cues. Here, we contend that ethnic identity matching between service providers and customers can lead to incongruent customer behaviors; one scenario involves in-group favoritism and out-group derogation (Hogg and Abrams 1988), whereas the other includes in-group derogation and out-group favoritism (McNamara et al. 2011, Axt et al. 2018), both of which potentially impacting their levels of satisfaction with the service. Although dominant research based on social identity theory supports in-group favoritism and out-group derogation, individuals from nondominant groups might focus on stereotypes or negative attributes of their in-group, leading to diminished in-group favoritism (e.g., Axt et al. 2018). It remains an open empirical question whether these dynamics hold true across different contexts and ethnic cues.

Social presence, deindividuation, and social identity theories all address social interactions to understand the effect of identity disclosure. However, social presence particularly focuses on how the communication medium, empowered by identity disclosure, affects customer perceptions; deindividuation focuses on how service providers perceive anonymity and group influences, and social identity focuses on dynamics resulting from group identities. We integrate the theories to develop a more nuanced and comprehensive experimental framework and analyze identity disclosure strategies on two-sided platforms.

## 3. Study 1: The Role of Identity Disclosure in Service Performance and Customer Satisfaction

## 3.1. Institutional Details

To conduct our randomized field experiment, we collaborated with a multisided CCM platform that has the largest market share in Europe. The platform provides a venue for customers to express widely ranging complaints, such as service errors and objections to product prices and features (Figure 2). Customers first search for the firm or brand name and record their complaints using as many as 250 characters. If the platform fails to list the firm, the customer can add the company name, phone number, and address if available. The platform authenticates all complaints, verifies customer phone numbers, corrects grammatical errors, and eliminates

The chief executive officer of our partner platform reported that more than 80% of customers who have failed to resolve complaints through their initial contacts with firms expect online platforms to act as neutral arbitrators. Firms willingly work with the platform because it verifies that real customers submit their grievances, edits the complaints, and keeps cases open until they are resolved. Over time, platform visitors can observe whether firms address or ignore complaints. The platform ensures continued revenue through traffic, subscriptions, and advertisements by maintaining neutrality; improving services through postresolution surveys; and providing clean, high-quality, and wellarticulated complaints from various sectors.

The platform diverges from traditional review websites where customers share reviews. Instead, the platform encourages businesses to create accounts and respond to complaints through dashboards. When a customer lodges a complaint, business managers assign the case to a customer service provider who contacts the complainant to resolve the issue. When the case is completed, the platform sends a questionnaire to the customer to inquire about satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the resolution process and the customer’s propensity to stop buying products or services from the firm or switch to another firm, a process called churn. The platform gives a 72-hour grace period to resolve complaints before making them public. Unresolved complaints are published followed by a 48-hour window for resolution and a chance to issue a satisfaction survey. This process acknowledges that some complex complaints may need more time but encourages prompt resolution within the time frames.<sup>3</sup> An average of 40% of complainants answer the surveys. The platform then posts the average satisfaction scores, response rates, firm responses, resolution standings, and customer process ratings (Figure 2). At the time of the experiment, more than 140,000 registered businesses in various industries used the platform.<sup>4</sup> Two million customers had used the platform to lodge multiple complaints.

## 3.2. Experiment Design

To examine whether service provider identity disclosure affects customer and service provider behaviors, we established (1) a baseline control condition; (2) a customerInform condition (T1), in which customers received personal information about the providers but providers were unaware that their information had been disclosed;<sup>5</sup> and (3) a bothInform condition (T2), in which customers received personal information about the service providers and the providers knew that their identities would be disclosed through their dashboard (Figure 3). Table 1 illustrates our experimental design and the various interventions in each condition.

To examine how identity disclosure affects customers and service providers, we carefully designed the experiment as follows. First, most businesses employ multiple service providers and randomly assign them to handle complaints. Thus, it is challenging to track and control different treatments. To mitigate potential interference across treatment conditions, all service providers within a company were assigned to the same condition. Second, we disclosed provider information when customers completed the questionnaire to examine effects on customers when providers unconsciously thought they were incognito. Thus, we could compare differences between conditions because the control and customerInform conditions were identical before the transaction was completed. The fourth column in Table 1 provides examples of messages provided to customers at the beginning of the questionnaire. Figure 4 shows mes sage screenshots. The approach compared effects occurring when providers knew or did not know that their information was disclosed.

## 3.3. Data and Empirical Approach

We collected data on each complaint from the target platform, including random assignment information, complaint information (e.g., post time, length, and response indicator), customer demographics (e.g., identification (ID), gender, and age), business information (e.g., ID, name, and industry), service provider demo graphics (e.g., ID and tenure), and survey responses (e.g., request time, response time, overall satisfaction, and churn intention), from 6 weeks before the experiment until the end. During the experiment, the platform disclosed personal information about service providers for the first time, allowing us to observe how both customers and service providers initially reacted to new disclosure conditions.

We randomly selected 672 companies that were active on the platform. We assigned their 1,280 service providers to one of three treatment conditions. We randomized par ticipant assignment to the conditions at the company level to eliminate potential interference. The company-level randomization was a deliberate strategy to prevent contamination effects that might occur if service providers within the same company were subjected to different treatments. However, because of varying company sizes, it did not secure a balanced pretreatment distribution across groups.<sup>6</sup> Therefore, we conducted a difference-indifference analysis by estimating the treatment effect according to within-subject variations (Goldfarb et al. 2022). $$
\begin{array}{l} E x - \text { Post   Satisfaction } _ {i t} \mid 1 (\text { Survey } _ {i t}) \\ = \alpha + \delta \text { TreatmentGroup } _ {i} + \beta_ {0} \text { AfterTreatment } _ {t} \\ + \beta \text { TreatmentGroup } _ {i} \times \text { AfterTreatment } _ {t} + \text { Controls } _ {i} \\ + \mu_ {t} + \epsilon_ {i t}. \end{array} \tag {2}\tag{2}
$$

3.4.3. Heterogeneous Treatment Effects on Service Performance. We next explored whether treatment effects varied depending on provider characteristics and contextual factors, such as complaint features that may have generated heterogeneous responses. We first assessed treatment effects considering service provider features, such as tenure and the size of the customer service department. Long-tenured providers might see minimal benefits from identity disclosure as familiarity with their roles diminishes its impact (Tsui and O’Reilly 1989). Conversely, in larger departments, where individual responsibilities may blur, identity disclosure could enhance clarity and accountability (Quey 1971). Subsample analysis in Online Appendix B.3 revealed that providers who had shorter tenures and served in larger departments had higher answer rates and shorter resolution times under the bothInform condition, indicating that identity disclosure improves performance by reducing role ambiguity and enhancing accountability.

We thus used complaint complexity as a proxy for discretion, positing that simpler complaints are less risky, more clear cut, and resolved more readily and thus, showing that our treatments interact with the exercise of discretion by service providers. ## 4. Study 2: Influence of Ethnic Identity Disclosure on Customer Biases

Study 1 demonstrated that customers are more trusting and satisfied with service when they know a service provider’s name and job title. Recognizing the significance of disclosing basic identity information, we proceeded to conduct a second experiment to examine the effects of additional disclosures, specifically ethnicity, and the potential biases associated with such disclosures. This further investigation aimed to delineate the boundary conditions of identity disclosure, providing deeper insights into how and when revealing a service provider’s ethnic background affects customer reactions, potentially highlighting instances of bias in on social identity theory predicts that customers might respond more positively or negatively to service providers who share similar ethnic backgrounds because ethnic similarities either foster empathy, trust, and understanding or lead to devaluation of their own group. Thus, we conducted a second field experiment to provide a more comprehensive understanding about how companies can leverage various facets of identity disclosure to optimize customer satisfaction.

## 4.1. Experimental Design, Data, and Empirica Approach

Our partnering platform operates in Europe, so we could observe the effects of different ethnicities between customers and providers; however, numerous nationalities could also introduce a confounding effect in our field experiment. Instead, we focused on ethnic groups within Turkey, a single country where majority and minority ethnic groups are clearly distinguished; ethnic majorities represent approximately 70%–75% of the population, whereas ethnic minorities account for about 15%–20%. Although it is challenging to distinguish between two groups solely by name, they are notably different in appearance.

To minimize confounding factors, such as age, facial expressions, and attractiveness, we used generative AI to create synthetic images of service providers. We con ducted a separate pretest to confirm that ethnicity differences are the primary differentiating variable across images, whereas other perceptions, such as perceived age and attractiveness, are consistent across images (Online Appendix C.1).<sup>16</sup>

With the support of our partnering platform, we conducted Study 2 involving 10 sizable client companies in Turkey that agreed to participate. In Study 1, we randomized at the company level to mitigate dilution effects from treatment variations among providers within the same company. In Study 2, because the treatment does not impact provider behavior, randomization was conducted at the individual level. We randomly assigned customers to one of four experimental conditions: (1) a baseline control group without provider information; (2) a T1 group knowing only names and positions; (3) a T2 group having names, positions, and a photo of an ethnic majority service provider, and (4) a T3 group having names, positions, and a photo of a minority service provider. Four prevalidated photos of males and females were shuffled within the T2 and T3 groups to observe gender as additional stimuli (Online Appendix C.1 provides details). To ensure consistent service performance, all providers were told that customers knew their identities.

The experiment spanned 31 days from April 5 and May 5, 2024, and we analyzed 3,372 complaints raised by 2,710 customers.<sup>17</sup> During this period, the participating firms refrained from launching concurrent promotions or experimental activities and exclusively dedicated their operations to our study. Randomization checks using user profiles and complaint characteristics showed no significant differences and thus, validated our randomization procedure (Table C.1 in Online Appendix C.2). Study 2 captured the same variables comprising customer satisfaction and their profiles as in Study 1. Customer ethnic background was comprehensively captured based on the partnering platform’s customer database, including the name, region, and photo available in the profile. In the context of e-customer service, the dynamics of customer-provider interactions may be different from those traditional settings where in-group derogation has been extensively studied. Unlike coworkers who share similar roles, customers and service providers have distinct roles with different expectations and power dynamics, which can influence how biases emerge. One possible explanation for the observed behavior is the heightened expectation for empathy from in-group service providers; when these expectations are unmet, it can lead to greater disappointment and harsher judgments driven by in-group favoritism (Shinada et al. 2004, Everett et al. 2015). In addition, the power imbalance in these interactions may intensify these effects, making unmet expectations a significant factor in how in-group derogation manifests. Overall, the results emphasize that identity disclosure and matching ethnic identities significantly impact customer satisfaction on two-sided platforms.

## 5. Follow-up Studies on Underlying Mechanisms

Study 1 showed that service providers performed better when they knew that their names and job titles had been disclosed, aligning with our prediction that disclosure would enhance perceptions of accountability and reduce role ambiguity (Boles and Babin 1996; Mero et al. 2007, 2014). Study 2 further revealed that customers expressed greater satisfaction with providers of majority ethnicity, influenced by majority favoritism and in-group derogation. Interestingly, minority customers showed lower satisfaction when paired with providers from their own minority group, indicating a preference against their own ethnicity.

Whereas Study 1 provides support for the positive effect of identity disclosure, it had limitations in demonstrating the underlying mechanisms. Also, providers might react negatively to disclosure by fearing greater accountability and forming privacy concerns, despite the overall positive effects outweighing the negative ones (Johlke and Duhan 2000, Hochwarter et al. 2007). Furthermore, the ethnicity bias observed in Turkey in Study 2 may differ in other ethnic contexts. To overcome these limitations, we conducted four follow-up studies involving 1,211 participants.

Two studies examined the service provider side, demonstrating that identity disclosure positively impacts service performance by enhancing accountability (Study 3 with 376 American participants) and reducing their role ambiguity (Study 4 involving 189 service providers in the partnering platform). These studies also ruled out the potential impacts of fear of accountability and privacy concerns on these relationships. On the customer side, Study 5 with 315 American participants showed that disclosing even minimal information about a service provider, such as name and job title, can significantly increase customer trust and satisfaction. Finally, Study 6 involving 331 participants from the United States replicated Study 2 using the majority group and one of the minority ethnic groups to illustrate how perceived competence related to ethnicity influences the effects of identity disclosure. Online Appendix D provides detailed information for the follow-up studies. Table 8 summarizes the findings from Studies 1–6.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Our research is particularly innovative in that we use a CCM platform to investigate whether customers are more satisfied with services when platforms disclose the identities of service providers and whether providers perform better when they know that their identities are disclosed. In two field experiments and four follow-up studies, we uncover the underlying mechanisms that cause identity disclosure to influence both customer experiences and provider behaviors.

Studies examining impacts of information disclosure in online platforms focus mainly on one party without considering that service encounters involve two-sided interactions (e.g., Forman et al. 2008, Pu et al. 2020). For example, personal data utilization research focuses on how firms can obtain or use customers shared information (e.g., Bergemann and Bonatti 2011, Ho¨rner and Skrzypacz 2016) or on how the disclosure of an audit partner’s identity affects audit quality and service (Cunningham et al. 2019). Diverse online platforms, such as online communities, review boards, and crowdfunding, are studied, but e-customer service settings are largely neglected. We address the gap by utilizing a unique opportunity to collaborate with a CCM platform and to demonstrate how information disclosure affects both providers and customers in the online complaint management context.

Our studies confirm that disclosing service provider identity positively affects customer trust perceptions and ex post satisfaction. It also enhances service performance, especially for relatively inexperienced providers within large work teams handling simpler, discretionary complaints. In addition, customers show higher satisfaction when they recognize that service providers belong to majority ethnic groups. That said, minority ethnic customers are less satisfied when they share an ethnic identity with service providers.

## 6.1. Theoretical Implications

Social presence theory is most relevant in one-sided platforms where only the focal user’s identity is disclosed, such as in our customerInform condition (e.g., Pu et al. 2020, Gao et al. 2023). In contrast, we examine more complex two-sided platforms, where deindividuation theory and social identity theory are also relevant theoretical perspectives. Despite calls for research to examine two-sided effects (Johari et al. 2022, Zhou et al. 2024), platform designers lack guidance regarding how interactions are affected when parties know the identity of one another (Al-Ubaydli and Yeomans 2017). Leveraging a unique real-world experiment, we explore and validate specific impacts of identity disclosure for each party involved, considering several processes with potentially conflicting effects. Acknowledging that each side may experience ambiguous effects (e.g., Burtch et al. 2015, Huang et al. 2017, Pu et al. 2020, Chan et al. 2023), our findings indicate that identity disclosure is more beneficial than detrimental for both sides, thereby broadening the understanding of identity disclosure dynamics.

We introduce a critical dimension to the discourse on identity disclosure in e-customer service contexts by finding that ethnic identity matching may cause unintended biases. Specifically, customers from minority groups show in-group derogation and out-group favoritism (McNamara et al. 2011, Axt et al. 2018). They might have a deeper understanding of internalized societal critiques, stronger stereotypes, or a more realistic assessment of their challenges and weaknesses than customers from majority groups. This can lead to more critical or negative perceptions and attitudes toward their own group. Alternatively, ethnicity-based stereotypes, such as perceptions of low competence or job appearance incompatibility, may offer additional explanations, although this is just one possible interpretation. The interplay between self-perception, societal stereotypes, and expectations within service interactions is multifaceted and likely shaped by a range of psychological and social factors. Our findings highlight the importance of ethnic identity cues and suggest that future research should investigate how broader societal narratives shape these perceptions and contribute to the biases observed in service interactions.

## 6.2. Managerial Implications

Our work has implications for managers of complaint management platforms. Before third-party platforms emerged, customers were forced to take their complaints directly to firms, but today, the platforms enable disgruntled customers to easily contact customer services while allowing the public to observe how well service providers resolve complaints. As two-sided markets, platforms require better market design (Hos sain et al. 2011, Chen et al. 2021) if they are to continually maintain satisfaction among customers and firms. Our field experiment conducted on a complaint management platform shows how disclosure affects various industries and allows us to specifically recommend that platform designers disclose information about service providers.

Companies can use third-party complaint management platforms to provide in-house call centers that facilitate customer interactions, authenticate complaints, streamline complaint handling processes, make resolution processes more credible, and confirm final resolution statuses. We suggest that disclosure of provider identity can eliminate friction, defuse tension, and increase mutual trust (e.g., Bolton et al. 2013). We also suggest that names and titles may be enough to increase brand loyalty and satisfaction. In addition, in the absence of anonymity, service providers are likely to handle complaints with more care and attention, resulting in greater job fulfillment and more customer satisfaction.

The study reveals that businesses should be aware of critical societal prejudices that significantly influence customer satisfaction. Companies might establish training programs to foster inclusiveness and raise awareness about unconscious bias among all parties involved in customer services. Service providers who work in ethnically diverse societies will particularly benefit when companies establish robust antidiscrimination policies and consider the potential implications of identity disclosure in customer service operations. Moreover, these insights could inform policy discussions about discrimination in service industries and contribute to the broader discourse on social equality and diversity.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations that offer directions for future research. We recognize the potential for measurement inaccuracies in our system for tracking complaint resolutions, particularly when providers fail to update resolution statuses or when resolutions are handled off-platform without proper recording. Future research could make service performance measurement more accurate by incorporating data from off-platform resolutions. Second, we should directly address the potential for intradepartmental competition, which could amplify the effect size observed among service providers in larger departments. Future research could examine whether performance publicity and competition magnify effects of identity disclosure. Third, we acknowledge that identity disclosure may have uneven effects across time and may notably diminish as the novelty wears off as indicated by the limited impact on tenured service providers. Future research should focus on the longevity of identity disclosure effects. Fourth, we focused on the service provider’s name and ethnicity as identity cues, and although gender disclosure did not show significant differences in our context, future research should explore the effects of a broader range of identity cues. This exploration could provide valuable insights into how different aspects of identity influence perceptions and interactions in service settings. Last, our findings might have somewhat limited generalizability because we focus on a single platform. Future research could include multiple CCM platforms to expand observations across contexts.
