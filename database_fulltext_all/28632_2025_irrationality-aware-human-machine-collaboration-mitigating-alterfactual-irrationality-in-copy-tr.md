# Irrationality-Aware Human Machine Collaboration: Mitigating Alterfactual Irrationality in Copy Trading

Abstract. The data generated by human decisions inherit human irrationality, yet artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms often operate under the implicit assumption that the data they train on is rational. We challenge this convenient assumption and develop an irrationalityaware human-machine collaboration (IA-HMC) framework to address it. Within this framework, we propose a new concept of alterfactual irrationality, which identifies irrational human decisions influenced by irrelevant alternative inputs. We then develop irrationality aware machine learning methods to account for this irrationality and augment humanmachine collaboration via the contraction method. We apply the framework to mitigate th irrationality present in the decision-making process of copy trading, a practice that enables layperson investors (followers) to assess and replicate expert traders’ trades. We specifically identify two main sources of alterfactual irrationality prevalent among followers’ decisions: herding behavior and identity bias. Our results demonstrate that our proposed method out performs both the original follower decisions and the contraction method, improving the win ratio by 49.0% and 10.2%, respectively. This study marks the first attempt to design machine learning algorithms to overcome human decision irrationality, suggesting that aligning AI with human preferences needs to account for human irrational behaviors.

## 1. Introduction

With the growing sophistication and widespread use of artificial intelligence (AI), how to effectively integrate it into the human decision-making process has attracted significant attention (Kleinberg et al. 2018, Abbasi et al. 2024). Effective human-machine collaboration (HMC) is crucial for successful AI implementation as each brings complementary strengths (De Cremer and Kasparov 2021): Whereas machines excel in powerful analytical capabilities (Chen et al. 2024), humans contribute invaluable soft information and grapevine knowledge that machines may not observe (Liu 2022).

This study focuses on a fundamental challenge in HMC: using AI to address human decision irrationality. The data generated by human decisions inherit human irrationality, yet AI algorithms often operate under the implicit assumption that the data they train on is rational. Specifically, human decisions frequently deviate from prescribed ideals, leading to suboptimal decisions and irrational choices (Tversky and Kahneman 1974). AI algorithms, trained on data reflecting human behaviors, often conflate such systematic deviations as rational preferences (Morewedge et al. 2023) and mistakenly interpret human irrationality as underlying human values while attempting, futilely, to align with them (Gorman and Armstrong 2022, Morewedge et al. 2023). For instance, an Amazon hiring algorithm trained on its past hiring decisions inadvertently revealed previously unnoticed human bias in the recruitment process (Logg 2019). Likewise, many large language models (LLMs) are found to inherit the limitations of human decisions and exhibit similar cognitive biases and irrationality (Bubeck et al. 2023).

Addressing such human decision-making deficiencies in HMC requires an AI algorithm to be capable of identifying and mitigating human irrationality. Toward this end, this study explores three specific research questions. (1) What different forms of irrationality may arise in a specific human decision-making process? (2) How to account for irrationality present in data when designing AI algorithms? (3) How to align with human preference while mitigating decision irrationality in HMC?

To answer the first research question, we introduce a novel concept termed alterfactual irrationality. The classic rational decision theory in economics assumes that rational decision makers are utility maximizers who maintain consistent preferences between options, regardless of the presence or absence of irrelevant alternatives (Von Neumann and Morgenstern 2007). This assumption, known as the independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA), is often violated in both experimental and real-world settings (Guo 2016), reflecting inherent human irrationality (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). Recent AI literature has identified similar violations of rationality due to irrelevant features (decision cues), referred to as alterfactual information (Mertes et al. 2024). We build on the IIA assumption and incorporate the notion of alterfactual information to conceptualize alterfactual irrationality, which refers to the phenomenon that features supposed to be irrelevant to a choice may reverse user preferences when decisionmakers weigh available information.

In answering Research Questions 2 and 3, we propose a novel machine learning (ML) framework named irrationality-aware human-machine collaboration (IA-HMC) to facilitate effective collaboration between humans and machines by mitigating human decision irrationality. We demonstrate our framework’s applicability in the context of copy trading, a practice that enables layperson investors (followers) to observe and automatically copy the investments of expert traders (Apesteguia et al. 2020). First, we formalize alterfactual irrationality and operationalize the procedures to identify such irrationality in copy trading, pinpointing two specific sources of irrationality: herding behavior and identity bias. Second, we propose the irrationalityaware ML method and explore various architectures to effectively account for irrational human behaviors in the algorithmic design. Finally, we propose a personalized HMC by improvising the contraction method originally introduced in Kleinberg et al. (2018). This method applies machine predictions to augment the decisions made by an individual, effectively leveraging machine capabilities while respecting the individual’s preferences.

Our results demonstrate that the IA-HMC framework outperforms both the original follower decisions and the contraction method, improving the win ratio, the percentage of profitable trades in the next week, by 49.0% and 10.2%, respectively. Furthermore, we conduct extensive ablation analyses to empirically assess the efficacy of the proposed irrationality-aware ML methods and the contraction method. To ensure the framework’s robustness, we experiment with various substantiations such as choice of different ML methods (XGBoost, long short-term memory (LSTM), Transformer) and different ways of accounting for irrationality (regularization, weighting, multiobjective training, and collaborative attention methods). Additionally, we explore the root causes of irrationality inherent in human decision making, such as herding behavior induced by cognitive load and salient information, and identity bias resulting from overemphasizing irrelevant source credibility over trader performance. We show how our approach effectively mitigates irrationality by de-biasing the weights put on these alterfactual features. Lastly, we conduct a simulation where we control the data generating process for alterfactual irrationality to demonstrate the generalizability of our framework.

At a broad level, we show that our framework, less susceptible to irrationality, is able to address the deficiencies inherent in human decisions. As such, this study makes theoretical, methodological, and practical contributions. First, it represents the first attempt to design ML algorithms specifically aimed at overcoming human decision irrationality. We define and operationalize the identification of alterfactual irrationality, a prevalent phenomenon rarely explored in the decision science literature. Second, methodologically, we develop an irrationality-aware ML method to mitigate decision irrationality. Our method integrates cognitive science into formal algorithmic methods, signaling the emergence of a new research direction that can be termed “behavioral science of algorithm design” (Morewedge et al. 2023). Third, our study extends the literature on human–machine collaboration. Collaboration becomes challenging when machines fail to understand humans’ personal preferences (Kleinberg et al. 2023). Our contraction-based method addresses these challenges by excluding high-risk choices from the human selection pool while aligning with individuals preference for each decision. Lastly, we contribute to the behavioral economics and finance literature on how to address irrational human decision in copy trading. Practically, we identify sources of trading irrationality and develop algorithms to mitigate it. We show that investors’ copy-trading decisions can be significantly improved once their irrational behaviors are kept in check.

## 2. Literature Review

This section reviews three streams of related literature: irrationality in decision making, human irrationality and machine learning, and human–machine collaboration.

## 2.1. Irrationality in Decision Making

Extensive literature has examined various manifestations of irrationality, including phenomena like sunk cost fallacy (Herrmann et al. 2015), herding behavior (Koch 2017) and representativeness bias (Ahmed and Safdar 2017). These forms of irrationality have been explored in diverse contexts such as consumer choice (Jena et al. 2022), online auction (Simonsohn and Ariely 2008), and investment strategies (Liu et al. 2014). Irrationality occurs, partially because, rather than comprehensively analyzing available information and weighing potential outcomes, individuals frequently resort to heuristics (Graham et al. 2017) and emotional cues (Heeb et al. 2023) in their decisionmaking processes, leading to suboptimal decisions. In the context of trading, traders’ investment decisions may be subject to erroneous emotional beliefs and psychological biases, such as the gambler’s fallacy and loss aversion (Graham et al. 2009).

One classic explanation of such phenomena is Simon’s theory of bounded rationality, which postulates that cognitive limitations constrain human decision making, resulting in irrational choices (Simon 1955). Because of these cognitive restrictions, humans are only able to process a limited range of options and features, typically those that are representative and easily accessible (Kahneman and Tversky 1972). However, these features may not be valuable to inform the outcomes (Dessaint and Matray 2017). Rational decision makers need to be able to leverage relevant decision cues while disregarding irrelevant information.

Toward this end, this study introduces the concept of alterfactual irrationality and develops an algorithmic approach to addressing it. This is in sharp contrast to the behavioral irrationality-mitigation approaches. For example, the nudge mechanism has been widely used in public policy (Zhang et al. 2023), consumer behavior (Zeng et al. 2023), and financial investment (Medina 2021) to correct behavioral biases.

## 2.2. Human Irrationality and Machine Learning

Recently, the ML literature has started to explore human irrationality, seeking to understand and predict the occurrence of irrational behaviors in human decision making (Jena et al. 2022, Macmillan-Scott and Musolesi 2023). Particularly, there is a risk of training ML algorithms using human-generated data that may be subject to biases and irrationality (Gorman and Armstrong 2022). It has been advocated that language models need to factor in the fact that human languages may contain human biases such as prejudices and stereotypes (Caliskan et al. 2017), and models failing to account for such systematic human irrationality may generate inappropriate contents (Chan et al. 2021).

Although researchers have begun to recognize the impact of human decision irrationality on ML, few studies have directly considered irrationality in algorithm design. Somewhat related literature includes the research that incorporates human factors into the design of ML algorithms. One such approach is the collaborative attention mechanism, which embeds high-level human knowledge into deep learning models. For example, Ralekar et al. (2021) used visual attention maps derived from human eye-tracking data to improve image classification. Two popular techniques in this domain are parallel coattention (Lu et al. 2016) and merged attention (Dou et al. 2022), both of which enable joint attention between humangenerated texts and the corresponding visual inputs. These methods bridge machine and human data by uncovering latent representations within highdimensional attention spaces.

Another prevalent practice in LLMs is aligning AI with human preferences using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al. 2022). RLHF frequently uses the regularization technique via a reward function that penalizes misalignment with human preferences in its objective function. The intuition behind regularization is to inject “common sense” knowledge into a model, in the form of a prior preference that cannot be obtained from the data (Morad et al. 2020). Arguably, regularization is the most popular technique to steer the model toward prior preferences due to its compatibility with different forms of loss functions, model architectures, and tasks (Santos and Papa 2022).

Regularization has been extensively used to address overfitting mitigate unfairness in ML (Caton and Haas 2024) and facilitate multitask learning (Zhang and Yang 2021). Originally, regularization was proposed to penalize the complexity of the ML model to inhibit overfitting (Krogh and Hertz 1991). But its use has been extended to different settings. Kamishima et al. (2011) used regularization to penalize “unfair” outcomes. In multitask learning, regularization involves adding the loss of auxiliary tasks to the main loss as a penalty, ensuring that learned representations are both task specific and shared among tasks (Lin et al. 2019). Because of its generalizability and flexibility, the regularization approach is well suited for incorporating various forms of irrationality into diverse ML algorithms.

## 2.3. Human–Machine Collaboration

Most studies in this area conclude that ML can enhance human productivity across diverse contexts such as lending (Fu et al. 2021), bail decisions (Kleinberg et al. 2018), and drug discovery (Agrawal et al. 2019). Human and machine each has unique strength, and neither dominates the other (De Cremer and Kasparov 2021). Collaboration between the two often proves most effective. Although machines excel at algorithmic prediction, humans contribute domain knowledge that machines may not readily apprehend (Choudhury et al. 2020). Many business tasks benefit from a “human in the loop” approach, where humans and machines work together (Pavlou 2018). In trading, humans can uniquely gather and process “soft” information, such as opinions, ideas, and rumors, which provides distinct advantages (Liu 2022). Tantri (2021) demonstrated that directly obtaining soft information through personal visits to borrowers significantly enhances lending decisions.

Conversely, machines do not suffer from the cognitive limitations that bound humans and are therefore less susceptible to the deficiencies inherent in human decision making (Choudhury et al. 2020). For instance, lenders’ use of ML can reduce delinquency and benefit borrowers affected by natural disasters (Liu et al. 2023). Cowgill (2020) demonstrated that hiring algorithms could outperform HR managers in recruitment, where human recruiters tended to erroneously favor “traditional” applicants who graduated from elite colleges with high-profile credentials.

Various strategies for integrating human and machine intelligence have been explored in the literature, which can be broadly categorized into parallel and sequential approaches. In the parallel strategy, humans and machines make independent decisions simultaneously, which are then combined post hoc using ensemble methods (Wei et al. 2022). However, a common criticism of such an approach is the independence of the human and machine decisions. As Mehrabi et al. (2021) pointed out, failing to consider the driving mechanisms behind each may inadvertently amplify their limitations rather than rectify them.

Another widely adopted strategy is the sequentia decision-making process. In one sequential approach, human decisions provide initial inputs for machines, whereas machines make the final decision. However, this method poses two challenges. First, the biases inherent in human-generated decisions can perpetuate suboptimal or discriminatory outcomes in machines (Chen 2023). Second, there is the issue of AI misalignment, where machine objectives may not align with human intentions, resulting in unintended and potentially harmful outcomes (Dung 2023). Another sequential approach uses ML to provide initial recommendations, with humans making the final decision based on these suggestions (Fu¨ gener et al. 2021). Although this method increases overall prediction accuracy, humans can exhibit algorithm aversion when the machine errs (Dietvorst et al. 2018). Further, although humans can leverage the superior analytical capabilities of machine, it does not necessarily help overcome their decisionmaking irrationality.

In sum, although recent AI studies have started to incorporate human factors into algorithm design, they have not provided targeted strategies for addressing human irrationality in specific tasks. In contrast, we propose an irrationality-aware, preference-preserving, and decision-specific HMC framework.

## 3. Research Context and the IA-HMC Framework

In this section, we begin with an illustrative example in the research context of copy trading and then propose an overarching framework.

## 3.1. Research Context

We choose copy trading as the empirical context, where followers observe information about the financial positions of others and, uniquely, can choose to copy the financial decisions of other traders. Unlike traditional retail investing, which relies on professional analysts for financial advice, copy trading democratizes this process by allowing any trader to share thei expertise. Investors can follow these traders and copy trades in real time, a process automated in their own accounts for a fee.<sup>1</sup> They can follow multiple traders simultaneously and cease following a trader at any time, at which point all copied positions are closed at the current market price.

Copy-trading services offer several unique characteristics compared with other wealth management services. First, trades are executed directly within the retail investor’s own account, eliminating the need for fund transfers to brokers as is done in traditional fund trading. This grants investors greater decision-making autonomy, enabling them to flexibly start or stop copy trading at their discretion. Second, unlike traditional funds that typically only disclose aggregate performance statistics monthly or quarterly, copy-trading platforms provide real-time access to transaction records. This transparency empowers investors with up-to-date, transaction-level information for informed decision making. Third, many copy-trading platforms integrate social feedback modules, such as online forums, to facilitate private discussions between followers and traders. This virtual community feature enables the sharing of trading strategies, trader assessments, and investment insights.

Although copy trading lowers the investment barrier for layperson investors who lack the skills to select assets, a new challenge arises: They need to decide which trader to select. This decision is referred to as the “following” or “copying” decision, which is the focus of our study. Most copy-trading platforms provide extensive performance metrics of traders along with their actual transactions to assist followers in evaluating traders. However, this is a nontrivial task, as evidenced by the low proportion of followers (around 30%) achieving a positive return (Zignaly 2022). Given the presence of many traders with outstanding strategies and returns on the platform, this poor performance is likely attributable to followers suboptimal copy decisions. Prior research has demonstrated that irrational trading behaviors such as the herding effect (Gemayel and Preda 2018), the disposition effect (Heimer 2016), and excessive risk taking (Apesteguia et al. 2020) can be magnified through social interactions among investors in copy trading.

Our specific research context is the copy trading service at ZuluTrade, one of the earliest and leading copy-trading platforms. On the ZuluTrade platform, each trader has a public profile page (Figure A.1 in Online Appendix A) displaying their entire trading history and 25 representative features. These features include overall and weekly performance statistics (trades, winning rate, return on investment (ROI), profit, etc.) and overview information (Zulurank, views, following amount, number of followers, and identity). Detailed definitions of these 25 variables are provided in Table B.1 in Online Appendix B. Followers make copy decisions based on this set of information public to all, and the platform records every action. This enables us to observe followers’ decisionmaking processes and outcomes, which is necessary for identifying irrationality and rectifying it with ML.

## 3.2. Overview of Framework IA-HMC

We propose a human–machine collaboration framework that identifies and mitigates human decisionmaking irrationalities. This framework, called IA-HMC, consists of three main parts as outlined in Figure 1.

1. Identifying Human Decision Irrationality (corresponding to Research Question 1): We aim to identify the sources of irrationality from the data generated by human decisions. We examine how individuals weigh various features in their decision making and measure the extent of their irrationality accordingly, drawing on established concepts from behavioral economics. Section 4 will formalize and operationalize the identification process of alterfactual irrationality.

2. Developing Irrationality-Aware ML Methods (corresponding to Research Question 2): This step guides the algorithm to maximize model objectives while accounting for irrationality. Specifically, we detail the process of integrating irrationality regularization into ML’s objective function. Additionally, we explore alternative model architectures with different model inputs, objective functions and model structures.

3. Augmenting Human Decisions with ML via Contraction Method (corresponding to Research Question 3):

We propose an HMC approach that aligns with individuals’ preferences while minimizing irrational choices, building on the contraction method. The approach emulates a quasi-random experimental design, allowing for a comparison between machine-augmented counterfactual decisions and original human decisions.

Our framework is intended to serve as a general, plug-and-play design rather than a one-size-fits-all solution. The specific source of irrationality, the specific choice of baseline ML algorithm, and the approach to account for irrationality can be treated as domain knowledge and customized for different decision tasks.

## 4. Identifying Irrationality

We first define a novel concept of irrationality: alterfactual irrationality, and present some model-free evidence of its existence in copy trading. We then operationalize it by identifying two key sources of irrationality: herding effect and identity bias.<sup>2</sup>

## 4.1. Defining Alterfactual Irrationality

Rightfully, common approaches to studying the rational decision-making process focus on examining the information that is relevant and important for the decision. However, to fully disentangle the decision process, researchers must also be aware of how irrelevant information impacts a decision. Recent research in explainable AI (XAI) has highlighted related issues, suggesting that explaining the AI decision-making process should consider the reality where alternative, irrelevant features of an AI’s inputs may change the output of an AI algorithm, a phenomenon referred to as alterfactual explanations (Mertes et al. 2024). Building on this observation and the IIA assumption, we define alterfactual irrationality as the irrationality observed when the presence of irrelevant features in a decision’s input space may reverse the preference of an individual over two alternative choices.

We formalize alterfactual irrationality as follows: Let $C = \{ C _ { 1 } , C _ { 2 } , C _ { 3 } , \ldots , C _ { N } \}$ be the set of available choice options $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ candidate traders in copy-trading). Each option $C _ { i } \in C$ is characterized by a vector of attributes. Without loss of generality, we distinguish between two types of attributes: $\pmb { X } = \tilde { ( } \pmb { X } _ { r } , \pmb { X } _ { i r } )$ . Here $X _ { r }$ represents task-relevant attributes, such as the ROI, which serves as a commonly used benchmark to evaluate a trader’s performance in the finance industry and should be considered in followers’ decisions. On the other hand, $X _ { i r }$ represents task-irrelevant attributes that are given excessive weight by humans, despite not contributing directly to the task. Examples of such attributes include the number of followers, which could be driven by non–task-related factors.<sup>3</sup> With the choice set C and the attributes $X ,$ a decision maker’s preference of option $C _ { i }$ is denoted as $P R ( C _ { i } | X )$ , whereas the expected outcome $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ expected performance of following a trader) of option $C _ { i }$ is $\pi ( C _ { i } )$ . Alterfactual irrationality is defined as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { For } i, j \in \{1, 2, 3,... N \}, i \neq j, \pi (C _ {i} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}) \preceq \pi (C _ {j} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}) \\ \text { and } P R (C _ {i} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}) \preceq P R (C _ {j} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}); \pi (C _ {i} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}, \boldsymbol {X} _ {i r}) \preceq \pi (C _ {j} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}, \boldsymbol {X} _ {i r}), \\ \text { but } P R (C _ {i} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}, \boldsymbol {X} _ {i r}) > P R (C _ {j} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {r}, \boldsymbol {X} _ {i r}). \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

The definition states that a rational decision maker should base the choice on task-relevant attributes $X _ { r }$ rather than task-irrelevant attributes $X _ { i r }$ . If option $C _ { j }$ dominates option $C _ { i }$ in the choice set C in terms of decision outcome, a rational decision maker will choose $C _ { j }$ based on $X _ { r }$ . However, irrationality occurs when we observe a choice reversal, that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ option $C _ { i }$ is chosen instead due to the mere presence of the alterfactual attribute $X _ { i r }$ . In short, our definition of alterfactual irrationality captures the scenario of decision reversal when irrelevant features are included. In this sense, it bears similarity to the notion of “preference reversal” that captures the disparity between people’s valuation and choice (Loomes and Pogrebna 2017).

We visualize the presence of alterfactual irrationality within the context of copy trading using the Zulu-Trade data. This is done by contrasting the factors that influence followers’ copy decisions with the associated decision performance (win/loss in the next period when following a trader). To ensure comparability, we standardize the copy decision, outcomes, and 25 variables describing traders. These 25 variables are orga nized by ZuluTrade into two categories: Overview Information, which provides descriptive account details for each trader; and Performance Statistics, which evaluate the trading performance of a trader. We apply the approach from Mullainathan and Obermeyer (2022), running copy decisions and outcomes as dependent variables (DVs) separately on these 25 predictors via univariate regressions. Each regression yields a correlation coefficient. Figure 2 plots the univariate correlations of each variable with the follower’s winning outcome (x axis) and copy decision (y axis).<sup>4</sup>

Figure 2 sheds light on potential irrational behavior among followers. Distinct patterns emerged from separate regressions on the two variable categories. For the Overview Information category, we observed a significantly positive correlation, implying that followers may overvalue variables such as number of followers, following amount, identity, and views, despite their negative correlations with outcomes. Conversely, in the Performance Statistics category, the relationship is nearly horizontal with a slight negative slope around zero, suggesting followers may undervalue the positive signal (correlation) between performance indices and winning outcomes. These observations suggest the presence of alterfactual irrationality that task-irrelevant features may alter the copy decisions of followers regarding which trader to follow. They further reveal two possible sources of irrationality: herding effect and identity bias.

## 4.2. Herding Effect

Herding refers to decision makers’ tendency to follow the popular actions of peers. ZuluTrade highlights a pool of top traders who attract the most followers, most following amount, and most views. Such salient information may trigger a herding effect, prompting followers to flock to these traders. The herding effect can be rational if it reflects informed, deliberated decisions based on past knowledge (Zhang and Liu 2012). However, when decision makers blindly imitate others without fully understanding potential consequences, herding becomes irrational (Simonsohn and Ariely 2008).

Formally, we measure herding according to the classic Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny (LSV) metric proposed by Lakonishok et al. (1992), capturing the similarity in the following decisions among a group of followers. The logic behind this metric is that followers are assumed (under the null hypothesis) to be making decisions independently. However, if we observe correlated following decisions in excess of random occurrences, herding is present. Specifically, the LSV herding measure $( H M _ { i t } )$ of followers for trader i during period t is

$$
H M _ {i t} = | p _ {i t} - E [ p _ {i t} ] | - E | p _ {i t} - E [ p _ {i t} ] |,\tag{2}
$$

where $p _ { i t } = n f _ { i t } / n _ { i t }$ captures the proportion of new followers, $n f _ { i t }$ is the number of new followers of trader i during period $t ,$ and $n _ { i t }$ is the total number of active followers (including new and churning followers) of trader i during period $t ; E [ p _ { i t } ]$ denotes the expected proportion of new followers relative to the total number of active followers on the entire platform during period t, which provides a baseline (expected) share of new followers for a random trader. The expression $E | p _ { i t } - E [ p _ { i t } ] |$ is an adjustment factor that controls the random variation around $E [ p _ { i t } ] .$ . This quantity is computed by assuming a binomial process for the number of followers during each period, where the parameter n is the total number of active investors and $p$ is the average proportion of new followers on the platform during the period. Therefore, $H M _ { i t }$ measures the level of herding to trader i.

## 4.3. Identity Bias

Source credibility theory posits that the perceived credibility of information can significantly influence decisions. People tend to trust information more when it comes from a source with verified identity markers, such as names or contact details (Wang et al. 2018). On ZuluTrade, traders voluntarily disclose their identities by providing verifiable credentials, such as ID, name, affiliation, or even pictures. Such credible identity strongly impacts copying decisions (Lee et al. 2015); however, it does not necessarily translate into usefulness. As shown in Figure 2, investors are more likely to follow traders with verified identities, reflecting the significant role of source credibility in follower decisions. Yet, a trader with a credible verified identity does not necessarily possess proficient trading skills (Agrawal et al. 2015).

Following a similar process for Figure 3(a), we partition traders into bins based on their returns and then calculate the average return and the proportion of verified identities in each bin. We observe that top traders with higher returns (ROI) often opt to keep their identities concealed (Figure 4(a)). One plausible reason could be these traders prefer anonymity to protect their trading strategies. In contrast, the worst performers tend to also remain anonymous (likely because there is nothing to boast about), whereas mediocre traders are more inclined to reveal their identities. We then compare the average return between unidentified traders and identified traders. Figure 4(b) indicates that unidentified traders on average outperform those with verified identities (a two-sample t-test yields $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). Therefore, although traders with verified identities tend to attract more followers, their performance is generally poorer, potentially misleading followers into irrational copy decisions.

## 5. Irrationality-Aware ML Method

In this section, we develop an irrationality-aware ML method that aligns with rational human objectives. Our approach draws inspiration from recent AI align ment research in LLMs, which rewards a model for alignment with human preferences while penalizing it for misalignment (Ouyang et al. 2022). We tweak it by aligning the ML objective with the rationality of follower choices, penalizing the model for irrational decisions. Specifically, we use the regularization technique for the alignment process. Regularization offers two key advantages. First, it constructs constraints derived from human behavior, narrowing the solution space that cannot be inferred from data alone. Second, regu larization, using a penalty term for misalignment, is a generic and flexible approach adaptable to a wide range of ML algorithms.

We first consider XGBoost as the baseline ML algorithm under the IA-HMC framework. XGBoost is a scal able tree-boosting algorithm (Chen and Guestrin 2016), one of the most powerful and widely used ML methods. Owing to its high explainability (in contrast to black box methods like deep learning) and superior performance, XGBoost has gained popularity in trading (Chatzis et al. 2018). Additionally, XGBoost is commonly employed as a baseline method in the contraction approach as done in Kleinberg et al. (2018) and Fu et al. (2021). In Section 7.4, we also explore alternative model architectures with different inputs, objective functions, and structural designs.

We discuss the generic mathematical representation of the XGBoost algorithm within our context. Given N traders indexed by $i = 1 , \ldots , N _ { \mathrm { \scriptsize { \cent } } }$ , each of which is characterized by an m-dimensional feature vector $\begin{array} { r } { { X } _ { i } = ( x _ { i , 1 } , } \end{array}$ $x _ { i , 2 } , \ldots , x _ { i , m } )$ . The outcome $Y _ { i }$ denotes the ground-truth performance of the copy-trading activity following the focal trader i. For simplicity, we convert the outcome into a binary variable that equals one if the followers lose money in the next period (e.g., week) after copy-9 ing the trader, and zero otherwise.

XGBoost is an ensemble model that combines the predictions from each individual tree by taking their sum. Formally, the prediction for instance i with feature vector $X _ { i }$ is as follows:

$$
\hat {Y} _ {i} = \phi (\boldsymbol {X} _ {i}) = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} f _ {k} (\boldsymbol {X} _ {i}),\tag{5}
$$

where K is the number of regression trees, and $f _ { k }$ represents the mapping function of the kth individual tree; $f _ { k } ( { \mathbf { X } } _ { i } )$ can be interpreted as the predicted (propensity) score of Y given $X _ { i }$ . To train the model, the following objective function is optimized:

$$
L (\phi) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} l (Y _ {i}, \hat {Y} _ {i}) + \sum_ {k} \Omega (f _ {k}),\tag{6}
$$

$$
l (Y _ {i}, \hat {Y} _ {i}) = - Y _ {i} * l o g (\sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i})) - (1 - Y _ {i}) * l o g (1 - \sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i})),\tag{7}
$$

where l is the cross-entropy loss function, Ω is a regularizer for the tree complexity, and $\begin{array} { r } { \sigma ( z ) = \frac { 1 } { 1 + e ^ { - z } } } \end{array}$ is the classic sigmoid function. Formally, let $\hat { Y } _ { i } ^ { ( t ) }$ be the prediction for the ith instance at the tth iteration. The trees are constructed in a greedy manner by adding $f _ { t }$ to minimize the objective. The objective function can be approximated using Taylor expansion as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} L ^ {(t)} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} l (Y _ {i}, \hat {Y} _ {i} ^ {(t - 1)} + f _ {t} (\boldsymbol {X} _ {i})) + \Omega (f _ {t}) \\ \simeq \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} [ l (Y _ {i}, \hat {Y} _ {i} ^ {(t - 1)}) + g _ {i} f _ {t} (\boldsymbol {X} _ {i}) + \frac {1}{2} h _ {i} f _ {t} ^ {2} (\boldsymbol {X} _ {i}) ] + \Omega (f _ {t}), \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

where $g _ { i } = \partial _ { \hat { Y } _ { . } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } } l ( Y _ { i } , \hat { Y } _ { i } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } )$ and $h _ { i } = \partial _ { \hat { Y } _ { . } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } } ^ { 2 } l ( Y _ { i } , \hat { Y } _ { i } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } )$ are the first- and second-order gradient statistics of the loss function, respectively, and f is the predicted score from the tth tree. Equation (8) incrementally adds the predicted score obtained from the tth tree to those after the (t � 1)th iteration. XGBoost uses the second-order Taylor expansion as an approximation for its loss function. In subsequent sections, $l ( Y _ { i } , \hat { Y } _ { i } )$

will be denoted as $l _ { C r o s s E n t r o p y }$ to differentiate it from the irrationality loss.

Then we discuss how to build irrationality-awareness into XGBoost. Recall that we identified two sources of irrationality in copy trading: herding effect and identity bias. Drawing inspiration from the AI alignment literature, where human preferences serve as a reward signal in the AI’s objective function to fine-tune the algorithm, our approach similarly incorporates human irrationality as a penalty to XGBoost’s loss function. For the herding effect, we propose the following irrational herding regularizer:

$$
\begin{array}{c} l _ {H e r d i n g} = - F H M _ {i} * Y _ {i} * \log (\sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i})) - U H M _ {i} * (1 - Y _ {i}) \\ * \log (1 - \sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i})), \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

where the herding measures FHM<sub>i</sub> and UHM<sub>i</sub> are defined in Section 4.2. The rationale behind $l _ { H e r d i n g }$ is that a trader’s predicted risk of losing increases when choosing this trader due to herding and should be penalized accordingly. Specifically, the loss function will incorporate a penalty based on FHM (followingherding) when the model predicts that following a trader due to herding would result in a loss (irrational following-herding). Conversely, the loss function will impose a UHM (unfollow-herding) penalty when the model predicts that unfollowing a trader due to herding would result in a loss (irrational churning-herding).

As to the identity bias, let us assume that, without loss of generality, $b _ { i } = 1$ signifies that trader i has provided a verified identity, whereas $b _ { i } = 0$ indicates otherwise. We propose the following regularizer to measure irrationality stemming from identity bias. This regularizer penalizes the weights of traders who were chosen primarily because they have verified identities. Specifically, penalties are applied when the model predicts that following an unidentified trader will result in a loss, or conversely, that following an identified trader will lead to a win. Under this setup, the regularizer subtly adjusts the model’s weights to nudge predictions in favor of unidentified traders, encouraging the model not to overlook them only because they do not disclose their identity.

$$
l _ {I d e n t i t y} = - b _ {i} * l o g (\sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i})) - (1 - b _ {i}) * l o g (1 - \sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i}))\tag{10}
$$

With these two irrationality regularizers defined, we set the hyperparameter $\beta$ as the free parameter to be estimated from the data, which determines the relative importance of these two types of irrationality as follows:

$$
l _ {I r r a t i o n a l i t y} = \beta * l _ {H e r d i n g} + (1 - \beta) * l _ {I d e n t i t y}.\tag{11}
$$

Our irrationality-aware method, referred to as RXGBoost (rational XGBoost), is characterized with an overall loss function that combines the traditional log-likelihood loss function with an additional irrationality regularizer,

as follows:

$$
l _ {R X G B o o s t} = \alpha * l _ {C r o s s E n t r o p y} + (1 - \alpha) * l _ {I r r a t i o n a l i t y},\tag{12}
$$

where α determines the strength of the regularizer. The learnable parameters α and $\beta$ are optimized using the tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE), a Bayesian optimization method (Watanabe 2023).<sup>6</sup> We then need to recompute the gradient $\hat { g } _ { i }$ and hessian $\hat { h } _ { i }$ for the new objective function (the superscript t � 1 is omitted to avoid notation clutter):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {g} _ {i} = \partial_ {\hat {Y} _ {i}} l _ {R X G B o o s t} \\ = \alpha * (\sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i}) - Y _ {i}) - (1 - \alpha) * \beta * F H M _ {i} * Y _ {i} * (1 - \sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i})) \\ + (1 - \alpha) * \beta * U H M _ {i} * (1 - Y _ {i}) * \sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i}) \\ + (1 - \alpha) * (1 - \beta) * (\sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i}) - b _ {i}). \end{array} \tag {13}
$$

Similarly, we can derive $\hat { h } _ { i }$ as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\hat {h} _ {i} = \partial_ {\hat {Y} _ {i}} ^ {2} l _ {R X G B o o s t}} \\ & {\qquad = \sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i}) * (1 - \sigma (\hat {Y} _ {i})) * (\alpha + (1 - \alpha) * \beta * F H M _ {i} * Y _ {i}} \\ & {\qquad + (1 - \alpha) * \beta * U H M _ {i} * (1 - Y _ {i}) + (1 - \alpha) * (1 - \beta)).} \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

In summary, our method modifies the tree-building process of XGBoost by incorporating a new gradient and hessian. To handle time series data, we follow the data partitioning approaches suggested by Guo et al. (2022), allocating the first two thirds of the data for training and the remaining one third for testing. During the training process, key hyperparameters are optimized by minimizing the new loss function using the time series cross-validation exclusively on the training set.<sup>7</sup> The evaluation metric for selecting the best model is the area under the curve (AUC). Hyperparameters are chosen based on their performance on the validation set, ensuring that the testing set is completely excluded from the tuning process. Once the optimal model parameters are selected, we estimate the final model using the training set.

## 6. Combining ML with Human: Contraction Technique

## 6.1. Challenges and Solutions

After obtaining the predictions from RXGBoost, we develop an HMC method to incorporate these predictions into human decisions rather than replacing them. This is necessary for two reasons. First, humans possess unique private information not accessible to ML algorithms, for example, insights garnered from private interactions with traders and their posts on message boards. Relying solely on ML predictions could thus be suboptimal as these additional insights also critically influence human preferences.

Second, ML algorithms may lack ground-truth labels for training, and more importantly, label availability could be influenced by the choices of human decision makers, a phenomenon known as the “selective labels problem” (Lakkaraju et al. 2017). In our context, followers’ return outcomes are selectively recorded; we only observe returns when a follower actually copies a trader but not when the follower decides not to follow a trader.<sup>8</sup> These complexities pose challenges in effectively merging human and algorithmic decision making.

To address these problems, we propose an HMC method based on the contraction approach. It operates by excluding the poor decisions made by humans in a counterfactual manner. First, it tackles the issue of selective labels by focusing on the labeled part of the data. It suggests that a follower should have avoided traders predicted by machines as likely to lose money, among the pool of traders they are following. This circumvents the need for imputing unlabeled data while preserving the strength of human decisions, as the

Second, the method addresses the influence of unob served factors by creating a quasi-experimental setup. This involves grouping followers with similar trader encounters into a single “cell,” thereby creating homogeneous and comparable groups of followers. Within each cell, some followers receive ML augmentation by removing bad traders (the treated group), whereas others make decisions independently (the control group). This provides a counterfactual comparison of machine-augmented versus human decisions and allows potential effects of unobserved factors to be averaged out across different groups.

The high-level intuition behind the method can be exemplified as follows. Consider a scenario where follower i is following 15 traders among a pool of 100 candidate traders, whereas another follower j is copying 10 of the same 100 traders. We seek to determine if reducing follower i’s trader pool to mirror follower j’s (by removing the five poorest performing traders) would result in a comparable trading performance for both. This selection of the five traders to eliminate is guided by the irrationality-aware ML method, which flags the ones most likely to incur losses in the upcoming period. After removing these traders, we then reassess the performance based on the remaining 10 traders. If “follower i plus machine” performs significantly better than other j-type followers, we conclude that the machine successfully helps augment the follower’s decision.

## 6.2. Steps of the Contraction Technique

The step-by-step procedure of the RXGBoost contraction method is summarized in Table 1 and elaborated in the context of copy trading below.

Step 1. Define Evaluation Metrics: The primary goal of followers is to identify and copy profitable traders to maximize returns<sup>10</sup> while minimizing investment risks, known as the risk-return tradeoff. We accordingly define two metrics. (1) Loss rate: This is the fraction of traders incurring losses out of all traders considered by a follower. For instance, if a follower chooses 15 traders from a pool of 100 candidate traders and 5 of these traders incur losses, the follower’s loss rate is 0.05.<sup>11</sup> A follower chasing high returns would equivalently seek to minimize this loss rate. (2) Follow rate: This refers to the proportion of traders being followed by a follower. Risk reduction through diversification means following more traders in our context. Suppose a follower is following 15 traders among 100 candidate traders, then the follow rate is $1 5 / 1 0 0 = 0 . 1 5$ . These definitions echo “failure rate” and “acceptance rate” concepts introduced by Lakkaraju et al. (2017). Thus, a follower’s challenge lies in balancing the loss rate and follow rate, aiming to reduce the former while increasing the latter.

Step 2. Train the Irrationality-Aware Method: As discussed earlier, we only observe the ground-truth label $Y _ { i }$ for traders who were followed $( \boldsymbol { F } _ { i } ^ { \phantom { } } = 1 )$ but cannot observe it when the traders were not followed $( F _ { i } = 0 )$ , presenting a “selective label problem.” Therefore, we train RXGBoost only on the observed training set of follower-chosen traders $( F _ { i } = 1 )$ to predict the likelihood of receiving a negative return, denoted as $\phi ( X _ { i } | F _ { i } = 1 )$ . Step 3. Form Cells: We construct cells to ensure that followers draw from the same trader distributions (quasi-random assignment of traders to followers). To achieve this, we group followers into cells based on the four follower characteristics: country, year, month, and week.<sup>12</sup> This step results in a total of 810 cells. We require a minimum of five investors per cell to ensure a meaningful analysis, as recommended by Fu et al. (2021) and Kleinberg et al. (2018). This requirement reduces the number of cells being considered to 195, accounting for 70.9% of the follower sample. Each cell contains an average of 14.2 followers with 69.1 following pairs. Within these cells, the average observable characteristics of traders are similar and comparable.

Step 4. Form Quintiles: Followers with different risk preferences may strategically choose different levels of follow rates to diversify their portfolio of traders. To make followers more comparable, we further create five follower quintiles, as done in Kleinberg et al. (2018), based on followers’ follow rates. Each quintile captures a different group of followers at a particular risk preference level, as indicated by follow rate, with the first quintile’s followers copying the most number of traders (the most diversified portfolio). These five quintiles’ average follow rates are 11.5%, 7.2%, 5.3%, 4.1%, and 2.9%, respectively. Each quintile point is an aggregation of all cells by taking the weighted average of the follow rate-loss rate performance of the same quintile point across all cells, weighted by the total number of instances in each cell.

Step 5. ML Contraction: The pivotal step of the contraction method involves ML filtering out poorperforming traders from the individual follower’s portfolio. For each cell, traders are progressively removed starting from the first quintile, based on their loss probability predicted by RXGBoost: $P ( Y _ { i } = 1 | X _ { i } )$ This is done in a descending order of risk, with the rationale being that high-risk traders predicted by ML, should be avoided.

As an additional trader is removed, the follow rate decreases, and we recalculate the loss rate for the reduced trader set. Because the outcomes (win or lose) for all these traders are known, the loss rate can be accurately calculated (in a hindsight manner). Fol lowers with a lower loss rate at a given follow rate are considered more capable of filtering out bad traders. The contraction procedure ends when the follow rate for the quintile reaches zero. Consequently, by progressively removing poor-performing traders, we create a “follow rate–loss rate” curve, illustrating how followers in this quintile could have enhanced their investment performance with ML assistance (i.e., HMC personalized at the individual decision level).

The contraction technique proxies a quasi-random assignment across cells: Given a set of traders considered by followers, the first quintile followers receive ML augmentation (treatment), whereas the other four quintile groups remain untreated and make decisions by themselves. The first quintile followers, assisted by

ML, serve as a counterfactual for the other four quintiles’ followers. Comparison between the treated and control groups can effectively neutralize the potential impacts of unobserved factors. The validity of the contraction method can be established by examining the premise of quasi-random assignment, that is, whether trader characteristics, both observed and unobserved, remain uncorrelated with follower quintile assignment (based on follow rate). We confirm this through the “random assignment test” (to prove the similarity in observed $X _ { i }$ across all five quintiles) and the “similar selection on unobservables test” (to prove the similarity in followers’ capabilities of selecting unobservable factors across all five quintiles). The details of these two tests are reported in Online Appendix D, with the results indicating that both tests pass, confirming the validity of applying the contraction approach in our case.

As depicted in Figure 7, RXGBoost contraction achieves a higher lift than RXGBoost. A similar trend is observed with XGBoost contraction compared with XGBoost. This result demonstrates the value of the contraction component and highlights the importance of leveraging human intelligence through contraction rather than merely replacing it with machine intelligence.

## 8. Mechanisms: Persistence and Dissolution of Irrationality

We demonstrated the presence of human irrationality in copy trading and implemented an IA-HMC framework to mitigate it. But why does irrationality arise in the first place? Also, why does our framework work? We delve into the underlying mechanisms to shed light on them.

## 8.1. Why Irrationality Persists in Human Decisions

8.1.1. Information Salience. One key factor contributing to suboptimal decision making is human’s tendency to overvalue easily accessible information (Alok et al. 2020). Figure 2 reveals that variables related to overview information significantly influence followers decisions, suggesting an overemphasis on these salient attributes beyond their actual predictive value. To gain further insights, we empirically examine whether humans, compared with ${ \mathrm { M L } } ,$ are more likely to overweigh salient information as suggested by the information salience theory (Bordalo et al. 2012).

We categorize the 25 variables in Figure 2 into three groups: (1) $X _ { H e r d i n g }$ containing the 4 variables leading to herding (Following amount, Number of followers, Views, Zulurank), (2) $X _ { I d e n t i t y }$ including the identity variable causing identity bias, and (3) $X _ { P e r f o r m a n c e }$ encompassing the 20 remaining variables that are performance related. For ease of analysis and interpretation, we consolidate the variables under each group into a single indicator. This is done by running XGBoost on each set of the three variable groups separately to predict π(X), the traders’ winning probability in the next period. Then the predicted probability serves as the sole predictor for the regressions in Table 6. The first three columns in Table 6 report the results by regressing followers’ copy-trading decisions on $\hat { \pi } ( \dot { \mathbf { X } } _ { H e r d i n g } )$ $\hat { \pi } ( X _ { I d e n t i t y } ) .$ , and $\hat { \pi } ( \bar { X } _ { P e r f o r m a n c e } ) .$ , respectively. Columns (4), (5), and (6) report the results by regressing the winning outcome on these predictors.

The herding effect significantly and positively influences copy decisions (coefficient � 0.861, $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ but not the winning outcome (coefficient � $0 . 0 2 4 , \ p > 0 . 1 )$ . The results align with our definition of alterfactual irrationality and echo the findings that “investors rely on simple signals and likely do not engage in sophisticated learning about managers’ alpha” as reported in Ben-David et al. (2022, p. 1723).

8.1.2. Source Credibility. The source credibility theory asserts that humans subconsciously assess source credibility when determining the value of the information (Hovland and Weiss 1951). The identity bias we describe here represents a negative use case of it. This is likely because, in trading, what ultimately matters is a trader’s ability to trade, often measured through the trader’s track record (historical trading performance). Whether someone’s identity is credible may not matter as much, if it does not reflect the trader’s prowess. We confirm this observation in Table 6. This presents evidence that overweighing identity information yields alterfactual irrational decisions. These findings enrich the source credibility theory by showing that overly attending to source credibility may lead to adverse effects.

## 8.2. Why Irrationality Dissolves in Our Framework

Building on the observation that human irrationality is due to overvaluing credible sources and salient information, we then explore why machines are able to overcome human cognitive limitations. One key difference lies in their cognitive capacity: Humans are constrained by limited working memory and processing ability (Sweller 1988), whereas machines have superior analytical capabilities. To demonstrate this, we present empirical evidence by comparing their ability to utilize variables in decision making, following Mullainathan and Obermeyer (2022) and Liu (2022). We employ a forward stepwise selection procedure over all the features to examine which trader features, listed in Table B.1 in Online Appendix $\mathrm { B , }$ impact followers’ copy decisions. Beginning with an empty regression model, we progressively add the remaining variables one by one with the lowest pvalue. We record the $R ^ { 2 }$ for each step of the stepwise regressions. A feature is considered influential in decision making if its contribution to $R ^ { 2 }$ exceeds a predefined threshold. We also apply this procedure to the machine’s decisions (based on RXGBoost contraction).

Our results, visualized in Figure 8 with the number of variables on the x axis and $\overline { { R ^ { 2 } } }$ on the y axis, show that machines effectively utilize 18 of 25 variables to predict traders’ loss probability, whereas human followers only use 5. These 18 variables together yield a much higher $R ^ { 2 }$ . These results indicate that cognitive constraints hinder investors’ decision-making abilities, as they are limited to processing only a small subset of useful variables, unlike ML algorithms.

8.2.1. Algorithms’ Ability to Dissolve Irrationality. What abilities do machines possess in mitigating irrational ity? To answer this, we look into whether machines can resolve preference reversals caused by followers’ overemphasis on irrelevant features. We first use XGBoost to predict human copy decisions, capturing the feature importance in these human decisions (as predicted by XGBoost). Feature importance is quantified by Shapley values (Shapley et al. 1953, Lundberg and Lee 2017). We similarly obtain the feature importance for XGBoost and RXGBoost when making their own copy decisions alone. Figure 9 displays the average Shapley values for task-relevant (performance) and task-irrelevant features (irrationality) separately under three decision modes: human, XGBoost, and RXGBoost.

Surprisingly, we find that human decisions are predominantly influenced by task-irrelevant factors, whereas task-related metrics have a negligible impact. In contrast, XGBoost neutralizes this, placing more weight on performance features, although RXGBoost further reverses this pattern toward the right direction. Importantly, this demonstrates that our irrationalityaware algorithms are able to successfully mitigate irrationality by rebalancing feature importance.<sup>19</sup>

8.2.2. Needing Human in the Loop. The contribution of the contraction method would demonstrate the efficacy of integrating human and machine decisions in HMC. Hence, we calculate the difference in the winning rate (the percentage of winning traders) across follower’s quintiles between RXGBoost contraction and standard RXGBoost when both are applied to achieve the same follow rate increase. A positive difference indicates the superior performance of RXGBoost contraction in identifying good traders compared with RXGBoost, given the same increase in follow rate. The predominantly positive average difference across the entire range in Figure 10 underscores the significance of leveraging human intelligence through contraction.

## 9. Discussion

This study develops a novel ML solution, the IA-HMC framework, for mitigating irrationality in human decisions. To this end, we introduce a new notion of irrationality, alterfactual irrationality, and propose an operational procedure for its identification. Our IA-HMC framework identifies irrationality in individual decisions, incorporates these insights to regularize ML algorithms, and subsequently augments human decision making using the contraction approach. Applying this framework to copy trading, we find that followers are susceptible to the herding effect and identity bias, leading to irrational trader choices and inferior trading performance. We then demonstrate that IA-HMC significantly enhances the performance of copy-trading decisions made by followers,<sup>20</sup> thus highlighting the importance of accounting for human irrationality in ML design and employing the contraction technique to enhance decision making. Furthermore, we uncover the root causes of this irrationality from the perspective of human cognitive limitations, including overemphasis on salient information and excessive reliance on source credibility, as prescribed by alterfactual irrationality. Consequently, this study provides insights into both “which” choices humans should make (e.g., which trader an investor should follow) and “why,” enabling a deeper understanding of human decision irrationality and proposing a practical mitigation solution within an HMC framework.

The proposed framework yields several practical implications. First, it offers a new perspective on ML model design by leveraging machine impartiality to counter human decision irrationality and facilitating effective collaboration between human and machine decisions. This approach improves the alignment with the rational behavior of followers while maintaining individual preferences, providing a solution to the pressing issue on AI alignment (Ji et al. 2023, Bergman et al. 2024). Second, we show that the metrics provided by copy trading platforms to assess traders can inadvertently misguide investors toward irrational choices. We caution that, although these recommendations simplify decision making, they might also be a source of irrational choices, emphasizing the need for careful selection of recommendation indicators. Interestingly, this is echoed by Saifee et al. (2020) who found that highly-rated physicians could lead to poorer outcomes for chronic disease patients. Third, for investors, our insights into the sources of irrationality can serve as a guide to avoid decision-making pitfalls. We reveal that credible sources with verified identities do not necessarily translate to beneficial sources. Hence, investors should utilize analytical tools such as ML rather than relying purely on conspicuous information.

Notably, our framework is applicable to a wide range of decision contexts—such as lending, medical diagnosis, and bail decisions—where irrationality is pervasive. In these scenarios, human attention can be inadvertently lured away by task-irrelevant features, often resulting in preference reversal. Mullainathan and Obermeyer (2022) reported that when diagnosing a heart attack, physicians may overemphasize representative factors, such as chest pain, leading to overtesting where predictably low-risk patients are tested but do not benefit. Our IA-HMC framework is well suited to these scenarios to effectively identify and mitigate such alterfactual irrationality. To demonstrate the generalizability of our proposed framework across different HMC settings, we conduct additional simulations using synthetic decision data, as detailed in Online Appendix J. Our main findings still hold, suggesting that our IA-HMC framework is applicable to generic decision settings where alterfactual irrationality persists.

This study also bears several limitations. First, the framework is primarily implemented and tested within the copy-trading context. Although the copy trading itself is a significant business problem, it would be interesting to extend our study to other HMC applications where irrational choices permeate. Second, our framework simplifies decisions and outcomes to binary forms (following a trader or not, win or lose). Future research can extend our work by investigating continuous decisions (e.g., how much to invest) and continu ous outcomes (e.g., the actual return of following a trader). Third, decision makers may have multiple objectives such as maximizing returns and diversifying asset allocation simultaneously. These multiobjective scenarios present exciting opportunities for the field of multitask ML to explore. Forth, human irrationality is multifaceted and context specific. The framework might not capture all dimensions of irrational behavior, other forms of irrationality may require different identification and mitigation strategies.
