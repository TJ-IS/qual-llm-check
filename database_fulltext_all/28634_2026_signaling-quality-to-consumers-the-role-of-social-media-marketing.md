# Signaling Quality to Consumers: The Role of Social Media Marketing

Abstract. Firms have increasingly turned to social media marketing (SMM) as a means of enhancing their competitive edge and overall performance, yet they vary widely in their SMM spending. Building on an established game-theoretic framework of quality signaling, we consider the dual roles of SMM in (i) increasing product awareness and (ii) informing consumers about product quality (i.e., SMM’s “information revelation” role) in order to analyze a monopolist firm’s pricing decisions and strategic SMM spending. We first study the benchmark case, in which the only role of SMM is to increase product awareness, and then, we analyze the case in which SMM also plays an information revelation role. Our results are in stark contrast to prior results in the literature and yield new managerial insights. We show in particular that—in both the benchmark and information revelation cases—there is no separating equilibrium. Moreover, only two types of equilibria are possible: a partial pooling equilibrium, where only those firms whose products are of low or middle quality pool together and then, choose the same level of SMM spending; or a full pooling equilibrium, where the same SMM spending is chosen by all firms regardless of their respective products’ quality. These results suggest that despite the increased precision of external information (e.g., third-party reviews) available to consumers via SMM, highquality firms will limit their market size by reducing their SMM spending and thereby, deter lower-quality firms from mimicking them. Finally, we discover that when SMM does serve an information revelation role, high-quality firms find it more difficult to distinguish themselves from lower-quality firms.

## 1. Introduction

Social media has transformed the dynamics of interaction between firms and consumers, who now play an increasingly active role in creating product information and influencing firms’ marketing strategies through social interactions (Li et al. 2021, Tuten 2023). In contrast to traditional marketing’s one-way communication, in which firms advertise to increase product awareness, social media empowers consumers to create product information, engage in product discussions, and share with peers their take on various brands and products (Peters et al. 2013, Hamilton et al. 2016, Li et al. 2021). This shift in information control by way of social media marketing (SMM) has attracted the attention of firms (Drenik 2021), but there is still wide variation in firms’ SMM expenditures. For example, high-quality firms may allocate less spending to SMM than lowerquality firms (Gasbara 2018, Hanley 2019, Hollenbeck et al. 2019), and an inverted U-shaped relationship between quality and SMM spending has been reported based on data from Yelp and TripAdvisor (Hollenbeck et al. 2019, Nian and Sundararajan 2022).

Motivated by these observations, we adopt the game-theoretic model of Nian and Sundararajan (2022)—with the same notation and assumptions—to study a monopoly firm’s pricing decision and strategic spending on SMM. Thus, we consider a firm selling a product of high, middle, or low quality. The firm has private information about its product’s true quality, but consumers know only that product quality varies among these levels. Consumers receive a noisy external information signal, such as online word of mouth or third-party reviews, which informs them about the product’s true quality but is beyond the firm’s control. A firm’s SMM spending serves two roles: (i) increasing the number of consumers who are aware of its product, thereby expanding the firm’s consumer base; and (ii) providing more precise information about the product’s true quality and thus, enhancing the accuracy of the external information signal (this is the SMM’s information revelation role). Before making their purchase decisions, consumers observe the product’s price and the firm’s level of SMM spending in addition to receiving the external information signal.

First, we analyze the benchmark case, where SMM has no information revelation role. We show that there is no separating equilibrium but that there do exist pooling equilibria (where high-, mid-, and low-quality firms all choose the same product price and level of SMM spending) as well as a partial pooling equilibrium (where the mid- and low-quality firms pool together by choosing the same price and SMM spending, whereas the high-quality firm separates by choosing a different price and SMM spending). These results imply that contra Nian and Sundararajan (2022), firms in the benchmark case do not signal their true quality by choosing different spending levels. So, in the benchmark case, there are only two possible equilibria. (i) The high-quality firm spends less on SMM to maintain a small market and to prevent low- and mid-quality firms from mimicking it, whereas the mid- and lowquality firms choose (the same but) a higher spending level to expand their market size, or (ii) the high-, mid-, and low-quality firms all choose the same price and level of SMM spending.

Second, we analyze the case where the SMM does play an information revelation role. We establish that there is neither a separating equilibrium nor a partial pooling equilibrium (in which high- and low-quality firms pool by choosing the same price and SMM spending, whereas the mid-quality firm separates and chooses a different price and SMM spending). The implication of this finding is that contrary to the Nian and Sundararajan (2022) claim, there is no inverted U-shaped relationship between SMM spending and product quality. Moreover, we demonstrate that if SMM has an information revelation role, then pooling equilibria always exist, and a partial pooling equilibrium—where mid- and low-quality firms pool together, whereas the high-quality firm separates—can emerge under certain conditions. In this latter equilibrium, the high-quality firm reduces its SMM spending to maintain a small market base, thereby precluding mimicry by lower-quality firms that pool together and spend more on social media marketing. This result is consistent with observations in practice (Gasbara 2018, Hanley 2019, Hollenbeck et al. 2019).

Finally, we examine how the SMM’s information revelation role affects a firm’s expenditures on strategic marketing. It is interesting that the information revelation role of SMM makes it more difficult for the high quality firm to distinguish itself; the result is fewer instances of the partial pooling equilibrium in which mid- and low-quality firms pool, whereas the high quality firm separates. Also, if SMM does play an infor mation revelation role, then consumers obtain a more precise external information signal; hence, mid- and low-quality firms are compelled to lower their prices, spend less on SMM, and earn reduced profits if they opt to pool together but do not mimic the high-quality firm. That profit reduction explains why mid- and lowquality firms are less likely to prefer pooling over mimicking the high-quality firm.

The rest of this paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, we present the model setup and our assumptions. Section 3 discusses results for the benchmark case. In Section 4, we examine the outcomes of social media marketing and assess the impact of its information revelation role. We conclude in Section 5 with a brief summary of our methods, findings, and practical applications. All proofs are given in the Online Appendix.

## 2. Model Setup

This section is devoted to describing the model, nota tion, and assumptions of Nian and Sundararajan (2022). Consider a monopoly firm selling a product of high (H), middle (M), or low (L) quality, where $H >$ $M > L$ and L is normalized to one. That is, the product quality q has three types: $q \in \{ H , M , L \}$ . The product’s quality also determines the firm’s type. A firm that sells a product of quality H, M, or L is called, respectively, a high-quality, mid-quality, or low-quality firm. The fixed production cost is zero, and the unit production cost of the high-, mid-, and low-quality firm is equal to c , $c _ { M } ,$ and $c _ { L } ,$ respectively; here, $c _ { H } > c _ { M } > c _ { L }$ , and $c _ { L }$ is normalized to zero.

## 2.1. Consumers and the Firm’s SMM Spending

The total number of consumers is normalized to one. Consumers are homogeneous in their valuation of the product, and their valuation is equal to the product’s quality $q \in \{ H , M , L \}$ . Among all consumers, only a fraction $r \in [ 0 , 1 )$ is initially aware of the product; the remaining fraction $( 1 - r )$ is not aware of it. The firm spends $s \geq 0$ on SMM, and consumers can observe the firm’s SMM spending. As is the case with traditional advertising, a firm’s SMM spending increases product awareness among previously unaware consumers $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the 1 � r fraction of them). The fraction of initially unaware consumers who become aware of the product because of SMM spending s is equal to $f ( s ) = s / ( 1 + s )$ Therefore, the total fraction of consumers who become aware of the product in response to a firm’s SMM spending s is given by

$$
r + (1 - r) \frac {s}{1 + s}.\tag{1}
$$

Consumers who are aware of the product are not fully informed about its quality. Through online word of mouth or third-party reviews, they receive a noisy external information signal that imperfectly informs them about the product’s quality. The term $\dot { \lambda } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ is used to denote the precision of that external information signal. That is, the external information signal is the same as (respectively, different from) the product’s true quality with probability λ (respectively, $1 - \lambda )$ The information signal is perfectly informative when $\lambda = 1 . ^ { 1 }$

Social media marketing differs from traditional advertising in that it also has an information revelation role of increasing the external information signal’s precision. After the firm spends s on SMM, the precision λ of the external information signal increases to

$$
\lambda^ {\prime} = \lambda + (1 - \lambda) \frac {s}{1 + s}.\tag{2}
$$

Receiving an external information signal of precision $\lambda ^ { \prime }$ and observing the firm’s SMM spending s and product price $p ,$ consumers update their belief about the true product quality. Let $\mu ( q | s , p , \lambda ^ { \prime } )$ denote consumers posterior probability that the product’s true quality is q after they receive the external signal $\lambda ^ { \prime }$ and observe the firm’s SMM spending s and price p.

## 2.2. Assumptions

We adopt the same assumptions as in Nian and Sundararajan (2022). First, $0 = c _ { L } \ : < c _ { M } < c _ { H } < 1 = L < M < H$ ensures that producing a higher-quality product is more costly, although the high-quality firm still makes a positive profit when pooling with a lower-quality firm. Second, the inequalities $( \bar { H } - c _ { H } ) ( 1 - r ) > \bar { 1 }$ and $( M -$ $c _ { M } ) ( 1 - r ) > 1$ ensure that, respectively, high- and midquality firms engage in some SMM spending under complete information. Without these conditions, all firm types would abstain entirely from SMM spending (as detailed in Nian and Sundararajan 2022, section 4.1). Third, $H - c _ { H } > M - c _ { M }$ ensures that—again, under complete information—the high-quality firm spends more on SMM than does the mid-quality firm. Fourth, $0 \leq r < 1$ guarantees that not all consumers are aware of the product, a circumstance that renders SMM spending an effective means of expanding the firm’s consumer base. Finally, $1 / 2 \le \lambda < \bar { 1 }$ ensures that the external information signal is informative (cf. Nian and Sundararajan 2022, note 8).

## 2.3. Sequence of Events and the Firm’s Profit

The firm and consumers play a sequential signaling game over two periods (see Nian and Sundararajan 2022, figure 1) as we describe next. In the first period, the firm (which knows its true product quality) determines its level of SMM spending s and product price p. In the second period, all aware customers—having received an external information signal with precision $\lambda ^ { \prime } = ( \lambda + s ) / ( 1 + s )$ and observing both the firm’s SMM spending s and its product’s price p—update their beliefs about the product’s true quality and decide whether to purchase it. Following Nian and Sundararajan (2022), we assume that the firm uses SMM spending s only to signal its quality, with the price p determined based on the chosen s. To update their beliefs about the product’s quality, consumers use two sources of information: (i) the external information signal generated by social media activities (e.g., online word of mouth, third-party reviews) and (ii) the firm’s observed pricing and SMM spending decisions.

We use backward induction to characterize the perfect Bayesian equilibrium (PBE) of the sequential game just described; thus, we first study the problem of aware consumers in the second period and then, study the firm’s problem in the first period. It should be clear that consumers who are aware of the product will pur chase it in the second period if and only if their expectation $E ( Q )$ about the product’s true quality is greater than its price ${ p . } ^ { 2 }$ In the first period, although anticipating how consumers will behave depending on its own actions and the external information signal, a firm whose product quality is $q \in \{ H , M , L \}$ chooses its price $p ( E ( Q ) )$ ) and SMM spending $s _ { q }$ so as to maximize its profit π:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi (E (Q), q; p (E (Q)), s _ {q}) \\ = \bigg [ r + (1 - r) \frac {s _ {q}}{1 + s _ {q}} \bigg ] \mathbb {1} _ {\{p (E (Q)) \leq E (Q) \}} (p (E (Q)) - c _ {q}) - s _ {q}; \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

here, $\mathbb { 1 } _ { \{ \cdot \} }$ is an indicator function set equal to one (respectively, to zero) if the condition within braces is true (respectively, false).

Finally, as in Nian and Sundararajan (2022), we characterize the subgame-perfect Bayesian equilibrium of this sequential game in pure strategies by considering three possible equilibria: separating, partial pooling, and pooling. In the separating equilibrium, high-, mid-, and low-quality firms choose different prices and SMM expenditures; hence, aware consumers can infer the firm’s type by observing its price and SMM spending. In the partial pooling equilibrium, two of the three firms pool together and choose the same price and SMM spending, whereas the third separates and chooses a different price and level of SMM spending. For the set $\Lambda$ of firms that pool, three types of partial pooling equilibria are possible: $\Lambda = \{ H , M \} , \Lambda = \{ H , L \}$ , and $\bar { \Lambda = } \{ M , L \}$ . In the event of a partial pooling equilibrium, aware consumers can infer the true quality of the firm type that separates based on its product price and SMM spending; however, an external information signal is needed to update their posterior belief about the types of firms that pool together.

In the pooling equilibrium, the high-, mid-, and lowquality firms all pool together; they choose the same price and SMM spending $( \Lambda = \{ H , \dot { M } , L \} )$ , from which it follows that aware consumers can no longer infer the true quality of a firm by observing its price and SMM spending. So, in a pooling equilibrium, aware consumers must rely on an external information signal in order to update their posterior belief about the firm’s true quality. When all three firm types pool, the likelihood of the true product quality being equal to one of the two that differ from the external information signal is $( 1 - \lambda ) / 2$ (see Nian and Sundararajan 2022, p. 555, n. 9).

## 3. Benchmark Case

Following Nian and Sundararajan (2022, section 4.2.1), we first analyze the benchmark case where SMM spending s does not play the information revelation role (i.e., the precision of quality signals does not depend on s); rather, such spending simply increases the number of consumers who are aware of the product as given by (1). So, in this benchmark case, SMM is identical to traditional advertising.

## 3.1. Separating Equilibrium

We first focus on the separating equilibrium for the benchmark case. In a separating equilibrium, the decision problem faced by a firm of quality $q \in \{ H , M , L \}$ is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {s _ {q}, p (q)} \pi (q, q; p (q), s _ {q}) \\ = \left[ r + (1 - r) \frac {s _ {q}}{1 + s _ {q}} \right] \mathbb {1} _ {\{p (q) \leq q \}} (p (q) - c _ {q}) - s _ {q} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

subject to

$$
\pi (q ^ {\prime}, q ^ {\prime}; p ^ {*} (q ^ {\prime}), s _ {q ^ {\prime}} ^ {*}) \geq \pi (q, q ^ {\prime}; p (q), s _ {q}) \quad \forall q ^ {\prime} \neq q \in \{H, M, L \},\tag{5}
$$

$$
\pi (q, q; p (q), s _ {q}) \geq \pi (q ^ {\prime}, q; p ^ {*} (q ^ {\prime}), s _ {q ^ {\prime}} ^ {*}) \quad \forall q ^ {\prime} \neq q \in \{H, M, L \}.\tag{6}
$$

In these expressions, a superscript asterisk denotes the equilibrium actions of the type $q ^ { \prime }$ firm. For $q = H ,$ the decision problem presented by $( 4 ) – ( 6 )$ is identical to the high-quality firm’s decision problem as presented by Nian and Sundararajan (2022, p. 547 in section 4.2.1). For $q = L .$ , the decision problem here is identical to the low-quality firm’s decision problem in

Nian and Sundararajan (2022) (see (B.1)–(B.5) in Online Appendix B.1), and for $q = M ,$ , it is identical to the midquality firm’s decision problem in Nian and Sundararajan (2022) (see (B.6)–(B.10) in Online Appendix B.1).

Nian and Sundararajan (2022, proposition 1) characterizes a unique separating equilibrium in which the mid-quality firm’s product price and SMM spending are given by $p ^ { * } ( M ) = M$ and $s _ { M } ^ { * } = M - 1$ , respectively, whereas the corresponding price and spending of the low-quality firm are given by $p ^ { * } ( L ) = L$ and $s _ { L } ^ { * } = 0$ . We next show that if the equalities $p ^ { * } ( M ) = M , s _ { M } ^ { * } = M - 1$ $p ^ { * } ( L ) = L ,$ , and $s _ { L } ^ { * } = 0$ all hold, as the authors claim, then the constraint in (6) for $q = M$ and $q ^ { \prime } = L$ is violated, and so, the mid-quality firm will be better off mimicking the low-quality firm. Therefore, the firm’s pricing and SMM spending described in Nian and Sundararajan (2022, proposition 1) cannot be a part of a separating equilibrium. Substituting $p ^ { * } ( M ) = M , \quad s _ { M } ^ { * } = M - 1$ $p ^ { * } ( L ) = L ,$ , and $s _ { L } ^ { * } = 0$ into (3), we obtain

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi (M, M; M, M - 1) = r - \left(1 - \frac {1 - r}{M}\right) c _ {M} \text { and } \\ \pi (L, M; L, 0) = r - r c _ {M}. \end{array}
$$

It is evident that because $M > L = 1 _ { \cdot }$ , we must have $\pi ( M , M ; M , M - 1 ) < \pi ( L , M ; L , 0 )$ . But, this inequality amounts to a violation of the constraint in (6) for $q = M$ and $q ^ { \prime } = L ,$ , which corresponds to Nian and Sundararajan (2022, constraint (B.9) in appendix B.1). So, if $p ^ { * } ( M ) = M , s _ { M } ^ { * } = M - 1 , p ^ { * } ( L ) = L ,$ and $s _ { L } ^ { * } = 0 ,$ , then the mid-quality firm would prefer to deviate and mimic the low-quality firm. It follows that the equilibrium characterized in Nian and Sundararajan (2022, proposition 1) cannot be a separating equilibrium. Indeed, our first proposition argues that there is no separating equilibrium in the benchmark case.

Proposition 1. In the benchmark case, no separating equi librium exists.

In any separating equilibrium, the low-quality firm always sets its price at $L = 1$ and spends nothing on SMM (formally, $p ^ { * } ( L ) = 1$ and $s _ { L } ^ { * } = 0 )$ . To prevent being mimicked by the low-quality firm, the mid-quality firm must invest in a higher level of SMM spending (i.e., greater than $M - 1 )$ . Yet, instead of spending more than $M - 1$ , the mid-quality firm is better off mimicking the low-quality firm: setting its price at $L = 1$ and making no SMM expenditures. For this reason and as stated in Proposition 1, no separating equilibrium will arise in which the mid-quality firm separates itself from the low-quality firm.

The literature on advertising has established that when greater ad expenditures do not increase consumer awareness, higher-quality firms spend more to signal their superior quality (see, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , Kihlstrom and Riordan 1984, Milgrom and Roberts 1986, and Sahni and Nair 2020). In contrast, Proposition 1 implies that if advertising increases consumer awareness, then a higher-quality firm should not signal its quality by spending more—that is, because the lower-quality firm would mimic such spending in order to increase awareness and thus, acquire more customers. This response would force the higher-quality firm to spend even more, ultimately making that firm worse-off than if it was perceived as being lower quality in the first place.

## 3.2. Partial Pooling Equilibrium

Next, we focus on the partial pooling equilibrium. We shall use Proposition 2 to show that in the benchmark case, the only possible partial pooling equilibrium is one in which the mid-quality and low-quality firms pool $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } \Lambda = \{ M , L \} )$ , whereas the high-quality firm separates itself. It is important to note that the PBE imposes no restrictions on beliefs about off-equilibrium paths. Proposition 2. In the benchmark case, the following statements hold.

(i) There exists no partial pooling equilibrium in which the high-quality firm pools with either the mid-quality firm or the low-quality firm.

(ii) There exists a least-cost partial pooling equilibrium— in which the mid- and low-quality firms pool by choosing the price $p _ { M L } ^ { * } = \overline { { q } } _ { M L }$ and SMM spending $s _ { M L } ^ { * } \in [ s _ { M L } ^ { 1 } , s _ { M L } ^ { 2 } ] ,$ whereas the high-quality firm separates by choosing the price $p _ { H } ^ { * } = H$ and SMM spending $s _ { H } ^ { * } ( s _ { M L } ^ { * } ) < \bar { s } _ { M L } ^ { * } .$ —if and only if

Much as in Nian and Sundararajan (2022, proposition 2), Proposition 2(i) shows (for the benchmark case) that the high-quality firm never pools with either the midor low-quality firm to form a partial pooling equilibrium—although the reasoning differs. For any SMM spending level at which the mid-quality firm mimics the high-quality firm and pools with it, the lowquality firm will always have an incentive to do the same. The reason is that the low-quality firm benefits more from mimicking the high-quality firm because of the former’s higher profit margin, which results from lower production costs. In addition, if the high- and low-quality firms pool together, then the mid-quality firm must choose a level of SMM spending that is either too low or too high to prevent mimicking. This separation from the pooling high- and low-quality firms would become too costly, which gives the mid-quality firm an incentive to mimic them.

In contrast to Nian and Sundararajan (2022, proposition 2), however, part (ii) of Proposition 2 shows that there exists (in the benchmark case) a partial pooling equilibrium where the mid- and low-quality firms pool, whereas the high-quality firm separates—provided that (a) the gap between M and L � 1 is large so that $\overline { { q } } _ { M L }$ is sufficiently high, (b) H is not too high, and (c) the proportion r of aware consumers is small. This partial pooling equilibrium arises because unless H is too high, the highquality firm opts for minimal SMM spending in order to separate itself from the mid-quality firm. This strategy results in limited consumer awareness and low demand for its product, which in turn, makes mimicking the highquality firm costly for the mid-quality firm. Additionally, as long as H is not too high, the low-quality firm similarly earns lower profits from mimicking the high-quality firm thanks to the low level of SMM spending and the consequent low demand. Yet, by pooling with the mid-quality firm, the low-quality firm can spend more on SMM and enjoy appreciably higher demand.

Proposition 2(ii) also suggests that if ad spending expands the market base by increasing the number of aware consumers but does not affect consumers perception of product quality, then lower-quality (i.e., low- and mid-quality) firms can allocate more funds to traditional marketing than do high-quality firms. This finding accords with Zhao (2000), who explores a similar setting with only two firm types; however, it contradicts the classical view of Nelson (1970, 1974) that products supported by higher advertising expenditures are of higher quality. Our finding also informs empirical research on the relationship between advertising expenditures and product quality, although there are conflicting empirical results about this relationship; Caves and Greene (1996) as well as Moorthy and Zhao (1996) identify no positive relationship between such spending and quality, but Horstmann and Moorthy (2003) report an inverted U-shaped relationship (albeit of limited robustness).

## 4.1. Separating Equilibrium

In a separating equilibrium, the firm’s true quality is fully revealed to consumers. Therefore, whether SMM serves to reveal information has no effect on the existence of a separating equilibrium. So, in the information revelation case, just as in the benchmark case, there is no separating equilibrium. This result is stated formally in our next proposition.

Proposition 5. When the SMM plays an information revelation role, there exists no separating equilibrium.

The intuition behind Proposition 5 is the same as for Proposition 1 in the benchmark case. Proposition 5 runs counter to Nian and Sundararajan (2022, proposition 3 and section 4.2.2, paragraph 2), who argue that if the SMM has an information revelation role, then there exists a unique separating equilibrium in which all three firm types choose different product prices and levels of SMM spending.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusions

Social media marketing harnesses the power of consumers connecting through social ties and mutually influencing firms (Li et al. 2021). In addition to enhancing customer acquisition through word of mouth (Trusov et al. 2009), social media marketing facilitates the dissemination of consumer-generated content—a critical firm resource (Goh et al. 2013; Kwark et al. 2014, 2017). However, it remains unclear why there is so much variability in firms’ SMM spending and how firms can effectively leverage this resource to develop successful SMM strategies.

We explored these questions by applying the gametheoretic model of Nian and Sundararajan (2022) while maintaining its original assumptions to analyze the relationship between firms’ quality and strategic SMM spending. Our analysis focused on two key functions of SMM: (i) expanding consumer awareness and (ii) information revelation—that is, enhancing the precision of external product quality information received by consumers. We challenge the findings of their paper and offer new insights along with practical implications based on our updated results.

We first analyzed a benchmark case in which SMM serves solely to increase consumer awareness, as in traditional marketing, without revealing any information about product quality. In this scenario, we find that no separating equilibrium exists; hence, firms of different qualities can spend the same amount on social media marketing. Instead, a partial pooling equilibrium may arise where the mid- and low-quality firms spend more on marketing, whereas the high-quality firm spends less in order to differentiate itself. We also identify pooling equilibria in which all firms spend identical amounts on marketing; these equilibria always exist and satisfy the Cho and Kreps (1987) intuitive criterion. Our findings suggest that if advertising simply enhances consumer awareness, then (a) marketing expenditures might not be an effective signal of product quality, and (b) the relationship between these two factors need not be monotonic. In contrast to prior studies that show high-quality firms spending more on “dissipative” advertising to signal quality (Nelson 1974, Kihlstrom and Riordan 1984, Milgrom and Rob erts 1986, Sahni and Nair 2020), we found that if advertising improves awareness (i.e., is not purely dissipative), then firms of different quality levels may struggle to separate themselves via signaling. This outcome holds in particular when high-quality firms are less efficient and/or multiple quality types exist because separation is less profitable for a high-quality firm with lower production efficiency. Our analyses provide practical insights and offer alternative explanations for the diversity observed in traditional marketing strategies among firms of different quality levels (Caves and Greene 1996, Moorthy and Zhao 1996, Horstmann and Moorthy 2003).

We then examined the case where social media marketing not only increases consumer awareness but also, enhances the precision of external signals about product quality—that is, SMM’s information revelation role. Our analysis established that just as in the benchmark case, a separating equilibrium does not emerge. The information revelation role of SMM does not change this outcome vis-a\`-vis traditional marketing because consumers are already aware of the firms’ quality levels. Therefore, additional information makes it no easier for firms to separate. This finding challenges the conventional belief that SMM’s information revelation role facilitates separation among firms of different quality levels.

Furthermore, we discovered that if SMM plays an information revelation role, then the high-quality firm can pool with both the mid-quality and low-quality firms but never with only one of them. The only partial pooling equilibrium that can emerge under SMM with information revelation is one where the mid- and lowquality firms pool together by spending more on SMM, whereas the high-quality firm signals its type by spending much less. So, for all equilibria in the case of SMM with information revelation, the high-quality firm consistently spends (weakly) less on SMM than firms of lower quality; thus, there is no inverted U-shaped relationship between a firm’s SMM spending and its quality. We, therefore, conclude, contrary to Nian and Sundararajan (2022), that the information revelation role of SMM cannot itself explain the inverted U-shaped relationship—observed in practice—between quality and marketing expenditures. Our result also diverges from the countersignaling literature, which shows that a high-quality firm typically spends less on advertising to pool with or mimic a low-quality firm (see, e.g., Feltovich et al. 2002 and Mayzlin and Shin 2011). However, our finding is consistent with empirical evidence showing that high-quality firms often spend less on SMM (see, e.g., Hollenbeck et al. 2019) and thus, provides an alternative explanation for why brands like Apple and Tesla abstain from SMM, whereas their lower-quality competitors invest in it (Gasbara 2018, Hanley 2019).

Finally, our research contributes to the literature on SMM and its practical implications. Existing studies focus primarily on the effects of consumer word-of-mouth interactions or product reputation on the signaling power of ad spending (Joshi and Musalem 2021, Pan and Feng 2024). They also examine the differing roles of celebrities and microinfluencers in endorsing products of various quality (Nistor and Selove 2024) as well as the interaction between consumer reviews and the signaling function of product pricing (Chen et al. 2025). In contrast, we highlight the dual roles of SMM: expanding consumer awareness and revealing product quality information. Our analysis explores how the information revelation role of SMM affects firms’ strategic SMM spending. We find that this role complicates the ability of high-quality firms to distinguish themselves from lower-quality competitors, often leading to a more opaque market outcome in which firms of all quality levels converge on the same pricing and SMM spending strategies. In sum, the qualitysignaling function of SMM spending is weakened by its information revelation role. Our results yield valuable predictions and suggest potentially fruitful avenues for future empirical research.
