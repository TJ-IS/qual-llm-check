# Beyond Complements and Substitutes: A Graph Neural Network Approach for Collaborative Retail Sales Forecasting

Abstract. Relation-empowered retail management has gained increasing attention. Although evidence on enhanced retail sales forecasting (RSF) for a focal product by leveraging information on related products has been acknowledged, prior studies suffer from high risk of either erroneously introducing irrelevant relations or missing informative ones, as well as incompetence to simultaneously tackle multifaceted and complex product relations. Beyond the well-known relations of complements and substitutes, on the ground of the cross-category choice dependence theory, we discern product relations along both relation type and temporal dimensions: positive and negative relations (from the relation type perspective, with the characteristics of indirectness and asymmetry) as well as asynchronous and dynamic relations (from the temporal perspective). Revolving around how to identify inherent or recurring related products precisely and comprehensively, how to simultaneously leverage indirect, asymmetric, positive, and negative product relations, and how to leverage asynchronous and dynamic product relations, we propose a graph neural network-based method named CL4RSF with a novel data-driven product relation identification strategy and capability of incorporating all above-mentioned product relations. In addition, to further adapt to the RSF context, we design an end-to-end deeplearning architecture equipped with capabilities of multistep forecasting and multisource information fusion. Empirical evaluation on two real-world retail data sets demonstrates the superior forecasting performance of our proposed end-to-end method over state-ofthe-art benchmarks and verifies the utility of key designed components in CL4RSF as well as that of leveraging diverse product relations. Further explanatory analyses render insights into cross-category effects and various inferred product relations.

## 1. Introduction

Relations among various participants, such as collaboration with supply chain partners (Aviv 2007, Dong et al. 2014), cannibalization and complementarity in multichannel retailing (Avery et al. 2009), and spatial cannibalization effect across adjacent locations (Glaeser et al. 2019, Ergin et al. 2022), are ubiquitous and indispensable for effective retail management. In addition to these relation-empowered retail scenarios, exploring product relations also holds significant importance for various marketing and operations management activities, such as recommendation, cross-selling, assortment planning, and shelf space allocation. In this study, we focus on exploring information regarding other products related to a focal one for collaborative retail sales forecasting (RSF), which establishes a groundwork for a variety of managerial decision-making scenarios, such as production, purchasing, marketing, financial and workforce planning, and inventory management (Huang et al. 2019, Fildes et al. 2022b).

Product relations have traditionally been perceived mainly in terms of complements and substitutes.

Complements (e.g., cellphones and cellphone cases) are products frequently used together to provide an enhanced level of overall utility, whereas substitutes (e.g., Apple iPhone and Huawei Mate) are interchangeable products with similar or comparable functions. In economics, if raising (or lowering) the price of one product leads to an increase in the sales of another, then the two products are deemed substitutes (or complements) (Mas-Colell et al. 1995). For two complements, increased sales for one are generally associated with increased sales for the other (Kotler and Armstrong 2010), exhibiting a positive relation. By contrast, two substitutes typically show a negative relation in their sales (Zhang and Bockstedt 2020).

However, the complements/substitutes dichotomy cannot fully cover plausible product relations that are potentially beneficial for collaborative RSF. Whether a relationship, in terms of sales, exists between products is determined not solely by their inherent properties but also by consumers’ preferences (Dhar et al. 2014). For example, two products, just because of their close display on a shelf in a brick-and-mortar store, may be purchased together by many customers. In this sense, although they are not inherent complements, their sales may exhibit a positive relation. Therefore, for collaborative RSF, in addition to inherently related products, those with frequently similar or opposite<sup>1</sup> patterns in sales, which may result from complex external drivers influencing consumers’ purchasing choices, should not be neglected. In this regard, we strive to consistently identify inherent or recurring product relations and guard against coincidental and transient ones. On the one hand, for example, phones and phone cases are inherently related. Their relations should be inferred consistently during the identification process. On the other hand, for example, for two products that concurrently undergo distinct promotional campaigns, the occurrence of similar fluctuations in their sales is merely coincidental. In this vein, inferring a relationship between them should be discouraged.

Identifying inherent or recurring product relations is an imminent yet challenging prerequisite for collaborative RSF. Extant efforts are prone to either erroneously introducing irrelevant relations or missing informative ones. Several past studies have assumed that products belonging to the same category (Bi et al. 2022) or co-viewed online (McAuley et al. 2015a) are substitutes. However, a recent study (Kwark et al. 2021) found conflicting evidence regarding these assumptions; among co-viewed product pairs, 47.23% in the home category and 20% in the technology category were unrelated. Incorporating information from unrelated products will likely incur predictive bias and hurt RSF performance on a focal product because of the introduction of excessive noise. Another widely used way of related product identification is to investigate similar products based on product attributes (Ma 2024, Voleti et al. 2015). However, it is generally impossible to ascertain all influential attributes. Similarity across products may be gauged along various dimensions, including not only observable attributes (e.g., price, brand (Huang et al. 2014, Borah and Tellis 2016), and product characteristics) but also latent ones (e.g., perceived quality, response to promotions, and consumption context) (Voleti et al. 2015). In this vein, seemingly unrelated yet actually related products (e.g., diapers and beer) may be overlooked, inevitably missing informative cues and thus impeding their contribution to collaborative RSF. In summary, effective collaborative RSF necessitates precise and comprehensive identification<sup>2</sup> of inherent or recurring product relations. Hence, we delve into our first research question (RQ1): how to consistently identify inherent or recurring product relations as precisely and comprehensively as possible?

In response to RQ1, we design a product relation learner (PRL) that identifies related products in a data-driven learning manner. PRL dispenses with the unrealistic assumptions involved in category-based and copurchase-based (co-view-based) strategies and bypasses the impractical need of explicitly determining all influential attributes. Moreover, PRL is guided and supervised by sales, a direct and externalized indicator of various intrinsic and external factors’ interplay, in contrast to structured features (e.g., category and brand), textual reviews, and customer behavior (e.g., co-view and copurchase), and is expected to identify related products more precisely and comprehensively than prior efforts. Furthermore, we delegate our preferences for consistent identification of inherent or recurring product relations to the design of loss terms that guide the PRL process. Specifically, we design a graph robustness loss to encourage consistent identification of related products and a graph contrastive loss to discourage capturing coincidental and transient relations. The graph contrastive loss will impose a large penalty on the connection of two products whose sales patterns are neither similar nor opposite in most time periods such that only products with frequent occurrences of similar or opposite sales patterns tend to be inferred as being related.

Following the identification of related products, another challenging problem is to disentangle the relations and then leverage them appropriately for collaborative RSF. Given that consumer choice indeed impacts aggregated sales (Jiang et al. 2024), we draw on the cross-category choice dependence theory (CCCDT) (Russell et al. 1999, Shocker et al. 2004) and disentangle product relations along both relation type and temporal dimensions beyond complements and substitutes. From the relation type perspective, product bundling and crosscategory consideration in CCCDT imply positive and negative product relations, respectively, including but not limited to complements and substitutes. Moreover, these positive or negative relations may be indirect (Ogaki 1990) (e.g., computer → printer → printing paper) and asymmetric (Lee et al. 2013, Voleti et al. 2015, Gelper et al. 2016) (e.g., main product and add-on). From the temporal perspective, cross-category learning in CCCDT conveys asynchronous influences. For example, a customer who is satisfied with a previously purchased Xiaomi phone and has perceived the quality of this brand is likely to purchase a Xiaomi robotic vacuum cleaner (i.e., brand spillover effect). In addition, product relations are dynamic in terms of both relation strength and type (Shocker et al. 2004). On the one hand, relation strength presents dynamicity. For example, with the development of technology and improvement of infrastructure (e.g., charging stations), the competitiveness of electric cars relative to traditional fuel-powered cars has been increasing. On the other hand, relation type may also change. For example, products previously perceived as complements, such as camcorders and smartphones, may transition into substitutes.<sup>3</sup> Multifaceted influences from other related products can be concurrently exerted on the sales of a focal product. Therefore, drawing on CCCDT, we endeavor to simultaneously leverage various product relations, including positive, negative, indirect, asymmetric, asynchronous, and dynamic ones, for collaborative RSF. Hence, we delve into our second research question (RQ2): how to simultaneously leverage the multifaceted and complex product relations, disentangled from the relation type and temporal perspectives, for collaborative RSF?

To address RQ2, given the need to model indirect product relations, we adopt a graph neural network (GNN) approach. However, existing GNN methods are incompetent in leveraging certain product relations (especially negative and asynchronous ones) as well as in simultaneously accommodating all the multifaceted and complex relations. Hence, we tailor an RSF-specific GNN method to leverage information from other related products to potentially enhance sales forecasting for a focal product. Particularly, first, it is imperative to differentially process information from positively and negatively related products; the focal product should overlay the effects of positively related products onto itself but deduct those of negatively related products. To achieve this goal, and meanwhile taking the asymmetry of relations into account, we design a directionapplicable and relation-adaptive information interactor (DRAI). Second, to simultaneously leverage synchronous and asynchronous relations, we extend PRL into a dual-channel PRL (DC-PRL) and design a synchronous and asynchronous-aware temporal encoder (SATE).

Overall, in this computational design research (Rai 2017, Abbasi et al. 2024), on the basis of the discernment of various product relations drawing on a theoretical underpinning (i.e., CCCDT), we propose a theoryguided design (Abbasi et al. 2024) of a novel GNN method named CL4RSF (Collaborative Learning for RSF) with three main modules: SATE, DC-PRL, and DRAI. In addition, we consolidate CL4RSF and other influential factors within an end-to-end deep-learning architecture named MS2RSF (Multi-Source Multi-Step RSF) with capabilities of multisource information fusion and multistep forecasting. In experiments on two retail data sets, our end-to-end method outperformed Facebook’s Prophet (Taylor and Letham 2018), Amazon’s DeepAR (Salinas et al. 2020), and other state-of-the-art benchmarks. Experiment results also show the utility of key designed components in CL4RSF as well as that of leveraging diverse product relations for collaborative RSF. Further explanatory analyses render insights into cross-category effects and various identified product relations.

## 2. Literature Review

The information systems (IS) discipline has a rich history of networks and customer analytics-oriented design. Dhar et al. (2014) used a co-purchase-based product network to predict products’ future demands. On a signed word network developed according to syntax and semantic information, Lee and Ram (2024) proposed a structural balance theory-based false information identification artifact by measuring the consistency between a claim and its evidence. Liu et al. (2020b) used an interfirm network of human capital flow and proposed a human capital overlap metric to predict labor market competition. Leveraging the relationship marketing theory, Kitchens et al. (2018) developed a framework incorporating a variety of relationshiporiented data to enable advanced customer analytics. Conforming to customers’ browsing behaviors, Chen et al. (2024) leveraged four types of customer attentions paid at varying review granularity levels for enhanced sales prediction. In this study, we draw on a consumer choice theory, that is, CCCDT, to provide theoretical justifications for why our approach simultaneously leverages positive and negative product relations as well as asynchronous and dynamic relations. Subsequently, we strive to learn product networks and effectively leverage the above-mentioned product relations for collaborative RSF.

## 2.1. Retail Sales Forecasting

Researchers have well recognized the value of predictive models in operations management (Misˇic ´ and Perakis 2020). Prominent companies also spare no effort to develop advanced forecasting methods, such as Facebook’s Prophet (Taylor and Letham 2018) and Amazon’s DeepAR (Salinas et al. 2020). Extensive efforts have been devoted to building accurate RSF models with an emphasis on adopting advanced methods and exploring informative variables.

From the methodology perspective, methods investigated include statistical, traditional machine-learning (ML), and deep-learning ones. Whereas statistical methods, for example, the autoregressive (AR) model and its variants, merely utilize historical sales data, evidence on the superiority of ML methods over statistical ones is abundant (Fildes et al. 2022a). ML methods generally formulate RSF as a regression task, performing feature engineering based on historical data and influential factors and then feeding the handcrafted features into various algorithms, such as support vector regression (Huang et al. 2019), random forest (Cui et al. 2018), and LightGBM (Makridakis et al. 2022). More recently, deep learning has reshaped the landscape of RSF with datadriven feature learning (Ma and Fildes 2021, Ma 2024).

From the informative variable perspective, exogenous variables typically explored include calendar events (Huber and Stuckenschmidt 2020), weather (Steinker et al. 2017), consumer review content (Liu et al. 2019), and social media data (Cui et al. 2018). In addition, as an unneglectable factor, the impact of other related products’ information on the sales of a focal product has been acknowledged (Jabr and Zheng 2014, Voleti et al. 2015, Ma et al. 2016). We focus our review of this line of research on finding related entities and incorporating others’ information for RSF, as summarized in Table A.1 (Online Appendix A).

For finding related entities, a plethora of studies have indiscriminately incorporated all others (Bandyopadhyay 2009, Gelper et al. 2016, Wilms et al. 2018). Moreover, some studies have imposed strong assumptions, for example, assuming that substitutive or competitive effect holds between products belonging to the same category (Bi et al. 2022), sharing the same nameplate or brand (Huang et al. 2014, Borah and Tellis 2016), or emanating from recommendation (Jabr and Zheng 2014). Other studies have assumed spatial cannibalization effect across adjacent locations (Glaeser et al. 2019, Ergin et al. 2022), explored the complementary effect between base products and add-ons (Huang et al. 2019), computed similarity according to observable attributes (Voleti et al. 2015, Ma 2024), relied on co-purchase data (Dhar et al. 2014), leveraged LASSO Granger causality test (Ma et al. 2016), or resorted to demand correlations (Chen et al. 2023c). In addition, Lei et al. (2024) investigated product-category hierarchical relationships and leveraged category sales information to aid individual product demand prediction.

We also briefly review studies on inferring or leveraging substitutes or complements, although they were not geared toward RSF. Shopper (Ruiz et al. 2020), a sequential probabilistic model using fine-grained basket data, estimates latent factors on various aspects, such as item features, laying a foundation for inferring complementary and substitutable products. Jiang et al. (2024) estimated substitution patterns by developing a choice model with consumer clickstream data. Some research on recommendation has formulated the task of inferring complements or substitutes as link prediction (McAuley et al. 2015a, Liu et al. 2020a), which is supervised by labels obtained with the assumption that co-viewed (copurchased) products are deemed substitutes (complements). Regarding modeling and leveraging positive and negative relations, prior GNN methods were performed on either separate substitutable graphs and complementary graphs (Zhang et al. 2021a, Wu et al. 2024) or a heterogeneous graph with different edge types (Chen et al. 2023a). Kwark et al. (2021), with crowdsourcing labeling, built a supervised learning model that leverages product characteristics and textbased similarity measures. Another line of research per tinent to inferring substitutable products mined product competition from customer favorite data (Liu et al. 2020c), Web search logs (Wang et al. 2021), or review texts (Qian et al. 2023).

To incorporate others’ information for RSF, models that dominated this stream of research encoded others information into explanatory variables and then employed VAR (vector autoregression) (Borah and Tellis 2016, Wilms et al. 2018) and its variants (e.g., sparse VAR (Gelper et al. 2016) and BVAR (Bandyopadhyay 2009)). However, the predominantly used VAR and its variants fail to capture nonlinear dependency and suffer from high risk of overparameterization and resultant overfitting because of the quadratically increasing number of parameters with regard to the number of related products. Recently, ML and compu tational design science have gained increasing attention. Several studies have applied support vector regression (Glaeser et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2023c). Driven by sales competition among stores in the same region, Bi et al. (2022) proposed a design artifact using tensor factorization. Ma (2024) used a conventional gated GNN method to investigate cross-item interactions.

## 2.2. Relation-Empowered Time Series Forecasting

We consider collaborative RSF as a multivariate time series forecasting task and thus review existing studies related to relation-empowered time series forecasting (Re-TSF), which may shed light on the collaborative RSF task. We categorize existing relevant studies according to the relations captured: heterogeneous relations or homogeneous relations. Heterogeneous relations are inter dependencies between time series of different entity types. For example, Zhu et al. (2021a) captured crossaxial dependencies between human and object motion sensors. More generally, dependencies between the target time series (e.g., sales series) and influential factors (e.g., weather) are heterogeneous. Homogeneous relations are interdependencies between time series of the same entity type, such as sales series of two products (i.e., the focus of this study). Table A.2 (Online Appendix A) summarizes prior research on Re-TSF.

To address heterogeneous relations, a convolutional neural network (CNN) has been used to permutate input series as a grid (Zhu et al. 2021a). To explore homogeneous relations, prior studies have investigated matrix factorization (MF) (Yu et al. 2016, Sen et al. 2019) and the recently flourishing GNN, which has considerably advanced Re-TSF. GNN has achieved compelling performance in various tasks, such as stock prediction (Chen et al. 2018, Feng et al. 2019, Hsu et al. 2021), traffic flow prediction (Lv et al. 2020), and passenger demand prediction (Bai et al. 2021). In the financial domain, GNN can be employed on predefined graphs, such as financial investment (Chen et al. 2018), intra-sector stock (Feng et al. 2019, Hsu et al. 2021), and company supplier-customer (Feng et al. 2019) graphs. When a concretely specified graph is unavailable, previous studies have inferred a graph according to correlation of historical data (Lv et al. 2020, Bai et al. 2021). Recently, directly learning graphs based on learnable node embeddings has attracted increasing attention (Chen et al. 2023b, Cai et al. 2024).

## 2.3. Research Gap to be Filled in This Study

Our review of prior studies reveals their limitations for effective collaborative RSF. First, effectively identifying related products remains an open problem. Although Re-TSF and other studies pertaining to inferring substitutes or complements provide instrumental insights, they do not fit the collaborative RSF context seamlessly. Existing efforts suffered mainly from capturing irrelevant relations (deemed noise) and missing informative ones. In category-level studies, incorporating all other categories (Bandyopadhyay 2009, Gelper et al. 2016, Wilms et al. 2018) inevitably introduces excessive noise. The strategy that finds products belonging to the same category, nameplate, or brand (Huang et al. 2014, Borah and Tellis 2016, Bi et al. 2022) may overlook crosscategory effects, and products belonging to the same category are not necessarily related (Kwark et al. 2021). The strategy using behavior data (e.g., co-purchase and co-view) (Dhar et al. 2014, McAuley et al. 2015a, Liu et al. 2020a) is prone to noise inclusion because co-purchased or co-viewed products are not bound to be related, and additionally, it overlooks asynchronously related products. The Shopper method (Ruiz et al. 2020), using fine-grained basket data, overlooks asynchronous relations. The strategy relying on attribute-based similarity (Ma 2024) generally fails to capture positively related products. More importantly, it is challenging or even impossible to explicitly express and quantify all driving attributes. The strategy investigating short-term sales correlations (Lv et al. 2020, Bai et al. 2021, Chen et al. 2023c) is susceptible to coincidentally similar patterns and lacks robustness. Conventional graph structure learning (GSL) (Chen et al. 2023b, Cai et al. 2024) is far from optimal because of the lack of effective guidance for learning.

Second, existing methods cannot adequately deal with multifaceted and complex product relations. VARfamily (Borah and Tellis 2016, Wilms et al. 2018) and ML-based (Glaeser et al. 2019, Huang et al. 2019) methods in general explicitly treat related products’ information as external variables, thus falling short in capturing indirect, asynchronous, and dynamic product relations. MF-based methods (Sen et al. 2019, Bi et al. 2022) generally lack the expressive capability for indirect, asynchronous, and asymmetric relations. Existing Re-TSF GNN methods typically ignore the exploration of negative and asynchronous relations. Other extant GNN methods, despite having demonstrated superiority in diverse domains, cannot effectively leverage negative and asyn chronous relations for collaborative RSF, either. Fo example, in terms of simultaneously leveraging positive and negative relations, GNN methods performed on separate graphs generally fail to capture high-order relations like “the enemy of my enemy is my friend.” Those with reliance on edge types generally endeavor to explore balanced paths to leverage high-order rela tions. However, the prevalent unbalanced paths in retail data sets render these balanced path-based methods, such as the signed graph convolutional network (SGCN) (Derr et al. 2018), incompetent for collaborative RSF (see Online Appendix B).

Although GNN has achieved tremendous success, it is still largely underinvestigated in the context of collaborative RSF, as evidenced by the two latest RSF studies (Lei et al. 2024, Ma 2024) that respectively employed XGBoost and a conventional gated GNN method. This gap motivates us to build a bridge between the managerial problem and advanced deep-learning methods. Characteristics of the collaborative RSF problem entail specially designed solutions—the objective of this study.

## 3. Design Rationales, Methodological Challenges, and Proposed Solutions 3.1. Design Rationales

A sound design rationale is important for establishing a design science contribution (Gregor and Hevner 2013). Effective abstraction through, for example, the use of theory-guided design “is the key to ensuring a healthy shelf life of IS design research papers in the highvelocity era of AI” (Abbasi et al. 2024). We draw on CCCDT to guide the design of CL4RSF (Walls et al. 1992), as shown in Figure 1. Our design starts with CCCDT (Russell et al. 1999, Shocker et al. 2004), which frames the conceptual characterization of product relations in the retail domain. Grounded on this theory, we disentangle and reorganize product relations along both relation type and temporal dimensions. On the one hand, positive and negative relations may exist between retail products from the relation type perspective. On the other hand, product relations may exhibit asynchrony and dynamicity from the temporal perspective. To identify and leverage these relations, we determine three meta-requirements (i.e., Req1, Req2-1, and Req2-2) revolving around how to identify related products robustly and effectively and how to accommodate characteristics of CCCDT-informed product relations. Accordingly, we provide meta-designs, ultimately producing an artifact (i.e., CL4RSF).

Beyond complements and substitutes, there exist other product relations that do not fit into this dichotomy. For example, the story of “diapers and beer” shows a positive relation between the two seemingly unrelated products (Ke and Wang 2022). CCCDT (Russell et al. 1999) offers a broader taxonomy. It identifies three types of cross-category effects: cross-category consideration, product bundling, and cross-category learning (Russell et al. 1999, Shocker et al. 2004). Crosscategory consideration means forming one choice outcome from several alternative product categories (and options or brands in each category) that fulfill consumption purposes (Srivastava et al. 1984). In product bundling, bundles are assembled products that jointly contribute to meeting customers’ needs (e.g., convenience by being accessible from nearby shelf spaces in brick-and-mortar retailing). The story of “diapers and beer” falls into this type. Cross-category learning suggests that cross-product effects can be induced by previous purchases, meaning that the choice of a product can be affected by prior experience with, use of, or possession of other products. For cross-category consideration and product bundling, customers generally make their purchasing decision on a single or proximate shopping occasions, whereas cross-category learning takes into account sequential purchases and temporally expanding bundles (Shocker et al. 2004). Moreover, beyond static relations, Shocker et al. (2004) extended the product dependency framework by incorporating dynamic relations in terms of both relation type and strength. It is evident that customers’ choice decisions impact downstream sales (Jiang et al. 2024). To determine product relations potentially beneficial for collaborative RSF, we reorganize these cross-category effects informed by CCCDT from the relation type and temporal perspectives.

(1) From the relation type perspective. Conforming to product bundling and cross-category consideration, the sales of other related products and those of a focal product can be positively or negatively related. In addition, product relations may be indirect (Ogaki 1990). Here is an illuminating example, offered in Samuelson (1974)

“ … sometimes I like tea and cream … I also sometimes take cream with my coffee. Before you agree that cream is therefore a complement to both tea and coffee, I should mention that I take much less cream in my cup of coffe than I do in my cup of tea. Therefore, a reduction in the price of coffee may reduce my demand for cream, which is an odd thing to happen between so-called complements.”

In this example, coffee and cream, despite being direct complements, are considered indirect substitutes in relation to tea; a reduction in the price of coffee leads to reduced demand for tea because tea and coffee are sub stitutes, consequently resulting in reduced demand for cream. Another example in an online e-commerce scenario is as follows. Assume that book B is recommended when viewing book A (i.e., book A and book B are directly related) and book C is recommended when viewing book B (i.e., book B and book C are directly related). Consequently, book C and book A, although not directly appearing in each other’s recommendation list, are indirectly related (Jabr and Zheng 2014).

Moreover, extensive research has pinpointed the asymmetry of product relations (see, e.g., Lee et al. 2013, Voleti et al. 2015, and Gelper et al. 2016); that is, one product can be more dependent on the other (Lee et al. 2013). For example, the sales of a base (main) product generally positively affect those of an add-on (secondary) product (Huang et al. 2019), whereas there may be little effect in the reverse direction.

(2) From the temporal perspective. Cross-category learning in CCCDT conveys that the sales of previously purchased other products may exert asynchronous impacts on the sales of a focal product, meaning that the impacts do not occur immediately. For example, consider a scenario where a customer purchases a product from a brand and, following a period of usage, perceives the merits of this brand. This experience is likely to foster a predisposition for the customer to purchase other products from the same brand. This spillover effect across products from the same brand elucidates one of the possible asynchronous influences between products.

In terms of relation dynamicity, on the one hand, with respect to relation type, transition between complements and substitutes may occur. Complements can become substitutes (e.g., camcorders and smartphones), whereas products initially conceived as imperfect substitutes may eventually coexist as complements (e.g., a customer uses two mobile phones, with one serving as a backup). On the other hand, the strengths of product relations are time-varying, owing to the dynamic market environment.

## 3.2. Methodological Challenges and Proposed Solutions

Requirements induced by the multifaceted and complex product relations for collaborative RSF pose great methodological challenges, necessitating specialized solution design (see Figure 2 and Table 1). Beyond challenges arising from dealing with individual relations (even the underinvestigated negative or asynchronous relations), it is more challenging to simultaneously rather than individually accommodate indirect, asymmetric, positive, negative, asynchronous, and dynamic relations. For example, when conditioned on our proposed solution resorting to spectral signals to model positive and negative relations, simultaneously modeling asymmetric relations, which individually have been frequently addressed in GNN modeling, introduces a new challenge. As another example, simultaneously dealing with synchronous and asynchronous relations is nontrivial because typically each product is represented as a node in the product graph and assigned one embedding. Our detailed design begins with handling indirect relations (Ogaki 1990). Considering the inherent strength of GNN methods in expressing indirect relations, we adopt GNN as our overarching architecture. Under this premise, following the standard pipeline for time series forecasting using GNN, we inject certain innovative elements designed specifically for collaborative RSF into each module along the pipeline.

In response to Req1, to overcome limitations of extant efforts pertaining to RSF, we design a PRL with GSL. The GSL strategy, which has drawn wide attention in the ML community (Deng et al. 2020, Wu et al. 2020), aims to infer the edge connectivity of a graph with learnable parameters. For simplicity and capability of modeling asymmetric relations, we focus on learning a kNN (k-nearest neighbor) graph based on cosine similarity. However, existing GSL methods, generally without any constraint, may be suboptimal for consistently identify ing inherent or recurring product relations. To resolve this issue, we impose certain constraints, thereby contributing a robust and effective GSL. On the one hand, to pursue consistent identification of related products, we strive to encourage the top k similar nodes for each node to be consistent during the learning process and across multiple experiments, thereby reducing the chance to capture volatile neighbors. Accordingly, we design a graph robustness loss, which encourages the dis tribution of each node’s similarity with other nodes to deviate from the uniform distribution. On the other hand, to identify inherent or recurring product relations, thus avoiding introducing excessive noise (see Online Appendix C.1 for adverse effects of noise on collaborative RSF performance), we strive to enforce adjacent nodes to be similar or opposite and nonconnected nodes to be neither similar nor opposite, thereby encouraging products with frequent occurrences of similar or opposite sales patterns to be connected. Contrastive learning, which focuses on pushing forward positive pairs and pushing away negative pairs (Hadsell et al. 2006), fulfills our need and hence, is adopted to guide our design of a graph contrastive loss. Despite the maturity of the contrastive learning-based objective (Wu et al. 2021), we need to modify it to account for opposite sales patterns via refining the similarity measure (see Section 4.3.2). This modification, albeit subtle, is important for a rigorous design to ensure proper adaptation to the retail domain. By tuning the weight of the graph contrastive loss, our method can adapt to specific characteristics of different data sets, balancing between noise and informative cues when accounting for similar or opposite sales patterns.

In response to Req2-1, product relations stemming from the relation type perspective entail a computational model equipped with the ability to differentially leverage positive and negative relations, meanwhile tak ing indirect and asymmetric relations into account.

However, we encounter significant methodological challenges in this regard. First, conventional GCN (graph convolutional network), with its nature of lowpass filter (Balcilar et al. 2021), can effectively explore positive relations but not negative ones because oscillating signals (i.e., high-frequency signals) are likely to be filtered out and thus ignored. To tackle this challenge, we design a strategy that maps positive and negative relations to low- and high-frequency signals, respectively. The strategy aims to pass low-frequency information to positively related products and high-frequency information to negatively related ones.<sup>4</sup> As compared with extant GNN methods that use different edge types to differentiate positive and negative relations, our spectral-based method, by innovating a new perspective, avoids the exacerbated complexity for GSL. More importantly, it circumvents the unbalanced path issue, which is a bottleneck hindering the direct application of SGCN (Derr et al. 2018) to the collaborative RSF context. Second, on the premise of designing a spectral-based GNN method to capture low- and high-frequency signals, its mathematical foundation requires a symmetric matrix, hindering the direct application of existing spectral-based GNN methods (e.g., frequency adaptation graph convolutional network (FAGCN) (Bo et al. 2021)) to the collaborative RSF context, which requires the accommodation of asymmetric product relations. In response to Req2-2, product relations stemming from the temporal perspective entail a computational model equipped with the ability to deal with asynchronous and dynamic relations. How to simultaneously leverage synchronous and asynchronous relations is the first challenge because typically each product is represented as a node in the product graph and assigned one embedding. To address this challenge, we design a dual-channel PRL, with one channel for synchronous relations signified as a synchronous graph and the othe for asynchronous ones signified as an asynchronous graph. In the asynchronous graph, we design a virtual node mechanism; that is, each product is separately assigned two nodes, flagged with “previous\_” and “current\_”. Subsequently, asynchronous temporal patterns of the “previous $\mathbf { \delta } _ { - } ^ { B ^ { \prime \prime } }$ node in this example should be appropriately expressed. To tackle this issue, we generate multiple preceding time intervals in a sliding window manner. Given the varying importance of these preceding time intervals, we adopt the attention mechanism to adaptively assign them different weights. In this vein, we contribute a novel approach incorporating a new information source (i.e., asynchronously related products). To model dynamic relations, in terms of both relation type and strength, we use an adapted attention activation function to support attention scores in the range of [�1,1]. This strategy can yield time-variant type impact weights, thus facilitating the identification of the overriding relation type during a certain period. ## 4. Proposed Method

The theoretical rationales and requirements (Table 1) motivate us to design a GNN-based deep-learning method (i.e., CL4RSF) specifically for collaborative RSF. We consolidate our proposed solutions and form three main modules: SATE, DC-PRL, and DRAI.

## 4.1. Overview of CL4RSF

Figure 3 outlines the architecture of CL4RSF. The SATE module provides synchronous and asynchronous temporal patterns, which serve as initial product representations. To implement the dual-channel mechanism, DC-PRL learns asynchronous and synchronous product relations (presented as adjacency matrices in the GNNbased method) separately and produces an asynchronous graph and a synchronous graph, respectively. Each product has two corresponding nodes in the asynchronous graph, with the green and red nodes in Figure 3 representing “current\_” and “previous\_” flags of one product, respectively. For example, if product no. 5 asynchronously impacts product no. 1, then there will be an edge from “previous $J ^ { \prime \prime }$ (red node) to “current\_1” (green node) in the asynchronous graph. Moreover, to enhance the robustness and effectiveness of the product relation learning, we propose two novel loss terms: a graph robustness loss and a graph contrastive loss. DRAI conducts graph message propagation on the learned asynchronous and synchronous graphs separately and yields two hidden embeddings for each product correspondingly.

Subsequently, these two embeddings are fused for the downstream prediction. The parameters in all three modules are jointly trained to avoid error propagation.

To enable nodeadaptive attentions, we define $\alpha _ { u v } ^ { G } =$ tanh $( g ^ { T } [ h _ { u } | | h _ { v } ] )$ where $g \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 d _ { 1 } }$ . The design of $\alpha _ { L o w } ^ { u }$ and $\alpha _ { H i g h } ^ { u }$ enables the coexistence of positive and negative relations. The positive relation dominates if $\alpha _ { u v } ^ { G } > 0 .$ , whereas the negative relation dominates if $\alpha _ { u v } ^ { G } < 0 .$ . Therefore, we use the tanh(·) activation function to scale $\alpha _ { u v } ^ { G }$ into the range of [�1; 1], enabling adaptive determination of the overriding relation type (positive or negative). Also, besides being node-adaptive, $\alpha _ { u v } ^ { G }$ is time-sensitive because h varies over time. Therefore, benefiting from the incorporation of $\alpha _ { u v } ^ { G } ,$ our method can model dynamic product relations. The convolution operation of MagNet-based DRAI is defined as

$$
\tilde {\boldsymbol {h}} _ {u} ^ {l} = \sigma \left(\mathcal {E} \boldsymbol {h} _ {u} ^ {0} + \sum_ {v \in N e i (u)} \frac {\alpha_ {u v} ^ {G} \mathbf {A} _ {u v} \exp (\mathrm{i} \boldsymbol {\Theta} ^ {q}) _ {u v}}{\sqrt {d _ {u}} \sqrt {d _ {v}}} \tilde {\boldsymbol {h}} _ {v} ^ {l - 1}\right) (1 \leq l \leq L),\tag{16}
$$

where σ is a complex variant of ReLU(·) and is defined as $\sigma = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { x \ \frac { - \pi } { 2 } \leq a r g \left( x \right) < \frac { \pi } { 2 } , } \\ { 0 \qquad o t h e r w i s e } \end{array} \right.$ where arg (x) denotes the complex argument of $x \in \mathbb { C }$ . We unwind $\tilde { h } _ { u } ^ { l }$ into two real-valued vectors, $\tilde { h } _ { u } ^ { l \_ r e a l }$ and $\tilde { h } _ { u } ^ { l \_ i m a g }$ . The generated representation of each node at the lth layer is $\tilde { \boldsymbol { h } } _ { u } ^ { l } = \grave { \boldsymbol { W } } _ { 2 }$ $[ \tilde { h } _ { u } ^ { l _ { - } r e a l } | | \tilde { h } _ { u } ^ { l _ { - } i m a g } ]$

For the asynchronous graph, the MagNet-based DRAI is not suitable, because it can lead to information leakage (i.e., current time interval impacting previous ones). In contrast to representing asymmetric product relations in the synchronous graph, direction informa tion in the asynchronous graph conveys sequential dependency; that is, the current sales of a product are impacted by previous sales of other products. Therefore, for message passing on the asynchronous graph, we employ an alternative method, FAGCN-based DRAI (Bo et al. 2021) (see details in Online Appendix D.1).

## 4.5. The End-to-End Architecture for RSF

We further propose an end-to-end deep neural network for RSF named MS2RSF (see details in Online Appendix D.2). We adopt the mean absolute error (MAE) on produced sequences $\hat { \mathbf { Y } } _ { t } = ( \hat { \pmb { y } } _ { t + 1 } , \hat { \pmb { y } } _ { t + 2 } , \dots \hat { \pmb { y } } _ { t + h } )$ w.r.t the target sequences $( { \pmb y } _ { t + 1 } , { \pmb y } _ { t + 2 } , \dots { \pmb y } _ { t + h } )$ as the prediction loss function. The final loss function is $\begin{array} { r } { \mathcal { L } = \frac { 1 } { h } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { h } \frac { 1 } { N ^ { \prime } } \sum _ { n = 1 } ^ { N ^ { \prime } } } \end{array}$ $\left| y _ { n , t + i } - \hat { y } _ { n , t + i } \right| + \lambda _ { 1 } \mathcal { L } _ { K L } + \lambda _ { 2 } \mathcal { L } ( \mathbf { X } )$ , where $y _ { n , \ t + i } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 1 }$ and $\dot { \hat { y } } _ { n , t + i } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 1 }$ are the ground-truth and predicted value of each sample at time step $t + i ,$ , respectively, h denotes the forecasting horizon, N<sup>′</sup> is the total number of samples in each batch at each time step, and $\lambda _ { 1 }$ and $\lambda _ { 2 }$ are hyperparameters that adjust the penalty extents of the graph robustness loss $\mathcal { L } _ { K L }$ and the graph contrastive loss L(X) on the optimization objective, respectively. The loss is back propagated across the entire architecture from the decoder’s outputs.

## 5. Experiments 5.1. Data

To evaluate the effectiveness of our designed method, we have conducted a series of experiments on two retail data sets: Drugs and Grocery.<sup>5</sup> The Drugs data set was provided by a corporate collaborator, a pharmaceutical retailer. This data set contains 37,012,251 transaction records, involving 15,077 SKUs (stock keeping units) and 240 drugstores in a focal city in east China, from the period of January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2018. For empirical evaluation, we chose drugs for respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, which are prevalent in China as well as the rest of the world. Another reason for our choice of these two categories is that we would like to examine categories that seem to have no apparent interrelation with each other but are related in sales, like the story of “diapers and beer.” This data-sampling strategy is aimed at highlighting challenges of identifying subtly related products and underscoring the capability of DC-PRL in identifying such relations. In fact, there is a probability for drugs belonging to the two seemingly unrelated categories to be associated with each other in terms of sales because of some common factors, for example, weather (Lin et al. 2009). We aggregated the transaction data by week and the sales volume of each SKU across all drugstores. We selected some SKUs following two principles. (1) We chose SKUs without consecutively many (specifically, seven) zero values. (2) To ensure that all selected SKUs have the same time span, we dropped certain time steps and filtered out SKUs whose sales data are unavailable during the investigated time span. As a result, in our main experiments, we used 150 SKUs, whose mean and standard deviation of weekly sales volume are 171.38 and 76.82, respectively, with the time span from May 1, 2016, to December 30, 2018. We also considered weekly averaged temperature collected from a public website of the China Meteorological Administration (https://weather.cma.cn/).

The Grocery data set is from Corporacio´n Favorita. The raw data set involves 3,901 products sold at 54 stores located in Ecuador between January 1, 2013, and August 15, 2017. To support certain individual store-based decision-making (e.g., localized assortment planning), we aimed to experiment with sales data from a specific store. In our main experiments, we derived a data set by aggregating the weekly sales volume of the SKUs in store no. 18. We chose this store because it involves relatively large numbers of SKUs and average daily transaction records. Following the above-mentioned SKU selection principles, in the end, we selected 209 SKUs belonging to 11 product families. The mean and standard deviation of weekly sales volume are 48.73 and 21.92, respectively. The selected time span is from May 18, 2014, to August 14, 2016. Other investigated variables include promotion and holiday information.

The main reason we derived the two data sets differently is that we would like to evaluate our method in different scenarios: focused forecasting for selected product categories across all stores (in the Drugs case) and broad forecasting for all products in a particular store (in the Grocery case). For a robustness check, we also compared MS2RSF against benchmarks on the complete data sets (including all products and all stores) in both cases (see details in Online Appendix E).

We chronologically split each resulting data set into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 6:2:2.<sup>6</sup> We normalized the sales of each SKU and extraneous variables separately with the max-min normalization to avoid model deviation. Following the practice of previous studies (Sen et al. 2019, Bai et al. 2021), we rescaled predictions back to the original scale for performance evaluation.

## 5.2. Main Experiments

We conducted four main experiments. In Experiment 1, we compared our end-to-end deep-learning architec ture (i.e., MS2RSF) against 10 selected benchmarks with various forecasting horizons (i.e., h � 1, 4, 8, and 12).We first selected a statistical method, that is, (1) Facebook’s Prophet (Taylor and Letham 2018), a modular regression method with three main components, i.e., trend, seasonality, and holidays. We then selected nine stateof-the-art deep-learning methods. (2) Amazon’s DeepAR (Salinas et al. 2020) is a probabilistic forecasting model employing RNN. (3) TFT (Lim et al. 2021) is a transformer-based deep-learning architecture that uses interpretable self-attention layers to capture long-term dependencies and recurrent layers for local processing. (4) STGCN (Yu et al. 2018) is a deep-learning architecture designed for traffic forecasting. It leverages graph convolutional structure to capture spatial information and convolutional sequence structure to model temporal dependencies. To adapt it to the RSF context, we provide a similarity-based graph, in place of the original road traffic network in the traffic domain, as its input. (5) DCRNN (Li et al. 2018) uses diffusion convolution to capture spatial dependencies. (6) DeepGLO (Sen et al. 2019) is a hybrid method that captures local patterns of each time series as well as global patterns using MF. (7) MTGNN (Wu et al. 2020) learns a unidirectional graph and explores temporal and spatial dependencies between multiple time series. (8) GTS (Shang et al. 2021) signals the effectiveness of GSL for yielding improved time series forecasting performance. (9) Shopper (Ruiz et al. 2020) is a sequential probabilistic model that leverages basket data to predict customer choice and infer latent features, which can be used to identify sub stitutes and complements. To adapt it to the collaborative RSF context, we applied GAT (graph attention network), a conventional GNN method, on the product graph derived from Shopper-generated latent features (Shopper-GAT). (10) MSGNet (Cai et al. 2024) captures multiscale inter-series correlations for general-purpose multivariate time series forecasting. Among these deeplearning benchmarks, STGCN, DCRNN, DeepGLO, MTGNN, GTS, Shopper-GAT, and MSGNet account for cross-series dependencies. When implementing STGCN and DCRNN, we adopted a similarity-based graph as input, that is, the adjacency matrix’s element $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { A } _ { i j } = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { 1 , ~ s i m i l a r i t y ( x _ { i } , x _ { j } ) > \delta } \\ { 0 , \qquad \quad \mathrm { o t h e r w i s e } } \end{array} \right. } \end{array}$ , where δ is a threshold, and similarity(x<sub>i</sub>, x<sub>j</sub>) measures the cosine similarity between products i and j using raw sales data within a time window. When implementing Shopper-GAT, the adjacency matrix’s element $\mathbf { A } _ { i j } = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { 1 , \mid \pmb { \rho } _ { i } ^ { T } \pmb { \alpha } _ { j } \mid > \delta _ { 1 } } \\ { 0 , \mathrm { ~ o t h e r w i s e } } \end{array} \right.$ where $\delta _ { 1 }$ is a threshold, $\pmb { \rho } _ { i }$ represents inferred coefficients of product i, and $\pmb { \alpha } _ { j }$ represents inferred latent attributes of product j. For the probabilistic forecasting model (i.e., DeepAR), we used the median value as the point forecasting.

In Experiments 2 � 4, we examined the utility of key designed components in CL4RSF as well as that of leveraging diverse product relations. Specifically, in Experiment 2, to examine the utility and superiority of DC-PRL, we replaced it with several prevalent product relation identification (PRI) strategies, keeping other modules in CL4RSF intact. The investigated alternative PRI strategies include (1) Category-Based, (2) Copurchase-Based, (3) Shopper-Based, (4) Correlation-Based (computing sales correlations within a time window), and (5) Conventional GSL (CGSL), in which we removed the two loss terms, the main novelties in our PRL, to examine their utility. Experiment 3 assessed the utility of leveraging positive and negative product relations through ablation analyses. To gain a deep understanding of sales patterns for each product category, we also performed Experiment 3 within each of the two categories in the Drugs case.<sup>7</sup> In Experiment 4, we examined the utility of leveraging asynchronous relations by comparing the predictive performance without $( \mathrm { w } / \mathrm { o }$ asyn) and that with leveraging asynchronous relations (with asyn).

We used two performance metrics widely used for time series forecasting: MAE and root mean square error (RMSE). To ensure the reliability of performance estimation, we repeated each experiment involving deep-learning methods 10 times with different seeds for their random initialization. Further information on the implementation, experiment execution, and hyperparameter settings is available in Online Appendix F. We conducted sensitivity analysis on the hyperparameters Topk, λ<sub>1</sub>, and λ<sub>2</sub> (Online Appendix G). For statistical significance testing, we used the Diebold and Mariano test (Diebold and Mariano 2002) with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons (Online Appendix H). We also conducted explanatory analyses to derive further insights into cross-category effects and various inferred product relations (Online Appendix I).

## 6. Contributions and Managerial Implications

## 6.1. Contributions

This study makes multiple contributions. First, for collaborative RSF, beyond the well-acknowledged relations of complements and substitutes, we discern various product relations along both relation type and temporal dimensions, including positive, negative, indirect, asymmetric, asynchronous, and dynamic relations, thus broadening our outlook regarding product relations that can be leveraged to enhance sales forecasting.

Second, to the computational design science paradigm of IS, we contribute a novel design artifact and two generalizable design principles. We propose a new GNNbased deep-learning artifact that encodes multifaceted product relations, advancing relation-empowered retail management. Although this artifact is designed specifically for RSF, it is quite general and can be adapted to address other business and societal problems that need to consider all or some of these relations. We also contribute two design principles that are generalizable to other computational design research contexts. One principle is “from pipeline to joint optimization”: the pipeline paradigm suggests that product relations should be determined in advance, whereas our method jointly optimizes the product relation learner, information interactor, and other modules, thus avoiding error propagation. The other principle is “spin and weave”; we resort to “current\_” and “previous\_” nodes for modeling asynchronous relations, opening new possibilities for yielding dual representations for each SKU.

Third, our proposed asynchronous-aware GNN with the dual-channel-based asynchronous GSL and asynchronous-aware temporal encoder, robust and effective GSL with two novel loss terms, and DRAI constitute methodological contributions. DRAI especially provides new insights regarding leveraging low- and high-frequency signals to model positive and negative relations, which have generally been addressed by distinguishing graph edges and then investigating complex balanced path generation strategies in extant research (Derr et al. 2018). Hence, DRAI innovates a new perspective for modeling and leveraging positive and negative relations (e.g., follow or block someone in a social network). These new strategies, individually or in combination, can provide insights for tackling unresolved issues, thus guiding the development of other models or further enhancing the capability of corresponding models in diverse contexts, as exemplified in Table 8.

## 6.2. Managerial Implications

Our proposed end-to-end deep-learning architecture, with its superior forecasting performance over state-ofthe-art alternatives thanks to its incorporation of various product relations, has managerial implications for various stakeholders (e.g., retail store managers, inventory department, purchasing department, operational staff, marketers, and retail platforms) in two broad ways. On the one hand, compared with existing RSF models, the improved forecasting could provide more solid datadriven decision support for supply chain management, resource planning, inventory control, and purchasing planning, thereby contributing to reduced cost and increased financial profits. On the other hand, derived insights on product relations could facilitate reasonable pricing and promotion strategies (Ke and Wang 2022), enhance the relevance and diversity of recommendation systems, and provide benefits for developing plausible and optimal assortment planning, cross-selling, and shelf space allocation models. We will illustrate how the semblance of predictive power demonstrates operational utility (in Section 6.2.1) and explicate the implications of our design artifact for two possible downstream applications, that is, recommendation (in Section 6.2.2) and assortment planning (in Online Appendix J). We chose these two applications because product relations play a pivotal role in complementary and substitutable product recommendation (CSPR), whereas both predictive power and product relations matter for assortment planning.

6.2.2. Downstream Application: Complementary and Substitutable Product Recommendation. For effective CSPR (Zhang et al. 2021a, Papso 2023, Zhou et al. 2023), high-quality product relation information is critical. Several prior studies explored textual or image features to infer complementary and substitutable products (McAuley et al. 2015a, b). However, the feature-based strategy is prone to missing related products. For example, a tennis racket and a sports top, two potentially complementary products, exhibit no similarity on their textual or image features. In recent years, the user behavior data-based strategy has been spotlighted with the assumption that co-purchased and co-viewed product pairs are ground-truth labels for complements and substitutes (Liu et al. 2021, Zhang et al. 2021a). On the ground of this assumption, prior relevant studies either directly implemented recommendation algorithms on co-purchase or co-view data to utilize observable complements or substitutes (Zhang et al. 2021a, Zhou et al. 2023) or conducted link prediction using a gold corpus built on user behavior data to identify unobserved complements or substitutes (Liu et al. 2021). However, the user behavior data-based strategy is far from optimal. On the one hand, there exists strong evidence indicating the inaccuracy of the crude correspondence between co-purchased (co-viewed) product pairs and complements (substitutes) (Hao et al. 2020, Kwark et al. 2021). On the other hand, this strategy generally sacrifices cross-category recommendation diversity because most relevant studies tend to be performed on categoryspecific data sets, perhaps out of computational concern because of daunting volumes of cross-category user behavior data.

Product relations derived by our artifact lay a foundation for CSPR to potentially deliver relevant, well-timed, and diverse recommendations. First, in terms of relevance, given a purchased product, an effective recommendation system tends to recommend positively related products, whereas it delists negatively related ones. For example, suppose an e-commerce platform, by implementing CL4RSF, obtains a synchronously pos itive relation between a Xiaomi phone and Xiaomi earphones and a synchronously negative relation between a Xiaomi phone and a phone from another brand. On this premise, when a Xiaomi phone has just been purchased, the platform can recommend Xiaomi earphones during the concurrent period, whereas the other phone should be excluded from the recommendation list. The superiority of our method over category-based and co purchase-based strategies for RSF indicates the better quality of CL4RSF-inferred product relations, holding the potential to provide more relevant downstream recommendations. Second, extant CSPR efforts gener ally solely concentrate on the relation type dimension (i.e., leveraging positive and negative product relations). CSPR models based on product relations derived by CL4RSF can additionally incorporate the temporal dimension and account for relation latency (i.e., differentiating synchronous and asynchronous product relations). Asynchronous product relations, in conjunction with temporal attentions, shown in Figure I.11 (Online Appendix I.6), can empower recommendation models (especially sequential recommendation models) to determine not only what but also when to recommend, thus enabling customers to access the right products at the right time. For example, suppose an asynchronously positive relation from a Xiaomi phone to a Xiaom robotic vacuum cleaner is derived by our model. On this basis, the platform can recommend a Xiaomi robotic vacuum cleaner after a while following the purchase of a Xiaomi phone. Third, incorporating cross-category product relations, guided by the theoretical underpinning of this paper (i.e., CCCDT), has the intrinsic advantage of enhancing recommendation diversity, a desirable property (Hao et al. 2020). For example, when a tennis racket has been purchased, the recommendation list is likely to be composed of its cross-category positively related products, such as tennis balls, sports tops, and sports water bottles.

## 7. Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study has some limitations, which may be addressed in future research. First, DC-PRL in our method works on existing products, dynamic graph-enabled GSL and GNN methods that support new products (i.e., new nodes and potential new edges) deserve further investigation in the future. Second, we incorporate a certain number of neighbors for each product (i.e., kNN graph) in the product relation learner, a promising future research direction would be to investigate more flexible GSL strategies. Third, although our experiments, economic value analysis, and discussions of possible downstream applications support the operational utility of our method, its actual value in real-world settings may be further tested through field experiments.
