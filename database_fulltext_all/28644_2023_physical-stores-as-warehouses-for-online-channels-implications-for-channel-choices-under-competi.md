# Physical Stores as Warehouses for Online Channels: Implications for Channel Choices Under Competition

Ping Tang,<sup>a</sup> Jianqing Chen,<sup>a,</sup>\* Srinivasan Raghunathan<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. The Internet and online channels have immensely transformed retailing, which has traditionally relied on brick-and-mortar and physical channels. In recent years, a hybrid and special omnichannel structure referred to as New Retail promising to tightly integrate online and physical channels has emerged within the industry; some industry experts tout New Retail as the future of retailing. For instance, in the New Retail model, retailers use physical stores not only as front ends for physical channel consumers but also as small local warehouses to serve online consumers. In this paper, we provide insights into competing firms’ retail channel choices among online channel, physical channel, and omnichannel under the New Retail model. The online and physical channels could differ in terms of geographical market coverage, consumer shopping cost, and consumer valuation: An online channel can serve both city and remote (suburban) consumers, whereas a physical channel may only be able to serve city consumers, and consumers could have a lower shopping cost in the online channel than in the physical channel but a lower valuation for the online channel than for the physical channel. We find that an equilibrium in which at least one firm operates the omnichannel emerges only when consumers have a higher valuation for the physical channel. Moreover, neither firm would operate only the online channel in this case. In contrast, when consumers have a higher valuation for the online channel than the physical channel, neither firm is likely to operate the omnichannel. Th market and channel characteristics have nonuniform and counterintuitive impacts on firms’ profits under different equilibria. For instance, firms might not benefit from either increasing the differentiation between the channels within a firm or increasing the differentiation between channels across firms. Furthermore, an increase in the number of city consumers relative to suburban consumers can hurt the firm that serves only city consumers. The tradeoffs among interfirm competition, market expansion, consumer segmentation, and intrafirm cannibalization effects of firms’ channel choices drive our findings.

## 1. Introduction

The advent of the Internet and e-commerce has transformed retailing significantly. Academics and practitioners alike have highlighted the enormous benefits online retailers offer to consumers and the disruption online retailers create for traditional brick-and-mortar (physical, hereafter) retailing. For instance, in one of the early academic papers, Brynjolfsson et al. (2003) cite lower prices, “frictionless” commerce, greater convenience, lower shopping cost, and wider selection of products offered in online retailing compared with physical. The growth of online retailing has outpaced that of physical retailing, supporting the touted benefits of online retailing.<sup>1</sup> In 2020, all the retail growth came from online retailing, and online retail sales in the

United States accounted for 19.1% of total retail sales in 2021.<sup>2</sup>

Recognizing the market for online retailing, many traditional physical retailers have launched online channels of their own. Many have also scaled down physical retailing by closing stores. For instance, U.S. retailers announced 9,302 store closings in 2019.<sup>3</sup> Some retailers such as Abercrombie & Fitch and Nordstrom have closed numerous stores while boosting their spending on online channels simultaneously (Cook 2014, Barrabi 2019). On the other hand, we have also observed the reverse phenomenon of traditional online retailers and digital brands opening physical locations.<sup>4</sup> The two e-commerce giants, Amazon and Alibaba, have entered the physical retailing market by purchasing Whole Foods and Intime, respectively. Amazon is also experimenting with the convenience store concept “Amazon Go,” and Alibaba has been developing its own futuristic physical grocery store “Hema.” Other examples of this phenomenon include Boll & Branch, which sells luxury bedding,<sup>5</sup> and Casper, which sells mattresses.<sup>6</sup> Some industry experts predict an expanding trend of digital brands launching physical stores and physical retailers expanding their online presence, referred to within the retail industry as New Retail.

Although the term New Retail is becoming more common in the popular press, there is no consensus on its definition. The following quote captures the salient aspects of this emerging retail model: “New Retail is an integrated retail delivery model where offline, online, logistics, and data converge to enhance customer experience.”<sup>7</sup> The New Retail paradigm seeks to leverage the positive features of online and physical retail channels. For instance, online channels offer a huge product selection and communicate the digital or search attributes of products effectively and efficiently. On the other hand, physical channels can convey the nondigital or experience attributes more effectively than the online channels. Furthermore, the New Retail setup seeks to offer consumers greater convenience and better shopping experiences by integrating online and physical channels more tightly. It also takes the power of digitization and big data to create new efficiencies in logistics, marketing, and product assortment. Although several aspects characterize this emerging model, one important and unique feature of New Retail, the focus of this paper, is that physical stores serve as both a sales outlet for physical channel consumers and as warehouses for online channel consumers. For example, when consumers place orders online, Hema uses algorithms to distribute them to the pickers at the grocery store. The pickers then collect items for the orders and put them on a conveyor belt to be delivered.<sup>8</sup> New Retail can also be viewed as a new practice of omnichannel retailing with the development of new technology and big data.

Our anecdotal observations suggest that the shift toward the New Retail paradigm is not uniform across all retailers or all products, and the retailers’ channel strategies seem to vary depending on the product category or type. For instance, Amazon’s and Alibaba’s physical channels are focused on supermarket items such as grocery and produce. Other digital brands that have launched physical stores (e.g., Bonobos, Glossier, Casper, and Warby Parker) sell products such as beds, mattresses, clothing, and eyeglasses. A distinctive feature of these products is that the quality is difficult to capture digitally, making physical inspection very desirable. Moreover, the channel strategies of retailers competing in the same product category are also not uniform: Although some operate both online and physical channels, others operate either only physical or only online channels. For example, the department store Ross operates only physical stores, but Macy’s operates both online and physical channels.

The current observations in the retail industry are likely the results of retailers still adjusting their strategies in response to rapid developments in technology that affect both online and physical channels, so these observations are unlikely to represent retailers’ equilibrium choices. Still, they do raise a number of unanswered questions given our current understanding of the online and physical retail channels. The online and physical channels are traditionally viewed as serving the needs of different consumer segments. For instance, online channels benefit consumers who find shopping in physical channels to be costly or inconvenient, whereas the physical channels tend to serve consumers who live close to the stores and for whom shopping in a physical channel is not too costly. If competing retailers converge to operating both online and physical channels, as opposed to specializing in either the online or the physical channel, it remains unclear whether they would compete more intensely, which could hurt them. Moreover, if online channels have less friction (i.e., are more substitutable from a consumer’s perspective) than physical channels, why would physical retailers open online channels and engage in direct competition<sup>9</sup> with online retailers that could also cannibalize their physical channels? Would retailing converge to the New Retail scenario as predicted by some experts or would retailers choose to specialize in a channel?

The answers to the previous questions are of interest to academic researchers in several disciplines such as strategy, marketing, and, in particular, information systems. The global e-commerce giants, which have revolutionized the retailing industry through information technology (IT), are leading the New Retail innovation. It is often said that these e-commerce firms are first and foremost IT companies.<sup>10</sup> They used IT to integrate their customer relationships, digital payments solutions, and logistics infrastructure even when they operated only online retail channels. Now, they are extending their IT capabilities to create seamless omnichannel experiences for consumers under the New Retail model, at least for some product categories. On the one hand, the New Retail model enables these firms to innovate their op erations; on the other hand, the firms also struggle to align their business strategy with these IT-driven innovations. This paper examines this broad nexus between the operational strategies enabled by IT and channel strategies driven by market conditions. Moreover, even in the online retailing context, channel competition, channel integration, and channel design have attracted substantial scholarly attention within the information systems (IS) literature (Granados et al. 2012, Gallino and Moreno 2014, Gu and Tayi 2016, Kumar et al. 2019). This work has focused on aspects such as inventory coordination, showrooming, product assortment, and consumer-behavior prediction. We build on these streams of IS literature and focus on the New Retail model’s integration of physical stores as storefronts and online-channel warehouses.

To answer these questions and provide insights into retailers’ channel choices (physical, online, or both), we develop a game-theoretic model in which two firms that sell products in the same product category decide whether to operate only a physical channel, only an online channel, or both online and physical channels (omnichannel, hereafter), and subsequently, the prices in these channels. Consumers are geographically dispersed; some fraction live in the city where physical stores are located, and others live in the suburbs away from the city. The cost to shop can differ across the channels and across consumers depending on their geographical location. For instance, consumers incur a transportation cost when they shop in the physical channel and incur delivery (shipping) cost and consumption-delay cost when they shop in the online channel. Moreover, consumers could have different valuations for the online and physical channels, which could arise from the differences in product variety and effectiveness in assessing product quality in the two channel types. Consumers make purchase decisions after observing firms’ channel choices and prices.

We find that an equilibrium in which at least one firm operates the omnichannel emerges only when consumers have a higher valuation for the physical channel than the online channel. In this scenario, the equilibrium in which both firms operate the omnichannel is likely to emerge when the channels have comparable shopping costs and the fraction of city consumers is not very high; the equilibrium in which both firms operate only the physical channel is likely to emerge when the shopping cost is relatively higher in the physical channel and the fraction of city consumers is high; and the equilibrium in which one firm operates the omnichannel and the other operates the physical channel emerges as the equilibrium under other conditions. Thus, when consumers have a higher valuation for the physical channel than the online channel, neither firm would operate only the online channel. In contrast, when consumers have a higher valuation for the online channel than the physical channel, neither firm is likely to operate the omnichannel. In this scenario, the equilibrium in which both firms operate the online channel only is likely to emerge when the channels have comparable shopping costs and the fraction of city consumers is not very high; the equilibrium in which both firms operate only the physical channel is likely to emerge when the shopping cost is relatively higher in the physical channel and the fraction of city consumers is high; and the equilibrium in which one firm operates the online channel and the other operates the physical channel emerges as the equilibrium under other conditions.

The market and channel characteristics not only lead to different equilibrium channel choices for the firms but also have nonuniform impacts on firms’ profits under different equilibria because the nature and intensity of interfirm competition are different. Some of these impacts and hence their implications for firms are counterintuitive. For instance, increasing the differentiation between the two firms’ physical (or online) channels does not always benefit firms. Analogously, increasing the differentiation between the online and physical channels does not always benefit the firm that operates both of these channels. Furthermore, an increase in the number of city consumers relative to the suburban consumers can hurt the firm that serves only city consumers.

In addition to providing predictions about the equilib rium likely to emerge under different market conditions and how the firms could be affected by these conditions differently under different equilibria, our analysis and findings are significant regarding the insights they gen erate about the driving forces that influence firms channel choices. Evidently, the firms’ channel choices critically depend on the nature and intensity of the com petition between the firms. We reveal that the channel configuration (i.e., the channel choices of both firms) determines which channels compete directly with each other and hence the intensity of competition between firms. The channel configuration also gives rise to three other effects—market expansion, consumer segmentation, and intrafirm cannibalization—that also shape the firms channel choices. Introduction of an online channel could expand the market potential for a firm by attracting con sumers that find shopping in a physical channel prohib itively costly. Although market expansion provides an incentive for a firm to operate the online channel, market expansion is neither necessary nor sufficient for a firm to do so. When a firm operates the online channel along with a physical channel, the online channel not only attracts the suburban consumers, but could also attract city consumers who are not close to the firm’s physical store. In this case, the city consumers are partitioned into different segments: those close to a firm’s physical store who would buy from the physical chan nel and those farther from the store who would buy from the online channel. If both firms operate the online channel and the physical channel, direct competition exists only between their online channels, and their respective physical channels have local monopoly seg ments. The local monopoly allows firms to set high prices in their physical channels in this case. On the other hand, when a firm operates both online and phys ical channels, some consumers that would have bought from the physical channel in the absence of the online channel could switch to the, possibly lower priced, online channel, leading to intrafirm cannibalization and limiting the price increase in the physical channel. The tradeoff among these effects (market expansion and segmentation being the positive effects and cannibalization being the negative one), along with the nature and intensity of competition resulting from firms’ channel choices, determine the outcomes. The magnitudes of these effects and the tradeoffs among them are affected by market conditions such as the fraction of consumers who live in the city (or suburbs), the shopping costs in the channels (or the degree of differentiation between the firms in these channels), and the relative valuations of the two channels by consumers, leading to different outcomes under different market conditions.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss the related literature and contributions of this paper. In Section 3, we present the model. We provide the analysis of the price competition in Section 4 and study firms’ channel choices and the impact of market and channel characteristics in Section 5. In Section 6, we consider various model variants to check the robustness of our main findings. Finally, we discuss the managerial implications of our results and conclude in Section 7.

## 2. Literature Review

This paper primarily relates to three research streams in the information systems and marketing literature— retail channel competition, channel integration, and channel design.

The retail-channel-competition stream focuses on the competition between online and physical retailers. Studies in this stream of research show that the competition between physical and online retailers is affected by relative prices between retailers (Granados et al. 2012), transportation cost (Forman et al., 2009), and product assortment differences between retailers (Choi and Bell 2011). Chiang et al. (2003) show that if a manufacturer decides to sell his product using a direct online channel, in addition to a retail channel, the retailer may not always be hurt because it could lead to a reduction in the wholesale price. Forman et al. (2009) show that sales of the online retailer in a geographical region diminish if a physical store enters that region. Jing (2018) finds that showrooming by consumers intensifies competition between a traditional retailer and an online retailer and hurts both firms. Hao and Kumar (2019) show that even though a physical retailer loses some consumers because of showrooming, consumers’ showrooming behavior may benefit the physical retailer by preventing the supplier from charging a higher wholesale price. Kuksov and Liao (2018) find that consumers’ showrooming behavior may lead to increased profitability for brick-and-mortar stores when considering manufacturer’s decisions. Our paper departs from this stream of literature in one key dimension: Although the channel-competition literature examines different channels operated by different retailers, we focus on the channel design problems of two competing retailers, both of whom decide whether to operate one or both of these two channels. Thus, the firms in our context deal with not only interfirm channel competition, but also the intrafirm channel-integration problem.

The channel-integration literature focuses on channe choices for a single retailer. Wallace et al. (2004) find tha compared with retailing through a pure physical store, dual-channel retailing can improve consumer satisfaction and foster consumer loyalty. Herhausen et al. (2015) examine the impact of online channel and physical chan nel integration, defined as integrating access to and know ledge about the offline channel into an online channel They show that channel integration creates synergies and not cannibalization. Gallino and Moreno (2014) ana lyze the impact of the implementation of a “buy-online, pick-up-in-store” strategy and show that customers may use different channels during different stages of thei purchase process. Gu and Tayi (2016) investigate how strategic placement of products between offline and online channels for the same retailer increases profit and consumer surplus. Kumar et al. (2019) find that retailers store openings result in an increase in online purchases from existing customers. Gao et al. (2021) investigate how a multichannel retailer should decide the size of physical stores and show that a higher return rate for online purchases motivates the retailer to have fewe physical stores that are larger in size. Using an online survey of omnichannel users, Shen et al. (2018) find tha channel integration quality significantly affects the per ceived fluency across different channels. Geyskens et al. (2002) show that the operation of an online channel in addition to a traditional physical channel can also lead to reduction in total demand. Deleersnyder et al. (2002) show that when online and physical channels are substi tutes, channel cannibalization hurts a firm that operates both channels. Wang and Goldfarb (2017) find substitu tion across channels and complementarity in demand. Thus, the results of prior studies in this literature abou the impact of channel integration on retailers are mixed. Whereas the studies in this literature stream primarily consider a single firm with exogenously specified chan nels, we allow two competing firms to make their chan nel choices (operating only an online channel, only a physical channel, or omnichannel). To focus on the channel choices, we abstract away details related to produc assortment, store locator, and inventory. Retailers in our context must account for the impact of competition on benefits of (or loss from) channel integration which is absent in the single-retailer context.

Our paper is also related to the literature on channel design. For example, Bernstein et al. (2008) examine the equilibrium channel structure in an oligopoly setting and find that click-and-mortar is the equilibrium outcome although it does not necessarily imply higher profits for retailers compared with other structures. Zhang (2009) analyzes competing retailers’ channel and advertising strategies and shows that dual channel and advertising are not always optimal for retailers, and the equilibrium depends on the nature of product, the competitiveness of the market, the retailer’s costs, and the competitor’s strategies. Ofek et al. (2011) study retailers channel decisions and the impact of an online channel on pricing strategies and physical-store support levels in a duopoly market. They show that retailers can be worse off when they add an online channel, which may result in an asymmetric equilibrium where only one retailer operates the dual channel. These studies examine the channel-design questions from the perspective of a traditional physical retailer who decides whether to open an online channel. Moreover, these papers focus on how operational variables, such as e-fulfillment costs, advertising, and sales support, affect the retailer’s decision. In contrast, we examine the channel-design problem without assuming any baseline channel. We abstract away the operational details and focus on strategic aspects related to competition. In particular, we offer insights into how the channel configuration impacts the nature and intensity of competition, and such factors as segmentation and cannibalization that, in turn, shape the equilibrium channel structure.

## 3. Model

We consider a market with geographically dispersed consumers. While a fraction of consumers are located within the “city,” others are located in the “suburbs” outside the city. The market is served by two firms. Each firm can operate a physical channel, an online channel, or the omnichannel. For simplicity and to focus on the effect of channel- and market-related features on the firms’ channel choices, we assume both firms sell products from the same product category in all their channels, and the consumers have a consumption need for one product in that category only.

We use Hotelling’s linear city model to denote city consumers’ locations. Specifically, θ fraction of consumers are city consumers and they are located uniformly between locations 0 and 1 along a line of unit length. The remaining (1 � θ) fraction of consumers are suburban consumers located outside the Hotelling line. Half of the suburban consumers are located at location �α (left suburb) and the other half are located at 1 + α (right suburb). We normalize the total number of consumers in the market to one. Figure 1 illustrates their relative locations.

Firm A is located at 0 and Firm B is located at 1. We assume fixed exogenous firm locations to focus on the firms’ channel design questions. When a firm operates a physical channel, the physical store is located at the firm’s location; that is, A’s and B’s physical stores would be located at 0 and 1, respectively. When a firm operates an online channel, it delivers the product to consumers from its location; that is, A and B would deliver products from 0 and 1, respectively. Thus, consistent with the New Retail concept, a firm’s location serves as the warehouse for the online channel and as the storefront for the physical channel. In the baseline model, we consider that if both firms operate only physical channels, the suburban consumers’ transportation cost to visit any of the physical stores is so high that they would not be served. On the other hand, if an online channel exists, then the market will be fully cov ered. In other words, a firm’s online channel has a larger market potential than the physical channel. In Section 6.1, we consider the scenario where the market is covered even just by the physical channels so that there is no market expansion because of online channels.

Consumers have a base valuation of v for the product when it is purchased in a physical channel and a valuation of v � δ when it is purchased in an online channel. The parameter δ captures the base valuation difference between the two channels. Consumers could value the physical channel more than the online channel because they can more accurately evaluate the products’ nondigital attributes by touching or trying in the physical channel. On the other hand, consumers could value the physical channel less because of the relatively smaller product variety available in the physical channel compared with the online channel. Therefore, we make no assumption about the sign of δ. We would expect δ to be positive when consumers face greater uncertainty about product quality and fit or greater privacy, product return, and other risks in the online channel than the physical channel. For instance, for products whose attributes are difficult to capture digitally, consumers may face a higher degree of product uncertainty and risk in the online channel than in the physical channel. On the other hand, δ could be negative if product attributes can be captured digitally and the online channel offers advantages such as a greater product variety compared with the physical channel such that consumers are able to find a product that is closer to their needs and tastes. In the appendix, we provide a formal microeconomic model and analysis to derive δ using product variety and product information precision differences between the online and physical channels.

The transportation cost per unit distance for a consumer to visit and purchase from a physical store is $t _ { \mathrm { s } } .$ Thus, the transportation cost for a consumer located at x to purchase from A is $| x | t _ { \mathrm { s } }$ and that from B is $\left| 1 - x \right| t _ { \mathrm { s } } .$ When shopping online, although there is no physica transportation cost for the consumer, there is a cost of waiting to receive the product after the order is placed. This online shopping cost depends on the distance between the consumer’s location and the location of the warehouse from which the product is delivered. Consumers close to the warehouse receive the order in less time, and those far away from the warehouse wait for a longer time to receive the order (Bortolini et al. 2016, Millstein et al. 2022). Specifically. for a consumer located at $x ,$ the shopping cost to purchase from an online channel of A is $| x | t _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and that from B is $| 1 - x | t _ { \mathrm { o } }$ . We refer to the transportation cost in case of the physical channel and the cost associated with the online channel as the “shopping cost” of the respective channel hereafter. The shopping cost also measures the degree of the differentiation in that channel between the two firms. We assume that the unit shopping cost is higher for the physical channel than the online channel $( \mathrm { i . e . , } t _ { s } > t _ { o } ) .$ , consistent with the observation that online channels exhibit less friction or stickiness in consumers’ firm choice than physical channels. In the baseline model, we assume that the shopping cost is exogenous and is proportional to the distance between the consumers and the store for the online and physical channels. However, it is possible that in the online channel, the shopping cost could have a fixed component (e.g., shipping fee charged by the seller) and a variable component (i.e., the waiting time proportional to the distance) and that a firm may set the shopping cost. We examine this model variation in Section 6 as a model extension and show that the results do not change qualitatively under this extension.

We use index $i , i \in \{ \mathrm { A } , \mathrm { B } \}$ , to denote the firm and index $j , j \in \{ \mathbf { s } , \mathbf { o } \}$ , to denote the channel, with s and o denoting physical and online channel, respectively. We denote $p _ { i j }$ as firm i’s price in channel $j .$ We assume that both online-channel and physical-channel prices are common knowledge among consumers. Consistent with the common practice, we allow the price in a firm’s online channel to be different from that in its physical channel. We can then formulate the net utility for a consumer located at x when buying from firm i’s channel j as

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} U _ {\mathrm{As}} = v - p _ {\mathrm{As}} - | x | t _ {s} \\ U _ {\mathrm{Bs}} = v - p _ {\mathrm{Bs}} - | 1 - x | t _ {s} \\ U _ {\mathrm{Ao}} = v - \delta - p _ {\mathrm{Ao}} - | x | t _ {o} \\ U _ {\mathrm{Bo}} = v - \delta - p _ {\mathrm{Bo}} - | 1 - x | t _ {o}. \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

The timing of the game is as follows. In Stage 1, each firm decides the channel strategy—each firm chooses to use only the online channel, only the physical channel, or the omnichannel. In Stage 2, each firm announces the prices for all its channels, which are observed by all consumers. In Stage 3, consumers make their purchase decisions—they decide which firm and which channel to patronize. We assume that when a firm is indifferent between the omnichannel and a physical channel only (or the online channel only), it will choose the latter. For ease of exposition, we consider the parameter space in which corner solutions do not arise as an equilibrium when a physical channel competes with an online channel. Specifically, we assume that the fraction of city consumers, $\theta ,$ in the market is not too small and the relative valuation of online channel compared with physical channel is not too high (i.e., δ is not too small) so that nei ther firm targets only suburban consumers.

## 4. Analysis of the Price Competition

Using backward induction, we solve the game and derive the subgame-perfect equilibrium. Based on firms channel choices in Stage 1, we have six different possibil ities in the pricing subgame in Stage 2: (i) both firms operate only physical channels, (ii) both firms operate only online channels, (iii) one firm operates the online channel only and the other firm operates the physical channel only, (iv) both firms operate omnichannels, (v) one firm operates only the physical channel and the other firm operates the omnichannel, and (vi) one firm operates only the online channel and the other firm operates the omnichannel. In this section, we derive the prices for each possible subgame in Stage 2. We use a superscript to indicate the firms’ channel choices in Stage 1 and to distinguish the equilibria for different subgames in Stage 2 in the rest of the paper. We let $\mathbf { O } , \mathbf { S } ,$ and m denote the online channel, physical channel, and the omnichannel, respectively. We use pairs of $\mathbf { O } , \mathbf { S } ,$ and m to denote firms’ choices in Stage 1, with the first element in a pair indicating Firm A’s choice and the second element Firm B’s choice. When firm i operates only a single channel, we omit subscript j from $p _ { i j }$ because the reference to channel bears no ambiguity.

In sum, the equilibria in the pricing subgame demonstrate that the nature and intensity of interfirm price competition differ across channel configurations and that the prices depend critically on in which channels the two firms directly compete with each other. Furthermore, we find that when consumers have a higher valuation for the online channel than the physical channel, if a firm operates the omnichannel, it would not induce any demand in its physical channel, making the omnichannel strategy equivalent to the online-channel-only strategy. In contrast, when consumers have a higher valuation for the physical channel than the online channel, consumers would be induced to buy from a firm’s online or physical channel in the presence of omnichannel, and thus firms must account for consumer segmentation and cannibalization effects within a firm. On the other hand, the prices in channels that directly compete with each other remain the same regardless of the firms channel choices. Therefore, in this case, the firms’ channel choices would depend not only on their impact on interfirm competition but also their potential impacts within the firms.

Clearly, the firms’ equilibrium channel choices would be affected by the nature and intensity of interfirm competition induced by their choices. As revealed by the analysis of the pricing subgame, the interfirm competition is shaped by which channels compete directly with each other. When both firms operate only online or only physical channels, we have the well-known result that the unit shopping costs in these channels determine the degree of differentiation between the firms and, hence, the intensity of interfirm competition. In other cases, the equilibrium channel choices are driven by three other effects as well: market expansion, consumer segmentation, and intrafirm cannibalization. Figure 3 illustrates these effects on Firm A using the scenario when both firms operate the omnichannel, based on a benchmark of both firms operating only the physical channel.

When a firm supplements an existing physical channel with an online channel, the firm’s market expands from only city consumers to suburban consumers as well. Such a market expansion benefits the firm, ceteris paribus. Moreover, the online channel not only attracts the suburban consumers but could also attract city consumers who are not close to the firm (i.e., those that are in the middle of the city). In this case, city consumers are partitioned into segments in the sense that those close to a firm could buy from the physical channel and those fa from the firm could buy from the online channel. If both firms operate the omnichannel, direct competition exists only between the online channels, creating local monopo lies for the firms in their respective physical channels. We label this the consumer-segmentation effect. On the other hand, some consumers who would have bought from the physical channel in the absence of the online channel could switch to the possibly lower priced online channel. We label this the intrafirm-cannibalization effect; this effect limits a firm’s ability to exploit the local monopoly created by the segmentation. The tradeoffs among these effects determine the firms’ equilibrium channel choices and explain the intuition for Proposition 1.

We first consider the case in which the consumers have a higher valuation for the physical channel than for the online channel. In this case, first of all, we note that a firm would always have an incentive to operate a physical channel, and operating only the online channel does not arise in equilibrium. This is because, when only the online channel is operated, the firm could benefit from the segmentation effect and improve its profit by adding a physical channel, regardless of the rival’s channel choice. Specifically, the firm could charge a higher price for the physical channel and attract some consumers close to its physical store as long as the higher price and their higher shopping costs can be compensated by the higher valuation embedded in physical channel.

Second, if both firms operate only the physical channel, whether one or both have an incentive to open an online channel depends on the tradeoffs among the various effects mentioned previously. Thus, the asymmetric equilibrium with one firm operating the omnichannel and the other operating only the physical channel arises in this case. If the differentiation between online channels is high $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , t _ { o } > t _ { o 1 } ^ { * } ) .$ , both firms operate the omnichannel. In this case, they benefit from market expansion and segmentation effects, but do not suffer severely from the competition between online channels or the cannibalization effects.

When consumers have a higher valuation for the online channel than the physical channel (the equilibrium for which is stated as Proposition 1(b)), first, no firm would operate the omnichannel, as discussed in Lemma 4. In this case, a consumer’s utility would be higher for the online channel than for the physical channel because of the higher valuation and lower shopping cost associated with the online channel. Thus, a firm would have to set a lower price in the physical channel than the online channel to attract any demand for the physical channel, but a low price does not come with any benefit relative to operating only the online channel. As a result, each firm operates only a single channel, and the segmentation effect and cannibalization effect do not arise in equilibrium. Consequently, the equilibrium strategies are deter mined solely by the market-expansion and competition effects. Operating only an online channel is attractive to both firms only when $t _ { o }$ is high; otherwise, the competition between online channels would be intense. On the other hand, if θ is high, the firms prefer to operate only physical channels; operating an online channel does not offer significant demand expansion benefit, but it leads to intense price competition. If the demand expansion is indeed significant and the online competition is intense $( \mathrm { i . e . , } t _ { o }$ is low), then firms curb the price competition by adopting the asymmetric strategy of one firm operating the online channel and the other firm operating the physical channel.

Proposition 1 shows that even when the online channel offers a higher valuation than the physical channel, the firms may choose to operate only the physical channel. However, if the physical channel offers a higher valuation than the online channel, they do not choose to operate only online channels. Moreover, the use of the omnichannel (online channel only) by one or both firms requires that the consumers have a higher (lower) valuation for the physical channel than the online channel, and the market conditions that favor the use of the omnichannel (online channel only) depend critically on the distribution of city and suburban consumers and the shopping cost in the two channels.

Moreover, firms can benefit from market expansion by operating the online channe (or omnichannel). On the other hand, Proposition 2 shows that if δ is sufficiently negative, an increase in the online channel’s shopping cost can also make both firms more likely to operate only physical channels. In sum, the analysis of the firms’ equilibrium channel choices reveals that (i) the scenario of both firms adopting the omnichannel is likely in a market where consumers have a higher valuation for the physical channel, the shopping costs in the two channels are not significantly different, and the fraction of city consumers is not too large, (ii) the scenario of both firms adopting the physical channel only is likely in a market where the physical channel’s shopping cost is significantly larger than online channel’s and the fraction of city consumers is high, (iii) the scenario of both firms adopting the online channel only is likely in a market where consumers have higher valuation for the online channel and the online channel’s shopping cost is large, and (iv) an asymmetric equilibrium in which each firm specializes in a different channel (physical, online or omnichannel) is likely in a market where the fraction of city consumers is not high and the physical channel’s shopping cost is significantly larger than online channel’s.

## 5.3. Market and Channel Characteristics’ Effects on Equilibrium Profits

The market and channel characteristics affect the firms profits as well. The examination of firms’ equilibrium profits is important because the nature and intensity of competition is different under different equilibria, suggesting that the market and channel characteristics could affect the firms’ profits differently within different equilibria, leading to different implications for firms.

Proposition 4. Both firms’ profits (i) increase in $t _ { s }$ in the (physical, physical), (omni, physical), and (online, physical) equilibria, (ii) are unaffected by $t _ { s }$ in the (online, online) equilibrium, and (iii) decrease in $t _ { s }$ in the (omni, omni) equilibrium.

Because $t _ { s }$ measures the extent of differentiation between the physical channels, one would expect an increase in $t _ { s }$ to benefit both firms, ceteris paribus. Proposition 4 shows that this expectation holds in many channel configurations where at least one firm operates a physical channel. However, the opposite holds in equilibrium when both firms operate the omnichannel; an increase in $t _ { s }$ hurts both firms in this equilibrium. This is because when both firms operate the omnichan nel, direct competition exists between online channels only. If $t _ { s }$ increases, in each firm, some consumers will shift from buying through the physical channel to buying through the online channel that has a lower price than the physical channel. Thus, the cannibalization problem becomes more severe, hurting the firms. In all other equilibrium structures with a physical channel, an increase in $t _ { s }$ softens the competition because either the physical channels directly compete with each other, or a physical channel competes with an online channel. In the case of direct competition between the physical channels, the traditional explanation of increased dif ferentiation resulting from an increase in $t _ { s }$ holds. In the case of competition between the physical channel of one firm and the online channel of the other firm, the firm that operates the online channel increases the price in its online channel that can also reduce the cannibali zation if it also operates the physical channel, and, as a result, both firms’ profits increase.

More importantly, the results demonstrate that some of the traditional strategies such as maximal differentiation may not curb competition and benefit firms when they compete across multiple channels, as the adoption of multiple channels can change the nature and intensity of competition and also lead to intrafirm segmentation and cannibalization.

## 7. Conclusion

The Internet has transformed retailing immensely with online retailing gaining ground over physical retailing in terms of market share. At the same time, retailers are experimenting with different channel-design strategies to deal with the disruptions caused by the Internet. Some industry experts tout New Retail, which promises to take advantage of the positive aspects of online and physical channels, as the future of retailing. In this paper, we examine competing firms’ retail-channel choices among online channel, physical channel, and omnichannel. The online and physical channels could differ in terms of geographical market coverage, consumer shopping cost, and consumer valuation; an online channel has a higher market potential, and a consumer has a lower shopping cost in the online channel than the physical channel but can have a higher valuation for either the physical channel or for the online channel. We find that an equilibrium in which at least one firm operates the omnichannel emerges only when consumers have a higher valuation for the physical channel than the online channel. Moreover, neither firm would operate only the online channel in this case. In contrast, when consumers have a higher val uation for the online channel than the physical channel, neither firm is likely to operate the omnichannel. The market and channel characteristics also have nonuniform and counterintuitive impacts on firms’ profits under different equilibria. For instance, neither increasing the differentiation between channels across firms nor increasing the differentiation between the channels within a firm necessarily benefits firms. Furthermore, an increase in the number of city consumers relative to the suburban consumers can also hurt the firm that serves only city consumers. The tradeoffs among interfirm competition, market expansion, consumer segmentation, and intrafirm-cannibalization effects of firms’ channel choices are the driving forces that lead to our findings. As robustness check, we also provide several model variants. Our main results remain valid in these variations.

The findings offer several managerial implications. First, the product category strongly influences the firms channel choice. If consumers find it difficult to assess the quality or fit of products based on information provided in online channels and they require tactile or sensory information (such as visual, touch, and smell) to assess quality and fit, then firms may have no incentive to operate only online channels. Experience goods such as grocery, clothing, and mattress would likely fit this category. The presence of omnichannels for these product categories is likely to be high when the shipping and other online shopping cost is comparable to the physical shopping cost. For instance, in the case of perishable grocery items that require temperature-controlled transportation or bulky items such as mattresses, the delivery cost can be substantial. If consumers can accurately evaluate product attributes online, and the online channel can provide a greater product variety and useful product information such as online reviews, firms may have no incentive to operate the omnichannel. Search goods such as books and electronics would likely fit this category.

Second, in addition to product category, product variety is likely to be an important factor that determines firms’ channel choices. A larger product variety suggests that the consumer is more likely to find a product that fits her taste and need. Typically, firms are able to offer more variety in online channels than physical channels because of stores’ physical and other constraints. If consumers are able to assess quality accurately based on online information and online technologies such as search and recommendation system help consumers identify the product that meets their needs from a vast variety, then consumers would likely have a higher valuation for the online channel than the physical channel. In this case, firms will likely adopt a single channel, either physical or online based on market conditions. The electronics product category seems to fit these characteristics.

Third, the online shopping cost relative to the physical shopping cost affects whether firms adopt symmetric or asymmetric channel strategies. The role of online shopping cost is especially significant because retailers have been introducing innovative technologies (such as automation and robotics) and programs (such as membership-based free and expedited shipping) to reduce the consumers’ online shopping cost burden. If the online shopping cost is significantly lower than the physical shopping cost, then asymmetric strategies where one firm operates omnichannel or online channel and the other firm specializes in physical channe are likely to be more common. On the other hand, if the online shopping cost is comparable to the physical shopping cost, then symmetric strategies where both firms compete directly using the same channel choices are likely to prevail. Of course, whether the symmetric strategy includes the omnichannel or only the online channel could depend on the product category. Thus, as technological transformations, such as sophisticated search, recommendation systems, virtual and augmented reality, and innovations in logistics and shipping programs, continue to change retailing, the channel choices can be expected to continue evolving. This transformation could include a shift toward the New Retail model, only online channels, only physical channels, or each firm specializing in one channel type, depending on the product category and market conditions.

This paper can be extended to several directions. For example, the theoretical findings of this paper show how the various market conditions can determine the equilibrium outcomes. These equilibrium outcomes can be viewed as testable hypotheses. Empirical examination of the impacts of these factors on firms channel choices that validate our theory is a valuable extension.

## A.1. Microfoundation of Valuation Difference

We consider n products in the focal product category. Online channels carry all these products, whereas physical stores only carry m products, where $m < n$ . We use a spokes model (Chen and Riordan 2007, Chen and Zeng 2022) to characterize consumer preference.<sup>16</sup> As shown in Figure $\mathrm { A . 1 , }$ we consider a n-spoke model. Each spoke, with length of ${ \scriptstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } } ,$ terminates at a center and originates at the other end. Product i is located at the origin of Spoke i.

Consumers are uniformly distributed on the spokes. The total mass of consumers is normalized to one, and the mass of consumers on each spoke is $\textstyle { \frac { 1 } { n } } .$ The distance between a consumer’s and a product’s locations along spokes measures the degree of misfit. For a consumer located on Spoke $i ,$ Product i is her preferred product because of the relatively lowe degree of misfit.
