# A Complex Adaptive Systems Perspective of Software Reuse in the Digital Age: An Agenda for IS Research

Abstract. Software is instrumental to the accelerated pace of digital innovation, and our ability to rapidly develop and deliver digital products and services is largely based on the reuse of existing software. In recent years, packaged software reuse has emerged as an important phenomenon driving the creation of new software, both proprietary and open source, as well as the emergence and evolution of entire software ecosystems. Arguing that our theoretical understanding of the nature and the magnitude of current packaged software reuse practice remains limited, our objective with this research commentary is twofold. First, we draw attention to the importance of packaged software reuse and its relevance as a prom ising phenomenon of interest for information systems (IS) researchers. Second, we mobilize the concept of generativity to conceptualize packaged software as a form of technologica innovation that fosters large-scale (re)combination and propose complex adaptive systems (CAS) as a theoretical foundation to help us engage with the current nature of the phenome non. Using key principles of CAS as the generative foundation for our conceptual scaffold ing, we offer a research framework for packaged software reuse and develop an agenda for IS research organized across three main axes. For each axis, we outline relevant research themes and research questions leveraging the nature of software as objects constituted of other pieces of software developed and maintained by heterogeneous groups of software developers. Shedding light on the renewed role of software reuse, our work contributes to ongoing conversations on generativity and software ecosystems as well as the design of digital products and services.

## Introduction

Software is at the nexus of the generation as well as rapid diffusion of digital products and services (Yoo 2013). Software is now more than the implementation of a set of requirements that have fixed boundaries: it is generative, it is epistemic, and, importantly, it is often part of ecosystems in which pieces of software are shared and made available for reuse in repositories. For example, the creation of countless software products that leverage artificial intelligence and machine learning (ML) is largely supported by the reuse of popular software libraries—packages—such as Keras, PyTorch, or TensorFlow,<sup>1</sup> enabling developers to implement ML rather than to write it from scratch.

The idea of building software using other pieces of software is not new. Software reuse, broadly defined as “the process of creating software systems from existing software rather than building software systems from scratch” (Krueger 1992, p. 131) is indeed an important phenomenon of interest with implications both for writing and managing software (Boehm 1999, Haefliger et al.

2008). Whereas there exist different forms of reuse, in this commentary, we focus specifically on a form of reuse involving self-contained, readily (re)usable packages downloaded and imported as technical dependencies, which we refer to as packaged software reuse (PSR). Our motivation to focus on PSR is based on the fact that this form of reuse has gained considerable traction in the industry to the point where “it has become virtually impossible in recent years to develop any significant software systems without relying at least to some degree on available component ecosystems” (Mikkonen and Taivalsaari 2019, p. 105). At the same time, our study of literature on the topic highlights that, perhaps paradoxically, it “has received surprisingly little attention among researchers” (Ma¨kitalo et al. 2020, p. 3)—particularly in information systems (IS) research.

Although past literature originally contributed to increase our understanding of the benefits and the undesirable consequences associated with reuse, it has recently been observed that “software reuse is occurring in a very different way than originally envisioned a few decades ago” (Mikkonen and Taivalsaari 2019, p. 109). Indeed, PSR is traditionally studied at the level of projects or organizations (e.g., Isoda 1995), yet recent evidence highlights the reality that it is now also a large-scale phenomenon that has important implications at higher levels, including for platforms and entire software ecosystems (e.g., Cimpanu 2019, 2020; Kol and Oberman 2020; Snyk Inc. 2020; Synopsys 2020). For example, PSR helps organizations release the software powering “connected” or “smart” devices at an unprecedented pace, but this is done at the expense of reusing software packages that can contain critical vulnerabilities, sometimes impacting millions of those devices (Seals 2020). The recent discovery of the Log4Shell vulnerability (The MITRE Corporation 2021b, Vial 2022) is another example showcasing the far-reaching implications of PSR when popular libraries become embedded in software products and services that are part of our daily lives.

In line with this evidence as well as burgeoning research on the topic (e.g., Mikkonen and Taivalsaari 2019, Feitosa et al. 2020, Ma¨kitalo et al. 2020), we argue that our theoretical understanding of the current nature of PSR and its implications as a phenomenon built on dynamics that take place between loosely coordinated groups of actors (e.g., producers, reusers) and that has implications both at the local level (i.e., for a project) and on a large scale at the collective level (i.e., for an entire software ecosystem) remains limited. In our view, this provides a unique opportunity for IS researchers to contribute new knowledge on this highly relevant topic.

Applying the concept of generativity (Zittrain 2006) to packaged software as the starting point of our conceptual scaffolding, we offer complex adaptive systems (CAS) as a foundation to theorize on the nature and the roles of actors—each with their own characteristics, objectives, and strategies—partaking in PSR. Specifically, we argue that key principles of CAS—aggregation, diversity, hierarchy, connectedness, emergence, self-organization, adaptation, coevolution, and nonlinearity—allow us to theorize PSR as a large-scale phenomenon emerging from lower level actions undertaken by heterogenous groups of actors, consistent with PSR practice. To explain the implications of this conceptualization, we develop a research agenda articulated across three interrelated axes undergirded by key principles of CAS: (1) agents, (2) signals, and (3) ecosystems. For each axis, we outline research themes and propose illustrative research questions to foster research on the topic, drawing attention to an understudied and undertheorized facet of the development of software-based products and services which, in our opinion, carries tremendous potential for IS research.

## Packaged Software Reuse: A Brief Review

Research greatly contributes to increase our understanding of the nature as well as the ambivalence of the outcomes associated with PSR. Literature on the topic indicates that PSR primarily involves two main categories of actors: (1) producers, who create and maintain software packages and who also may reuse packages created by others, and (2) reusers (who may also at times act as producers), who use packaged software created by producers. Additional details on our review of PSR literature are provided in Online Appendix 1.

A significant portion of the literature focuses on the impacts of PSR as a practice put in place within organizational settings (e.g., Banker et al. 1993, Gupta et al. 2009), consistent with the contexts in which reuse was first im plemented. Reuse initiatives were originally undertaken by firms seeking to reduce internal development time (e.g., Henry and Faller 1995, Boehm 1999) and to increase developer productivity (e.g., Kim and Stohr 1998). At the same time, studies find that producing software fit for reuse requires additional design and development time to anticipate and account for future use cases (e.g., Morisio et al. 2002, Morel and Alexander 2004). Findings also point to the fact that firms must devise tactics to foster communication and coordination as well as to create and publicize searchable repositories to encourage reuse (e.g., Ghanam et al. 2012).

Subsequent research on the topic focuses more specifically on single groups of actors, mostly reusers, to understand whether the expected benefits of reuse can effectively be achieved in the context of both proprietary and open-source software (OSS) packages publicly avail able over the internet. Indeed, beyond the development of the internet, one of the most significant drivers of reuse has been the OSS movement (Laurent 2004), which has enabled individuals and organizations to create and safely share and reuse software under various permissive licenses (e.g., GNU General Public License, BSD licenses) (e.g., Va¨lima¨ki 2005). In these contexts, PSR is found to reduce project development time (e.g., Haefliger et al. 2008) as well as increase developer productivity (e.g., Ma¨kitalo et al. 2020). PSR also allows reusers to access a vast pool of external expertise crystallized into packages (Sojer and Henkel 2010) that can be of higher quality than if they had been developed in-house (e.g., Feitosa et al. 2020). Nevertheless, there are also search costs related to locating suitable packages (e.g., Haefliger et al. 2008) as well as support costs associated with the integration of packages within a code base (e.g., Holmes and Walker 2013). In some instances, the need to under stand (e.g., Mikkonen and Taivalsaari 2019) and work around functionalities or issues embedded in reused packages can also lead to additional design and develop ment time (e.g., Sherif et al. 2006) or to the accumulation of technical debt (Feitosa et al. 2020). Finally, there is evi dence that increased dependence on external projects and teams (Sojer and Henkel 2010, Ma¨kitalo et al. 2020) as well as licensing issues can be problematic for reusers (e.g., Barros-Justo et al. 2019).

More recently, research in computer science and software engineering has started to account for the fact that the ongoing enactment of PSR at the local (i.e., project) level contributes to the existence of rich software ecosystems. In this context, the notion of software ecosystem is broadly defined as “communities built around shared programming languages, shared platforms, or shared dependency management tools, allowing developers to create packages that import and build on each other’s functionality” (Bogart et al. 2021, p. 3). This definition extends the notion of an ecosystem generally held in IS research that is based on the existence of a platform actively managed by an entity that has some degree of explicit control over complementors (Tiwana et al. 2010, Wareham et al. 2014, Hukal et al. 2020). One important implication of this distinction in the context of software development is that we can expect patterns of reuse to emerge more freely when a software ecosystem is not tied to a particular platform because there is a priori no central actor (i.e., a platform owner) who can de facto force others to use certain software packages (e.g., for authentication purposes) as illustrated in Figure 1(a) and (b). This burgeoning stream of research highlights the uncoordinated nature of software ecosystems and the loose connections that exist between actors that are part of those ecosystems, including the possibility for seemingly small events to have widespread consequences, such as when critical vulnerabilities are discovered (e.g., The MITRE Corporation 2021a).

In our view, the current state of knowledge on PSR reveals three knowledge gaps providing unique opportunities for IS researchers. First, research generally assumes that producers and reusers are the only actors involved in PSR. Although they are certainly central to the phenomenon, there are indications from practitioner sources that other actors, such as repository maintainers (e.g., Currie and Jackson 2016) or security researchers (e.g., Brown 2022), have an important role to play in this context. At the same time, we currently have little knowledge regarding the process through which producers and reusers decide to release or reuse new software packages as research focuses on initial reuse decisions. Among other benefits, we surmise that furthering our understanding of the nature and the roles of actors involved in PSR would help us understand how certain specific types of events can influence decisions for producers and reusers (e.g., when a critical update for a package is released following the discovery of an important vulnerability as illustrated in Solomon 2021). Second, research greatly contributes to increase our understanding of the immediate outcomes associated with reuse for producers and reusers. However, our knowledge of the dynamics of reuse as a process taking place over time and involving multiple groups of actors remains limited. For instance, we currently have little knowledge on the nature of the interactions that may take place between actors. In line with this idea, Palyart et al. (2018) argues that we need to expand our view that social interactions take place exclusively within a project and incorporate the idea that these interactions occur on a larger scale between projects maintained by different groups of producers and reusers. Third, PSR research so far focuses primarily on studying PSR at the level of projects or organizations, for example, in the context of reuse programs (see Online Table A1.2). Yet a few recent studies (e.g., Bogart et al. 2021) suggest that there is a need to engage more systematically with the nature of PSR at the collective level, that is, as a large-scale phenomenon involving producers, reusers, and others as loosely connected groups of uncoordinated actors participating in the ongoing emergence of software ecosystems (e.g., Um et al. 2022).

Together these knowledge gaps illustrate the reality that, despite its prevalence, our theoretical understanding of the nature and the implications of PSR remains limited. In other disciplines, some researchers recently argued that the scale of PSR “has received surprisingly little attention among researchers” (Ma¨kitalo et al. 2020, p. 3), whereas “researchers have not realized how significantly the effortless availability of vast numbers of open-software components is affecting software development” (Mikkonen and Taivalsaari 2019, p. 109). Considering that software development is a core element of the IS discipline (Sidorova et al. 2008, Jeyaraj and Zadeh 2020), we believe that it is important to draw the attention of IS researchers to these exciting developments that are reshaping our understanding of software development theory and practice. To explain the expansion of PSR as a large-scale phenomenon and support the crafting of novel theoretical contributions to address knowledge gaps on the topic, we propose to formalize packaged software as a form of generative technology that fosters reuse. We then introduce complex adaptive systems as a generative lens that can help us study interesting conceptual and empirical research questions on the topic by effectively engaging with the current nature of PSR.

## Generative Packaged Software

Digital innovation is fueled by software most often built by reusing existing software packages. This (re)combination of software may be the result of arbitrary design considerations, opportunistic behavior, or a combination thereof, and it is observed that “some developers rely very heavily on software reuse and see their role mainly as writing ‘glue-code’ to integrate various pieces of reused code” (Sojer and Henkel 2010, p. 879). This state of the practice of software development takes place at two levels: at the local level, a piece of software is built and evolves based on the reuse of multiple pieces of software produced by different groups of developers; at the global

In the context of digital platforms, researchers have turned to generativity—defined as “a technology’s overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences” (Zittrain 2006, p. 1980)—as a conceptual foundation to study the large scale, uncoordinated (re)combination of digital technologies (Yoo 2013). A key manifestation of generativity is the development of new products and services as an organic, uncoordinated series of moves that are more in line with emergence than controlled evolution as is the case with PSR. Using the PC as a running example of a seminal generative technology, Zittrain (2006) proposes four dimensions of generativity that together define “a function of a technology’s capacity for leverage across a range of tasks, adaptability to a range of different tasks, ease of mastery, and accessibility” (p. 1981) and that we illustrate in the context of packaged software (see Table 1).

## Applying Dimensions of Generativity to Packaged Software

Inspired by the application of the concept of modularity to source code (e.g., through function calls and routines), developers produce modular packages for reuse across multiple contexts. Modularity enables the production of new software by reusing multiple software packages. These packages can each have their own purpose and be made available in a manner that encourages their leverage in the production of other software. Consistent with modern programming patterns, such as microservices architecture (Newman 2015) in which elements are decoupled from one another to facilitate reuse, a piece of software may primarily, therefore, be written as “glue-code” (Sojer and Henkel 2010, p. 879) based on the reuse of other packages as illustrated in Figure 2.

Packaged software also typically conforms to the principles of abstraction encouraged by programming patterns (e.g., interfaces). This, in turn, fosters adaptability and ease of mastery for reuse across a variety of use cases as advocated by the concept of generic programming defined as “the idea of abstracting from concrete, efficient algorithms to obtain generic algorithms that can be combined with different data representations to produce a wide variety of useful software” (Musser and Stepanov 1988, p. i). PSR is also only achievable in practice if packages can be easily (1) located and (2) imported by reusers. Packaged software can easily be uploaded to searchable repositories (Kagdi et al. 2007). These repositories have greatly contributed to increase the accessibility and ease of mastery of packaged software because they integrate with tools that enable the resolution of package dependencies. For instance, the installation of package A may first require the installation of specific versions of packages B and C. If those dependencies are declared in the software packages and available in software repositories, they can automatically be installed, reducing search costs and increasing the efficiency of PSR. Finally, OSS packages contribute to reduce the costs associated with the reuse of software through permissive licenses, enabling accessibility. Although resources are still required to execute software (e.g., a server), it has become not only possible, but often easier to acquire and bundle software packages with computing resources without the purchase of dedicated assets (Armbrust et al. 2010) through virtualization (e.g., using venv to manage local virtual environments in Python) and cloud services that can be easily customized (e.g., using containerization techniques), furthering accessibility.

## From Generativity to Complexity

Although packaged software conforms to the four dimensions of generativity, encouraging reuse, we argue that generativity alone does not provide the conceptual apparatus required to address current knowledge gaps on PSR because it focuses on technology at the expense of the role of actors. In addition, the literature emphasizes the desirable outcomes of generative technologies without much consideration for their potential downsides (see Zittrain 2006, pp. 2003 and 2027). One exception is literature on digital platforms in which research identifies the existence of tensions between the benefits associated with generativity and the need to govern platforms to ensure their sustainability (e.g., Wareham et al. 2014, Tiwana 2015, de Lima Fonta˜o et al. 2019, Huka et al. 2020). In software ecosystems, however, these findings may not always apply because of the potential absence of platform owners and power over actors.

We argue that PSR is a complex phenomenon because it involves “many different and interconnected parts” (Oxford Dictionaries 2016). The scale of the phenomenon, the loose couplings between producers and reusers as well as other actors (e.g., repository maintainers), and the ambivalent nature of the outcomes associated with PSR call for us to adopt a perspective that can engage with these attributes at both the local (i.e., for a given piece of software) and collective levels as software ecosystems evolve on a continuous basis. Accordingly, the next section offers CAS, a branch of complexity sciences that focus on the study of “the phenomena which emerge from a collection of interacting objects” (Johnson 2009, p. 3) as a theoretical foundation to study PSR.

## A Complex Adaptive Systems Perspective of Packaged Software Reuse Complex Adaptive Systems

CAS is one of several conceptual lenses that focus on studying the origins, manifestations, and impacts of complex phenomena (Axelrod and Cohen 2000). Extend ing the contributions of systems theory (Von Bertalanffy 1972, Phelan 1999, Ryan 2008) as well as other complex ity lenses, such as complex systems (Ladyman et al. 2013), CAS assumes that a system, broadly defined as an entity composed of lower level entities that relate to one another and that interacts with its environment (Simon 1991, Ryan 2008), continuously changes through the evolution of its lower level, constitutive entities, exhibiting “system-level regularities” (Nan and Lu 2014, p. 1136). At a high level, a CAS is conceptualized as an aggregation of diverse groups of agents that share common characteristics (Holland 1995, Anderson 1999, Jones and Corner 2012, Benbya et al. 2020). Agents are loosely connected with one another, each following rules that together form a strategy that aims at achieving that agent’s objectives (e.g., survival) (Holland 1995, 2006; Axelrod and Cohen 2000; Neely 2015).

The lens of CAS and its associated methods (e.g., agent-based simulations) prove useful in several disciplines, including but not limited to management theory (Dooley 1997), operations management (Choi et al. 2001), or organization science (Anderson 1999), offering novel insights on phenomena such as the strategic renewal of firms (Albert et al. 2015), international development (Neely 2015), or mentoring relationships (Jones and Corner 2012). In IS, this lens contributes to inform our understanding of IT use (Nan 2011, Nan and Tanriverdi 2017), information systems development (Benbya and McKelvey 2006, Vidgen and Wang 2009), online communities (Nan and Lu 2014), the development of open source projects (Muffatto and Faldani 2003, Link and Germonprez 2016), and the diffusion of innovations (Nan et al. 2014) as well as the sustainability of IS alignment (Vessey and Ward 2013), among others.

The richness and diversity of the body of work on CAS has, at times, been considered challenging (Levin 1998), in part because definitions of the root concept of a complex system vary (Ryan 2008, Ladyman et al. 2013). For Simon (1991), a complex system can be described as one that is “made up of a large number of parts that interact in a nonsimple way. In such systems, the whole is more than the sum of the parts, not in an ultimate, metaphysical sense, but in the important pragmatic sense that, given the properties of the parts and the laws of their interaction, it is not a trivial matter to infer the properties of the whole” (p. 468). Building on past research, Ladyman et al. (2013) proposes a definition of a complex system as “an ensemble of many elements which are interacting in a disordered way, resulting in robust organisation and memory” (p. 57). Most descriptions of CAS incorporate elements of definitions of complex systems and augment those elements with the ability to adapt (e.g., McKelvey 1997, Levin 1998, Carmichael and Hadzˇikadic ´ 2019, Benbya et al. 2020).

While there is no single, authoritative list of applicable principles that characterize CAS (Carmichael and Hadzˇikadic ´ 2019), over the years, literature has built on the contributions of seminal works (e.g., Holland 1995, Axelrod and Cohen 2000) to highlight key principles characterizing the properties and the behaviors of CAS (e.g., Turner and Baker 2019): aggregation, diversity, hierarchy, connectedness, emergence, self-organization, adaptation, coevolution, and nonlinearity. Online Appendix 3 provides an overview of those principles.

## Applying Complex Adaptive Systems Principles to Packaged Software Reuse

In our view, these principles are particularly well-suited to address the three knowledge gaps we identify on PSR. Indeed, actors involved in software ecosystems exhibit a high degree of diversity: PSR literature identifies producers (e.g., Bogart et al. 2015) and reusers (e.g., Abdalkareem et al. 2017) as the primary actors involved in software ecosystems. At the same time, evidence of the presence of other actors, such as repository maintainers (e.g., Decan et al. 2018), software vendors, or public agencies (e.g., Vial 2022), who gravitate around software ecosystems, calls for research to study those actors to understand their role in PSR. To that effect, the nature of systems as relative, hierarchical entities allows researchers to determine whether those actors are relevant based on the conceptual boundaries of the environment in which they study PSR (e.g., within a company, an OSS community), consistent with the principle of aggregation.

Within software ecosystems, actors are loosely connected with one another, and there may not exist a central authority that dictates the nature of the interactions that are allowed among these actors. For instance, producers and reusers may only interact with one another through the reuse of packages as technical dependencies, whereas in other instances, groups of developers may communicate using digital technologies (e.g., using GitHub to report bugs or contribute source code). The formation, evolution, and self-organized nature of these connections provide a fertile terrain for emergence as actors of software ecosystems continuously adapt and coevolve (Um et al. 2022). For example, a producer may decide to move its software packages from one ecosystem to another (e.g., moving JavaScript packages from npm to an opensource repository such as Bit following npm’s purchase by GitHub, which is owned by Microsoft), bringing reusers to either alter their dependency on those packages or migrate to another ecosystem as well. From the perspective of CAS, the study of these localized moves begs the question as to whether they may reflect simple rules that lead to collective level regularities, consistent with the famous example of Langton’s boids simulating a flock of birds.

Finally, the study of PSR at the local, project level can help us understand higher level, collective patterns of self-organization based on the principles of emergence and nonlinearity. For example, if a critical bug is found in a software package, it creates a disruption for the ecosystem in which it is published: reusers must decide whether to upgrade upon a new release of that package, potentially disturbing their own product roadmap and causing other reusers of their own packages to have to upgrade as well. Local events can create a cascading need for adaptation as bugs located within a reused software package can have consequences for individual projects as well as for large parts of software ecosystems, even affecting the security of physical devices (e.g., Seals 2020; additional evidence is provided in Online Appendix 2).

Overall, literature on PSR reflects the nature of software as a hierarchical system composed of aggregated elements (Mitleton-Kelly 2003, p. 57) each produced by diverse groups of software developers (i.e., agents in the language of CAS) whose strategies and patterns of interactions vary (e.g., if they do not work on the same project or platform or within the same organization), consistent with the loose coupling associated with generativity. Unlike most literature on generativity, however, the lens of CAS does not make any a priori propositions with regards to the nature of the outcomes that occur at the local and collective levels, consistent with the ambivalence of the outcomes of PSR. Applying CAS to the context of PSR implies that packaged software are generative artifacts (Axelrod and Cohen 2000), that is, objects made of computer code that can be shared and reused by agents to produce other artifacts. These artifacts change as the agents that produce them pursue their goals and adapt their strategies to achieve those goals. At a basic level, current knowledge on the topic therefore allows us to describe PSR as a phenomenon that takes place within software ecosystems in which groups of agents depend on one another—either knowingly or unknowingly—through the production and reuse of artifacts as well as the management of repositories in which those artifacts are made available (see Figure 3).

## Packaged Software Reuse: An Agenda for IS Research

Evidence supports the idea that PSR is likely to continue to increase in relevance because of the exponential nature of the phenomenon in the context of both OSS as well as proprietary software (Zimmermann and Nagappan 2008, Decan et al. 2018). The principles of CAS provide the necessary generative, conceptual scaffolding to increase our theoretical understanding of the current nature and implications of PSR, paving the way for novel contributions that have both practical and scientific utility.

As illustrated in Figure 4, these principles allow us to conceptualize producers and reusers as well as other, lesser known actors (represented with dashed graphical elements in the figure, e.g., repository maintainers) as agents, each with its own objectives and strategies. Although interactions that occur among these groups of agents are located at a lower level of analysis, conceptualizing reuse from the lens of CAS implies that the adaptation of agents results in coevolutionary patterns leading to emergence at higher levels. To further illustrate the implications of adopting this lens to contribute to address knowledge gaps on PSR, we propose an agenda for IS research, presented in the following sections and summarized in Figure 5. We organize this agenda across three axes, each defined by the relative importance of key principles of CAS to address a specific knowledge gap. As we develop each axis, we provide illustrative research questions and discuss potential hybridization (Oswick et al. 2011, Lang ley and Sloan 2012) with other lenses (e.g., Kim and Kaplan 2011).

Types of Agents and Their Characteristics. In CAS, agents are fundamental units of analysis that are characterized by their properties, their objectives, and the strategies at their disposal to achieve these objectives (Holland 1995, Axelrod and Cohen 2000). In the context of PSR, research currently informs our knowledge of the properties of producers and reusers: producers aim at producing software packages (i.e., artifacts) that are downloaded and reused by others (e.g., Haefliger et al. 2008, Sojer and Henkel 2010). To that end, they publish their packages on repositories and advertise their progress to increase visibility for others. Reusers can be conceptualized as agents seeking to gain access to knowledge crystallized into functioning packages to improve their productivity or the quality of their own packages (Bauer and Vetro 2016). To that end, they search for packages either directly on repositories or indirectly via other means available to them (e.g., Q&A platforms, such as StackOverflow) (Bogart et al. 2021). Extant literature already provides important clues that can help us define the properties, objectives, and strategies that are instrumental to model and simulate the behavior of those agents.

Doing so, however, draws attention to the importance of aggregation and diversity as key principles of CAS. Indeed, whereas it is possible to summarize generic characteristics of producers and reusers, there are factors that call for us to account for diversity among those actors. For example, although most producers publish packages to help reusers, there is a growing problem associated with the publication of packages that contain malicious code in popular software ecosystems (e.g., Seals 2020, Goodin 2021, Brown 2022, Tung 2022). There are also important properties that need to be accounted for us to better understand and model the behaviors of producers and reusers. Some packages are produced by volunteers, whereas others are maintained by corporate entities (e.g., TensorFlow is published by Google). Oftentimes, the same groups also act as producers and reusers as the packages they publish are based on the reuse of other packages. Understanding how producers and reusers behave as agents requires that we deepen our knowl edge of their properties, objectives, and strategies to better model the reality of the reuse phenomenon, an endeavor that could start, for example, with the creation of a taxonomy (Nickerson et al. 2013, Oberla¨nder et al. 2019) of PSR actors.

In addition, interactions between producers and reusers are greatly facilitated by the existence of software repositories and the presence of other agents that channel interactions on a large scale, contributing to maintain software ecosystems at a level of complexity between order and chaos that is propitious to emergence (Tanriverdi et al. 2010, Benbya et al. 2020). For example, vulnerability disclosures often involve security researchers and other external entities (The MITRE Corporation 2021b) that have an impact on changes in software packages and patterns of PSR. Depending on the level of aggregation at which the researcher defines the area of interest on PSR (and the types of events studied), actors that are typically seen as peripheral and inconsequential may effectively contribute to significant changes in a software ecosystem or contribute to alleviate the cascading effects of certain events, helping to maintain resilience at the ecosystem level. Events documented in recent news can contribute to shape how we approach and complement extant knowledge on the nature and the roles of actors involved in PSR. Some research questions to study under this theme therefore include: (research question 1) What are the main types of actors involved in PSR? (research question 2) How do the roles assumed by actors vary based on nature of events occurring in a software ecosystem?

Adaptive Tensions Faced by Reusers and Producers. Literature showcases the existence of implicit tensions between the desirable and undesirable outcomes of PSR for producers and reusers (e.g., see Online Table A1.1). From the lens of CAS, this reveals the existence of adaptive tensions (Benbya and McKelvey 2006), highlight ing a “discrepancy between where individual agents are and where they want to be in an adaptive landscape” (Nan and Lu 2014, p. 1153). Although these tensions may exist at the local, project level, aggregation also means that they can have far-reaching, nonlinear implications for software ecosystems (e.g., see Online Appendix 2). One way to theorize on these adaptive tensions is to engage with the notion of tension and its related concepts—including dialectics, trade-offs, and paradoxes<sup>2</sup> (e.g., Lewis 2000, Putnam et al. 2016, Gaim et al. 2018) to conceptualize the ambivalent implications of the adaptation strategies avail able to producers and reusers.

Research primarily contributes to our understanding of the factors that affect the decision to reuse as well as the outcomes associated with this practice. Nevertheless, explanations on the factors that affect the selection of packages as part of the process of reuse are currently lacking. For example, changes in software licenses may create potential legal hazards for reusers such that they must find other packages to reuse or modify the design of their packages to accommodate those incompatibilities (Valim¨ aki ¨ 2005). In addition, we currently have little knowledge about the factors that affect reusers’ decision to (dis)continue reusing a given software package over time. Refactoring efforts (e.g., Sangwan et al. 2008) may aim at lowering technical debt or simplifying the architecture of a piece of software, at the same time having the side effect of increasing that software’s dependency on other packages. Although this may have a positive impact on source code readability and maintainability, it can create liabilities as a team effectively delegates code maintenance to other groups of software developers, highlighting the existence of a tension between PSR and source code ownership. As teams seek to expand the functionality of their software, they also have to decide whether to adapt by developing that functionality themselves or further engaging in reuse.

Furthermore, it is argued that, despite its high stakes and associated costs, software maintenance and evolution has received little attention in research over the years (Bennett 1996, Banker et al. 1998, Mens and Demeyer 2008). Yet, as the prevalence of PSR continues to increase, important questions arise on this topic for producers. Of specific interest is the question of the tension between backward compatibility and evolution in software packages. To continue to motivate others to (re)use their software, producers strive to ensure backward compatibility because it sustains accessibility, a key dimension of generativity. However, this may not always be a viable solution as software can reach a point at which the implementation of new features can only be performed at the expense of backward compatibility. For example, to support Unicode in Python, the creators of the programming language decided to create a new version of Python (Python3) and maintain the previous version (Python2) until it reached its official end of life on January 1, 2020. During this period of time, producers of third-party packages (e.g., Django) had to choose whether to support both versions of Python or focus exclusively on one version, thereby limiting their potential reuser base. Of crucial importance here is the fact that every producer’s decision about this tension was influenced by other agents’ (producers and reusers) decisions as well, leading to the emergence of coevolutionary patterns in the Python ecosystem.

If they maintain backward compatibility, producers incur some level of technical debt. Although this shifts the costs onto producers and ensures that reusers can continue to use a software package without having to change their own code, it creates pressure for producers to continue to maintain backward compatibility over time. Conversely, if producers break backward compatibility for a software package, they run the risk of seeing some reusers abandon that software package even if this reduces technical debt for the producer. We currently have little knowledge as to how producers and reusers manage the tensions inherent to this issue despite its importance in the context of digital platforms and ecosystems. Actors may view these adaptive tensions as series of trade-offs between new feature development and reach, or as paradoxes that require mitigation techniques to be resolved depending on their properties and objectives (e.g., if they own a platform, act as comple mentors, or are stand-alone contributors working outside of the context of digital platforms). Understanding the ramifications of changes in software packages that are part of software ecosystems can be very useful for producers seeking to determine when and how backward compatibility should be maintained (Bogart et al. 2015, 2021). We therefore propose to study the following research questions: (research question 3) How do produ cers evaluate the trade-offs between backward compatibility and feature development? (research question 4) What elements influence reusers’ decision to adapt their dependency on software packages?

Contributions of Repository Maintainers. A significant portion of the interactions between producers and reusers take place in the context of package reuse scenarios enabled by software repositories, bringing us to question the nature and the role of the actors who create and maintain those repositories. Indeed, while a minority of works explicitly acknowledge that repositories are instrumental to enable exchanges between producers and reusers who locate, acquire, and reuse those packages (e.g., Bogart et al. 2021), the fundamental role of the actors who manage these repositories has, so far, not been studied. At the same time, there is mounting evidence that repository maintainers have an important role to play to ensure the perennity of popular ecosystems (e.g., Goodin 2021). Among other things, this entails the creation and maintenance of repositories for software packages as well as tools that allow developers to interact with those repositories to publish and reuse packages. For the most part, repository maintainers have, so far, allowed software packages to be published under the assumption that producers act in good faith without taking responsibility regarding issues or vulnerabilities contained in those packages. Yet evidence points to the fact that problematic packages have important ramifications for reusers (e.g., Brown 2022, Tung 2022).

Consistent with the principle of diversity, we argue that studying repository maintainers as agents enabling interactions between producers and reusers is important as these actors enable the consolidation of small-scale software package production and reuse on a large scale, and their actions contribute to the ongoing evolution of software ecosystems. The decisions that maintainers make to implement governance mechanisms (Peterson 2004, Wareham et al. 2014) to manage repositories have direct consequences on interactions, raising questions that have important implications for research and policy. For example, can repository maintainers foster sustained evolution and avoid ecosystem stasis or decline by encouraging variety in reuse and substitutability, and by discouraging large-scale dependency on a small number of trivial packages (e.g., Abdalkareem et al. 2017) consistent with ideas from CAS? Our knowledge on these types of questions is currently limited as PSR research treats software repositories as neutral spaces enabling exchanges between producers and reusers (e.g., Decan et al. 2018), whereas IS research primarily focuses on the context of digital platforms without engaging specifically with the practice of reuse (e.g., Hukal et al. 2020). The maintainers of a repository may decide to impose certain rules to ensure that the quality of published packages meets certain standards in an effort to thwart issues related to the publication of malicious packages (Goodin 2021, Brown 2022), among others. Doing so may hinder diversity within the ecosystem as some (e.g., smaller) producers may not be able or willing to abide by these rules, leading to undesirable consequences for the entire ecosystem over time, or perhaps even questioning whether software ecosystems can lose their complexity as they move away from the edge of chaos toward order. We, thus, propose the following initial research question on this topic: (research question 5) What types of governance mechanisms are in place in software ecosystems?

## Axis 2: How Connectedness Enables Adaptation Through Signals

From the perspective of CAS, reuse is a process wherein (software) artifacts emerge and evolve as the result of the work of loosely coupled groups of agents, each with their own projects, roadmaps, and agendas. Although there is some evidence that interactions do take place between these groups of agents (Palyart et al. 2018), the nature of those interactions and their impacts are still largely unknown as it is generally assumed that producers and reusers are hardly in contact with one another, with some authors observing that “coordination is a major challenge in software ecosystems, since packages tend to be highly interdependent yet independently maintained” (Bogart et al. 2021, p. 1). In this second research axis, we propose to mobilize the principle of connectedness to study how signals between groups of agents support adaptation.

Properties of Signals. In CAS, the ability for agents to intercept signals sent by other agents is not guaranteed because those agents are loosely connected with one another. For instance, producers may release a new version of a software package and advertise the release on their project page, for example, on GitHub. However, the ability for reusers to become informed of this release is contingent upon their awareness of the existence of those signals and their commitment to intercept them when they are emitted (e.g., Singer et al. 2014). Considering that software is typically built on the assemblage— both direct and indirect—of multiple packages, there is no guarantee that reusers will receive these signals, especially if they are initiated by producers of software packages used in an indirect manner, that is, via transitive dependencies (as illustrated with the example of Hadoop in Online Appendix 2). We currently do not have a clear idea as to how reusers intercept and keep up with signals sent from producers, nor do we know how effective producers are at reaching reusers when they attempt to communicate with them. Even in contexts in which a single reuser can communicate with a producer through dedicated channels (e.g., by creating or exchanging on issues in the producer’s GitHub repository if applicable), our understanding of the properties of sig nals and their impact on the process through which these interactions can trigger adaptation of other reusers based on the decisions made by producers to act on those signals is limited. From an empirical standpoint, a first step in this research direction could be to build on existing theory, such as media synchronicity theory (Dennis et al. 2008), to classify the types of media and messages agents (e.g., producers, repository maintainers) use to communicate with one another. By disentangling the properties of signals and the channels through which they are broadcast to other agents, we gain a deeper understanding of a fundamental aspect of the interactions taking place among agents that enable adaptation in software ecosystems. Accordingly, we propose the following (research question 6) What are the main properties of the signals used by actors involved in PSR?

Impacts of Signals. Furthermore, the study of signals can help us understand actual patterns of adaptation in software ecosystems. For instance, while the development of new features is usually based on feedback from a user community or an internal development roadmap, issues can sometimes reveal the role played by developers relay ing information via social media and microblogging platforms (Singer et al. 2014, Bogart et al. 2021). Consider the following example that took place in 2021: a security bug in Log4j, one of the most widely used logging frameworks in the Java language, was publicly disclosed and nicknamed the Log4Shell vulnerability after it was discovered (The MITRE Corporation 2021b, Solomon 2021). Although a bug fix was released within days, it required reusers of that library (including software vendors who acquired that package through the reuse of other packages) to issue and deploy new versions of their own software using the fixed version of Log4j. This issue revealed the nonlinear impacts that can be observed when local level issues are found within a single software package, affecting many reusers, most of which are not directly connected to the original producers of that package. Other examples of these nonlinear manifestations include the Heartbleed security bug in OpenSSL, a critical library for the secure transmission of data across the internet (Durumeric et al. 2014) and the Spring4Shell vulnerability found in the popular Spring framework in Java (Kovacs 2022).

These examples highlight two important, understudied aspects of signals in PSR. First, as previously suggested, some PSR scenarios involve understudied groups of agents other than producers and reusers who play an important role to disseminate information in software ecosystems when certain events occur. In the example of Log4j, the official listing of the issue in the MITRE Corporation’s vulnerability registry played an instrumental role in communicating the severity of the issue to other actors, leading to widespread awareness of the vulnerability beyond the software development community, thanks to the viral diffusion of the information in the media. Second, we need to deepen our understanding as to how patterns of adaptation occur in the context of PSR following the emission of different types of signals. Current contributions remain limited to the identification of “fragile” packages in software ecosystems based on their rate of change over time (Decan et al. 2018), but our knowledge on the impacts of signals as well as how to effectively reach other groups of loosely coupled agents is still limited (Bogart et al. 2021). One way to investigate this avenue could be to measure the rate at which adaptation occurs for reusers following the release of a package, comparing the rate of adaptation based on the degree of separation from that package or based on the properties of the signal that triggered the release to inform effective strategies for diffusion of information across networks of agents. Some relevant research questions thus include (research question 7) How does the discovery of vulnerabilities in software packages influence changes in related packages? (research question 8) How can the design of signals contribute to improve adaptation from the part of re-users?

## Axis 3: Leveraging Emergence and Self-Organization to Study Software Ecosystems

Recent arguments from literature (e.g., Mikkonen and Taivalsaari 2019) support the idea that the scale of reuse facilitated by the generative properties of packaged software is unprecedented. At the same time, research remains primarily focused on studying PSR from a microlevel perspective without theorizing on macrolevel implications of PSR for software ecosystems. In the few instances in which these ecosystems are taken into consideration (e.g., Soto-Valero et al. 2021), they are generally assumed to be static contexts in which interactions between reusers and producers take place, save for a few notable exceptions in which those interactions are studied over time (Decan et al. 2018, Zerouali et al. 2019, Um et al. 2022). In practice, software ecosystems are constantly evolving as a result of the interactions that take place among diverse groups of participating actors. Consistent with this idea, the lens of CAS invites us to consider software ecosystems as dynamic, macrolevel phenomena that emerge from “selforganizing agent interactions” (Benbya and McKelvey 2006, p. 17). Accordingly, this third axis builds on the seminal importance of the principles of emergence and self-organization to inform our knowledge of software ecosystems. Specifically, we argue that conceptualizing soft ware ecosystems as dynamic, emergent structures draws our attention to two elements undergirding the generic concept of an ecosystem: (1) scale and (2) sustainability.

Software Ecosystem Scale. Oftentimes, the concept of scale is equated with the notion of size, broadly referring to an order of magnitude. While it would be possible, under the lens of CAS, to use such a measure of scale to describe a software ecosystem (e.g., by measuring the number of ties that exist between software packages as interactions between agents as a proxy for complexity and scale), doing so hides the fact that scale is inextricably linked to changes in those interactions over time. Applying a CAS lens to study PSR, thus, calls for theorizing on the concept of scale to better understand software ecosystems.

In organization studies, Bansal et al. (2018) propose scale as a construct of interest to study organizations attention to issues. The authors’ conceptual development begins with the idea that space and time play a critical role in the unfolding of processes, defined as “real struc tures and things in constant formation, reformation, and transformation over space and time that exhibit real spatial and temporal attributes and exist irrespective of their experiences” (p. 220). For example, a local weather event may span a restricted area and take place during a short period of time. However, the repetition of such an event across several areas over time may signal that a larger pattern of climate change is emerging. Conceptually, the authors define scale as “the spatial and temporal attributes of processes” (p. 220) in which spatial scale is “the geographical area in which the dominant process(es) of interest manifest” (p. 222), and temporal scale is “the patterned variations in processes over time” (p. 223).

We argue that the notions of space and time proposed by Bansal et al. (2018) are also relevant to theorize on the emergent nature of software ecosystems. While the authors conceptualize spatial scale based on physical geographical properties, we view this dimension as a (topo)- logical property characterizing PSR patterns. For example, developers may produce a software package reserved for other developers within the same organization (e.g., Zimmermann and Nagappan 2008), meaning that the potential spatial scale of PSR for that package is localized to an organization, rendering it less susceptible to exogenous shocks. Conversely, a generic package made available on public software repositories has the potential to be reused on a larger spatial scale. Spatial scale also defines logical boundaries that inform the level of interest from the par of reusers or researchers. For example, some software packages are specifically designed to help produce software for a given platform (e.g., WordPress, Microsoft Azure). Conceptually, this entails that, within a given soft ware ecosystem, agents face different yet related fitness landscapes (Waldrop 1993).

Temporal scale also plays an important role to study patterns of PSR over time. For example, the frequent release of a package informs reusers that they can update their dependency on that package for the newer version, a form of adaptation. The more frequently this takes place, the more frequently adaptation is likely to occur. However, in line with the principle of self-organization, we can reasonably hypothesize that, if these events take place at a frequency that exceeds the capacity for other agents to adapt to change, the resilience of the software ecosystem may be threatened, consistent with the law of requisite complexity (Benbya and McKelvey 2006, Boisot and Mc-Kelvey 2010). Conversely, if those events occur too rarely, local level adaptation may not occur frequently enough to maintain sufficient dynamism in the ecosystem (e.g., if reusers consider that the ecosystem has become stale because of a lack of releases from producers).

Incorporating spatial and temporal scale at a lower level of analysis can help us understand initial conditions that influence variations in patterns of self-organization and inform our understanding of software ecosystems as emergent structures, a key aspect of CAS that has, so far, remained unexplored in PSR research. Scale invites us to consider that the properties of agents and their actions influence the properties of artifacts (i.e., software packages), the potential for reuse of those artifacts as well as their coevolution. Although there are multiple ways to study this aspect of PSR, one example is to compare the temporal evolution of chains of technical dependencies between generic packages and those packages that are specifically designed to be used with an external entity (e.g., a specific digital platform) as this property can alter the potential spatial scale of reuse. This can help us understand how the presence of these external entities may indirectly breed or hinder diversity and resilience at the ecosystem level over time. Therefore, we offer the following research questions: (research question 9) What is the nature of scale in the context of software ecosystems? (research question 10) How do properties of PSR actors inform dimensions of scale in software ecosystems?

Software Ecosystem Sustainability. From the lens of CAS, software repositories enable energy transfers between agents that are instrumental to the survival of software ecosystems. At the same time, the uncoordinated nature of PSR renders those repositories susceptible to unexpected threats that can have consequences exhibiting nonlinear properties. For example, in 2016, left-pad, an npm package consisting of 11 lines of source code defining a function to add padding characters to a numerical string, was unpublished by its owner following an argument with the owners of npm. Although seemingly benign, this action broke thousands of packages that relied on the availability of left-pad in npm (the package had been downloaded more than 2.4 million times at the time), leaving the entire npm ecosystem unusable. The maintainers of npm had to quickly adapt to this shock by reimplementing left-pad’s functionality in the repository to prevent further damage (Currie and Jackson 2016, Abdalkareem et al. 2017). This event highlights the substitutability of agents in software ecosystems as supported by permissive licenses and tools that enable seamless forking of artifacts’ source code, fostering sustainability at the local, project level (Chengalur-Smith et al. 2010, Negoita et al. 2019) and resilience at the ecosystem level. These and other examples culled from recent news (e.g., Cimpanu 2019, The MITRE Corporation 2021b, Brown 2022, Kovacs 2022, Ronacher 2022, Tung 2022) provide powerful illustrations of the uncoordinated, nonlinear nature of PSR (Jackson 2019) and the dynamism of soft ware ecosystems that results from the behaviors of the actors involved in this phenomenon.

Ecosystems evolve based on self-organized interactions among agents (e.g., producers, reusers, repository maintainers) that are associated with feedback loops enabling those ecosystems to remain in a liminal state of emergence between order and chaos (Kauffman 1993, Waldrop 1993, Boisot and McKelvey 2010, Benbya et al. 2020). While, in principle, this idea is sensible, its implications for PSR research are significant. Although the concept of sustain ability and its focus on the ability to observe a balance between the production and consumption of resources in an environment is, of course, relevant to this idea, what constitutes sustainability in the context of PSR remains unclear or may vary across software ecosystems based on the values, tools, policies, and practices shared by actors (Bogart et al. 2021). We may, for example, conceptualize sustainability based on coevolutionary patterns of produc tion, reuse, or a combination thereof, consistent with the principle of self-organization. We could also incorporate properties of artifacts, such as the combination of intrinsic quality attributes of stable, reliable packages along with other cutting-edge, fast-evolving packages, which together could foster a balance between exploitative and explor atory reuse within a software ecosystem. Alternately, we can consider sustainability based on the degree of substitutability of PSR actors (e.g., through forking) regardless of the artifacts they produce, which would then depend on the properties of those actors, such as whether they are independent or working together for the same organiza tion. Although there is currently no literature on this important area of concern, we offer the following research questions as initial forays into sustainability in software ecosystems: (research question 11) What are the main features characterizing sustainability in a soft ware ecosystem? (research question 12) How do actors contribute to the sustainability of a software ecosystem?

## Concluding Remarks

In this commentary, we have drawn attention to the relevance of PSR and the potential for IS researchers to craft novel contributions accounting for the changing nature of this phenomenon. To contribute to the accumulation of scientific knowledge on this topic, we posit that packaged software is a form of generative technology and propose CAS as a conceptual foundation to engage with the nature of PSR as a large-scale phenomenon taking place among loosely coupled, heterogenous groups of actors that each have their respective properties, strategies, and objectives. This conceptualization enables the development of a generative research framework fostering the formulation of research questions that are theoretically and practically relevant and which are amenable to hybridization with other relevant concepts, such as scale (e.g., Bansal et al. 2018) or tensions (e.g., Putnam et al. 2016), among others. Owing to the nascent nature of this stream of research, some of the questions provided here inevitably focus more specifically on foundational aspects of PSR. Notwithstanding, our research agenda illuminates the potential for CAS to help address knowledge gaps on the topic and paves the way for a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics at play in the unfolding of PSR. For practice, our work highlights the oft taken-forgranted reality that a software project is rarely the work of a single team anymore. It involves multiple groups of loosely connected actors who may or may not be aware of how others reuse the software packages they produce. Being aware of this reality and understanding its implications through the lens of CAS invites practitioners to consider the long-term, global consequences of PSR beyond its immediate benefits at the local, project level. In our view, PSR is an unavoidable reality of modern software development, and striving to mitigate its potential undesirable outcomes and maximize its benefits—for projects and software ecosystems—requires a comprehensive outlook on the nature and the scale of the phenomenon.
