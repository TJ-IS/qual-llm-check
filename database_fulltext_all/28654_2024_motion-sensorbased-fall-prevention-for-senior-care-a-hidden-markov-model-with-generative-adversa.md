# Motion Sensor–Based Fall Prevention for Senior Care: A Hidden Markov Model with Generative Adversarial Network Approach

Abstract. Whereas modern medicine has enabled humans to live longer and more robust lives, recent years have seen a significant increase in chronic care costs. The prevention of threats to mobility, such as falls, freezing of gait, and others, is critical for chronic disease management. Researchers and physicians often analyze data from wearable motion sensor–based information systems (IS) to prevent falls because of their convenience, low cost, and user privacy protection. However, prior studies on fall prevention often achieve suboptimal performance because of their limited capacities in modeling data distributions. In this study, we adopt the computational design science paradigm to develop a novel fall prevention framework, which includes the hidden Markov model with generative adversarial network (HMM-GAN) that extracts temporal and sequential patterns from sensor sig nals and recognizes snippet states, and a logistic regression that utilizes the snippet states and determines whether and when to trigger protective devices to prevent fall injuries. Drawing upon the HMM, deep learning, and a new expectation-maximization instantiation, the proposed framework addresses limitations of existing methods by automatically extracting features from motion sensor data, accounting for both independent and sequen tial information in data snippets, operating on sensor signals with varying distributions and sharp peaks and valleys, allowing lead times, and being applicable in both semisupervised and supervised modes. We evaluate the proposed fall prevention framework against prevailing fall prevention models and the HMM-GAN component against state-of-the-art sensor analytics models on selected large-scale ground truth data sets containing thousands of falls and normal activities. Through an in-depth case study, we demonstrate how the proposed framework can lead to significantly reduced potentially catastrophic falls by senior citizens and produce more than \$33 million of economic benefits over competing models. Besides practical health information technology contributions, HMM-GAN offers methodological contributions to the IS knowledge base for scholars designing novel information technology artifacts for healthcare applications.

## 1. Introduction

Modern medicine has enabled humans to combat deadly diseases and live robust lives well into their 60s, 70s, and 80s. However, the cost of chronic care has been steadily increasing. Effective interventions to prevent, detect, and manage chronic conditions and their related threats to mobility have attracted interest from practitioners, researchers, and health policymakers because of their potentially significant implications for senior care (Lin et al. 2017). Fall prevention, in particular, has garnered considerable interest for several reasons. First, falls are closely related to numerous chronic conditions, including Parkinson’s disease and diabetes (Immonen et al. 2020). Preventing falls is an important goal for managing such chronic conditions. Second, recent years have seen fall events emerge as a leading cause of injury and death among senior citizens. There were 54.1 million U.S. citizens (16.5% of the total population) aged 65 and over in 2019 (U.S. Census Bureau 2020), among whom approximately one third fall each year (Jehu et al. 2021). Finally, in 2015, fall events cost approximately \$50 billion in the United States in fatal and nonfatal damages (Florence et al. 2018). This significant societal issue motivates numerous physicians and health researchers to prevent falls promptly and unobtrusively.

Information systems (IS) scholars have demonstrated that wearable motion sensors can play a pivotal role in monitoring the status of chronically ill patients, predicting adverse events, and preventing the onset of chronic diseases (Bardhan et al. 2020, Yu et al. 2022). The demand for motion sensor–based and artificial intelligence–equipped smart health systems for senior care surged during the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic. Motion sensors are also changing what care is being delivered (e.g., precision medicine) and how and where care is delivered (e.g., remote monitoring) (Grewal et al. 2020). They can produce up to 200 data points per second—far beyond the rate of other healthcare-related data (e.g., health records). Moreover, motion sensor data are streaming and high-velocity and have three levels of granularity (Figure 1): (1) a data point is a triaxial acceleration value with three orthogonal components, (2) a snippet is a temporal sequence of data points within a predefined time window, and (3) a sample is a temporal sequence of all data points collected in an experimental trial (e.g., a complete fall) comprising multiple snippets.

Because a single data point contains limited motion information and sample-level analysis usually does not pinpoint the exact timing of falls, prevailing analytical approaches rely on snippet-level analysis. Two categories of snippet-level information are often leveraged for fall prevention: independent and sequential. Independent information comprises features within snippets (e.g., the standard deviation of x-axis acceleration values). Sequential information captures how a current snippet’s state influences the following snippet (formal definition in Online Appendix A). The prevailing approach accounting for both independent and sequential information is applying a hidden Markov model (HMM) with or without Gaussian mixture models (GMM) in addition to the features extracted within snippets (Liang et al. 2018, Xu et al. 2021). However, extracting features within snippets often requires ad hoc and manual feature engineering. Moreover, GMMs in HMM-GMM often suffer when the real data contain sharp peaks and valleys (Johnson et al. 2016). Although deep learning (DL) models have been proposed in sensor analytics to address these limitations, most do not support a lead time between when the model recognizes an upcoming fall and when the fall occurs, which is critical for successfully triggering protective devices and preventing fall injuries. In addition, most studies operate only in a supervised mode, which can involve high annotation costs to develop ground truths. These challenges motivate a novel health information technology (HIT) to prevent chronic condition–related falls.

Designing novel information technology (IT) artifacts (e.g., models, techniques, and systems) to address practical needs is a core focus of IS research (Hevner et al. 2004). This stream of design research is becoming increasingly relevant given the surging interest in internet of things and predictive analytics research, particularly for HIT (Bardhan et al. 2020). The research motivations are often to obtain valuable insights through the development of advanced analytics techniques (e.g., DL) and the use of large and rich data sources that were previously unavailable or underutilized (e.g., motion sensor data) (Lin et al. 2017). Therefore, in this study, we adopt the computational design science paradigm (Rai 2017) to develop and evaluate a novel fall prevention framework with two stages: (1) a novel hidden Markov model with generative adversarial network (HMM-GAN) that extracts temporal and sequential patterns from sensor signals and recognizes snippet states (e.g., normal, collapse, impact, or inactivity) and (2) a logistic regression (LR) that utilizes the snippet states and determines whether and when to trigger protective devices to prevent fall injuries with sufficient lead times. Drawing upon HMM operations and emerging DL methods based on adversarial learning, the proposed HMM-GAN addresses limitations in healthcare predictive analytics by automatically extracting features from sensor signals with varying distributions and peaks and valleys. The proposed HMM-GAN includes a new expectation-maximization (EM) instantiation, which enables the joint learning of HMM-GAN parameters in both semisupervised and supervised modes. We evaluate our fall prevention framework against prevailing fall prevention models on large-scale data sets. Because HMM-GAN is designed to extract temporal and sequential patterns from sensor signals and is applicable to other sensor analytics applications (such as fall detection and activity of daily living (ADL) recognition), we also evaluate HMM-GAN’s performance against state-of-the-art sensor analytics models proposed in computer science (CS) and IS. Finally, we illustrate the potential economic benefits of the proposed approach. The major methodological contributions of this work are:

1. A novel HMM-GAN model that replaces the conventional HMM’s GMM with entropic GAN to extract temporal and sequential patterns from sensor signals for more accurate recognition of snippet states and improved prevention of falls. Despite its main application, this model is generalizable to other motion sensor–based applications studied by IS scholars in the past, such as recognizing ADLs and detecting falls after they occur.

2. A new instantiation of the EM algorithm, specifically designed for HMM-GAN, to enable the joint learning of HMM-GAN parameters in a semisupervised mode. We also present how the instantiation can be adapted in a supervised mode.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we review IS literature on HIT and computational design science, motion sensor–based fall prevention, and GANs. Second, we identify key research gaps from our review and pose research questions. Third, we detail our research design and testbeds. We then summarize the experiment results and follow up with our contributions to the IS knowledge base and their implications for practice. Finally, we highlight promising directions for future research and conclude this paper.

## 2. Literature Review

Three literature streams guide this research. First, we review IS literature on HIT and computational design science to examine gaps in and prescribed guidelines for developing novel IT artifacts for data-driven research inquiries. Second, we examine motion sensor–based fall prevention studies to identify the unique data characteristics of motion sensor data and how prevailing fallprevention methods operate. Finally, we review GANs to understand how they automatically learn data distributions and features. As part of this review, we also identify GAN variants that can be combined with HMMs for fall prevention.

## 2.1. IS Literature on HIT and Computational Design Science

HIT is “a broad concept that encompasses an array of technologies to store, share, and analyze health information” (Baird et al. 2018, p. 1). Recent years have seen IS scholars leverage their methodological expertise to examine HIT usage, investment, implementation, impacts, and more. Selected recent HIT research based on examined HIT, focus, and thematic cluster can be found in Online Appendix B. Most research examines human behavior, such as HIT adoption and ease of use, via theoretical frameworks. Recently, predictive healthcare analytics has analyzed hospital readmissions (Bardhan et al. 2015), adverse events for chronic conditions (Lin et al. 2017), and protections against privacy disclosure (Li and Sarkar 2014). With the rapid advancement of sensor technology, Bardhan et al. (2020) points out that wearable motion sensors can play a pivotal role in monitoring the status of chronically ill patients, predicting adverse events, and preventing the onset of chronic diseases, which necessitates the consistent development of novel algorithms and systems. The computational design science paradigm provides concrete prescriptions for designing novel algorithms, computational models, and systems to resolve practical and emerging business problems (Rai 2017). This paradigm suggests that scholars develop novel IT artifacts inspired by their problem domain’s unique characteristics. Artifact novelty, validity, and utility must then be rigorously evaluated using benchmark evaluations against competing state-of-the-art methods and selected demonstrations.

IS scholars have utilized motion sensors and the computational design science paradigm in topics such as activities of daily living recognition with interactive convolutional neural network (CNN) (Zhu et al. 2021), human identification with transfer learning (Zhu et al. 2020), fall detection with hierarchical attention–based convolutional neural network (Yu et al. 2021), and chronic condition severity assessment with deep multi source multitask learning (Yu et al. 2022). However, preventing chronic condition–related threats to mobility, such as falls, remains a critically needed yet understudied aspect of IS literature. We next review motion sensor–based fall prevention to understand the problem characteristics and state-of-the-art models.

## 2.2. Motion Sensor–Based Fall Prevention

Recently, sensor analytics has attracted scholars’ attention not only in IS but also in CS communities. For example, Chen et al. (2021) summarize various fields related to sensor-based human activity recognition and group relevant models into four categories: (1) timefrequency-spectral, (2) recurrent neural network (RNN), (3) CNN, and (4) hybrid, all of which are deep learning models. Although falls can be considered a special type of human activity, preventing them requires an understanding of their unique characteristics. Therefore, we discuss such characteristics next.

Fundamentally, a fall occurs when a person inadvertently comes to rest on a lower level (e.g., floor, ground, etc.) than intended. A fall comprises three phases: collapse, impact, and inactivity (Yu et al. 2017). Collapse is when a person loses balance and accelerates toward a lower level. Impact occurs when the person contacts the lower level. Inactivity is when a person cannot get up after impact (Saadeh et al. 2019).

Fall detection and prevention are the two major aspects of fall management. Fall detection aims to identify a fall on or after the impact phase and is more extensively studied than fall prevention. By detecting a fall, timely assistance can be dispatched to avoid long-lie situations. In contrast, fall prevention is built upon fall prediction, which aims to identify a fall before the impact phase. To prevent falls, a system must fully activate protective devices before the person approaches the floor (i.e., an impact occurs). To achieve this goal, three types of latencies should be considered: data collection and transmission, model execution, and protective device activation (e.g., airbag inflation). A fall is successfully prevented only if the system can trigger the protective device before the initial impact and the lead time between triggering and impact is greater than or equal to the sum of the preceding latencies. The need for lead times makes fall prevention unique among sensor analytics applications.

Fall prevention potentially offers more value by reducing or eliminating related physical injuries. Therefore, we summarize major motion sensor–based fall prevention literature in Online Appendix C based on the analytical granularity (data point, snippet, or sample), exploited snippet information (independent or sequential), lead time (the time between when the model recognizes an upcoming fall and when the fall occurs), learning type (supervised, semisupervised, or manually tuned or set), and models.

Many recent studies prevent falls at the snippet level. Using snippets instead of data points can provide a more accurate prediction of upcoming falls. A single data point in motion sensor data is a triaxial acceleration value, and such data points are generated at very short intervals (e.g., 200 data points per second). A single data point contains very little information about a person’s motor status. This necessitates extracting features from a series of data points (i.e., snippets) for subsequent machine learning algorithms. The lead times vary significantly across different studies from 40 to 700 milliseconds; Gelmini et al. (2020) suggest that the lead time should be at least 300 milliseconds to ensure that the protective devices can be fully activated. Another critical observation is that most prior studies on fall prevention rely on supervised learning, in which all samples in the training data set are manually annotated. Whereas supervised learning can often achieve more desirable accuracy for predictions, annotation costs limit its application. Whereas unsupervised learning does not require labeled data, it lacks predictive power. Semisupervised learning combines the advantages of supervised and unsupervised learning: it can leverage data sets with limited annotations to predict labels for unseen data.

Thus, semisupervised learning is especially beneficial for motion sensor–based fall management (Fahmi et al. 2012, Liu et al. 2019) given the high-velocity nature of such data being generated and the difficulty of completing manual annotations.

The prevailing approach extracts features from independent snippets and then applies a threshold-based method or inputs the features into classifiers, such as support vector machines (SVMs), decision trees, or deep learning–based algorithms (e.g., CNN). Common features include the vertical velocity, sum of acceleration magnitudes, and standard deviation of acceleration vectors. Whereas valuable, independently processing temporally dependent data misses state transition information (e.g., if the current state is “impact,” then the next state is likely to be “inactivity”). Sequential snippet information is commonly captured using sequential models such as an RNN or HMM (Khan et al. 2014, Lim et al. 2014, Cao et al. 2016, Yu et al. 2017). HMM is preferred over RNNs or CNNs as the decision process of HMM is fully observable via its transition matrix and observation models.

HMM is a particular case of a Bayesian network that models a Markov process and has been used for decades in numerous temporal pattern recognition applications (Rabiner 1989). An HMM for fall prevention models a sequence of hidden states, in which the states can be the three phases of a fall (collapse, impact, and inactivity) plus a normal state (i.e., no fall is in process). To prevent falls, protective devices can be triggered upon the analysis of a series of states (e.g., a transition from “normal” to “collapse”). Each state uses a probabilistic distribution to describe the likelihood that a snippet is an instance of that state. Most studies adopt GMMs as HMM’s generation model as they can model continuous observations without manual thresholding (Piyathilaka and Kodagoda 2013, Cao et al. 2016). GMMs calculate the likelihood that sensor signal observations are generated from a combination of normal distributions.

The Baum–Welch algorithm is an instantiation of the EM algorithm that trains HMM by jointly obtaining state transition probabilities and GMM parameters (Rabiner 1989, Bilmes 1998). As with other EM algorithms, Baum– Welch uses E- and M-steps to obtain the maximum likelihood estimate of HMM parameters. The E-step calculates the expected likelihood based on the probability of states at time t with current and previous observations (i.e., for ward procedure) and the probability of future observations given current states at each time t (i.e., backward procedure). The M-step updates HMM parameters to maximize the expected likelihoods (Rabiner 1989). After convergence, the HMM-GMM is trained to identify hidden states from new snippet observations. Semisupervised HMM is proposed to handle data sets with both labeled and unlabeled data. The main idea is to (1) pretrain the HMM with labeled data, (2) apply the pretrained model on unlabeled data to infer their labels, and (3) use the labeled data and initially unlabeled data with inferred labels together to retrain the pretrained HMM. This approach is widely used in past studies $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Tamposis et al. 2019).

Although HMM-GMMs can model state transition information, there are two key drawbacks. First, most HMM studies manually engineer features to model independent information within snippets. This includes those employed in IS research, such as modeling user contributions in online communities (Chen et al. 2018), recommender systems (Zhang et al. 2016), customer usage behavior (Lee et al. 2017), and healthcare (Kwon et al. 2017). Feature engineering is often an ad hoc and labor-intensive process. Moreover, the features produced from a feature engineering exercise may not generalize across domains (LeCun et al. 2015). Second, applying GMMs as an observation model assumes the data follow a mixture of Gaussian distributions. However, sensor signal data often contain numerous peaks and valleys. Including additional GMM components can potentially capture these spikes but often causes model overfitting and can limit model generalizability (Lindsay 1995, Huang et al. 2017). These issues motivate an alternative generative model to capture observation distributions without manual feature engineering or GMMs automatically. Deep generative models (DGMs), a branch of deep learning, can help address these issues.

## 2.3. GANs and Entropic GANs

DL methods use multiple layers of artificial neural networks with nonlinear transformation (i.e., activation) functions, backpropagation, and error correction computations to automatically learn features from data (LeCun et al. 2015). Deep discriminative models, such as CNNs and RNNs, use labeled data sets to learn an optimal function mapping between input data and output labels. In contrast, DGMs, such as GANs and variational autoencoders, learn the underlying distribution of a data set to generate synthetic data. GANs’ parallelizability, lower computational cost, speed, and quality of generated data make them the prevailing DGM in CS and IS literature (Goodfellow 2016, Ebrahimi et al. 2022). GAN applies adversarial training, in which a generative G and a discriminative D network compete in a zero-sum game. Network G aims to learn a data set’s distribution and generate synthetic data that resembles real data as its output. Network D aims to discriminate between real and synthetic data. Network G and D are improved alternately in iterations of the adversarial training process. Within healthcare, GANs have successfully generated synthetic brain magnetic resonance imaging (Yu et al. 2019), chest X-rays (Madani et al. 2018), retinal images (Mahapatra et al. 2019), and patient records (Choi et al. 2017).

GAN is a promising DGM that could address the limitations of GMMs in HMMs for motion sensor–based fall prevention. However, GANs cannot directly replace an HMM’s GMMs as they do not generate the observation likelihoods that the EM algorithm requires to train an HMM. Balaji et al. (2019) propose entropic GANs, a GAN variant with an optimal transport loss function and Shannon entropy regularization, to address this issue. They prove that, in entropic GANs, the real observation likelihoods can be well-approximated by the so-called surrogate likelihoods. The surrogate likelihood for a new observation $y ^ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ is the sum of three factors. The first factor is the expected distance of $y ^ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ to the learned model distribution. A larger distance implies a smaller likelihood that $y ^ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ is generated by GAN. The second factor is the Shannon entropy of the input random variable conditioned on $y ^ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ (the coupled random variable). A larger entropy implies that the variable has larger randomness and a larger likelihood that $y ^ { \mathrm { n e w } }$ is generated by GAN. The final factor is the likelihood of the variable. Considering these three factors, the surrogate likelihoods approximate the observation likelihoods. However, it is unclear how the calculation of surrogate likelihoods of entropic GANs can be used as a component for HMMs, especially semisupervised HMMs. In addition, Baum–Welch is designed for jointly training HMM-GMM and other common HMM variants but not for use with DL-based models. For instance, Baum– Welch is not able to update GAN parameters. Therefore, a new EM instantiation is needed for HMM-GAN parameter learning.

## 2.4. Research Gaps and Questions

We identify several research gaps based on our literature review. First, most sensor analytics models do not support lead times, whereas most motion sensor–based fall prevention approaches that support lead times employ manual feature engineering or simple thresholds. They either utilize independent snippets only or build sequential models, such as HMMs, but with limited modeling capacities (e.g., discretized or GMM observation distributions). Both are suboptimal for temporal sensor signal data. Whereas GANs can address these limitations, the Baum–Welch algorithm cannot be directly applied to learn GAN parameters. Consequently, a new instantiation of the EM algorithm for jointly learning GAN and HMM parameters is required. Although semisupervised HMMs are proposed, to the best of our knowledge, no prior studies examine how semisupervised HMMs can work with DGMs, such as GANs. The vast number of parameters in GANs require a revision in conventional semisupervised HMM training methods. Based on these research gaps, we pose the following questions for study:

1. How can HMM be combined with GAN to capture independent and sequential information from varying data distributions without manual feature engineering?

2. How can a new EM instantiation be developed to learn GAN and HMM parameters jointly?

3. How can HMM-GAN parameters be learned in a semisupervised mode?

4. How can the states inferred from the integrated HMM-GAN model be used to trigger protective devices and prevent falls with lead times?

## 3. Research Design and Testbed

We carefully designed a novel fall prevention research framework (Figure 2) to address the identified research gaps and posed research questions. This framework consists of three major components: (1) data collection and preprocessing; (2) a two-stage fall-prevention model consisting of the proposed HMM-GAN with its parameter learning algorithm and logistic regression; and (3) experiments on snippet state inference, fall prevention triggering, data distribution learning, and a case study. We summarize each major component of our proposed framework in the following sections.

## 3.1. Data Collection and Preprocessing

We use two publicly available data sets, the TST fall detection data set v2 (TST for short) (Gasparrini et al. 2015) and SisFall data set (Sucerquia et al. 2017), as research testbeds. Both are commonly used as ground truth data sets for fall-prevention research (Casilari et al. 2017, Sucerquia et al. 2018). We employ multiple data preprocessing and labeling steps consistent with best practices in motion sensor–based fall-prevention literature (Kerdegari et al. 2012, Yu et al. 2017). First, each sample in both data sets is segmented into 80-millisecond snippets. These snippets help precisely pinpoint when falls occur. Second, each segmented snippet is labeled normal, collapse, impact, or inactivity. For SisFall, each data point already has an accompanying normal, collapse, or impact label in the data set. However, in SisFall, all the data points in the normal activities and the inactivity state after a fall are labeled as normal. As the inactivity state is a critical indicator of the long-lie situation and should be correctly recognized, we relabeled all normal data points occurring after impact labels as inactivity for each SisFall sample. Then, each SisFall snippet label was determined by a majority vote of the accompanying data point labels in that snippet. For instance, a SisFall snippet containing 12 collapse data points and four impact data points was labeled as a collapse snippet. No manual annotation was involved; the entire annotation process was automated.

Unlike SisFall, the data points in TST do not have accompanying labels. However, each activity performed in TST has an accompanying video. This allowed us to label the data feasibly. We recruited two graduate students experienced in health research as annotators. Each annotator was asked to independently watch videos corresponding to TST samples and annotate each segmented 80-millisecond snippet with one of normal, collapse, impact, or inactivity labels. Cohen’s kappa, a widely used statistic for interrater reliability, was 0.9781, which shows excellent agreement between the two annotators. After the initial round of annotation, the two annotators met to discuss and resolve disagreements. The resolved snippet labels were used as TST ground truth. The entire annotation process was conducted over a week. We summarize the descriptive information and number of labeled snippets in Table 1.

## 3.2. Two-Stage HMM-GAN Fall-Prevention Model

3.2.1. Stage 1: Snippet State Inference. In stage 1, we aim to automatically infer each snippet’s state label (normal, collapse, impact, or inactivity). As noted in the literature review, extant approaches for modeling motion sensor data have three critical drawbacks. First, existing models for fall prevention rarely comprehensively lever age information within and between snippets. Second, the Gaussian mixture assumption of HMM-GMM is inflexible to alternate distributions and challenging for modeling sharp sensor signal peaks and valleys. Finally, manually engineering features can be an ad hoc and labor-intensive process. We propose a novel HMM-GAN model with two major components to address these limitations: (1) an HMM state transition matrix and (2) an entropic GAN to replace the conventional GMM in each HMM state. The HMM state transition matrix models the sequential information across snippets. Replacing GMMs with entropic GANs enables the proposed HMM-GAN to capture independent and sequential information, bypass manual feature engineering efforts, and automatically learn underlying data distributions from motion sensor data. An entropic GAN has a generative network that generates synthetic snippets and two discriminative networks: one for real and one for synthetic snippets. Details can be found in Online Appendices E and F. We compare the conventional HMM-GMM to the proposed HMM-GAN in Figure 3(a) and (b), respectively.

The proposed HMM-GAN operates as follows. Assuming the time stamp is τ, the HMM state at τ is $x _ { \tau } ,$ , the snippet at τ is $\pmb { y } _ { \tau } ,$ and the probability that $x _ { \tau }$ is state k is represented by the surrogate likelihood of ${ \pmb y } _ { \tau }$ generated by the GAN for state k. As suggested by Balaji et al. (2019), the surrogate likelihood is determined by three factors: (1) the expected distance of $y _ { \tau }$ to the distribution learned by the GAN for state $k , ( 2 )$ the entropy of the coupled random variable, and (3) the likelihood of the coupled random variable. We denote the distribution of the coupled random variable as $\mathbb { P } _ { z | y _ { \tau } } ^ { * }$ . As suggested by Balaji et al. (2019), the first factor is computed as $\mathbb { E } _ { \mathbb { P } _ { \tau | u } ^ { * } } \left( \ell ( \pmb { y } _ { \tau } , G _ { k } ^ { * } ( z ) ) / \lambda \right)$ , where $G _ { k } ^ { * } ( \cdot )$ is the generative net-<sup>τ</sup>work for state $k , \ \ell$ is the loss function defined as $\ell ( { \pmb y } _ { \tau } , G ^ { * } ( z ) ) = \ \| { \pmb y } _ { \tau } - G ^ { * } ( z ) \| _ { 2 } ,$ λ is the weight for the entropy regulation in the entropic GAN, and $z$ is the input vector randomly sampled from a uniform distribution. The second factor is computed as $H ( \mathbb { P } _ { z | y _ { \tau } } ^ { * } ) _ { . }$ , where H is the Shannon entropy. The third factor is computed as $\mathbb { E } _ { \mathbb { P } _ { z | v _ { \tau } } ^ { * } } ( - \Vert z \Vert ^ { 2 } / 2 )$ . As such, the surrogate likelihood, $p ( x _ { \tau } = k | y _ { \tau } ) .$ , can be lower bounded as

$$
\begin{array}{c} p (x _ {\tau} = k \mid \boldsymbol {y} _ {\tau}) \geq \exp (- \mathbb {E} _ {\mathbb {P} _ {z | y _ {\tau}} ^ {*}} (\ell (\boldsymbol {y} _ {\tau}, G _ {k} ^ {*} (\boldsymbol {z})) / \lambda) + H (\mathbb {P} _ {\boldsymbol {z} | \boldsymbol {y} _ {\tau}} ^ {*}) \\ + \mathbb {E} _ {\mathbb {P} _ {z | y _ {\tau}} ^ {*}} (- \| \boldsymbol {z} \| ^ {2} / 2) + C _ {k}), \end{array}
$$

where $C _ { k }$ is a constant. We denote the lower bound of the surrogate likelihood as $\hat { p } ( x _ { \tau } = k | y _ { \tau } )$ . Suggested by Balaji et al. (2019), $\mathbb { P } _ { z | y , } ^ { * }$ is computed as $\mathbb { P } _ { z | y _ { \tau } } ^ { * } = \mathbb { P } _ { \mathbf { z } } \exp ( D _ { 1 } ^ { * } ( y )$ $- D _ { 2 } ^ { * } ( G ^ { * } ( z ) ) - \ell ( y _ { \tau } , G ^ { * } ( z ) ) / \lambda )$ , where $D _ { 1 } ^ { * } ( \cdot )$ and $D _ { 2 } ^ { * } ( \cdot )$ are the two discriminative networks and $G ^ { * } ( \cdot )$ is the generative network. In this way, we can compute a likelihood $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \hat { p } ( x _ { \tau } = k | y _ { \tau } ) )$ for each snippet generated by the entropic GAN.

Given a new sample with snippets up to time $\tau ,$ denoted as $y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , \ldots , y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime }$ , we aim to infer the most likely state at time $\tau ,$ denoted as $x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } { } ^ { * }$ . We utilize the sequential and independent information for inference. Therefore, the current observation $y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime }$ as well as the previous snippets $y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , \ldots , y _ { \tau - 1 } ^ { \prime }$ are utilized in the calculation. $x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } { } ^ { * }$ is given by ${ \overline { { { x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } } } } } ^ { * } = \operatorname { a r g m a x } _ { k } ( p ( x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } = k | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , . . . , y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } ) ) . p ( x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } = k | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , . . . , y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } )$ can be expressed by (1) the probability of the last state $p ( x _ { \tau - 1 } ^ { \prime } = i | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , . . . , y _ { \tau - 1 } ^ { \prime } )$ , (2) the probability of the current state conditioned on the last state (represented by the elements in the HMM transition matrix), and (3) the surrogate likelihood from the entropic GAN. As shown in Online Appendix G,

$$
\begin{array}{c} p (x _ {\tau} ^ {\prime} = k | \boldsymbol {y} _ {1} ^ {\prime}, \ldots , \boldsymbol {y} _ {\tau} ^ {\prime}) = \\ \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} p (x _ {\tau - 1} ^ {\prime} = i | \boldsymbol {y} _ {1} ^ {\prime} , \ldots , \boldsymbol {y} _ {\tau - 1} ^ {\prime}) p (\boldsymbol {y} _ {t} ^ {\prime} | G _ {(w _ {k})}) A _ {i , k}}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {K} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} p (x _ {\tau - 1} ^ {\prime} = i | \boldsymbol {y} _ {1} ^ {\prime} , \ldots , \boldsymbol {y} _ {\tau - 1} ^ {\prime}) p (\boldsymbol {y} _ {t} ^ {\prime} | G _ {(w _ {j})}) A _ {i , j}}, \end{array}
$$

where $A _ { i , k }$ is the transition probability from state i to state k and $G _ { ( w _ { k } ) }$ is the GAN corresponding to state $k .$ Because $p ( x _ { \tau - 1 } ^ { \prime } = i | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , . . . , y _ { \tau - 1 } ^ { \prime } )$ takes the identical form as $p ( x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } = k | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , \ldots , y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } )$ , it can be recursively computed. The boundary condition is $p ( x _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } = k | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } ) = \frac { p ( y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } | G _ { ( w _ { k } ) } ) \pi _ { k } } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { K } p ( y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } | G _ { ( w _ { i } ) } ) \pi _ { i } } ,$ where $\pi _ { k }$ is the probability that the initial state is k. After solving $p ( x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } = k | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , \ldots , y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } )$ ∀k, we can obtain $x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } =$ $\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname { \mathrm { 1 r g m a x } } _ { k } ( p ( x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } = k | y _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } , \dots , y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } ) ) } \end{array}$ ). This represents the most likely state $x _ { \tau } ^ { \prime } { } ^ { * }$ for the observed snippet $y _ { \tau } ^ { \prime }$ .

3.2.2. EM-based HMM-GAN Parameter Learning Algorithm. We devise a new EM instantiation to jointly learn the HMM transition matrix and GAN parameters in the semisupervised mode. The training data set Y is split into two portions: labeled samples, ${ \tilde { Y } } ,$ , and unlabeled samples, Y<sup>¯</sup> . We denote the accompanying state label sequence for the labeled samples as $\tilde { X }$ . The goal of learning the HMM-GAN parameters $\mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } } \mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } } \mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } } \mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } } \mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } } \mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } } \mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } } \mathbf { \delta } _ { \mathbf { \theta } }$ is to maximize the likelihood function $p ( { \tilde { X } } , { \tilde { Y } } , { \tilde { Y } } | \pmb { \theta } )$ . However, this optimization is difficult because of the involvement of the unlabeled samples’ state label sequences, $\bar { X } ,$ , which cannot be observed directly. To solve this issue, we instantiate the EM algorithm, a common method for solving optimization problems with unobservable variables, on HMM-GAN. The instantiation comprises iterations of an E- and an M-step. In the E-step, we treat the parameters as constants (denoted as $\pmb { \theta } ^ { \mathrm { o l d } } )$ to identify the posterior distribution of X <sup>¯</sup> and compute the completedata log likelihood function $Q ( \pmb \theta , \pmb \theta ^ { \mathrm { o l d } } )$ . In the M-step, is updated by maximizing $Q ( \pmb \theta , \pmb \theta ^ { \mathrm { o l d } } )$ . The two steps repeat until converges. Details of the EM instantiation can be found in Online Appendix E1.

The proposed HMM-GAN can also operate in a supervised mode. The supervised mode is a special case of the semisupervised mode with all snippets having accompanying state labels, that $\mathrm { i s } , \tilde { X } = X , \tilde { Y } = Y .$ . As the snippet states are observable, we only use the M-step to learn the parameters as we do not need the E-step to infer the hidden state labels. Full details appear in Online Appendix E2.

3.2.3. Stage 2: Fall Prevention. Although HMM-GAN generates snippet state labels, it does not directly identify when the protective devices should be triggered. Thus, a decision-making process is necessary based on the state label information to prevent falls. To achieve this, we employ a classifier to determine whether the protective devices should be triggered at snippet τ based on the snippet state labels of snippets $\tau , \tau - 1 .$ $\dots , \tau - J + 1$ , that ${ \mathrm { i } } s ,$ the $J$ most recent snippet state labels. After testing and comparing logistic regression, autoregressive integrated moving average, SVM, long short-term memory (LSTM), and gated recurrent unit (details in Online Appendix H), we selected logistic regression because of its effectiveness and efficiency and empirically chose the value of $J = 5$ to balance model performance and model execution latency (a larger J may lead to longer model execution latency). A sensitivity analysis of J can be found in Online Appendix I. The probability of triggering the protective devices at snippet τ is as follows:

$$
p (\mathrm{trigger} _ {\tau}) = \frac {1}{1 + \exp \left(- \left(\beta_ {0} + \sum_ {i = \tau - J + 1} ^ {\tau} \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {\tau - i + 1} ^ {\mathrm{T}} \boldsymbol {h} _ {i}\right)\right)},
$$

where $h _ { i }$ is a one-hot vector corresponding to the state inferred by HMM-GAN for snippet i, that is,

$$
\boldsymbol {h} _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} [ 1   0   0   0 ] ^ {\mathrm{T}}, \text {if x_{i} ^{\prime*}} \text {is normal}, \\ [ 0   1   0   0 ] ^ {\mathrm{T}}, \text {if x_{i} ^{\prime*}} \text {is collapse}, \\ [ 0   0   1   0 ] ^ {\mathrm{T}}, \text {if x_{i} ^{\prime*}} \text {is impact}, \\ [ 0   0   0   1 ] ^ {\mathrm{T}}, \text {if x_{i} ^{\prime*}} \text {is inactivity}, \end{array} \right.
$$

$\beta _ { 0 }$ is a parameter, and $\pmb { \beta } _ { j } ( j = 1 , \ldots , J )$ are vector parameters to be trained.

We preserve the sequential information in the order of independent variables, $h _ { i } .$ . For example, $\pmb { \beta } _ { 1 }$ describes how $h _ { \mathit { \tau } _ { \tau } }$ , the most recent snippet state, affects whether to trigger the protective device at snippet τ, whereas $\pmb { \beta } _ { J }$ describes how $h _ { \tau - J + 1 . }$ , the least recent snippet state, affects whether to trigger the protective device at snippet τ. The order of $\boldsymbol { h } _ { \mathit { \tau } _ { \tau } } , \boldsymbol { h } _ { \mathit { \tau } _ { \tau - 1 } , \cdot \cdot \cdot , \boldsymbol { h } _ { \tau - J + 1 } }$ is not exchangeable.

## 3.3. End-to-End Illustrative Example: HMM-GAN for Fall Prevention

This section provides illustrative end-to-end examples of how our proposed model operates for fall prevention using motion sensor data. An example of model training can be found in Online Appendix J. When applied in real environments, every time a model receives a snippet, it makes a fall-prevention decision based on the current and all previous snippets (Figure 4):

• Phase 1: A new snippet of acceleration data are received.

• Phase 2: A trained HMM-GAN infers the probability of each possible state label (normal (N), collapse (C), impact (I), inactivity (A)) for the segmented snippets.

• Phase 3: The probability values of the four state labels are compared, and the state label with the highest probability is chosen to be the state label of that particular snippet.

• Phase 4: A logistic regression model processes the five most recent snippet state labels and decides whether to trigger the protective devices (+) or not (�), allowing sufficient lead times.

To assess whether the fall is successfully prevented, we calculate the lead time, $t _ { l } ,$ between the first time the model decides to trigger the protective devices and the initial impact. If $t _ { l } \ge t _ { m }$ (the minimum lead time), we consider the protective devices fully activated, and the fall is prevented. Note that $t _ { m }$ covers the latencies for data collection and transmission, model execution, and protective device activation.

## 4.2. Experiment 2 Results: Fall Prevention

In experiment 2, we benchmarked HMM-GAN with LR’s ability of triggering protective devices to prevent falls against state-of-the-art methods. HMM-GAN + LR is evaluated against these benchmarks on both data sets in semisupervised and supervised modes. Recall that we consider a fall is successfully prevented only when (1) the sample is a fall sample and (2) the lead time, $t _ { l } ,$ is greater than or equal to the minimum lead time, $t _ { m } = 3 2 0$ milliseconds. We present the results of this experiment in Table 3. The best results appear in boldface. We also varied $t _ { m }$ from 80 to 800 milliseconds (incrementing by 80 milliseconds, i.e., 80, 160, 240, etc.). We averaged the accuracy, F1-score, and AUC values across all possible $t _ { m } \mathrm { ' s }$ to investigate model performances in different $t _ { m } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { . }$ In addition, we plotted how the performance changes when $t _ { m }$ changes for HMM-GAN+ LR and selected competitive baseline models. For space considerations, we present the results of these variations in Online Appendix Q.

In the semisupervised mode, HMM-GAN + LR achieved an AUC of 0.8995 on TST and 0.8718 on SisFall, outperforming all baseline models. State-of-the-art sensor analytics models (+LR) achieved AUCs between 0.7832 and 0.8615 on the two data sets, whereas baseline fall-prevention models achieved AUCs between 0.5949 and 0.8026. Similar to experiment 1, HMM-GMM + LR performed poorly in this experiment (AUC: 0.7124 and 0.6315), which could be attributed to its inaccurate inference of snippet states as shown in experiment 1. The results in the supervised model generally follow those in the semisupervised mode. In the supervised mode, HMM-GAN + LR achieved an AUC of 0.9034 on TST and 0.9120 on SisFall, outperforming all baseline models. State-of-the-art sensor analytics models (+LR) achieved AUCs between 0.7532 and 0.8874, whereas baseline fall-prevention models achieved AUC between 0.6182 and 0.8585. The conventional HMM-GMM + LR attained AUC of 0.7047 and 0.6388. This experiment demonstrates how HMM-GAN can help prevent falls compared with conventional models.

## 5. Discussion: Contributions to the IS Knowledge Base and Practical Implications

In this research, we carefully followed the guidelines offered by the computational design science paradigm to design a novel IT artifact (HMM-GAN) for an impactful societal issue (preventing chronic condition–related falls). Several key contributions were made to the IS knowledge base to guide future scholarly inquiries through the search, design, evaluation, and demonstration processes. We discuss these contributions and related practical implications as follows.

## 5.1. Contributions to the IS Knowledge Base

IS scholars emphasize that novel computational IT artifacts should contribute prescriptive knowledge to the IS knowledge base (Hevner et al. 2004, Rai 2017). Successful contributions can often open new areas of investigation for IS researchers $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , collecting/processing/deriving new information and measures). Similarly, a proposed computational methodology, although designed, presented, and evaluated in the context of a functional area, could be generic to many application areas (Gupta 2018). We believe the major contributions of this work fit these guidelines in the following manner:

1. IS scholars note chronic disease management as a critical area of focus (Bardhan et al. 2020). They have made significant contributions to this emerging stream of research by analyzing social media and electronic health record data. However, motion sensor data are a promising yet underutilized data source in IS literature. In this study, the HMM-GAN and its constituent components (e.g., the new instantiation of the EM algorithm for semisupervised HMM-GAN) provide a unique approach to capturing the information in high-dimensional motion sensor data. The proposed approach could help open up new areas of investigation for IS scholars $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , collecting and processing motion sensor data), specifically for preventing chronic condition–related falls.

2. Although the HMM-GAN is presented in the context of fall prevention, we provide empirical evidence on how it can be used to execute other healthcare analytics tasks of interest to IS scholars, including ADL recognition (see Online Appendix M) and fall detection (see Online Appendix N). Although not demonstrated in this study (because of space and scope considerations), HMM-GAN has the potential to be applied to other related healthcare applications, including disease progression monitoring (e.g., recognizing the freezing of gait phenomenon) and rehabilitation therapy (e.g., assessing a person’s gait patterns).

To the best of our knowledge, these contributions are new to the IS discipline. Consequently, they can potentially offer IS scholars operational and/or engineering considerations when designing novel artifacts for selected research inquiries related to motion sensor–based healthcare analytics.

## 5.2. Practical Implications

IT artifacts developed through the lens of the computational design science paradigm should offer practical values to relevant stakeholders (Rai 2017). The proposed HMM-GAN can benefit senior citizens, their families, and organizations providing senior care. We discuss each stakeholder in turn.

5.2.1. Senior Citizens. To prevent falls, protective devices, such as airbags, can be designed in a garment similar to a life vest, which senior citizens would wear. An accelerometer in the garment collects motion data and passes the data to a smartphone with our system. When a fall is predicted, the smartphone inflates the airbags immediately to prevent potential fall injuries. Senior citizens can gain confidence and become more physically active with enhanced protection, which can, in turn, further lead to reduced risk of psychological disorders, such as depression and improved chronic disease management.

5.2.2. Families. Senior citizens’ falls can and often do lead to significant financial burdens on their families because of the high cost of related treatments. Moreover, fall events disrupt their families’ lives because their families need to care for the injured. Our system can enable prompt notification for senior citizens’ family members when fall events occur and prevent injuries from fall impacts by precise fall predictions.

5.2.3. Organizations Providing Senior Care. Organizations providing senior care, such as hospitals and retirement facilities, can benefit significantly from our proposed model. For example, hospital inpatient falls are common and can lead to debilitating injuries and prolonged hospitalizations (Schwendimann et al. 2008). Senior citizens in retirement facilities performed worse on tests of agility and balance than those in community living (Kang et al. 2004). By preventing falls, hospitals and retirement facilities can help senior citizens avoid vast amounts of medical expenditures (Florence et al. 2018). In addition, new paradigms of health services could be created by improving the allocation of health resources from a centralized (e.g., hospitals) to a decentralized model (e.g., individual homes).

## 6. Conclusion and Future Directions

Providing high-quality healthcare for senior citizens is an increasingly significant societal issue. Chronic conditions have become a leading threat to the health of senior citizens. Motion sensor–based analysis has emerged as a prevailing approach to prevent chronic condition–related falls. Existing methods, despite their promise, often rely on manual feature engineering (which can be ad hoc and laborintensive and require significant domain knowledge), omit temporal dependencies, and assume Gaussian mixture distributions. These limitations necessitate alternative approaches to help prevent chronic condition–related falls.

In this study, we adopt the computational design science paradigm to develop a novel HMM-GAN model to alleviate these issues. Grounded in established HMM operations and emerging deep learning and EM parameter learning algorithms, HMM-GAN automatically captures independent and sequential information from sensor signal data with varying distributions and sharp peaks and valleys in both semisupervised and supervised modes. We use a logistic regression upon HMM-GAN to determine whether and when to trigger protective devices to prevent fall injuries. We demonstrate how HMM-GAN outperforms prevailing sensor analytics and fall prevention models in semisupervised and supervised modes through benchmark experiments on two large-scale ground truth data sets. We also demonstrate HMM-GAN’s practical utility with an in-depth case study. Apart from significant practical contributions, HMM-GAN also offers methodological contributions to the IS knowledge base applicable to emerging healthcare applications of interest to IS scholars.

Future research can look to fuse environmental sensors (e.g., sensors attached to objects) with sensors attached to humans for more advanced, comprehensive, and interpretable prevention of falls. Additionally, future research can look to correlate the prevention of falls with physica assessments to obtain more precise and personalized performance. Methodologically, future studies can investigate more advanced algorithms other than EM to improve the training of HMM-GAN. Meanwhile, discovering insights into the temporal–spatial patterns of data beyond the HMM transition matrices by unwrapping the black box of deep learning is another important direction that requires future investigation. Ultimately, each future direction can help ensure safer and longer lives for aging individuals.
