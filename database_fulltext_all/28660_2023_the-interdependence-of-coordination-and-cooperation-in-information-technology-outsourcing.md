# The Interdependence of Coordination and Cooperation in Information Technology Outsourcing

Ghiyoung P. Im,<sup>a,</sup>\* Manju Ahuja<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. The success of information technology outsourcing projects largely depends on the extent to which partners are willing to learn through interactions with each other. This study investigates the antecedents and consequences of relational learning between clients and vendors in outsourcing projects. Drawing on the collaboration framework in interfirm relationships research, this study examines the interdependence of coordination and cooperation. The two antecedents of relational learning (i.e., shared domain knowledge and motivational elements) represent coordination and cooperation mechanisms. We investigate the independent (main) and interdependent (moderation) effects of these mechanisms on relational learning from both the client and vendor perspectives. The findings show that the interdependent model is better in explaining the impact of coordination and coop eration mechanisms in outsourcing projects. This finding enables partners to understand relationship dynamics more comprehensively and develop collaboration mechanisms that can bring synergy between coordination and cooperation. Our study also shows that the perceptions of motivational elements are not symmetric between clients and vendors.

## Introduction

A significant concern for firms engaged in information technology outsourcing (ITO) is the successful implementation of outsourced projects (Li et al. 2011). This concern has become more salient as firms are expanding outsourcing to new, innovative applications for revenue growth and better customer experience. For example, a leading outsourcing vendor created contact-center solutions by integrating cloud-based capabilities (Arora et al. 2021), which led to increased revenue (e.g., upselling), improved customer experience (e.g., chatbots and analytics), and operational excellence (e.g., dashboards). An ITO project involves a complex problem-solving process that requires a variety of skills, know-how, and knowledge among partners to tackle constant change in requirements and technological uncertainties (Li et al. 2011). Problem-solving can be facilitated when partners are engaged in active learning that enables them to take different perspectives, explore alternatives, and seek innovation (Van der Vegt and Bunderson 2005). As such, learning becomes a key enabler for successful problem-solving and project implementation (Ramasubbu et al. 2008, Chang and Gurbaxani 2012, Mani and Barua 2015).

We focus on learning because it is one of the core outcomes of collaborative activities among partners (Inkpen and Tsang 2008). Specifically, we address relational learning that occurs as a result of interactions between two or more parties (Mani and Barua 2015). In the context of outsourced projects, relational learning can be viewed as the improvement in one partner’s knowledge and skills related to outsourced project management that occurs from interactions with the other partner. Rela tional learning in ITO projects requires continuous collaboration among partners through carefully designed collaborative mechanisms. However, accomplishing col laboration in ITO projects is quite challenging because of differences in geography, culture, structure, motivation, and knowledge between partners (Rai et al. 2009). Unfortunately, differences in such areas do not allow partners to possess enough mutual knowledge to understand each other (Gardner and Mortensen 2015), making them not agree on how to coordinate or cooperate (Gulati et al. 2005). In particular, coordination challenges arise when partners do not understand interdependent workflows (Akkermans et al. 2021), whereas cooperation challenges take place when partners experience incentive conflicts (Aubert et al. 2004, Lioliou et al. 2019). These challenges can be effectively addressed by creating mechanisms that enable ITO partners to properly understand collaboration challenges and develop mitigation mechanisms. We suggest that ITO project research needs a deeper understanding of these mechanisms, and we aim to fill this gap.

An example of these collaboration challenges is well illustrated in an ITO of a major European port authority for its information technology (IT) services and infrastructure (Akkermans et al. 2021). The contract was driven by short-term cost reductions, and the workflow for the customer service processes was not designed properly. This ineffectiveness led to misalignment of incentives. The partners were able to reverse this vicious cycle by collaboratively designing service workflow and subsequently establishing shared norms, trust, and interest alignment. The synergy between collaborative design and interest alignment led to long-term focus on service quality improvements. This case study highlights the importance of understanding the interplay between coordination (i.e., collaborative design) and cooperation (i.e., interest alignment) for positive outsourcing outcomes. In the ITO literature, this understanding has yet to be examined.

Our study creates a holistic framework of collaboration mechanisms and investigates their effect on relational learning in the ITO project context. To do so, we employ the collaboration framework proposed by Gulati et al. (2012). Gulati et al. (2005) suggest that for successful collaboration, partners must address two major challenges simultaneously: coordination and cooperation (Gulati et al. 2005, White and Lui 2005). Cooperation is defined as “joint pursuit of agreed-on goal(s)” (Gulati et al. 2012, p. 533), whereas coordination is defined as “alignment or adjustment of partners’ actions to achieve jointly determined goals” (Gulati et al. 2012, p. 537). The framework of Gulati et al. (2012) is an appropriate lens for understanding the challenges that hinder relational learning. Coordination in ITO is essential for learning because learning requires the unification of efforts among ITO partners to understand (Heath and Staudenmayer 2000) and perform interdependent tasks (Venkatesh and Bala 2012). Cooperation is critical for learning because learning among partners is facilitated when different goals and interests are aligned and give way to relationship goals (Lioliou and Zimmermann 2015). These two collaborative mechanisms help partners address uncertainties and promote relational learning in ITO projects.

Coordination challenges occur because of task interdependence between partners, whereas cooperation challenges arise on account of partners’ inherent selfinterest and their incentive misalignment. Gulati et al. (2005) emphasize that these two are interrelated, and resolving incentive problems does not resolve coordination problems and vice versa, warranting attention to these two simultaneously. They also state that fail ures in either of these raise uncertainties in the relation ship and make partners achieve suboptimal result. Most relational learning research in ITO treats coordination and cooperation challenges independently, and there is scant research on how the two mechanisms jointly impact learning outcomes (Akkermans et al. 2021). Given this, in addition to the independent effects, we theorize and demonstrate that coordination and cooperation mechanisms are interdependent and jointly influence project outcomes (such as relational learning) (Gulati et al. 2012). Thus, a key contribution of our study is to demonstrates the value of investigating interdependence between these two factors in ITO project research.

Regarding coordination mechanisms, this study focuses on the role of knowledge, specifically shared domain knowledge (Okhuysen and Bechky 2009), among ITO partners. Shared domain knowledge exists when one party has developed the knowledge that pertains to the other party’s domain through previous project encounters or its own knowledge creation effort (Blumenberg et al. 2009, Tiwana 2013). Typically, vendors possess technical knowledge for system development and are responsible for successful delivery of the outcomes. Clients have business domain knowledge and deal with overall project management and control issues. However, when each partner has developed the domain knowledge prior to the project that is typically managed by the other party, they will find it easier to understand each other’s tasks, contexts, and challenges. Thus, the presence of shared domain knowledge between vendors and clients should facilitate relational learning.

With respect to cooperation mechanisms, this study addresses motivational elements that can align partners goals and interests (Salvato et al. 2017). Given that clients and vendors come to ITO arrangements with their own business goals and interests and that their perspectives are not always aligned (Williamson 1985), it is important to examine these motivational elements. Drawing on the goal framing theory (Lindenberg and Foss 2011), we iden tify the two elements that can motivate the partners toward joint production: strategic importance of the project and expectations of relationship continuity. Therefore, we ask the following question. (1) What are the impacts of shared domain knowledge and motivational elements on relational learning across clients and vendors?

Further, we investigate the interdependent effect of shared domain knowledge and motivational elements on relational learning. To accomplish relational learning, partners must establish basic levels of coordination and cooperation mechanisms. When partners enhance the level of cooperation mechanisms, they can expect a higher marginal effect of coordination mechanisms on relational outcomes and vice versa (Gulati et al. 2012). Alternately, a positive change in one mechanism can bring about subsequent positive changes in the other mechanisms (Akkermans et al. 2021). Thus, we investigate the following. (2) What are the interdependent (moderation) effects of motivational elements on shared domain knowledge across clients and vendors? Last, we attend to the increasing role of technology uncertainty on the vendor’s commitment to the partnership (Dibbern et al. 2004). This leads to the last question. (3) What is the moderation effect of technology uncertainty on the effect of expectations of relationship continuity?

By asking these questions, this study extends ITO learning and project research in the following ways. First, this study systematically theorizes collaboration challenges and provides an integrated theoretical framework to understand them by applying the collaboration framework (Gulati et al. 2012). Other studies of collaboration have either not addressed these challenges (Rai et al. 2009, Zimmermann et al. 2018) or only done so partially (Lioliou and Zimmermann 2015, Akkermans et al. 2021). Second, this study extends collaboration research by investigating whether the perception of coordination and cooperation mechanisms is symmetric or different across the partners. With the exception of a few case studies (Lioliou and Zimmermann 2015, Akkermans et al. 2021), ITO studies have mostly been conducted from either the client (Chang and Gurbaxani 2012) or vendor perspective (Rai et al. 2009). These studies assume that both parties view the outsourcing phenomenon the same way or that the view of one side represents the whole ITO relationship (Lumineau and Oliveira 2018). This assumption is likely to not be true because partners in an ITO relationship typically have varying goals, incentives, knowledge, and expectations. By comparing the perspectives of the two sides, this study provides a more holistic perspective of outsourcing collaboration research. Third, the study advances the collaboration research by empirically showing the value of investigating the interdependent effect between coordination and cooperation mechanisms on relational learning in the context of ITO project research. Strategy and operations research on interorganizational learning (Online Appendix A) focused mostly on either of the collaboration mechanisms and do not explicate their interdependence. Our study demonstrates that the interdependent (moderation effect) model has higher explanatory power than the independent (main effect) model in explaining relational learning. In particular, the results show that the effect of coordination mechanisms on relational learning is stronger in the presence of cooperation mechanisms and vice versa.

The specific context of our study is outsourced information systems development (ISD) projects. In this context, a knowledge gap exists between clients and vendors in terms of technical knowledge for system development (vendor domain) and business process and project management (client domain), both of which are needed for problem-solving. Therefore, we consider relational learning involving interactions be tween clients and vendors to be key for the ISD projects because it helps narrow the knowledge gap between partners. In testing our model, we collected data from two separate samples of 94 client and 186 vendor firms. Both data sets show that relational learning between cli ents and vendors positively influenced project success. The effect of shared domain knowledge was significant to client and vendor relational learning across both data sets. Clients recognized the value of both types of motivational elements for relational learning, whereas vendors only appreciated the value of expectations for relationship continuity. Strategic importance had a positive moderating effect on the client data set only. Expec tations of relationship continuity received support from both data sets. The findings show that compared with the independent model, the interdependent model has stronger explanatory power in explaining the impact of coordination and cooperation mechanisms in outsourcing projects. Also, they show that clients and vendors show divergent views on the value of motivational elements on relational learning.

## Theoretical Background Interorganizational Learning and ITO Project Success

Interorganizational learning starts as alliance partners are engaged in interdependent tasks and building new experiences (Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011). The engagement between the partners on an outsourcing project involves joint tasks and thus, provides continuous learning opportunities. Client and vendor learning in ITO accompanies a change in the firms’ knowledge associated with the process and product of the outsourced project. This study conceptualizes learning in ITO as relational learning that involves a change in firms’ knowledge as a result of the interactions within and in service of a specific collaboration among the partners (Argote 2011, Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011).

Much of the empirical ITO research is interested in outsourcing decisions or outcomes (Lacity et al. 2010). Information technology outsourcing decisions research is about make or buy decisions and specific IT functions to be outsourced, whereas ITO outcomes research is concerned with a client’s perceived success or satisfaction with outsourcing. Positive outcomes should be good indicators of evaluating the ultimate success of ITO. However, we suggest that successful outsourcing outcomes are driven by the success of an ITO project. Hence, our investigation on project success is essential to develop a deeper understanding of ITO success.

Coordination and Cooperation Challenges in ITO The general recognition of the importance of both coordination and cooperation in organizations was made long ago by giants such Barnard (1938) and March and Simon (1993). Barnard (1938) defines a formal organization as “a system of consciously coordinated activities or forces of two or more persons” (Barnard 1938, p. 73). He suggested that the functioning of the complex formal organization is affected by the three elements of communication, purpose, and personal willingness. Here, communication reflects coordination, whereas purpose and willingness are related to cooperation. In the strategy and operations management research, our review found that researchers have focused mostly on either coordination or cooperation mechanisms and do not address collaboration challenges simultaneously (Online Appendix A).

In ITO projects, partners need to investigate coordination and cooperation challenges simultaneously for successful project implementation (Akkermans et al. 2021). Such investigation reflects the reality of an ITO relationship where partners require mutually agreed upon goals and commitment to establish a coordination structure and also, a basic coordination agreement to reconcile disparate goals and interests.

In ITO learning and project research, several researchers have empirically investigated collaboration challenges (Table 1).<sup>1</sup> First, some studies have examined one or both elements of collaboration challenges, but they were not concerned with collaboration challenges and their interdependence. The coordination elements investigated were knowledge transfer and informal discussions (Williams 2011), shared understanding (Zimmermann et al. 2018), process investments (Ramasubbu et al. 2008), and structural and informal governance (Balaji and Brown 2014). The cooperation elements examined include trust (Rai et al. 2009, Zimmermann and Ravishankar 2014, Zimmermann et al. 2018, Akkermans et al. 2021) and norms (Rai et al. 2009, Mathew and Chen 2013). A few studies examined both elements of collaboration but did not examine their interdependence (e.g., Rai et al. 2009). Second, other studies have addressed collaboration challenges via case studies (Lioliou and Zimmermann 2015, Akkermans et al. 2021), but these studies offer partia understanding of collaboration challenges and do not propose an integrated theoretical framework. Lioliou and Zimmermann (2015) narrowly focused on opportunistic behavior, focusing on internal and behavioral uncertainty, which is different from coordination and cooperation challenges in partnerships. Akkermans et al. (2021) modeled the dynamics of relationship cycles using a process theory. Their study is a step forward to understanding collaboration challenges, and a more rigorous theoretical framework should enhance the value of their findings. Our study fills a gap in this stream of research that has only provided a partial understanding of collaboration challenges by offering an integrated theoretical framework for the phenomenon. We also offer mitigating factors in the form of coordination and cooperation mechanisms.

Coordination and Collaboration Mechanisms in ITO Following the collaboration framework (Gulati et al. 2012), we suggest that coordination and cooperation challenges can be addressed effectively when enabling mechanisms that promote the alignment of goals/motivations and activities between partners (Hong and Fiona 2009, Lacity et al. 2010).

Shared Domain Knowledge in ITO. Coordination challenges can be resolved when partners have proper coordination mechanisms (Van de Ven et al. 1976). Building on the five types of coordination mechanisms by Okhuy sen and Bechky (2009), we identify knowledge as a coordination mechanism. Puranam et al. (2009) explicitly recognized the role of knowledge in coordination and suggested that shared knowledge in the form of common ground enables partners to properly align and adjust their actions. Accordingly, we view knowledge, specifically shared domain knowledge, as a coordination mechanism that can foster relational learning between ITO partners.<sup>2</sup> Typically, each party possesses specialized knowledge that the other party does not have. Vendors are responsible for providing proper software solutions to meet the demand of clients. To this end, they possess technical knowledge for system development. Clients attempt to make the vendor understand their business and project goals. They control the development, process, and outcome and keep project management knowledge in the areas of integration, scope, time, cost, quality, and communication. Given such disparate knowledge bases, partners should make a concerted effort to coordinate because partners often do not adequately understand the challenges that belong to the domain of the other.

Coordination challenges can be alleviated when partners have shared domain knowledge (Lane and Lubatkin 1998, Wang et al. 2016) such that each party owns the knowledge that typically pertains to the other party. Shared domain knowledge is the outcome of actors’ engagement with previous projects or intentional investment for knowledge creation (Blumenberg et al. 2009, Tiwana 2013). Information systems research has well recognized the importance of shared domain knowledge between management and IT units. When top management possesses the requisite knowledge for IT activities, they can communicate freely with IT units and meet their needs (Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991). Similarly, to properly align IT with business, IT units should possess enough business knowledge for the domains that IT supports (Stephens et al. 1992).

Motivational Elements in ITO. In ITO, cooperation challenges can be addressed when partners have proper mechanisms to help reconcile goals and interests between the partners (Salvato et al. 2017). Prior research on cooper ation mechanisms can be summarized into four perspectives (Gulati et al. 2012, Salvato et al. 2017): organizational efficiency, social-structural, social-psychological, and management perspectives.

This study adopts the management perspective that focuses on motivational elements that are instrumental in promoting joint production motivation. This perspective notes a critical role of joint production motivation in enhancing cooperation in the organizational environment (Lindenberg and Foss 2011). In deriving the specific motivational elements relevant to ITO, we draw on the goal-framing theory (Lindenberg and Foss 2011) that explains the antecedents of joint production motivation. The theory postulates that joint production motivation is influenced by the four groups of motivational elements: perceived interdependence in joint production, direct and indirect supports for the normative goal frame, and the conditions for governing. The theory explains that partners in ITO will show cooperative attitude and behavior by abiding by joint appropriateness standards when they perceive interdependence in an ITO project, receive supports for the normative goal frame, and identify the conditions for governing. Among these four elements, this study focuses on the first element that has direct relevance to ITO project (in that they are focused on joint production) and relationship characteristics. The remaining three elements require much more active engagement between ITO partners and thus, are less relevant in contract-based ITO projects.

As such, the study examines the effect of two motivational elements that induce perceived interdependence in ITO partnership: strategic importance of the project and expectations of relationship continuity. The former element enhances perceived interdependence because the project requires more resources and commitment between the partners, whereas the latter increases perceived interdependence because the partners anticipate future engagement. We suggest that these two motivational elements help bridge different goals and interests between partners by making them perceive higher interdependence with each other.

## Research Model

Drawing on the collaboration framework (Gulati et al. 2012), this study develops the two antecedents of relational learning (i.e., shared domain knowledge and motivational elements) and tests their independent and interdependent effects on relational learning (Figure 1).

## Relational Learning and Project Success

We conjecture that the success of an outsourced ISD project depends on relational learning that occurs between the client and the vendor during project engagement. The vendor side has a higher level of expertise in the domain of technical knowledge for system development, and the client side has a higher level of knowledge in the domain of business process and project management. Both of these are essential for problem-solving, and the extent to which clients and vendors can learn from each other will help partners reduce the knowledge gap. With narrowing knowledge gaps, partners will have an easier time in reaching consensus on the solutions for the problem quickly and facilitating successful project implementation. Next, relational learning makes partners enlarge the stock of knowledge that will help them deal with new problems that emerged during project implementation (Im and Rai 2014). Existing stock of knowledge may have limited value in solving new problems. A larger stock of knowledge will increase partners’ capacity for solving different types of problems, contributing to successful project implementation.

Hypothesis 1. Client’s relational learning (a) and vendor’s relational learning (b) in an outsourced ISD project promote its project success.

## Client ISD Expertise and Relational Learning

Client ISD expertise refers to the client’s understanding of the ISD process (Kirsch et al. 2002). This expertise is acquired via a client’s intentional efforts (Blumenberg et al. 2009, Tiwana 2013) and includes the ability to articulate the inputs and outputs to the system and also, to understand the actual system development process. We posit that client ISD expertise would foster client relational learning (Lyytinen and Robey 1999). First, client ISD expertise facilitates information and knowledge sharing with the vendor because the client can take the perspective of the partner (Heath and Staudenmayer 2000). This sharing promotes coordination, and the partners can quickly build shared understanding of the project. Thus, the client can properly evaluate the quality of interim deliverables (Takeishi 2002), assess the project progress objectively, comprehend necessary adjustments with requirement changes better, and communicate with less ambiguity. Second, ISD expertise of clients enables them to build the same language from the project documents created by the vendor (Heath and Staudenmayer 2000). Clients and vendors live in different thought worlds and use different languages about the project. During development, vendors generate documents that require expertise in the ISD to properly make sense of them. Client ISD expertise removes the language barrier, fostering coordination and client relational learning. Also, we argue that client ISD expertise would promote vendor relational learning. Given knowledge redundancy, clients can communicate their requirements to vendors quickly, and at the same time, they can accurately comprehend the status of the development project and raise their concerns properly. Over the course of the ISD, the vendor needs to know the business processes and client’s technical infrastructure on which the current outsourced project is developed and integrated. When a client has the necessary ISD expertise, they should be able to better deliver the requisite knowledge to the vendor, and subsequently, the vendor’s learning will be accelerated.

Vendor Project Expertise and Relational Learning Vendor project expertise refers to the extent to which the vendor possesses project management knowledge that is relevant to clients’ business domain and is required to lead the project. This includes articulating specific project goals, routines, procedures, and practices for the client’s project. This expertise is developed and refined over various outsourced projects (Levina and Ross 2003) and different from vendors project management knowledge for software development. We conjecture that vendor project expertise would facilitate client as well as vendor relational learning. During development, clients need to continuously monitor the project progress, coordinate activities, and integrate their business processes associated with the project. In the presence of vendor project expertise, the client can delegate these coordination activities to the vendor (Rustagi et al. 2008) and can focus on mission-critical aspects of the project. In this case, the client activities can center on specifying and controlling the outcomes (Tiwana and Keil 2007). As a result, the project coordination between partners will be less information intense, but the client can develop better insights and understanding of the project. Addi tionally, we suggest that vendor project expertise would positively affect vendor relational learning. Given the project expertise, vendors would not need much help from clients in setting project goals, procedures, and routines and in defining deliverables (Levina and Ross 2003, Tiwana and Keil 2007). Such capabilities allow vendors to set aside their core resources for solving critical problems and enhancing their understanding of the project.

Hypothesis 3. The vendor project expertise in an outsourced ISD project has a positive effect on client relational learning (a) and vendor relational learning (b).

## Strategic Importance and Relational Learning

The strategic importance of the project is about how crucial the project is to the core mission of the client and implies the level of resources and commitment by clients to ensure the project’s success (Wallace et al. 2004). Outsourcing partners with strategically importance projects are likely to perceive high interdepen dence in the relationship. This is so because for the client, the success of the project will significantly influence their competitiveness and for the vendor, it will impact the likelihood of a future contract. Perceived high interdependence will make partners exhibit cooperative attitude and behavior by sticking to joint goals and a logic of appropriateness (Weber et al. 2004). We expect that strategic importance of the project would promote client relational learning. Strategic projects help clients enhance the competitive advantage and the market position to sustain an already attained competitive advantage (Wade and Hulland 2004). Clients would exhibit high joint production motivation and place more resources and commitment for strategic projects. Clients also would maintain cooperative engagement with their partners by treating vendors as vital partners, not as cost-cutting brokers. Such cooperative attitude and behavior from clients would elici cooperative response from vendors. In return, clients will have an easier time in understanding the project. In addition, we suggest that strategic projects would foster vendor relational learning. Strategic projects will make vendors more attentive to joint goals and joint appropriateness standards because project success affects their reputation and future contracts. Considering the project’s strategic position, the client will impose tighter control over its development process (Kirsch 1997) and display more cooperative attitude to the vendor. As such, the vendor will also provide corresponding resources and commitment and minimize opportunistic behavior (Lindenberg and Foss 2011). Such cooperative attitude in the relationship will make the vendor more focused on learning project challenges and solutions.

Hypothesis 4. The strategic importance of an outsourced ISD project has a positive effect on client relational learning (a) and vendor relational learning (b).

## Expectations of Relationship Continuity and Relational Learning

The expectation of relationship continuity is defined as the prospect that the current outsourcing relationship will continue into the future (Ravindran et al. 2015). According to a survey of chief information officers by Gartner, 70% of outsourcing clients consider changing their outsourcing partners and technology (Gartner 2016). This reshaping is driven by technological uncertainties. This motivational element should be seriously weighed in outsourcing relationships as technological uncertainties persist. We expect that continuity expectation would promote relational learning. First, partners will be willing to make relationship-specific investments so that the same resources can be redeployed in subsequent projects (Ravindran et al. 2015). This investment is made possible because partners consider each other as long-term partners and perceive high interdependence. From relationship-specific investments, clients expect less disruption of their services, whereas vendors accomplish maximum resource utilization. Next, when partners foresee repeat contracts, they will be more inclined to adjust to the need of their partners. For example, partners may be more willing to adjust to the changing requirements of the project over time (Johnson 1999). These two changes will make partners more cooperative and enable them to use their dedicated resources in the creation, transfer, and application of knowledge, facilitating understanding of the project.

Hypothesis 5. The expectations of relationship continuity associated with an outsourced ISD project have a positive effect on client relational learning (a) and vendor relational learning (b).

## Moderating Role of Strategic Importance

We posit that the impact of client ISD expertise on relational learning would be greater when the outsourced project has higher strategic importance. When the project has higher strategic importance, partners will be more cooperative by maintaining higher trust and commitment with each other. Partners also would cooperate extensively with given relational resources. Better cooperation will make partners better utilize existing ISD knowledge. As a result, they will have an easier time in learning project progress, setting strategic directions, and dealing with project uncertainties (Im and Rai 2014). Additionally, we expect that the impact of vendor project expertise on relational learning would be higher when the outsourced project has higher strategic importance. Given higher strategic importance, the client will treat the vendor more favorably. As such, the vendor would mobilize more project expertise to manage the project, and as a result, they could design better process and outcome controls, facilitating their understanding of the project.

Hypothesis 6. The positive effect of client ISD expertise in an outsourced ISD project on client relational learning (a) and vendor relational learning (b) is moderated by the strategic importance of the project such that the effect is stron ger for a project with higher strategic importance.

Hypothesis 7. The positive effect of vendor project expertise in an outsourced ISD project on client relational learn ing (a) and vendor relational learning (b) is moderated by the strategic importance of the project such that the effect is stronger for a project with higher strategic importance.

## Moderating Role of Expectations of Relationship Continuity

We expect that the impact of client ISD expertise on relational learning would be greater when the partners have higher expectations of relationship continuity. First, when partners have higher expectation of continuity, clients would invest more resources in expanding client ISD expertise to enhance coordination (Levinthal and Rerup 2006). Second, clients also would be motivated to refine formal and informal routines to make better use of their expanded knowledge base (Im and Rai 2014). This additional investment can enhance the level of coordination and heighten the effect of client ISD expertise by further facilitating problem-solving and understanding of the project. Furthermore, we conjecture that the impact of vendor project expertise on relational learning would be higher in the presence of higher expectations of relationship continuity. Given the long-term prospect, the vendor would invest more resources in project management. This investment will help the client free more of their resources for project coordination and instead, focus on core process and outcome controls. As a result, partners can make better use of their resources for project understanding.

Hypothesis 8. The positive effect of client ISD expertise in an outsourced ISD project on client relational learning (a) and vendor relational learning (b) is moderated by expectations of relationship continuity such that the effect is stronger for a project with higher expectations of relationship continuity.

Hypothesis 9. The positive effect of vendor project expertise in an outsourced ISD project on client relational learn ing (a) and vendor relational learning (b) is moderated by expectations of relationship continuity such that the effect is stronger for a project with higher expectations of relationship continuity.

## Moderating Role of Technology Uncertainty

We suggest that technology uncertainty will reduce the impact of expectations of relationship continuity on learning (Dibbern et al. 2004). When the technology involved in an ISD project is rapidly changing, the vendors are less likely to get involved in the activities that require a significant commitment of resources and personnel for the project. They might consider such an investment to be less useful for their future engagements because the technology is likely to soon become obsolete. To manage existing and future engagements, vendors may employ the passive strategy of making good-enough investments so that they implement the projects successfully and at the same time, deal with technological uncertainties. Such a situation may not bring about enough resources for the project progress, leading to lower relational learning than desired.

Hypothesis 10. The positive effect of expectations of relationship continuity in an outsourced ISD project on its client relational learning (a) and vendor relational learning (b) is moderated by technology uncertainty such that the effect is weaker for a project with higher technology uncertainty.

## Research Design

We collected data via online surveys from two independent client and vendor samples. Online surveys were used to contact client and vendor firms that had been engaged in ITO projects in the United States. We targeted respondents who were engaged in ITO projects at the midmanagement level, who would be involved in or aware of project-level decisions, and also, who would be in a position to evaluate the learning at the client/vendor level and project outcomes. To overcome data collection challenges, we recruited a professional market research company that had access to project manager panels in such outsourcing engagements. The data collection was conducted in two separate rounds. In the first round, for data collection from ITO clients, a total of 426 managers were invited to complete the survey, of whom 94 returned completed and usable responses (response rate: 22.1%). In the second round, for data collection from ITO vendors, a different research company sent the survey to its panel members for a target sample size of 200. After screening out for its quality, we retained 186 vendor responses. The sample demographics are presented in Online Appendix B.

## Measures

We developed a preliminary version of the measures and refined them through a few pilot studies. The measures were developed for the client view with respect to controls, learning, and outcomes (Kirsch et al. 2002) and also, customized for the vendor view. All measures in our conceptual framework were operationalized using multiitem reflective scales (see Online Appendix C for details). Our measure for project success was guided by Nidumolu (1995) and Grover et al. (1996). We measured client relational learning and vendor relational learning directly by asking whether the interactions with the partner improved their knowledge with respect to the overall project, the project’s documentation, and business processes. Client ISD expertise was measured using the three items taken from Kirsch (1996) and Kirsch et al. (2002) plus two new items. The items for vendor project expertise were grounded in the project control literature and based on Kirsch et al. (2002). We adapted existing measures for expectations of relationship continuity (Heide and John 1990), strategic importance (Wallace et al. 2004), and technological uncertainty (Ang and Cummings 1997). Also, we added four controls (project size, project duration, client industry, and project type).

## Endogeneity Tests

We suspect endogeneity in the following variables: vendor relational learning in Models (1) and (6), client ISD expertise in Models (2) and (4), and vendor project expertise in Models (7) and (9). As for instrument variables, we identified access to collaborative technology (yes/no) for Model (1), technological uncertainty for Model (6), client dynamic teaming (Tu et al. 2004) for Models (2) and (4), and vendor planning flexibility (Dibrell et al. 2014) for Models (7) and (9). These instrumental variables are expected to be correlated with the explanatory variables and uncorrelated with the error terms. First, access to collaborative technology is likely to influence vendor relational learning by facilitating knowledge sharing. Second, in Models (2) and (4), dynamic teaming may affect client ISD expertise because flexible work teams will find it easier to reorient their teams for learning new technologies. Last, in Models (7) and (9), planning flexibility will influence vendor project expertise because vendors can accumulate more project knowledge by experimenting different plans.

For the models, we performed Durbin–Wu–Hausman tests of endogeneity (Hausman 1978). Across the client and vendor data sets, we did not find any indication of endogeneity. On the client data, in Model (1), the residuals for vendor relational learning were nonsignificant (p � 0.495), and the robust F statistic from the first-stage regression was significant $( F = 7 . 6 8 9 , p = 0 . 0 0 7 )$ . In Models (2) and (4), the residuals for client ISD expertise were nonsignificant, and the F statistics were significant (above nine). On the vendor data, in Model (6), the residual for vendor relational learning was nonsignificant $( p = 0 . 3 7 4 )$ , and the F statistic was significant (F � 9.255, p � 0.003). In Model (7), we found nonsignificance of the residuals for vendor project expertise and significance of the F statistics (above 11).

## Discussion

Drawing on the collaboration framework by Gulati et al. (2012), this study empirically confirms the interdependent model that coordination and cooperation mechanisms jointly influence relational learning. The findings offer support of the main and moderation effects across the data sets. Table 7 provides a summary of results. The findings show similarities and differences across vendor and client perspectives. In both data sets, we found that both client relational learning and vendor relational learning are significant predictors of project success. Also, we found that shared domain knowledge significantly predicts client and vendor relational learning. The findings for the coordination elements are consistent across the client and vendor data sets. However, there are differences on how vendor firms view the effect of motivational elements compared with clients. The patterns for the moderation effect are largely consistent with those for the main effect.

The findings bring several significant theoretical implications. First, the findings support the main effects that both shared domain knowledge and motivational elements play essential roles for relational learning. From this finding, our study extends prior quantitative studies by showing the value of taking coordination and cooperation simultaneously as inseparable elements of ITO project collaboration. This extension is important because the integrated framework helps us avoid the reductionist perspective for ITO collaboration (e.g., Mathew and Chen 2013) and presents a holistic perspective that enables a comprehensive analysis for collaboration failures.

One significant finding from this result is that the effects of coordination and cooperation mechanisms are not symmetric between the partners. Identifying asymmetric perceptions is very important in understanding ITO project failures. We cannot simply assume that partners have identical perceptions on these mechanisms because their goals and expectations in the partnership are not always aligned (Im and Rai 2008, Lumineau and Oliveira 2018). As for the coordination mechanisms, clients and vendors equally recognize the value of shared domain knowledge on client and vendor relational learning. The findings across the data sets exhibit the usefulness of shared knowledge in bridging the knowledge gaps between partners, playing a role in facilitating communication between partners, and translating different thought worlds. Regarding cooperation mechanisms, clients and vendors show divergent views on the value of motivational elements on relational learning. The findings suggest that clients consider strategic importance, not repeat business, to be important for their own learning. They also expect vendors to be motivated by repeat business while discounting the strategic importance of the project. As for vendors, they are concerned with the prospect of repeat business for their learning and not motivated by the project’s strategic importance.

The findings imply that although partners share the same goal of project success, they may have different priorities in resource allocation. Clients perceive both strategic importance of the project and expectations of relationship continuity to affect relational learning for vendors and clients. Vendors appear to allocate more resources to relational learning when the project has higher expectations of relationship continuity than cli ents do. Interestingly, customers do not always perceive repeat contracts to be positive for their own learning, despite the favorable prospect for relational learning through familiarity and trust with a partner’s capabili ties (Inkpen and Tsang 2008). One plausible explanation for this perception is hidden costs of trust through repeat business (Selnes and Sallis 2003). Selnes and Sal lis (2003) stated that too little or too much trust can have harmful effects. When partners have too much trust (that can occur with repeat contracts), they would no longer seek or share critical information, nor would they engage in constructive conflict. Second, they may experience the narrowing of knowledge gaps and be come homogenous, leading to diminishing return fo learning (Selnes and Sallis 2003). By confirming asymmetric effects of collaboration mechanisms on relational learning, this study renews the interest in conceptualizing and empirically testing the two mechanisms simultaneously and raises the importance of recogniz ing divergent views between partners on motivational mechanisms. In sum, this study overcomes the limita tions of the studies that have a single-party focus.

Second, the findings support the moderation effects that the coordination and cooperation mechanisms have a joint impact on relational learning. The findings for the moderation effect are not consistent across the partners but consistent with those for the main effect. Clients perceive the impacts of motivational elements on shared domain knowledge for relational learning more strongly than vendors do. In the vendor data set, we found the moderation effect of expectations of relationship continuity on vendor project expertise for client and vendor learning and nothing else. In the client data set, we found the moderation effect of both types of motivational elements on shared domain knowledge for learning. Also, the client data set exhibits the moderation effect of strategic importance on vendor project expertise for vendor relational learning. The findings imply that vendors are interested in further applying shared domain knowledge only when they see prospects for repeat business.

The findings show that the interdependent model is better in explaining the impacts of collaboration mechanisms on relational learning than the independent model. This finding extends the ITO learning and project research (Table 1) that has dealt with coordination or collaboration in isolation. This finding is significant because the interdependent model enables partners to understand relationship dynamics more comprehensively and to develop collaboration mechanisms that can bring about synergy between coordination and cooperation and avoid project or relationship failures. These mechanisms are especially important in high-uncertainty situations, where project requirements are complicated and the partnership is in conflict or not well established. Given the understanding of the interdependence, partners can properly diagnose the misalignment between coordination and cooperation and come up with proper collaboration mechanisms that can break a vicious cycle (Gulati et al. 2012, Akkermans et al. 2021). Our study extends the Akkermans et al. (2021) study by providing a theoretical framework to systematically understand the relationship dynamics.

Third, the findings show that client and vendor relational learning promotes the success of ITO projects. The findings recognize that the improvement of knowledge and skills in the partnership through relational learning is an important driver of project success. The findings imply that both partners need to consider engaging in ongoing communication for problem-solving beyond knowledge sharing, especially when the outsourced project involves complex requirements, technological uncertainties, and various contingencies (Whitaker et al. 2010). This finding is also observed in a case study by Nuwangi and Sedera (2019), in which ITO partners experienced project failures because of lack of continuous discussions between the partners for the clarification of client requirements.

Finally, technology uncertainty is found to lower the impacts of expectations of relationship continuity on client and vendor learning in the client data set. This indicates that client perceptions of learning are much more influenced by technology uncertainty, despite expectations of relationship continuity. This finding suggests that clients may not like to make a significant investment in a project when technology uncertainty is high, even if the prospect of repeat business with the vendor is high.

Our work has practical implications. Our study suggests that coordination and cooperation mechanisms can promote a vicious or virtuous cycle, depending on how their interrelations are understood and managed. If partners can accurately identify the interrelations between the two mechanisms, they can devise arrangements for the synergy and avoid project failures. This implies that managers need to continuously work on maintaining a balance between coordination and cooperation mechanisms. For effective cooperation, managers need to create incentive arrangements that can align the self-interests of the partners (e.g., revenue or profit sharing) (Mart´ınez-Jerez 2014). At the same time, partners should work on creating coordination arrangements so that interdependent tasks can be successfully managed. Partners need to continuously monitor whether the ar rangements for cooperation and coordination are properly aligned.

Certain cautions are worth mentioning in interpreting the results of this study. First, our sample did not consist of a paired sample of clients and vendors because of the practical constraints of collecting an adequate sample size needed for meaningful analysis. The design and findings of our study help us better understand comparative, generalized motivations and approaches of vendors and clients in ITO collaborations. The findings are not, however, suitable for understanding the project level partnership dynamics of these effects because we did not rely on a matched-pair sample. In a matchedpair sampling design, future research can provide a full view of certain parts of the model (e.g., strategic importance). Next, further work is required to understand our findings better by identifying additional motivational factors that influence the investment for learning. Research is also required to investigate motivational factors that can narrow the discrepancies among partners and minimize learning asymmetry. The framework of coordination and cooperation is quite generic and can be applicable to ITO decisions and other ITO settings.

## Conclusion

This study represents a step forward toward a holistic understanding of the factors influencing client and vendor relational learning and project success. The study extends previous research by explaining the antecedents of relational learning from the perspective of collaboration challenges and how to resolve the challenges using the coordination and collaboration mechanisms. The findings show that the interdependent model is better than the independent model in explaining the effects of collaboration mechanisms on relational learning in ITO projects. This research contributes to the literature by showing that coordination and cooperation mechanisms are interdependent and jointly influence outsourcing outcomes and also, that the cooperation mechanisms are perceived differently across the partners.
