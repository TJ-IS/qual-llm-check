# Shedding Light on the Dark: The Impact of Legal Enforcement on Darknet Transactions

Abstract. Darknet markets have been used increasingly for the transaction of drugs in the last decade. The growth of illicit drug transactions on darknet markets has led enforcement agencies to invest a greater proportion of time and effort to monitor and crack down on criminal activities on darknet websites. Whereas large-scale site-shutdown efforts involving policing agencies across various countries can help in slowing down the growth of these markets, such enforcement strategies may not be sustainable in the long run given the cost and time they require to coordinate. Thus, there is a need for alternative, costefficient strategies to police darknet markets on a regular basis. In response, this study attempts to empirically evaluate and quantify the effectiveness of selectively targeting large drug vendors on darknet sites. Using data from the three largest darknet markets, we employ a difference-in-differences procedure to assess the impact of the arrest of a majo drug dealer on subsequent darknet activities. Specifically, we contrast various outcomes from the policed site (Silk Road 2) with those from nonpoliced sites (Agora and Evolution) and find that enforcement efforts on the policed site reduced subsequent transaction levels and the number of remaining vendors. The enforcement was not only effective in deterring users in the same country as the arrestees, but also had a spillover effect on darknet participants who were beyond the prosecutorial jurisdictions of the arrestees. Test results on heterogeneous effects further suggest that small darknet drug vendors were most deterred by the arrest event and drug vendors selling dangerous drugs were more deterred relative to those selling less dangerous drugs. Our findings have policy and theoretical implications for law makers, enforcement agencies, and academics.

## Introduction

Globally, transactions on darknet markets have been growing steadily over the last few years. Specifically, the average daily transactions on darknet markets involve at least \$2 million worth of goods and services (Irrera 2019). The majority of darknet transactions involve drug trading, which is estimated to account for two thirds of all transactions in this market.<sup>1</sup> Compared with the traditional sale of drugs on the streets, the risks of encountering violence (e.g., disputes with street gang members, robberies by desperate buyers) when transacting on darknet sites are much lower. At the same time, drug transactions performed on darknet markets are generally believed to involve lower risks of arrests (Hout and Bingham 2013).

For these reasons, digital platforms that facilitate illicit activities have experienced high participation levels and stable growth over time (Chan et al. 2019). The unabated expansion of darknet drug markets is concerning from a public health perspective. As an underground economy that is not governed by a regu latory system, darknet markets allow for the transactions of impure and specialized designer drugs that can cause dangerous and unpredictable side effects. For instance, some drug dealers mix fentanyl, a cheap but powerful synthetic opioid, with other drugs to increase their profits. Without knowing that their purchased drug contains fentanyl, users may overdose, suffering brain damage and even death as fentanyl is 50 times more potent than morphine.

To combat the growing drug trafficking issue on darknet markets, enforcement agencies are monitoring and prosecuting darknet crimes through several multinational darknet crackdowns. For instance, a large-scale international enforcement operation, Operation Onymous, was launched by police forces from 17 countries in late 2014 to take down several darknet markets, including Cloud 9, Hydra, and Silk Road 2.0 (SR2). More recently, Operation Disruptor, one of the largest global darknet takedowns, enabled police to make 179 arrests across six countries.<sup>2</sup> Site shutdowns are currently the most prevalent form of legal enforcement against illicit darknet markets. However, these large-scale enforcement efforts involve significant investment of time and resources (Davies 2020). Despite the hefty expenses and lengthy process involved in coordinating these global operations, the shutdown of darknet sites has only a short-lived effect (De´cary-He´tu and Giommoni 2017). As such, darknet scholars call for an investigation of alternative enforcement strategies against these markets (Soska and Christin 2015) with a focus on cost-effective policing strategies that can be applied continuously.

A novel policing strategy put forth in criminology literature involves the use of targeted enforcement interventions, particularly the active targeting of large-scale drug dealers (Felbab-Brown 2013, Soska and Christin 2015). The selective targeting of high-profile criminals not only enables overwhelmed enforcement institutes to overcome problems resulting from a lack of resources, but also can produce deterrence effects similar to those imposed by large-scale crackdown operations (Braga and Weisburd 2012). The repeated application of selective targeting is found to be effective in reducing subsequent off-line drug-related crimes (Corsaro and McGarrell 2009, Kennedy 2009). For instance, the selective targeting of drug criminals in High Point, North Carolina, and 25 other U.S. cities dissolved local drug markets and kept them from returning (Kennedy 2009). Repeated application of this low-cost enforcement strategy disrupts drug markets as enforcement efforts catch up to the speed with which criminals organize and search for new transacting partners. Despite anecdotal evidence of the effectiveness of selective targeting in curbing street drug crimes, its effectiveness on darknet drug markets remains unclear as darknet drug transactions differ from street drug transactions in a few important ways.

First, drug transactions on darknet markets present lower risks for both the buyers and sellers. The concealment of participants’ IP addresses (via Tor) as well as the use of cryptocurrency keeps the market participants anonymous, which, in turn, mitigates legal risks perceived by darknet participants (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime 2020). The heightened sense of security offered by darknet technologies can nullify the deterrence effect of darknet policing efforts. Second, because of the technical requirements of participation, sellers and buyers tend to be young males who are tech savvy and educated (Kruithof et al. 2016). Compared with the participants in the street drug trade, individuals in darknet markets may be more resourceful in seeking means to reduce the risks of detection and arrest (Aldridge and Askew 2017) and, consequently, be less deterred by policing efforts. These differences suggest that the demographic profiles of the market participants on darknet sites are likely to differ critically from those who buy and sell drugs on the streets, which could, in turn, affect their responses to selective targeting. Furthermore, to our knowledge, the effectiveness of selective tar geting of major drug dealers has never been empirically validated as existing evaluations of this novel policing strategy are largely qualitative in nature.

Before utilizing selective targeting widely on darknet markets, there is a need to first evaluate whether the arrest of major drug vendors can reduce subsequent darknet transaction levels at all and, if so, the extent of its impact. To address this question, we rely on a unique data set that details darknet transactions on three of the largest darknet sites during the arrest of a major drug dealer on one of those sites. Going beyond our main result, we further evaluate the scope of the deterrence effect by assessing if the police arrests affect the users in prosecutorial jurisdictions outside that of the arrestees. We do so by evaluating the change in transaction levels of darknet participants who reside in countries other than that of the enforcement agency that made the arrests. Next, we assess the heterogeneous effects of the enforcement on different vendors by examining how vendors of varying sizes are impacted differently to understand their risk–value trade-offs of operating in the darknet market. In addition, we investigate how sellers of addictive drugs differ from sellers of less addictive drugs in terms of their postshock outcomes. Finally, we investigate whether the policing shock affects the de mand and/or supply side of the market and whether the enforcement effort impacts market concentration of the policed market.

Our analyses show that the arrests of darknet participants put a downward pressure on transaction volume and weekly participation levels on the affected site. This result is further supported by analyses conducted at the site level. We also find that news of the arrests of U.S. vendors had spillover effects on non–U.S. vendors. Furthermore, we find heterogeneous effects in the enforcement effort, wherein smaller vendors were relatively more affected by the arrest shock. Interestingly, vendors selling dangerous drugs reduced their drug-selling activities much more than those selling less dangerous drugs. This set of results suggests that selective targeting enforcement could be effective in dampening the market participation of risk-averse sellers but is less so against risk-tolerant sellers. We further learned that news of the arrest did not affect the average drug prices of the market, and the site became more consolidated after the policing event. A deeper look at the time trend reveals that small-scale vendors attempted to clear out their remaining inventory by reducing drug prices after the policing event before exiting the site.

This work makes a few contributions. First, our work contributes to the literature on darknet markets. Given the difficulty of gaining access to transactional data on darknet markets, it is not surprising that empirical work examining the dynamics of darknet markets is scant. Within this thin set of literature, prior works focus on documenting the types of drugs transacted, locations served by vendors, vendor ratings, and user network structure (e.g., Dolliver and Kenney 2016, Hardy and Norgaard 2016). Despite these efforts, we continue to be relatively uninformed about how market participants perceive the risk of dealing drugs on darknet sites. Specifically, a better understanding of drug vendors is needed for the development of effective enforcement measures to curb the growth of darknet drug markets. In particular, we theorize and assess whether the darknet drug market is made up of different types of drug dealers to which each unique population would react differently to legal sanctions so as to develop a nuanced understanding of darknet markets.

Second, by evaluating the extent of enforcement effect on darknet activities, our work adds to the literature on deterrence effects (Pratt et al. 2006). There is a need for more works in this space as past works on deterring cybercrimes find mixed results (e.g., Danaher and Smith 2014, Aguiar et al. 2018). Not only is it unclear whether legal sanctions deter darknet participants, it is also of interest to understand if the arrest of a darknet drug vendor generates spillover effects to criminals from different prosecutorial jurisdictions who are operating on the same darknet site. At first glance, the scope of the enforcement effect should only be effective in the localities governed by prosecutorial reach. Yet our empirical investigations find counterintuitive results, providing new implications for the deterrence literature. Finally, our work adds to the criminology literature on selective targeting (Felbab-Brown 2013). By quantifying the effect size of selective targeting, we contribute directly to the assessment of the selective targeting paradigm, which mainly receives qualitative evaluations of its effectiveness. Through the process of quantifying the impact of selective targeting using a quasi-experimental approach, we also make a related contribution of presenting novel causal insights to the existing literature on darknet enforcement that mainly focuses on site shutdowns.

## Literature Review Darknet Markets

The rise of darknet markets has drawn increased academic interest. Information systems (IS) researchers explore various issues related to the darknet, which include identifying hacker threats and assets from the darknet (Samtani et al. 2017, 2020), developing frameworks for conducting darknet research (Benjamin et al. 2019), and understanding user reactions to negative feedback on the darknet (Batikas and Kretschmer 2018). Beyond IS research, there is also a small but growing body of darknet literature that attempts to understand how such markets are affected by law enforcement.

For instance, Soska and Christin (2015) collect and process data from a few darknet sites. Using this data, they compare the count of darknet transactions before and after site shutdowns. De´cary-He´tu and Giommoni (2017) add to this set of findings by considering the changes to drug listing prices on darknet sites before and after Operation Onymous, a major darknet site crackdown event. Van Buskirk et al. (2017) study the vendor count on darknet markets before and after Operation Onymous. Through descriptive evidence, these studies find that site shutdowns lead to temporary disruptions of darknet markets, which subsequently recover to their pre-enforcement level in a few months.

Whereas this descriptive evidence of the recovery of the marketplace is insightful, to our best knowledge, a systematic quantification of the impact of legal enforcement on darknet markets has never been performed. A formal quantification exercise is necessary to evaluate the value of policing efforts against their costs. At the same time, previous studies on darknet markets focus narrowly on the most extreme form of policing, which involves site shutdowns. Given their huge logistical costs, site shutdowns are unlikely to be cost-efficient and are unsustainable over time as shown in previous works examining the shutdown of piracy sites (e.g., Aguiar et al. 2018). Going forward, alternative, cost-efficient enforcement strategies should be used to keep darknet activities under control (Soska and Christin 2015).

## Deterrence Theory

The economic view of crime production posits that individuals choose to commit crime when it “pays”; that is, when the benefits of the crime outweigh the costs (Becker 1968). Although individuals may not be perfectly rational, they are reasonably aware of the potential costs and benefits of engaging in a criminal act. Knowing that one’s involvement in illicit activities will elicit punishment, individuals are inclined to think twice before acting and, thus, become deterred from committing crimes (Pratt et al. 2006). Accordingly, the choice to commit crime is less attractive when society puts in place policies that heighten the costs of illegal conduct (Nagin 1998). Under this reasoning, the central empirical claim of deterrence theory is that criminal justice efforts are necessary as they should, independent of other social processes, have an appreciable effect on criminal behavior (Tittle and Rowe 1974, Tittle 1980).

Hence, through the lens of deterrence theory, the perceived certainty of punishment should lower people’s likelihood of offending and have an inverse relationship with the crime rate (Blumstein et al. 1978). Despite this intuitive reasoning, prior studies examining the relationship between certainty of punishment and crime find mixed results (e.g., Hollinger and Clark 1983; Paternoster et al. 1983, 1985; Meier et al. 1984; Paternoster and Iovanni 1986; Bachman et al. 1992; Horney and Marshall 1992; Foglia 1997). In fact, a meta-analysis of more than 200 criminology studies reveals that variables reflecting perceived certainty of punishment in various contexts do not always correlate with crime rates (Pratt and Cullen 2005). The equivocality of this body of work has led to a call for academic studies to critically assess the validity of deterrence theory (Paternoster 1987, Pratt et al. 2006). In particular, scholars are asked to evaluate the presence of deterrence effects under various settings as a means to better understand the specific conditions that influence individuals’ perception of sanction threats (Williams and Hawkins 1986, D’Arcy and Herath 2011).

Within the IS domain, several articles on IS security and cybercrime draw on deterrence theory to explain deviant user behaviors. In the context of IS security research, Straub (1990) uses investment in security countermeasures (i.e., security policies, security staff hours, and technical controls) as proxies for perceived certainty of sanctions and finds that the use of these deterrents results in decreased incidence of computer abuse. Hearth and Rao (2009) find that the certainty of detection is positively associated with IS security policy compliance intentions. However, Harrington (1996) finds that instilling codes of ethics, a type of formal sanction, does not affect employee judgments or intentions to commit computer abuse. Similarly, D’Arcy et al. (2009) do not find an association between perceived certainty of sanction and IS misuse intentions. Akin to the broader literature on deterrence effects, this set of IS studies finds mixed support for the tenets of deterrence theory. The inconsistent findings could arise from the differences in the deterrent measure, study population, and organizational culture and norms.

Beyond IS security research, scholars also examine whether various enforcement policies deter cybercrimes. For instance, Hui et al. (2017) reveal that collective enforcement efforts, such as the Convention on Cybercrime (COC), which harmonizes national laws and increases international cooperation against cybercrimes, are generally effective in reducing distributed denialof-service attacks. However, the researchers find that variations in how participating countries adopt the COC can fundamentally influence whether the deterrence effect ensues. Using an event study methodology, Png et al. (2008) find limited evidence that domestic enforce ment deters cyberattacks originating from the respective country. Cybercrime scholars also examine the effective ness of enforcement strategies in deterring piracy. Deterrence measures, such as site shutdowns and graduated response policies, are found to be effective in some cases (Danaher and Smith 2014, Danaher et al. 2014) but inef fective in others (McKenzie 2017, Aguiar et al. 2018). Here, differences arising from inconsistency in enforcing the law (Adermon and Liang 2014) and individua beliefs about the illegality of piracy (Wingrove et al. 2011) may explain the discrepancy in effectiveness of these deterrence measures. Our work bears some similarity to Aguiar et al. (2018), who study the deterrence effect of news coverage of a major piracy site’s shutdown. Whereas part of the research objective of Aguiar et al. (2018) is to assess whether the news coverage causes nonoffenders to become offending users who visit piracy sites, our work focuses on the direct deterrence impact of offending users within the enforced darknet site. More importantly, because drug-related crimes and piracy come with different sets of risks and punishments, the deterrence effects in each scenario vary. Moreover, the perceived risks of punishment between the two study contexts are different given that the two studies examine different types of legal sanctions. In the case of a site shutdown, there may not be legal punishments for the digital pirates who use the site. However, the arrest of a major darknet vendor in ou study signals that darknet drug vendors can be prose cuted by law enforcement. These differences warrant a separate research effort to study the deterrence effect of the arrest event on users in the darknet market.

In sum, across various studies examining deterrence in digital settings, we learn that sanctions for discouraging unlawful and/or undesirable user behavior may not always have a deterrent effect. Given this ambigu ity, academics cannot assume that the effectiveness of various information technology–related sanctions will hold, and thus, there is a need to perform an independent evaluation of the deterrence effect of these sanctions in new digital contexts. That said, the deterrent effect of the arrest of darknet participants has never been empirically assessed to our best knowledge, and an evaluation of its efficacy in deterring future darknet participation and transaction is warranted.

Deterrence Factors Against Drug Crimes. Deterrence research shows that the deterrence effect of sanctions can differ drastically from individual to individual (Tittle 1980, Mann et al. 2003). A host of personal and contextual factors can influence the relationships specified by deterrence theory (Jacobs 2010). Drug sales are typically considered felony charges, whereas drug possession without the intent to sell constitutes as a misdemeanor or even an infraction on the level of a minor traffic ticket (U.S. Department of Justice 2020).<sup>3</sup> Guided by this aspect of drug markets, legal sanctions likely exert a much stronger impact on drug vendors compared with drug buyers. For this reason, we focus our discussion on vendor-related factors that likely play a role in impacting deterrence effects within the darknet drug markets.

First, because drug dealing is a risky endeavor, drug vendors tend to develop risk-reduction strategies to protect themselves against the associated legal punishments (Guan and Lo 2021). For instance, some drug vendors may engage in a legitimate business as a cover for their drug-dealing operations (Vannostrand and Tewksbury 1999). Such an approach is a type of “contextual assimilation” strategy wherein drug offenders intentionally create self-images that are consistent with a nonoffending identity to conceal their illicit activities (Jacobs and Miller 1998). Drug dealers may also reduce risks to themselves by diverting the most dangerous roles to gang members of lower rank. Specifically, in drug syndicates, lower level distributors are hired for the riskiest work, whereas the big boss and the higher-ups of the syndicate are far removed from actual drug transactions (Johnson and Natarajan 1995). Finally, drug lords are known to bribe governments, police, and prison officials with large sums of money (Cohan 2012). In the event of arrests, these bribed authorities let them off the hook or pronounce a lighter punishment. Thus, larger drug vendors with a greater ability to reduce their participation risk perceive a lower certainty of punishment, which, in turn, reduces the deterrence effect of sanctions on these individuals.

A second factor that could influence the deterrence effect is that of individual moral beliefs. Moral beliefs refer to the extent to which one perceives an illicit act to be morally offensive (Paternoster and Simpson 1996). Individuals with strong moral beliefs effectively restrain themselves from criminal behavior, whereas less morally inhibited individuals may justify their behavior in a given context as harmless and noncriminal in nature (Sykes and Matza 1957). Many drug sellers dismiss their crimes with neutralization techniques that include denying injury to their clients (Coomber et al. 2016) and resisting feelings of guilt by not seeing themselves as drug dealers (Jacinto et al. 2008). Through qualitative interviews with drug vendors, Dickinson and Jacques (2019) find that sellers of soft drugs rarely express guilt about their acts of selling these illicit drugs unlike sellers of hard drugs. Interestingly, some drug vendors even argue that their drugs provide physical and spiritual benefits for their customers, claiming that they help individuals with medical conditions lessen their pain and reduce their stress levels (Dickinson and Jacques

2019). Given differing moral beliefs, drug vendors of certain drugs may not be deterred by legal sanctions as they do not see themselves as drug dealers who infringe the law.

## Criminal Motives of Darknet Users

The motivation of criminals is another important determinant in the production of criminal behaviors (Tittle and Botchkovar 2005). A consideration of the motives of darknet users is needed to understand why enforcement strategies are (in)effective in deterring their subsequent participation. Transacting parties in the darknet drug trade are likely made up of a population distinct from cybercriminals examined in prior studies (e.g., hackers, digital pirates) such that their motivations might not overlap with those of cybercriminals examined previously. For instance, the psychological benefits of dominance and prestige represent a strong motivator for hackers to perform cyberattacks (Kshetri 2006), but such motivations may not always apply to darknet drug sellers.

A major motivation to sell drugs on the darknet comes from the resulting financial gains (Martin et al. 2020). The ability to conduct anonymous sales to a vast network of online drug buyers avoids the risks from both legal enforcement and physical violence from competing drug sellers or desperate customers. As darknet vendors are not constrained to selling to trusted clientele in their close proximity, participation in darknet markets can lead to increased profits (Moeller and Sandberg 2019). Within the safer darknet environment, drug vendors are more willing to cater to more customers, leading to more transactions and profits.

Among drug vendors who participate in the online drug trade for monetary reasons, there are sellers who are motivated by financial necessity and those motivated by greed alone. Through a qualitative interview with various drug sellers, Vannostrand and Tewksbury (1999) find that there was a group of individuals who had to derive their basic incomes from drug dealing because they experienced repeated failures and obstacles when seeking financial security through conventional means. Often, these dealers face a lack of employment opportunities and/or find drug dealing to far outweigh their earnings from a legitimate job. Some of these economically depressed individuals may also turn to drug dealing as a means to financially sustain their families. In contrast, another group of dealers interviewed by Vannostrand and Tewksbury (1999) report little need to supplement their incomes through drug dealing. These individuals often maintain conventional employment or operate profitable legal businesses but find the earning potential for drug sales to be far greater than their legitimate careers. Likewise, Mohamed and Fritsvold (2010) find that middle and upper class individuals sell drugs to fund an extravagant lifestyle.

These financial motives attract a variety of vendors to deal drugs on the darknet markets. The ability to earn extra income through drug dealing in a relatively secure environment lures individuals who would not have entered the drug trade previously to start selling drugs online. Interviews with darknet drug vendors support this view: “I hadn’t ever thought about selling drugs in any capacity because I dislike violence and it just seemed impossible to be involved in selling drugs in ‘real life’ without running into some sort of confrontation pretty quickly … I was always too scared and slightly nerdy to do that and never really contemplated it seriously until the dark web” (Martin et al. 2020, p. 568).

In addition to these individual vendors who operate independently, anecdotal evidence suggests that largescale drug syndicates also capitalize on darknet markets to expand their drug businesses.<sup>4</sup> Specifically, Aldridge and De´cary-He´tu (2014) find that a sizable proportion of drug transactions on darknet markets take place at quantity–price levels that are indicative of business-tobusiness sales. The study reveals that wholesale drug dealers are selling to retailers who, in turn, resell the drugs to individual consumers. The enforcement of darknet sites might exert differential deterrence effects across small- and large-scale vendors to which the extent of the overall deterrence is likely conditional on the relative proportion of each type of drug seller.

## Study Context and Data Description

In May 2014, the leader of a large, industrial, illegal, drugmanufacturing operation was arrested. The perpetrator, Jeremy Donagal (nicknamed the “Xanax King”) was arrested for producing and distributing significant quantities of illegal alprazolam (Xanax), gamma-hydroxybutyric acid, steroids, and other drugs on darknet sites, including Silk Road (defunct during our study period) and SR2. Donagal and his associates stamped trademarks on the manufactured pills to make them look like they were produced by the pharmaceutical company Pfizer. The account under Xanax King was run by a team of nine people, and it was involved in approximately 60 transactions each week between December 2013 and May 2014. Following the arrest of Donagal and his associates, his SR2 account was used by enforcement agencies to engage in controlled deliveries to drug buyers on SR2, which subsequently led to the arrest of close to 60 other individuals in the United States. After the series of arrests were completed, the arrest of Donagal was made known to the public in early June 2014.<sup>5</sup>

This arrest incident represents the first successful enforcement effort on SR2 in which legal agencies arrested a major drug vendor operating on one of the most popular darknet sites. In contrast to site shutdowns, the arrest of Donagal and his associates was an instance of selective targeting enforcement, wherein policing efforts were focused on the arrest of a largescale darknet drug dealer. The costs involved in tracking and arresting large-scale drug dealers can be lower than that of small-scale dealers because the more notorious an online moniker is, the more likely it will leave detectable trails for law enforcement to pick up. It can be difficult for drug vendors to find a balance between masking their identity to evade law enforcement and leaving their identity unmasked to attract potential criminal collaboration (e.g., suppliers) and customers (Davies 2020). Through repeated tracking and apprehension of large-scale vendors in the darknet market, a selective targeting strategy can be effective as it disables the most pernicious group of criminals and signals to the rest of the perpetrators that the risks of transacting in the darknet market are not as low as previously perceived (Felbab-Brown 2013).

We perform an initial assessment of the selective targeting of large-scale drug vendors by empirically quantifying its impact on subsequent transactions on SR2. SR2 came online a month after the first Silk Road site was taken down. The new site was similar to the original site in the way it appeared and functioned except that it had a new security feature that allowed users to employ Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) encryption as an additional authentication measure. Similar to other darknet sites, SR2 operated exclusively on the Tor network and required all its transactions to be paid in bitcoin to preserve user anonymity and evade detection by law enforcement. After placing the order, the buyer makes a payment, which is stored in escrow. The darknet site acts as the escrow that holds the buyer’s payment. After the item is shipped to the buyer, the buyer needs to finalize the purchase by providing seller feedback and rating on SR2. Upon receipt of the feedback rating, the darknet platform releases the escrowed money to the vendor (Christin 2013, Dittus et al. 2018). The finalizing process is mandatory: should buyers not complete the process, the darknet site automatically finalizes pending orders after some time to release the escrowed money to the seller (Christin 2013).<sup>6</sup> The escrow service allows the platform operator to resolve transaction-related disputes between buyers and vendors with respect to whether the item under consideration was sent and received.<sup>7</sup> This mandatory step of providing feedback as part of the purchase process across all darknet sites means that the number of reviews received is highly correlated to the transaction volume of darknet vendors.

In less than a year since its launch, the SR2 platform grew to be the leading darknet marketplace. In September 2014, it was estimated to generate monthly sales of at least US\$8 million and had approximately 150,000 active users. According to the FBI, the majority of the listings on SR2 were for controlled substances, including, among others, 1,783 listings for “psychedelics,”

1,697 listings for “ecstasy,” 1,707 listings for “cannabis,” and 379 listings for “opioids.”<sup>8</sup>

## Data Description

In our empirical assessment of the policing impact on darknet transactions, we rely on data from three major darknet markets in 2014. Our study period goes from April 2014 to October 2014, during which only one major police arrest took place on SR2. Specifically, we focus on SR2, Agora, and Evolution in our analysis for a few reasons. First, among all darknet markets, these three platforms ranked the highest in terms of market share across the darknet at that time. Thus, analysis results derived from these websites are likely to be representative of market behavior from other darknet platforms of interest (i.e., sizable darknet markets). The second reason relates to the fact that Agora and Evolution were both in full operation during the period when SR2 was online and were free from legal enforcement events. In addition, within our study period, there were no known legal enforcement activities conducted on SR2 after the arrest of the Xanax King (Branwen et al. 2015).<sup>9</sup> This time window enables us to avoid confounding influences from other arrests or police crackdowns, making Agora and Evolution suitable counterfactual platforms for contrasting the impact of policing shock on SR2. At the same time, by setting our analysis to begin in April 2014, we have a study period in which the sites have matured and stabilized, reducing the chance that our estimation is picking up the effects of abnormal site growth. We chose to end the study period in October 2014 because SR2 was shut down a month later in Operation Onymous, and we wanted to avoid any potential anticipation effects.<sup>10</sup> Figure 1 provides a timeline of the major events relevant to our study design.

Our analysis focuses on the drug trading activities on these three platforms as drugs are the largest class of transactions across these sites. Our data source comes from a darknet archive, which was scraped and made available by a group of darknet researchers (Branwen et al. 2015). This data set is used by several studies for a variety of purposes, including drug component analyses (Rhumorbarbe et al. 2016), descriptive statistics on darknet drug trading (Demant et al. 2018), and darknet web page structure analyses (Ghosh et al. 2017). The scrapes for these three websites were conducted on a daily basis to minimize the risks of missing data because of intermittent crawling blocks. The frequent scrapes allow for the recovery of a complete set of feedback for each vendor. Data were scraped from several mirrors to reduce missing data arising from the blocking of the crawlers by the websites. As Online Figure A1 shows, SR2 has the largest market share among the three darknet sites before the policing shock. Its market share dropped relative to that of the other two sites after the shock.

The online appendix presents a screenshot of the item pages on SR2. Online Figure A2 shows the item description page to which we parsed information of the vendor, the items sold, and their respective prices. Online Figure A3 shows the item feedback page, which has feedback details including text, rating, and date. Our parsing process is consistent with previous work on darknet transactions (e.g., Soska and Christin 2015). We recruited a research assistant to manually label the free responses in the “shipped from” field. Following prior works that utilize this data set (e.g., Rhumorbarbe et al. 2016, Demant et al. 2018), we identified duplicate feedback using the unique combination of vendor ID, product ID, rating, review text, and review date and removed these entries from our data set. We further corroborated the reliability of the scraped data by comparing it with the search results of a widely used darknet search engine, GRAMS. The comparison revealed that the number of products from the two sources is quite similar, suggesting that our scraped data are of an acceptable quality.

Darknet markets are unique in that these platforms transfer crypto-payments to the vendor only after the consumer verifies receipt of the ordered product and leaves a review for the vendor (Christin 2013, Dittus et al. 2018). Several darknet sites require this procedure in an attempt to make fraudulent behavior costly and incentivize vendors to transact honestly so that moral hazard problems on this digital marketplace can be reduced (Bhaskar et al. 2019). Relying on this rule, all darknet studies use the number of reviews as a proxy for the number of transactions on darknet markets (e.g., Christin 2013, Soska and Christin 2015). Furthermore, through an empirical validation with court evidence, Soska and Christin (2015) find that the number of reviews correlates strongly with the sales volume of darknet sites, making this variable a good proxy for darknet transactions. Thus, we follow the approach of utilizing the number of reviews as a proxy for the volume of transactions for each of the sites. The summary statistics of our data set are reported in Table 1. Given that the arrest event on SR2 targeted U.S. perpetrators, we further split our data set into U.S. and non–U.S. vendors, which we subsequently use to understand the differences in enforcement effects across user groups.

## Empirical Strategy

To assess the impacts of police enforcement on darknet transactions, we rely on the news release of the aforementioned enforcement effort as a treatment shock to the SR2 marketplace. The use of this arrest event is appropriate for assessing the impact of enforcement on future market behavior as the timing and success of this policing effort are likely to be exogenous. Given that the enforcement efforts on the perpetrators were conducted in secrecy, the arrested vendors and buyers did not have foreknowledge of the planned arrests and were unable to preemptively react to them. Furthermore, being that this was the first successful infiltration and enforcement of SR2, the remaining participants in the marketplace were not exposed to any prior arrest incidents on this darknet platform. This scenario is beneficial for deriving a clean identification of the policing effect by allowing us to abstract away from anticipation effects that users might have should they be exposed to other policing efforts on SR2.

The approach of utilizing police arrests of selected vendors as a shock to estimate the enforcement effect has the added benefit of offering a causal interpretation of the policing effect, which cannot be achieved in previous studies that examine site shutdowns. In the case of website-shutdown operations, one can only determine the policing effectiveness by checking if the count of transactions or vendor accounts recovered to its preshutdown figures on alternative sites (Soska and Christin 2015, De´cary-He´tu and Giommoni 2017). Such an approach contends with the issue of new users entering the market over time and is unable to produce numerical estimates to characterize the market-dampening effect of legal enforcement. Here, the use of a quasi-experimental approach quantifies the enforcement effects on darknet markets by accounting for temporal shocks through comparisons with the transactional volumes from control units

We rely on a difference-in-differences (DID) model to estimate the impact of the arrest shock on transaction activities on the focal darknet market. In our DID setup, we contrast the impact of the enforcement on transactions of treated vendors on SR2 with that of control ven dors on Agora and Evolution. Darknet sellers tend to provide their public PGP key on their profile, and consumers use that to encrypt sensitive order-related information (e.g., “ship to” address, recipient name) such that only the intended vendor can access this information. The use of an asymmetric encryption/decryption technique guarantees the confidentiality of communications between a pair of users to which the PGP key can be used to identify unique individual sellers. Hence, the PGP key can be used to identify accounts belonging to unique vendors across different darknet sites (Ho and Ng 2016). Following established practices in Brose´us et al. (2017) and Soska and Christin (2015), we dropped vendors that have the same PGP key across treatment and control sites to abstract from effects from multihoming sellers (i.e., sellers who deal drugs on multiple darknet sites). We also took the step of removing vendors that bear the same account name on both treated and control sites as these could potentially be multihoming vendors as well. Even without the removal of such vendors, our results remain highly similar. 11

Given that vendors on the control darknet sites were not policed during the study period and each darknet market was well-protected by security and anonymization features, vendors on Agora and Evolution were unlikely to be affected by the arrest incident on SR2. Yet one might argue that the U.S. market participants on Evolution and Agora could still be influenced by the arrest as they were policed by the same legal authorities that arrested the Xanax King. If this possibility were to hold, then some of these U.S. vendors may move away from Evolution and Agora. This movement of treated users can cause the transactions on the two control sites to vary systematically across the preshock and postshock periods, thereby invalidating the properties that the counterfactuals should bear.

Cognizant of these issues, we adopt two countermeasures in our estimation procedures. First, we restrict our control sample to include only the non–U.S. vendors transacting on Agora and Evolution<sup>12</sup> to reduce the chance that the control sample is affected by the arrest incident on SR2. Arguably, such a procedure may not be foolproof, which is why we further rely on the second countermeasure described next: we conduct a set of statistical tests to contrast the vendor count and transaction levels on Agora and Evolution before and after the police shock to see if the SR2 policing shock produced a systematic shift on these control sites. We report the results of these checks after our main estimation. We conduct our main analysis at the vendor level to derive insights on how the enforcement might influence individual trading behavior. Our main regression specification is as follows:

$$
y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} * P o l i c i n g E f f o r t _ {i t} + v e n d o r _ {i} + w e e k _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where the main dependent variable is the number of reviews each vendor i has in each week t. This outcome variable represents the equilibrium outcome stemming from changes in the supply and the demand sides of the market. Because of the skewed distribution of the review count, we log-transformed values of this variable. We further rely on a binary indicator that denotes the weeks in which vendors have a nonzero and positive number of reviews. This variable reflects whether vendors have active transactions each week and provides insights on each vendor’s willingness to transact on darknet sites and whether buyers were purchasing from these active vendors. The PolicingEffort variable is one for vendors that transact on SR2 after the news release of the arrest event (i.e., June 2, 2014) and zero otherwise. This term is our main coefficient of interest, and it is the DID estimator that captures how the transactions of the treated vendors change after the policing event relative to the same change of control vendors. Vendor-level fixed effects are included to account for heterogeneous vendor effects. Week-level fixed effects are included to account for temporal changes experienced in the drug market. Errors are clustered at the vendor level.

Whereas the vendor-level analysis provides insights on the individual level, it does not tell us the magnitude of the enforcement effort at the platform level.

Furthermore, these vendor-level results inform us of the efficacy of enforcement on darknet sites but do not inform us how the enforcement efforts work. In particular, users might react to the policing shock in two ways, for example, (1) users could decrease their subsequent transaction and participation levels and/or (2) vendors could exit the treated site altogether. Although the vendor-level analyses examine the first possibility, they do not tell us if the policing event caused a significant number of vendors to leave the site. To this end, we performed platform-level analy ses denoted in Equation (2) to supplement the vendorlevel estimates:

$$
\begin{array}{c} y _ {j k t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} * P o l i c i n g E f f o r t _ {j t} + w e b s i t e _ {j} + d r u g _ {k} \\ + w e e k _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {j k t}, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where $y _ { j k t }$ is the logarithm-transformed outcome variables, and we look at the total number of reviews at site j for each drug type k sold in week t. The total number of reviews on each site provides insights on how the overall transaction volume shifted as a result of the enforcement. We also rely on the number of remaining vendors to assess exiting behaviors. We are able to infer if a vendor remained on the site based on the date it received its last review on the darknet site.<sup>13</sup> Given that a seller has not been actively transacting for a period of time, we rely on this information to identify the vendors that remain in the market each week. The PolicingEffort variable in Equation (2) is denoted as one when the focal drug listing is on SR2 after the news release of the policing event and zero otherwise. With potential differences across the various darknet sites under consideration, we include a website fixed effect to account for potential heteroge neity. We include a drug type fixed effect in these DID specifications to account for heterogeneity across the demand and supply patterns for different drugs. We also include a week fixed effect to account for systematic temporal shifts in the drug market. All models are estimated using an ordinary least squares specification.

## Main Results

We first report the results of Equation (1) in Table 2. For vendor-level analysis, we restrict our data to only vendors that exist on the platform before the policing shock as DID specifications require the analysis unit to be observed in both pretreatment and posttreatment periods. Across columns (1) and (3) of Table $^ { 2 , }$ we see that the DID coefficient is negative and signifi cant, indicating that the number of reviews for each treated vendor and the policed site decreased significantly after the policing event relative to those of the control vendors on Agora and Evolution. Specifically, news of the arrest of U.S.-based participants on SR2 led to a 39.2% decrease in the number of transactions for each vendor on average (column (1)), and the number of reviews on SR2 decreased by 70.5% relative to control sites (column (3)). At the same time, we see that the number of vendors with active transactions decreased by 15.8% on average after the news of the arrest (Table 2, column (2)).

Next, we look at the number of remaining vendors on the platform. There is a 56.2% decline in the number of remaining vendors on the policed site (column (4) of Table 2). This result suggests that the policing effort induced a negative supply shock in which a significant proportion of drug sellers decided to exit SR2 altogether. The coefficient magnitudes for the weekly drop in transaction levels (\~2,771) are much larger than the weekly transactions of the arrested vendor (i.e., the Xanax King made about 60 transactions on average weekly). Furthermore, through the coefficient in column (4), we note that the estimated drop in vendor count (\~382 accounts) is much larger than the number of accounts belonging to the arrested vendor (one account). Because the arrest led to a decrease in transactions and vendor count beyond that of the arrested SR2 participants, the arrest incident must have affected transactions beyond those from the arrested vendor and his associates.

## Heterogeneous Effects and Mechanisms

The main analyses establish a negative effect of legal enforcement on the subsequent transactions and vendor count on the policed darknet site. This negative effect is found to hold for users who are within the same prosecutorial location as the arrested users (i.e., the United States). Whereas the quantification of the main impact of selective targeting on darknet markets is helpful, open questions about the extent of its deterrence effect and underlying mechanisms remain. First, the selective targeting may bear different levels of effectiveness on various groups of users. Thus, we assess whether non-U.S. SR2 users were also affected by the arrest. Second, we further investigate differential responses across vendor groups to get a sense of the effectiveness of the arrest in deterring different vendor segments (e.g., vendor size, drug type sold). Finally, it is informative to understand how policing affected the darknet site in terms of its market concentration and price.

Impact on Vendors from Non–U.S. Countries. Drug trafficking on darknet markets is considered an international crime such that policing agencies in different countries collaborate to persecute drug criminals. In our context, it is plausible that the arrest of U.S. darknet users might have a spillover effect on darknet users from different countries given that the successful enforcement at SR2 is evidence that the security of drug dealing on the darknet platform is not foolproof and enforcement agencies in non–U.S. countries may also crack down such activities.

We repeat our main analyses by using non–U.S. users on SR2 as the treated sample in place of U.S. users. The results of this analysis are reported in columns (1)–(4) of Table 3. Interestingly, we observe that the arrest event had a negative and significant impact on transaction volumes, vendors with active transac tions, and remaining vendor count. The results show that the number of total reviews from each non–U.S. vendor decreased by about 31.1% (column (1)) and vendors with active transactions fell by 9.5% (column (2)). For the site-level analyses, the average review count at the site level decreased by 63.6% (column (3)), and the number of vendors remaining on the site decreased by 53.2% (column (4)). We further conduct a set of chi-tests using postregression estimates to contrast these effect sizes with those from the U.S. vendors. We find that the impacts for non–U.S. vendors are significantly smaller than U.S. vendors across the various outcome measures. Based on these results, it appears that the policing efforts have a spillover effect on darknet users beyond the prosecutorial jurisdiction of the enforcement agency, but these effects are smaller than those experienced by U.S. users.

Heterogeneous Effects: Vendor Size. Thus far, our subanalyses reveal that the deterrence effect of the policing shock can extend beyond the immediate prosecutorial jurisdiction of the enforcement agency. Given that the extent of deterrence can vary with personal and contextual factors (Jacobs 2010), we explore the heterogeneous effect of the policing effect by assessing how different U.S. vendors were affected by the arrest news. As discussed in our literature review, vendor size likely influences the cost of operating in the drug-dealing business. To this end, we develop an analytical model to describe how the news of the arrest may influence the decisions of large and small vendors. In our model, each vendor chooses the quantity that maximizes their profit in a competitive drug market. Each vendor faces a variable cost that varies with the size of their business. We also model a site-wide fixed arrest cost, which increases with the number of active vendors in the market. After the news of the arrest, all vendors face an additional risk of arrest with every drug transaction they make. Following past literature on drug markets, larger vendors have more resources to evade detection and legal punishment (Johnson and Natarajan 1995, Jacobs and Miller 1998), which is reflected in our model setup. Under this model setup, we find that the smallest vendors leave the market first. Among the vendors that remain in the market, the decrease in transaction quantity is smaller among the largest vendors relative to the smaller vendors. Online Appendix B details the full model setup and the corresponding proofs.

Heterogeneous Effects: Drug Type. Drug vendors also differ in terms of the drugs they sell. Some drugs have a higher potential for abuse as they can create severe psychological and physical dependence. The literature on drug dealing provides suggestive anecdotal evidence that drug vendors could be deterred by different degrees depending on the drugs they sell. Specifically, vendors of hard drugs tend to perceive a greater certainty of punishment compared with vendors of soft drugs (Dickinson and Jacques 2019), which subsequently induces varying levels of deterrence to different groups of vendors. To empirically assess whether such differences in deterrence exist across the darknet vendors in our study, we rely on the drug categorization scheme provided by the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration to identify drugs of different potency and risk. Under this categorization scheme, there are five drug schedules (i.e., I to V), with schedule I–IV drugs having high-to-moderate potential for abuse and schedule V drugs having the lowest potential for abuse.<sup>14</sup> According to this classification, we classify vendors that sell drugs in schedule I–IV to be those selling dangerous drugs and those who do not sell any of these drugs to be selling less dangerous drugs. Vendors that sell a combination of schedule I–IV and schedule V drugs are not considered in our analysis. We run a regression that includes the indicator variable for sales of dangerous drugs and its interaction with the DID term. Results in column (1) of Table 5 show that the transaction volume of vendors selling relatively more dangerous substances was significantly lower than that of vendors that sold less dangerous drugs after the policing event. Similarly, we note that the vendors selling the more dangerous drugs had significantly fewer active transactions compared with those that sold less dangerous drugs after the arrest event (column (2)). These results support our theory that the drug type sold can induce different levels of deterrence on the vendors.

## Validity Checks and Robustness Tests

Matching. A concern of the main analyses is that there might be differences between the vendors on control darknet platforms and the vendors on the treated platform. To alleviate this potential issue, we match vendors in the control and treatment sites based on their characteristics before the arrest event. Specifically, we consider the following covariates in the matching process using coarsened exact matching (CEM): average price of drug listings, number of unique drugs sold, number of drug categories sold, vendor’s tenure on the site, and the squared term of the vendor’s tenure. Price is used as a matching covariate as it directly affects the demand level. The numbers of unique drugs and drug categories sold are good indicators of the size of the customer base that each vendor serves, and they are used to account for intervendor differences in transaction volume. Finally, vendors that have been operating on the site for longer likely accumulate a larger base of buyers, leading to larger transaction volumes. We include both a linear and squared term for vendor tenure to account for nonlinear growth effects in the transaction volume. After performing the matching procedure, we conducted t-tests to verify that the vendors in the control and treated groups are statistically equivalent across all matching covariates. As reported in Online Table A2, we see that CEM arrives at a balanced set of treated and control vendors, whereby the means of the main covariates are not statistically different across these two groups of vendors. Using these matched vendors, we reperformed our vendor- and site-level analyses. The results in Online Table A3 show that results from the matched sample are qualitatively similar to those reported in our main analyses.

Whereas CEM provides us with some confidence that the static differences across vendors are not driving the main results, it is possible that time-varying confounders may be influencing the results. As such, we further rely on synthetic matching to construct a statistically comparable set of control units through the weighting of counterfactual vendors’ information over each period so that time-varying characteristics can also be accounted for (Abadie 2005). To this end, we employ a generalized synthetic control method (He et al. 2020) with both time and drug fixed effects. The corresponding estimated treatment effects of the synthetic matching methods are presented in Online Table A4. The results under the synthetic matching are consistent with those derived from the unmatched analysis, providing assurance that our results are not driven by time-varying factors. Parallel Trend Test. A fundamental assumption for the DID analysis is the parallel trend assumption, which requires the difference between the control and treatment groups to be constant over time in the absence of treatment. Violation of this assumption invalidates the DID estimation framework. This assumption is generally not testable because the posttreatment counterfactual outcomes cannot be directly observed. Existing literature on the DID technique partially assesses the validity of this assumption by examining whether there is a preshock trend. A common approach to this check is to include estimates of the leads and lags of the treatment effect (Autor 2003). Should the parallel trend assumption be satisfied, the interaction with the lead terms should yield nonsignificant coefficients.

Using this procedure, we derive the estimated coefficients for the leads and lags for all outcome measures (Figure 2). Generally, the coefficients before the arresting shock are not statistically different from zero, indicating that the parallel trends assumption is satisfied, affirming the validity of the DID approach.

Validity of Control Platforms. A concern with the approach taken is that vendors might have left SR2 upon learning of the police arrests and migrated to the control darknet sites. If this happened, then the chosen control sites would not be appropriate counterfactuals as the arrest event exerts an indirect influence on these sites by increasing their count of incoming vendors. At the same time, the arrest incident on SR2 might also cause the control vendors to leave Agora and Evolution for the fear that the police might target them next.

By removing seller accounts that share the same PGP key and/or similar account names on control sites with those on the treated website, we attempt to account for potential issues that may stem from user migration. In addition, by relying strictly on non–U.S. vendors on control websites, we reduce the likelihood that the control vendors were affected by a spillover enforcement effect from SR2. Despite these steps, it remains plausible that the control sites may continue to experience an inflow or outflow of vendors after the policing event. We further consider how our estimates might be biased based on known effects uncovered in related previous works. Specifically, enforcement efforts can bring about either a deterrence effect or a displacement effect (Png et al. 2008). On the one hand, a spillover of enforcement effect toward alternative sites would cause the number of transactions on control sites to decrease as a result of deterrence. In such cases, our coefficients would be an underestimate of the true enforcement effect as the DID estimate should be higher if the outcomes of the control units have not decreased. On the other hand, if the enforcement effort led the treated vendors to migrate to control sites (i.e., displacement effect), the number of vendors and transactions in the control sites would increase in the posttreatment period. Such a scenario would lead to an overestimate of the enforcement effect. The earlier regression analysis showed that the arrest had a nonsignificant negative impact on the control websites transaction volume. If anything, this result suggests that biases described in the first scenario (i.e., spillover of enforcement effect) are more likely,<sup>15</sup> which means that our estimates are likely to be an underestimate of the true enforcement effect, should such biases exist in our setting.

Placebo Test. To determine whether the estimated significant treatment effects arose by chance, we performed a placebo test by randomly assigning vendors from the three darknet sites as the treated vendors. Specifically, we randomly selected 50% of all vendors across all sites to be treated units and reran the main DID analysis. We performed this procedure 10,000 times and took the average values of the coefficients and standard deviations. If the reported results arose coincidentally, the estimates under this placebo test should yield negative and significant coefficients on the DID estimate. The results of this test are reported in Online Table A5.<sup>16</sup> As the table shows, the DID estimate is no longer significant when treatment status is randomly assigned, indicating that the observed negative treatment effects are unlikely to arise spuriously.

Vendors with Unclear Origins. In our main analysis, vendors on Agora and Evolution who did not specify the United States as their origin in the “shipped from” field are treated as control vendors. Within the data, some vendors specified their “shipped from” information as “worldwide.” Because “worldwide” may include vendors from the United States, a stricter approach would be to exclude control vendors that specified “worldwide” in their origin location when conducting the analysis. We did so and repeated our main analysis under this stricter sample. Online Table A6 presents the results from this sample. We find that they are qualitatively similar to our main findings.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this study, we assess and quantify the impact of legal enforcement on darknet transactions using a unique data set that contains drug transactions of three prominent darknet sites. Relying on an exogenous shock involving a police arrest of a major vendor on SR2 during our study period, our difference-indifferences analyses reveal that a selective targeting enforcement effort reduces subsequent darknet activity on the policed site. This downward impact manifests in two ways, namely, (1) reduced transaction levels and (2) a decreased number of vendors transacting on the site. We find that the observed effect is robust to alternative modeling specifications and is unlikely to arise because of coincidental effects.

Some of these results warrant more discussions. First, with regard to the results related to vendor size, the findings that smaller vendors exited the market and reduced their transaction levels are in line with the prediction of our theoretical setup, which models the arrest event as a variable cost that varies with the number of transactions made by the darknet vendors. Whereas our study provides some evidence that the arrest event serves as a variable cost to the remaining darknet users, it remains plausible that the enforcement may also induce a fixed cost to the darknet vendors. However, the nature of our darknet data set does not allow us to directly test for this. Second, we speculate that the joint findings of smaller vendors leaving the market, increased transactions among the larger vendors, and increased sales concentration were because of the supply side of the market being relatively more affected by the enforcement event than the demand side. This interpretation appears reasonable when we consider that criminal punishment is more severe for drug sellers compared with drug buyers (U.S. Department of Justice 2020), which, in turn, imposes a greater deterrence effect on the drug vendors. Finally, it is interesting to see that the average drug prices did not change significantly after the arrest event. Based on other tests conducted (i.e., concentration ratio and HHI), we also find that the darknet market is competitive in nature. Taken together, we believe that drug sellers on the darknet market do not have the means to alter price levels even after a supply shock to SR2 as buyers are aware of outside options of purchasing drugs elsewhere (e.g., other darknet sites) and purchase elsewhere should drug prices on one site increase.

This study bears several theoretical and practical implications. Here, we discuss the finer theoretical insights along with the policy-relevant guidelines of the study results. First, our study addresses the call to evaluate alternative darknet enforcement strategies by empirically quantifying the causal impact of selective targeting on darknet activity (Soska and Christin 2015). Specifically, we find that the mere arrest of a large darknet drug vendor led to an average 39% reduction in vendor-level transactions and reduced the remaining number of drug vendors on the policed site by 56%. These figures are economically significant and serve as initial evidence in support of the effectiveness of selectively targeting largescale drug vendors to dampen darknet activities. Compared with site shutdowns, selective targeting is less costly and time-consuming yet still able to reduce drugdealing activities on the darknet market. In addition, by being one of the few empirical assessments of a selective targeting strategy, we add to this stream of literature by demonstrating evidence of its efficacy beyond anecdotal accounts.

Second, our study sheds light on the nature of the darknet drug market and its participants. Our results lend credibility to qualitative studies that find darknet drug markets consist of risk-averse individuals who have never dealt drugs previously. Given that these users entered the online drug market believing that the chances of detection and arrests of drug dealing on darknet are low (Martin et al. 2020), these riskaverse users are among the first to reduce their transaction activities or leave the policed site when they realize that enforcement agencies have the means to detect and arrest them. As our empirical tests show, smaller vendors tend to represent these risk-averse users. These individual vendors or small teams of dealers likely lack drug-dealing experience prior to selling drugs online. However, we also see that the arrest has limited impact on deterring the larger vendors in the market, which are likely drug syndicates that have the resources and means to evade legal arrests and punishment (Johnson and Natarajan 1995, Jacobs and Miller 1998). These results reconcile the findings of past studies by showing that both smallscale individual dealers (Martin et al. 2020) and largescale drug syndicates (Aldridge and De´cary-He´tu 2014) are operating on darknet sites. Knowledge of the composition of darknet participants is a necessary first step to arrive at appropriate strategies to arrest the growth of such markets. Moreover, our estimates indicate that at least 10% of the largest vendors are not deterred by the arrest event. Although policy makers and law enforcement agencies need different policing strategies to keep larger vendors at bay, it is nevertheless encouraging to see that a sizable portion of the darknet users are deterred by the arrest of major vendors on darknet sites.

Third, against the ambiguous backdrop of the effectiveness of legal sanctions against cybercrimes (Danaher and Smith 2014, Aguiar et al. 2018), this finding also adds to the body of work on deterrence theory by empirically showing that darknet participants may be deterred by legal sanctions on darknet markets. This finding is noteworthy given the prevailing belief that darknet users are savvy and resourceful and may not always be deterred by legal sanctions. Our work also makes the unique contribution of demonstrating that the arrest of major darknet vendors (as opposed to site shutdowns) is sufficient to raise the perceived certainty of punishment among darknet users.

More interestingly, we learned that the extent of this deterrence effect is not limited to the prosecutorial jurisdiction of the enforcement agency. However, the extent of deterrence experienced by these non–U.S. users is not as large as that of the U.S. users, possibly because of the reduced perceived certainty of arrest that arises from the geographical disparity between the arrestee and the non–U.S. users. Given that a spill over effect of darknet enforcement exists, it might be beneficial for legal authorities from various countries to launch their independent investigations and sanctions of darknet users in their locations as these arrests bear benefits of reducing darknet activity at an international level.

Finally, our work also adds to the literature by showing that the vendor size and drug type sold are contextual factors that influence the extent of deterrence experienced by darknet participants. In addition to the results related to vendor size, the extent of deterrence varies with the type of drugs that darknet users are buy ing and selling. Users dealing with less dangerous drugs may rationalize that they are simply selling/buying medicine from an alternative source other than from physicians. Because these drugs are used for licit reasons (i.e., to treat illnesses and feel better), users selling and buying these schedule V drugs may not think their transactions constitute a criminal act and, hence, are less deterred by the arrest incident.<sup>17</sup> This finding can also be intuitively understood if we consider the severity of punishment for engaging in the dealing of various drug categories. Take Pennsylvania laws, for instance: an individual charged with selling a schedule I or II drug risks being sentenced to as many as 15 years of jail time and fined up to \$250,000. However, the sentence for a seller of a schedule V drug is one year of jail time and a fine of up to \$1,000.<sup>18</sup> This difference in punishment severity translates into cost considerations that users factor in when making market participation decisions in support of the Beckerian framework that assumes criminals have rational decision-making behaviors.

## Future Work and Limitations

As with all studies, the study design and considerations that are put in place to allow for the inference of meaningful results inevitably create constraints for our study. That said, future work may wish to examine whether the effects uncovered in this research generalize to other settings and conditions. First, our work is based on the analysis of the policing effort on one darknet site, SR2. Whereas the evidence provided in our work pertains to a large darknet platform, it is unclear if the results generalize to other darknet sites. It is possible that newer darknet sites may have stronger security and anonymization innovations based on learnings of the failures met by predecessor sites. Should such technological innovations improve substantially over time, enforcement effects observed in this work might not apply to future sites. Thus, it would be worthwhile for future research to assess the impact of enforcement on newer darknet sites to see if our results continue to hold.

Second, we are unable to make claims on vendor migration that might have occurred because of the enforcement effort. Whereas we remove instances of individuals operating on multiple darknet sites, our methods of doing so (i.e., PGP key and usernames) may not be foolproof in identifying migrating or multihoming users. Thus, we avoid making overly broad inferences that may not be true. That said, we do note that there were no significant changes in the number of vendors on the control site before and after the policing shock. With more comprehensive data about darknet markets, future studies may wish to examine the question of how darknet enforcement efforts may influence migration behavior. Third, related to the issue of data limitation, our analysis is unable to account for the order quantity of each darknet transaction. Whereas we were able to infer the presence and count of transactions on darknet sites, we could not see detailed information about these transactions as darknet sites have intentionally left out this information to prevent it from being used as legal evidence against the transacting parties.

Finally, our work evaluates the efficacy of enforcement efforts through the arrest of a large-scale drug vendor on a well-known darknet site. It would be interesting to evaluate whether the arrest of smaller drug vendors or drug buyers could produce disruptive shocks to the darknet market and to contrast the relative magnitudes of their effects with those of the arrest of a large-scale drug dealer. In particular, it would be worthwhile to evaluate and measure whether the arrest of multiple small-scale darknet participants results in a similar deterrent effect as that observed for the arrest of one large-scale vendor. Understanding the magnitude of these effects would allow for the comparison of the relative enforcement costs and results of different policing strategies, which could provide insights into the optimal way of policing darknet-related crimes.

In conclusion, our work represents an initial effort to assess and causally measure the impact of selective targeting effort on darknet activity and demonstrate the extent of its deterrent effect. Our understanding of darknet markets and their users continues to be limited, and we hope that the future work draws more interest and research efforts toward this topic.
