# The Influence of Media Capabilities on Knowledge Contribution in Online Communities

Abstract. Online communities (OCs) have become common, both in work settings and in the personal lives of individuals. Communication among members of OCs is similar in many ways to communication among members of work and nonwork teams (e.g., both synchronous and asynchronous communication are possible). Historically, knowledge repositories have been the focus of OCs, but as OCs add more synchronous communication, it is impor tant to understand how different communication capabilities influence user commitment, individual growth, and knowledge contribution. We developed a theoretical model that argues that the five media capabilities of media synchronicity theory influence the two information technology (IT)-enabled public goods of communality (in the form of knowledge repositories) and connectivity (in the form of direct user-to-user interactions) and that these two in turn affect task outcomes such as community commitment, individual growth, and knowledge contribution. We tested this model using three waves of data collection (two sur veys and one set of archival behavioral data) from the same 452 members of a popular OC. Our results show that four of the five media capabilities have strong influences on connectiv ity and communality and through them on task outcomes. Connectivity (direct user-to-user interaction) has a stronger influence on knowledge contribution than communality (knowl edge repositories). Our model explains large effect sizes for connectivity and communality and medium to large sizes for task outcomes, suggesting its value to OC theory and practice.

## Introduction

Online communities (OCs) play an important role in organizational life, as well as life outside of work (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Dewan et al. 2017). An OC is a collective of users who communicate in a computer-mediated space around shared interests (Phang et al. 2009). Knowledge contributions are the lifeblood of OCs, which enable knowledge flow among participants (Faraj et al. 2016) and often foster creativity (Hwang et al. 2019), help participants reach useful solutions (Liu et al. 2020), and provide social and economic returns (Guo et al. 2017). These studies highlight the importance of increasing knowledge contribution in OCs. Individuals use OCs for many reasons, but two primary goals that influence knowledge contribution are connectivity (reaching other users with similar interests) and communality (accessing information and knowledge) (Phang et al. 2015), the two key elements in information technology (IT)-enabled public goods theory (ITPGT) (Fulk et al. 1996, Phang et al. 2015).

OCs have traditionally focused on asynchronous communication, but, in recent years, technological advance ments have enabled OCs to offer members new ways to communicate. OCs now blur the boundary between asynchronous and synchronous communication, with features such as news feeds (asynchronous), instant chats (synchronous), and multiuser chatrooms (both synchronous and asynchronous). These features enable OCs to increase connectivity (in the form of direct user-to-user interactions), and communality (in the form of knowledge repositories), both of which are IT-enabled public goods key to sustaining OC activities, which is why we use ITPGT.

From a theoretical perspective, ITPGT is silent on whether connectivity or communality is more important. Likewise, past empirical studies have not yet addressed this (Phang et al. 2014, 2015). Yet this is an important theoretical question, especially as OCs have gradually evolved to focus on both communality and connectivity and have added a host of new features to support both. However, whether connectivity should be prioritized over communality by OCs remains unanswered. Public goods (such as connectivity and communality in the OC context) are difficult to create (Otten et al. 2020), indicating the value of advancing our theoretical understanding of ITPGT to address an important practical question: should OCs allocate more resources to improving userto-user interaction (connectivity) or to improving knowledge repositories (communality)? Allocating resources to the less important of the two could limit OC growth. Thus, our first research question is as follows.

## Research Question 1. What is the relative importance of connectivity and communality for OC knowledge contribution?

ITPGT indicates that public goods are difficult to form and maintain owing to their nature, for example, freeriders can enjoy public goods without making an effort to create public goods in return. Hence, it is important to find ways for heterogeneous groups (e.g., OCs) to encourage all users’ collective effort (Otten et al. 2020). We argue that one important way to achieve this is by designing OC systems that enhance user perceptions of the key OC design elements, so that users become more ready to interact and engage in collective effort to create and maintain IT-enabled public goods. The changes in communication media available in OC systems also raise the question of what media capabilities are important for connectivity and communality. Media synchronicity theory (MST) is often used to examine communication in systems featuring both synchronous and asynchronous communication (Dennis et al. 2008). MST argues that synchronous and asynchronous communication offer a different balance of media capabilities in the form of transmission velocity, parallelism, symbol sets, rehearsability, and reprocessability (Dennis et al. 2008). These media capabilities can affect communication outcomes and performance (Dennis et al. 2008; George et al. 2013; Addas and Pinsonneault 2018a, b). For example, media capabilities can enhance group coordination (Addas and

Pinsonneault 2018b) and coordinated behavior (Dennis et al. 2008).

Most contemporary OC systems now enable a variety of communication options (e.g., fast communication and multiple concurrent conversations, in addition to various encoding means and abilities to modify and re-examine messages) that can facilitate both connectivity and communality, the two fundamental OC resources that make participation conducive. The OC features—both new and old—that enable connectivity and communality fit well into the media capabilities theorized by MST. Fo example, new features—such as hashtags and “best posts” lists—enable users to more easily reprocess messages that may be important to communality. Similarly, new one-on-one instant messaging enabling fast trans mission velocity may be important to connectivity. OCs that include a wide variety of symbol sets (e.g., images or videos) may increase the ease of accessing the shared knowledge pool, that is, increasing communality. These examples show how contemporary OCs have evolved from primarily text-based discussion forums and suggest that the media capabilities of MST are useful tools fo analyzing the features offered by contemporary OCs. These media capabilities may better enable communality and connectivity, thus increasing the generation of the public goods needed to grow and sustain OCs (Anderson and Agarwal 2010).

Theories such as ITPGT and MST have not addressed how media influence OC outcomes. Similarly, empirical research on OCs has not addressed which media capabilities influence OC outcomes and whether some capabilities are more important than others. Not understanding the importance of various media capabilities limits the ability to theorize about connectivity and communality and weakens the predictive power of empirical models. From a practitioner perspective, allocating OC resources to unimportant capabilities or failing to support important ones could hinder OC users’ knowledge contributions and therefore hinder OC growth. Thus, a better understanding of the effects of media capabilities is also theoretically and practically important. Hence, our second research question is as follows.

## Research Question 2. Which media capabilities are important in enabling connectivity and communality?

We built on MST to theorize that its five media capabilities influence the two central constructs of ITPGT (connectivity and communality) and used ITPGT to theorize that these two constructs in turn lead to the broader OC outcomes of individual growth, community commitment, and knowledge contribution. We tested our theo rized model using three waves of data collected from the same 452 members of a popular OC (a survey at time 1 to measure media capabilities; a survey at time 2 to measure connectivity, communality, commitment, and individual growth; and a set of archival data at time 3 to measure knowledge contribution). Four of the five media capabilities influenced connectivity and communality, which in turn influenced commitment, individual growth, and knowledge contribution, with medium to large effect sizes. Our findings make three contributions: (1) we identified the four media capabilities that have significant impacts on connectivity, communality, and OC outcomes; (2) we examined the theoretical mechanisms underlying the influence of these media capabilities; and (3) we discovered that connectivity has a stronger effect than communality on knowledge contribution, while communality has a larger effect on individual growth. This pattern of results shows that connecting with other OC members (connectivity) is key to fostering the OC commitment that leads to contributions which create the shared pool of knowledge (communality) that in turn drives individual growth—which offers important insights for future research and practical advice for OC managers. More generally, this pattern of results shows that these media capabilities have large effects on OC outcomes, indicating their value for researchers building theoretical models and OC managers building OC platforms.

## Prior Theory and Research

Research on technology support for communication and collaboration has a long history, much of which has focused on OCs and teams (Sarker et al. 2010, 2011; Raghuram et al. 2019). Previous research on OCs has examined why users contribute knowledge (Phang et al. 2009, 2015; Maruping et al. 2019), seek knowledge (Phang et al. 2009), create ideas (Hwang et al. 2019), reach solutions (Liu et al. 2020), and discover how to increase economic returns (Guo et al. 2017). Although many users receive knowledge, fewer choose to contribute knowledge (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Majchrzak et al. 2013). Various means can influence these activities, such as attracting users with high status capital and decision capital (Guo et al. 2017), introducing incentive hierarchies (Goes et al. 2016), motivating users to commit to the community (Maruping et al. 2019), and encouraging users to cite credible sources for their contributions (Liu et al. 2020). OC practitioners should enable knowledge tracking, enhance social interactivity (Phang et al. 2009), and design functions to connect users with others who share similar interests (Phang et al. 2015). These functions focus on improving OC users’ communication. As OCs can be categorized as media that spread and transmit knowledge, media features should be important mechanisms for influencing OC outcomes.

ITPGT argues that the two primary goals of OC members are to acquire and contribute knowledge (Fulk et al. 1996). Knowledge contribution is often a prosocial activity, whereas knowledge acquisition is usually done for individual growth (Kane and Ransbotham 2016b). Knowledge contribution must come before others can acquire the knowledge (otherwise, there is no knowledge to acquire), but from a theoretical perspective, knowledge acquisition often influences later decisions to contribute knowledge, although there are always free-riders who acquire knowledge but do not contribute (Fulk et al. 1996).

Recent research suggests that, in addition to these two knowledge-based outcomes, users’ commitment to the OC also encourages their participation (Kordzadeh and Warren 2017) and collective contribution (Tsai and Bagozzi 2014). For this reason, commitment to the OC and its members is an important OC outcome (Kane and Ransbotham 2016b). OC commitment has been shown to have strong effects on knowledge contribution (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Bateman et al. 2011, Kuem et al. 2017). Therefore, we include it as an important outcome. Community commitment, individual growth, and knowledge contribution have also been identified as important OC outcomes (Phang et al. 2009, 2014; Kane and Ransbotham 2016b; Maruping et al. 2019), justifying their inclusion in our study.

## Communication Goals in OC

ITPGT argues that OC outcomes are influenced by the two communication goals of connectivity and communality (Phang et al. 2015). Connectivity is the ease of reaching other users who share similar interests, whereas communality is the ease of accessing a shared pool of information (Phang et al. 2015). Connectivity and communality are “two key public communication goods” in “interactive communication systems” (Fulk et al. 1996, p. 60), such as OCs (Phang et al. 2014, 2015), and are the core elements of the ITPGT (Fulk et al. 1996, Phang et al. 2015). ITPGT posits that connective and communal resources in OCs are public goods because users’ consumption of them does not reduce their availability to others (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). OCs focus on publicly available knowledge, indicating the suitability of the ITPGT as the main theoretical basis to develop our model for OCs.

Connectivity focuses on users (reaching those who share similar interests), whereas communality focuses on knowledge (accessing a shared pool of information). Connectivity refers to point-to-point communication among users who share similar interests (Fulk et al. 1996). It describes the technological property of universal access embedded in an interactive media system (Fulk et al. 1996). By using this property of universal access to interact, users engage in personal interactions to acquire knowledge. On the other hand, communality offers users direct access to a common repository, so they do not depend on other users for access (Yuan et al. 2007). Communality is easy access to a knowledge repository within an OC but is not a substitute for connectivity (Yuan et al. 2007). Hence, we need to include both connectivity and communality.

Past research on connectivity and communality is summarized in Online Appendix A. This research shows that both are important in increasing participation intention (Phang et al. 2015), and participation quality and quantity (Phang et al. 2014). Hence, both connectivity and communality are included in our study. Much past research has focused on the importance of shared pools of knowledge available to OC members (i.e., communality) but less is known about the effects of interactions with other OC members (i.e., connectivity). Understanding the relative importance of connectivity and community is important both theoretically and practically, as it offers new knowledge to academics in developing theoretical models, and guidance to practitioners on making effective resource allocations.

In ITPGT, connectivity and communality are both forms of public communication goods that result from collaboration among OC members (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007, Raghuram et al. 2019). Many theories have been proposed to understand and explain how collaboration can be facilitated, with one of the frequently used theories being MST (Dennis et al. 2008). MST was developed to explain how different media capabilities influence communication goals and task outcomes in a range of shared tasks performed by groups of people (Dennis et al. 2008). MST has been used in a wide variety of contexts (Maruping and Agarwal 2004, Pinsonneault et al. 2017).

## Media Synchronicity Theory

Communications in OCs can be synchronous or asynchronous (Johnson et al. 2015, Wu et al. 2016). Posting and replying to articles can be synchronous (Dewan et al. 2017), and OC members can take joint actions either synchronously or asynchronously (Tsai and Bagozzi 2014). MST theorizes about both asynchronous and synchronous communication and argues that different media capabilities better enable one or the other (Dennis et al. 2008), suggesting the theory’s usefulness for OCs. A review of prior research (see Online Appendix B) shows that MST has not been applied in the OC context, suggesting an opportunity to better understand the effects of different media capabilities in OCs that increasingly incorporate synchronous features in addition to asynchronous features. It also suggests an opportunity to explore the extent to which MST’s media capabilities are useful in contexts beyond teams.

MST assumes that the purpose of communication is to create shared understanding in working toward a shared goal (Dennis et al. 2008). Thus, the central concept in MST is synchronicity, the extent to which media capabilities enable “a shared pattern of coordinated synchronous behavior with a common focus” (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 581). MST argues that communication is comprised of two processes (conveyance of information and convergence on its meaning), with synchronicity being critical for convergence but harmful for conveyance. Communication media have different capabilities that are more or less conducive to synchronicity (rehearsability, transmission velocity, parallelism, symbols sets, and reprocessability) so some media are better suited to conveyance (low synchronicity) and some to con vergence (high synchronicity).

One key difference between the team context of MST and the OC context is that OC members may not have the same shared goals (Kane and Ransbotham 2016b). Thus, shared understanding and synchronicity are less important in the OC context. Following Addas and Pinsonneault (2018b), we argue that focusing on media capabilities—not synchronicity—better fits our research context. This has also been done in other research that has adapted parts of MST to new contexts (Maruping and Agarwal 2004). Therefore, we use the five media capabilities from MST but not synchronicity.

MST includes two communication processes in team contexts: conveyance and convergence (Dennis et al. 2008, George et al. 2013). Conveyance is the “gathering and dissemination of information” (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 580) or knowledge (Robert et al. 2018) and is an essential part of OCs, because OC members routinely gather information from and disseminate information to other OC members (Phang et al. 2009). Past research shows that the information gathering and dissemination of the conveyance process takes one of two distinct theoretical forms in OCs: connectivity and communality (Fulk et al. 1996, Phang et al. 2015). Thus, we focus on these two OC-specific forms of information gathering and dissemination that are central to conveyance.

In addition to conveyance, MST theorizes about the other communication process, namely convergence, which refers to the process of developing shared meaning about information (Dennis et al. 2008), such as de veloping shared mental models (Robert et al. 2018). Convergence is a central part of team decision making because it helps team members accomplish their shared goals (Dennis et al. 2008). OC users do not usually have shared goals (Kane and Ransbotham 2016b) because there is no binding force requiring OC members to develop shared mental models or reach agreement on a shared decision or a shared course of action (Kane and Ransbotham 2016b). As a result, there is little need to build consensus on shared meanings (i.e., convergence) among members of an OC, because each member can choose to accept or not accept the views of others and perform the focal task as they like. For example, members of an OC focused on stock investing do not need to converge on a consensus about whether to buy a specific stock; each person can pursue their own investment decisions, independent of the opinions of other OC members. Some OC members may want to convince others of thei viewpoints, and argue passionately for them, but previ ous studies have not deemed this to be striving for “mutual agreement” on a “common understanding,” which is fundamental to convergence among individuals who “share a common fate” and must reach consensus on a shared course of action (Dennis et al. 2008). Therefore, we do not include convergence in our model.

MST proposes five media capabilities (Dennis et al. 2008): transmission velocity, the speed at which a message is delivered to recipients; parallelism, the ability to enable multiple simultaneous conversations; symbol sets, the number of encoding means for communication; rehearsability, the ability to modify a message during encoding and before sending; and reprocessability, media-enabled re-examination of the message after sending. These capabilities may or may not be well perceived by all users, and thus may or may not be appropriated and used as expected by all users (Dennis et al. 2008). These media capabilities are at the core of MST and reflect the capabilities that system designers may choose to focus on when designing OC systems. When designing OC systems, the features that OC members see as important should be understood.

Because OC members are free to interact as they like (Wu et al. 2016), rather than needing to coordinate their behavior, they often use different OC tools that have different capabilities (Wu et al. 2016) and may post content with or without viewing others’ content (Bulgurcu et al. 2018). Thus, an OC member does not need to appropriate and use tools in the same manner as other members, because there is no compelling need to coordinate with others. For example, one user may commonly use realtime messaging, whereas another may not be aware that the capability exists. Hence, different users may have very different perceptions of how the features of an OC enable the media capabilities, indicating the necessity of examining individual perceptions of all five media capabilities of MST.

## Research Model

In summary, we argued that the five MST media capabilities influence the two OC communication goals of connectivity and communality, which in turn influence the OC task outcomes of community commitment, individual growth, and knowledge contribution. The first five are the media capabilities from MST, the next two are communication goals derived from OC research using ITPGT, whereas the last three are task outcomes, as contextualized from OC general outcomes.

## Media Capabilities and Connectivity

One communication goal in OCs is connectivity, because it can increase participation (Phang et al. 2015), which is critical to OCs. Connectivity involves reaching those who share similar interests and connecting with them on an interpersonal level (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). Thus, connectivity is about discussion for social connection. Most OC discussion is asynchronous, but building connectivity often benefits from social interaction with faster and richer message delivery and conversational interchange. For example, many OCs provide real-time text-based discussion, for example, Quora (Munzal 2017), and some have audio chat, for example, Reddit Talk (Hutchinson 2021).

Fast conversational interchange increases social presence, which is the perception that others are actually present (Short et al. 1976, Rice 1993, Sia et al. 2002, Schultze and Brooks 2019, Schuetzler et al. 2020). When conversations are slower, there is less social presence, and the people one is communicating with seem more like objects than real persons (Short et al. 1976, Rice 1993, Schultze and Brooks 2019, Schuetzler et al. 2020). Likewise, parallel communications and information transmissions in multiple symbol sets (e.g., audio and video) are also desirable. Hence, the three media capabilities associated with information transmission (i.e., transmission velocity, parallelism, and symbol sets) should be particularly important in connectivity, because they primarily facilitate message delivery, whereas rehearsability and reprocessability are less important.

In MST, transmission velocity is the speed at which a message is delivered to recipients (Dennis et al. 2008). For example, some OCs enable users to transmit audio messages (e.g., Reddit Talk), which have faster transmission velocity than text-based media because speaking is faster than typing (Dennis et al. 2008). Users can also use text-based media in real time, albeit at a slower speed than speaking (Schultze and Brooks 2019). Transmission velocity increases social capital (Wu et al. 2016). Fast transmission enables all users to quickly respond to message senders. Some OCs provide text-chat capabilities that are intended to provide faster transmission velocity than traditional bulletin board postings. Fast transmission is a media capability, but it may not be used by all users, because not all users will choose to respond in real time for text-based media (Schultze and Brooks 2019). The shortened waiting time needed for a user to recognize that a fellow user shares their interest would enable the user to more easily identify and communicate with those who share similar interests (Schultze and Brooks 2019), which is at the core of connectivity (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

## Hypothesis 1a. Transmission velocity is positively related to connectivity.

The second media capability is parallelism, which is the ability to enable multiple simultaneous conversations (Dennis et al. 2008). Without parallelism, only one person can communicate at one time in a given medium (Dennis et al. 2008). For example, if one user speaks on an OC audio channel, all other users are prevented from speaking until that user has finished. With parallelism, multiple users can type simultaneously. Parallelism has a positive effect when the goal is to enable multiple people to “speak” at once (Dennis et al. 2008). Parallelism is a positive force for connectivity because it enables discussions among a greater number of users than if the media were not parallel. Without parallelism, discussions among larger groups of OC users become difficult, if not impossible, because only one user would be permitted to post at one time and this restriction may result in other users’ disengagement. Parallelism helps build relational capital by facilitating online friendships (Wu et al. 2016). Some OCs enable users to use several private message boxes at the same time (Online Appendix C), an exemplar means of enabling multiple simultaneous conversations. Multiple simultaneous conversations provide multiple opportunities to reach others, which may increase the possibility of users reaching more users with similar interests (i.e., connectivity).

## Hypothesis 1b. Parallelism is positively related to connectivity.

The term symbol sets refers to the number of encoding means for communication (Dennis et al. 2008), so that more symbol sets mean greater symbol variety (Maruping and Agarwal 2004). In MST, symbol sets influence both transmission and processing (Dennis et al. 2008). A greater number of ways to encode messages (e.g., text, images, and video) can improve the communication of meaning (Dennis et al. 2008). Sometimes meaning is more easily conveyed by an image or video, than by trying to describe something using text alone. For example, an OC member may seek help overcoming a challenge (e.g., in a golf OC, a player may share a video of their swing). OC users could describe the event in the spoken or typed word, but being able to share an image or a video would make the story easier to understand. A greater variety of symbol sets can also facilitate identification of those who share similar interests. For example, TikTok includes short videos (a type of symbol set according to MST) that can vividly display a user’s interest and expertise better than text (e.g., how well a user can play a musical instrument). Thus, they offer a more effective means to attract those who share similar interests, enable users sharing similar interests to communicate with each other, and thereby build relationships, compared with OCs that offer only text-based discussion (Dennis et al. 2008). Thus, a greater number of symbol sets leads to greater connectivity.

Hypothesis 1c. The number of symbol sets is positively related to connectivity.

## Media Capabilities and Communality

Another communication goal in OCs is communality, the ease of accessing a shared pool of information (or knowledge). The media capabilities associated with accessing and processing such information are most important to this OC goal. For example, most OCs provide “best posts” lists, for which administrators select the most useful posts and sort them into topics or categories, which may improve communality (i.e., ease of accessing shared information). Thus, symbol sets, rehearsability, and reprocessability are important in OCs because they enable users to share knowledge that is useful to knowledge seekers, whereas transmission velocity and parallelism are less important.

MST posits that the number of symbol sets influences information-processing capability (Dennis et al. 2008) and can improve the understanding of information (George et al. 2013). Symbol sets can include multiple cues and language variety (Maruping and Agarwal 2004), including visual cues (Robert et al. 2018). These cues can help community users better understand the shared pool of information, thus supporting communality (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). For example, an OC member may teach others about some aspect of interest and have the ability to provide an image or video instead of using only text (e.g., in a tennis community, of a member explaining how to hold a racket or how to serve). Moreover, symbol sets can be used to represent information or knowledge, thus increasing users ways to access the shared knowledge pool. In contrast, with a limited symbol set, providing access to the members’ full knowledge would be more difficult and could inhibit knowledge sharing. Thus, a greater number of symbol sets leads to greater communality.

Hypothesis 2a. The number of symbol sets is positively related to communality.

In MST, rehearsability is an information-processing capability that enables the sender to modify or fine-tune a message before sending it (Dennis et al. 2008). Rehearsability enables message senders to carefully craft messages that best convey their intended meaning (Dennis et al. 2008). Without rehearsability, OC users would not be able to edit contributions once they began typing or speaking (Dennis et al. 2008). With rehearsability, users are able to edit contributions before they are posted and visible to others, thus helping ensure that all titles, descriptions, and tags are clear, well worded, and use correct spelling. For example, in a computer networking community, if a poster misspells “server” as “sever,” this causes important knowledge difficult to locate. Thus, rehearsability helps users contribute accurate knowledge that enables other users to easily find and understand the shared pool of information, thus enhancing communality (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2b. Rehearsability is positively related to communality.

Reprocessability is a processing capability that enables users to re-examine messages after reading them for the first time (Dennis et al. 2008). For example, OCs maintain a history, keeping electronic trails of information that can be referred to later, and many OCs enable users to mark content that they want to return to (e.g., marking it as a favorite) (RADetailer 2019). Reprocessability offers users access to the shared knowledge contributed by themselves and others and means users can return to a previously read post to reread and reconsider it. For example, a community member may return to a contributed item of knowledge repeatedly in an attempt to understand and retain it (e.g., in a job skills community, returning to interview advice or best practice for resume writing). Thus, reprocessability facilitates a user’s access to and understanding of a shared pool of information regardless of time; that is, it enhances communality (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2c. Reprocessability is positively related to communality.

## Connectivity and Communality

Previous research has shown that connectivity and communality are related (Phang et al. 2015). High connectivity means there are many other users who can help locate relevant information (Phang et al. 2015). This aids the forming of relationships with users who share similar interests, thus building social capital, which encourages users to contribute more useful knowledge to the OCs (Wasko and Faraj 2005), and increasing the ease with which the shared pool of information can be accessed. Because the ease of reaching those who share similar interests is a public good—which, according to ITPGT, means its consumption does not reduce its availability to others (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007)—connectivity can constantly enhance communality regardless of com munity size.

Hypothesis 3. Connectivity is positively related to communality.

## Community Commitment

Community commitment is dedication to the OC. Connectivity helps users engage in social interactions with those who share similar interests, thereby embedding social values in the system. Social values encourage continued usage and commitment to the community (Teng 2018). Those who share similar interests likely have similar experience (of the focal activity), which facilitates the establishment of social relations (Yan et al. 2015). This can increase social support, which strengthens community commitment (Kuem et al. 2017). According to ITPGT, connective features are public goods that are available to al users (Fulk et al. 1996). Thus, connectivity enhances all users’ commitment to the OC (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Phang et al. 2015). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4a. Connectivity is positively related to community commitment.

According to ITPGT, communality is a public feature that is accessible to all users; that is, they have easy access to a shared pool of information, which enables them to engage in more productive knowledge exchanges (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007), thus offering them the benefits resulting from greater knowledge of the OC’s particular themes (Phang et al. 2015). Searching for and finding knowledge can enhance job performance (Zhang and Venkatesh 2017), indicating that a valuable pool of information can help users achieve their personal goals (e.g., obtaining rewards or experiencing flow). This increases the likelihood that they will return to the community and increases their commitment to it (Teng 2018). Receiving valuable knowledge also increases users’ community commitment (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Kane and Ransbotham 2016b). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4b. Communality is positively related to community commitment.

## Individual Growth

ITPGT posits that connectivity offers individuals opportunities to exchange knowledge with others (Yuan et al. 2007). Such knowledge should be useful to users in advancing their OC-focused activity. For example, knowledge exchange between users in an OC focused on fitness could enable them to learn about new equipment and training routines, thus helping them perform better. That is, knowledge would directly enhance users’ performance (i.e., their growth and development). Moreover, connecting to those who share the same interests may build helpful ties that enhance job performance (Zhang and Venkatesh 2017), implying individual growth. Furthermore, being connected with others who share a common interest can result in the acquisition of a shared language (e.g., terminology), which results in encouragement and social support (Huang et al. 2019), and further promotes individual growth, for example, in health literacy (Chen et al. 2019) and self-management (Yan and Tan 2014). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5a. Connectivity is positively related to individual growth.

Communality refers to users’ easy access to a shared pool of information, which enables active participation (Phang et al. 2014), and according to ITPGT, facilitates productive knowledge exchange (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). Such knowledge exchange can provide direct benefits to the OC-focused activity (Phang et al. 2015) and is often the primary reason users join OCs (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Because OC users seek knowledge to facilitate their progress in an activity, the primary knowl edge benefit of OCs should be individual growth in that activity, for exmple, increasing one’s ability to persuade others to accept a policy viewpoint (Phang et al. 2014). When the shared pool of information is easy to access, exploring knowledge becomes easier and helps users achieve their goals, progress further, and positively contribute to innovation (Ye and Kankanhalli 2018). Easy access to knowledge should stimulate OC users’ motivation to know more, which triggers innovative use. That is, an individual’s advancement may result from knowl edge consumption in the OC, that is, individual growth.

Hypothesis 5b. Communality is positively related to individual growth.

## Knowledge Contribution

Knowledge contribution is an important outcome for OCs (Faraj et al. 2015, Majchrzak and Malhotra 2016), because it fits the OC platform’s objective (Constantinides et al. 2018). However, it is difficult to manage knowledge contribution, as it increases and decreases with structural and social dynamics over time (Ridings and Wasko 2010). Abundant, high-quality knowledge is essential to OCs (Kane and Ransbotham 2016a). Thus, both knowledge quantity and quality are important (Phang et al. 2014). Users who actively contribute knowledge are often identified as com munity leaders (Faraj et al. 2015). All these studies support the importance of knowledge contribution to OCs.

Past research using ITPGT has found that connectivity can facilitate knowledge contribution (Phang et al. 2015), because reaching those who share similar interests should motivate users to engage in discussions on the OC-focused activity, that is, contributing users’ knowledge to the OC. Those who share similar interests often share a common language (terminology pertaining to the focal activity). Being connected to others with a common interest can also create a social environment that facilitates social interaction and the accumulation of social capital, which promotes contribution behavior (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Phang et al. 2009).

Hypothesis 6a. Connectivity is positively related to knowledge contribution.

Communality is central to a productive knowledge exchange (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007). Communality can increase OC users’ personal and collective benefits and trigger contribution intention (Phang et al. 2015). OC members are more likely to contribute knowledge when there is an existing stock of knowledge (Phang et al. 2014), so greater communality induces more contributions. A large knowledge stock may contain valuable information, and thus trigger more discussions and further facilitate subsequent knowledge contributions. Thus, greater communality means a larger existing knowledge stock, and further leads users to contribute more knowledge to the OC.

Hypothesis 6b. Communality is positively related to knowledge contribution.

An important factor that influences knowledge contribution is the users’ commitment to the OC (Bateman et al. 2011). Community commitment may enhance identification, which motivates contributions (Spaeth et al. 2015). It also increases individuals’ visits (Ren et al. 2012), participation (Kuem et al. 2017), and intention to contribute to the OC (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Commitment motivates repeated visits to the OC (Teng 2018) and the frequent posting of replies in the OC (Bateman et al. 2011). Posting replies is a form of knowledge contribution.

Hypothesis 6c. Community commitment is positively related to knowledge contribution.

Generally, OC users enjoy acquiring knowledge to improve their individual performance on a task outside of the OC, that is, individual growth (Hwang et al. 2019). That is, users expect to gain individual benefits from OCs (Phang et al. 2015). For example, users of a beauty OC would expect to acquire skills that help them become better at using beauty-related products. Growth benefits (e.g., skills enhancement) can encourage further engagement (Ye and Kankanhalli 2017). Obtaining growth benefits from the OC may lead individuals to feel indebted, and thus they may become more willing to contribute knowledge because this indebtedness creates the need for reciprocity, which facilitates knowledge contribution (Zhao et al. 2016). Thus, users who perceive more benefits from the OC are more likely to reciprocate by making knowledge contributions themselves (Faraj et al. 2015). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 6d. Individual growth is positively related to knowledge contribution.

We further theorize that connectivity should exert a stronger total effect than communality on knowledge contribution, where total effects include direct effects and indirect effects mediated through other factors (Preacher and Hayes 2008). We make three theoretical arguments for why connectivity should have a stronge total effect than communality, considering both the quantity and quality of knowledge contributions (Phang et al. 2014).

First, connectivity reflects user-to-user interaction, in which an OC user can consult other users who share similar interests. When a user can identify someone with relevant knowledge, they may foresee that their questions will be better answered by a direct question to the individual (connectivity) rather than a general posting to a shared pool of knowledge (communality) (Lu et al. 2017). Likewise, someone who receives a direct message may be more likely to see the query and respond to it, than to see and respond to a general, untargeted query posted in the shared pool of knowledge. Users are less likely to overlook direct messages, which means they are more likely to respond.

Second, the one-on-one interactions of connectivity can better offer social support than the many-to-many, highly parallel postings in the shared pool of knowledge of communality (Huang et al. 2019). Greater social support builds a greater sense of community (Kuem et al. 2017), which leads to more commitment and in turn to more knowledge contribution (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Thus, connectivity may exert stronger impacts than communality.

Third, from a theoretical point of view, ITPGT argues that people cooperate in producing public goods when they perceive the contributions of others (Anderson and Agarwal 2010). We argue that connectivity produces a stronger signal of cooperation than communality for two reasons. First, direct interaction (i.e., knowledge giving and receiving) among individuals with shared interests (e.g., community friends) can stir a strong desire for cooperation (Tsai and Bagozzi 2014) and thus should be stronger than the giving and receiving of knowledge via a shared pool contributed by those who are more socially distant. Second, connectivity facilitates knowledge social ization in the present, whereas communality includes knowledge and ideas that were contributed in the past (Phang et al. 2015). Thus, the recent cooperative contributions of others should be more visible through connectivity than communality. ITPGT argues that cooperation has a strong effect on knowledge contribution, so connectivity, relative to communality, should have a stron ger effect on knowledge contribution.

In summary, we argue that there are three theoretical reasons why connectivity should have a stronger effect on knowledge contribution than communality. Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 6e. Compared with communality, connectiv ity has a stronger total effect on knowledge contribution.

## Method

## Research Context

As our research context, we chose a popular OC focused on online gaming, namely Bahamut (gamer.com.tw), one of the top 10 most popular websites in Taiwan, with traffic similar to Wikipedia, Yahoo, and Instagram. Bahamut has an average of more than 2 million unique users per day (Bahamut 2016). Gaming OCs often rank among the most popular websites worldwide: for example, Twitch (United States, Finland, and Denmark), Sohu (China), and Gamewith (Japan) (SimilarWeb 2019). Bahamut provides an OC in which gamers communicate, exchange knowledge, and seek advice to improve their in-game performance. Bahamut users actively engage in communication and seek and contribute knowledge. Bahamut has features related to the five media capabilities. For example, instant messaging functions increase transmission velocity; multiuser chatrooms permit parallelism; emoticons offer multiple symbol sets; preview functions enable rehearsability; and article search functions enhance reprocessability. Hence, Bahamut and its users are appropriate for OC research.

## Data Collection Process

We collected data in three waves from two sources (i.e., two surveys and one set of archival data). This ensured temporal separation and data source separation between independent and dependent variables, both of which help reduce common method variance (CMV) (Podsakoff et al. 2003). In the first wave, we collected survey data from March 18 to April 8, 2019. The first-wave survey collected data on the five media capabilities. All items were related to the OC system and measured at the individual level. In the second wave, we collected survey data from April 9 to 30, 2019. The second-wave survey collected data on connectivity, communality, community commitment, individual growth, and demographic information. In the third wave, on May 31, 2019, we collected archival data by calculating individuals’ years since OC registration and knowledge contributions during May 2019.

Invitations to participate in our study were posted on Bahamut, stating that the researchers were interested in communication in OCs, and participants were directed to our survey web page to provide their informed consent. Participants were included in a lottery that awarded each of 150 randomly selected participants a gift certificate worth NT\$200 (approximately US\$6.50). The value of the gift certificate was moderate, and moderate compensation can help address self-selection bias (Jia et al. 2017).

To ensure the quality of the data, we excluded participants based on the following criteria: lacked a valid OC account; self-reported that they visited the OC for 0 hours or more than 168 hours per week (there are only

168 hours in a week); did not pass the attention check; were not 18 years or older; or had an email account that duplicated responses. We collected 1,133 complete responses from the first-wave survey. We eliminated 154 responses based on the exclusion criteria and retained 979 responses (86.4%). In the second-wave survey, we collected 1,074 responses, eliminated 157 responses, and retained 917 responses (85.4%). In total, 452 participants responded to both surveys, and their responses were used in our analyses. The sample size is sufficient for conducting structural equation modeling (Hair et al. 2019).

To test attrition bias, we compared the mean difference between those who participated only in the first wave and those who participated in both waves and found no significant differences for all but one construct. Participants who completed only the first wave reported lower transmission velocity (p � 0.03); the difference (0.09 on the five-point scale, or a Cohen’s d � 0.14) was less than what Cohen (2016) calls a “small” effect size (d � 0.20). Thus, we concluded there was no substantial attrition bias.

The third-wave data collection was done by manually checking the OC to obtain behavioral data on the 452 participants. These data included the following: (1) the number of posted articles and replies to articles; (2) the number of “good points” (i.e., the number of times other users clicked the “give good points” button for our participants’ posts); and (3) the number of years the user had participated in the OC.

Most participants were male (78%), young (93% were 35 years old or less), and college-educated or in college (71%), and 88% had an annual income of NT\$600,000 (about US\$21,400) or less. This profile reflects the user features in our sampling frame, that is, 83% (Bahamut 2016) or 84% male (Market Intelligence & Consulting Institute (MICI) 2015), and more than 80% of the gamers in Taiwan are under 35 years old (Market Intelligence & Consulting Institute (MICI) 2015). Our participants, on average, had participated in the OC for 7.6 years and used it for 7.17 hours per week.

## Measures

To measure media capabilities, we used the scales developed by Wu et al. (2016) for social networking sites and the definitions of the media capabilities in MST (Dennis et al. 2008) to adapt 18 items to our OC context.<sup>1</sup> We used the items in Phang et al. (2015) to measure connectivity (four) and communality (six). We used three items from Teng (2018) to measure community commitment. We adapted the three items that measured political career benefit in Phang et al. (2015) to measure individual growth; we changed the target from “political career” to “game progress.” Both knowledge quantity and quality are important (Phang et al. 2014), so we collected system-captured behavioral data for two indicators of knowledge contribution: (1) the number of posted articles and replies to articles and (2) the number of “good points,” where users gave OC currency to others to recognize good contributions. The scores on these two indicators ranged widely, so they were transformed using Blom’s rank-based normalization process (Solomon and Sawilowsky 2009) to produce scores from �3 to 3. The normalized scores were averaged to derive the measure of knowledge contribution. Our second indicator is a new measure of knowledge contribution.

Online Appendix D lists the measurement items, anchors, and their sources. We included discussion efficacy as a control variable, because self-efficacy influences the intention to use knowledge-based information systems (IS) (Phang et al. 2015). The users’ gender, age, education, income, and Internet experience are control variables commonly used in OC research (Kim et al. 2012, Phang et al. 2015, Zhao et al. 2016) and other online social contexts (Lin et al. 2017, Chen et al. 2021). A user’s number of years since OC registration is critical to motivating OC continuance (Kuem et al. 2020) and increasing contributions (Wasko and Faraj 2005), so we included years since a user’s registration in the community as a control variable. These have often been used as control variables in various contexts (Hsieh et al. 2012, Pinsonneault et al. 2017, Sarker et al. 2018). Information on the seven control variables was collected in the second wave: participants gender, age, education, annual income, Internet experience (familiarity with using the Internet, from 1 (not at all) to 5 (perfectly familiar)), years since OC registration, and discussion efficacy (four items).

## Results

We used LISREL v8.80 to test the hypotheses. We also examined the fit of the structural model and found it to be acceptable: CFI � 0.94, IFI � 0.94, NNFI � 0.93, and SRMR � 0.055. The model explained 33% of the variance in connectivity and 67% in communality, which are large effect sizes (Cohen 2016). The model explained 25% of the variance in community commitment, 71% in individual growth, and 26% in knowledge contribution, which are medium to large effect sizes (Cohen 2016).

Following past research, one-tailed tests were used, as the hypotheses predicted directional differences (Li et al. 2013, Chen et al. 2022). Figure 2 and Table 3 show the results. Transmission velocity, parallelism, and symbol sets all had significant positive effects on connectivity (supporting Hypothesis 1a, Hypothesis 1b, and Hypothesis 1c). Symbol sets and reprocessability had significant positive effects on communality (supporting Hypothesis 2a and Hypothesis 2c), whereas rehearsability only approached significance (Hypothesis 2b). Connectivity significantly affected communality (supporting Hypothesis 3). Connectivity and communality both had significant positive effects on community commitment and individual growth (supporting Hypothesis 4a, Hypothesis 4b,

Hypothesis 5a, and Hypothesis 5b). Community commitment significantly affected knowledge contribution (supporting Hypothesis 6c), but individual growth did not, and connectivity and communality had no signifi cant direct effects; Hypothesis 6a, Hypothesis 6b, and Hypothesis 6d were not supported.

Connectivity and communality had equivalent effects on community commitment (0.25 versus 0.27), with communality having greater effects on individual growth (0.21 versus $0 . 6 7 , p = 0 . 0 0 2 )$ . We examined the total effects of connectivity and communality on knowledge contribution (Hypothesis 6e) by including both direct and indirect effects, which is more comprehensive than examining either alone (Bollen and Stine 1990). Total effects are assessed in terms of z-scores (Preacher and Hayes 2008). We compared the distributions of the two total effects using their pooled standard deviation (0.032). The total effect of connectivity on knowledge contribution (0.06) was significantly larger (p � 0.015) than the total effect of communality on knowledge contribution (which was not significantly different from zero: �0.01); Hypothesis 6e is supported. These results answer the first research question: Connectivity has a stronger total effect on knowledge contribution than communality. They also answer our second research question: four media capabilities are important (transmission velocity, parallelism, symbol sets, and reprocessability) in enhancing connectivity and communality, and OC outcomes.

## Discussion

We developed a theoretical model to explain what media capabilities influence OC communication goals (connectivity and communality) and outcomes (community commitment, individual growth, and knowledge contribution). We believe the study makes two key contributions. First, we developed a theoretical model and found it to be useful in this context. Only 4 of our 16 hypotheses were not supported. Second, media capabilities explained a large amount of the variance in the two communication goals and a medium to large amount of variance in the outcomes. We conclude that the model is useful for explaining OC communication goals and outcomes.

Our findings indicate that transmission velocity, parallelism, and symbol sets are influential in connecting users who share similar interests (connectivity), whereas symbol sets and reprocessability influence easy access to the shared pool of information (communality). Thus, dif ferent media capabilities have different effects on connectivity and communality. We theorized that rehearsability would influence communality; whereas this relationship was not significant, it approached significance, so we are reluctant to definitively conclude that rehearsability has no effect. Rehearsability enables users to correct errors and fine-tune messages to improve logical flow (Dennis et al. 2008). The reason Hypothesis 2b was not supported may be that a large proportion of OC users are young people who frequently use short messages (Eckert et al. 2022) and may care less about typos, grammar, and logical flow (Shafto 2015). Hence, young users may perceive fewer benefits from rehearsability.

Our model also shows that connectivity had a stronger total effect than communality on knowledge contribution. This finding reflects the increasing importance of synchronous communication tools for OCs, where people value faster and more immediate communication that enables them to connect with other OC members. This finding also offers an important insight for OC theory and practice: If OCs want to increase knowledge contribution, the focus should be on facilitating users interpersonal connections rather than on building a large knowledge repository. As OCs have added more synchronous communication capabilities, user-to-user communication has become more important than knowledge repositories. Neither connectivity nor communality directly increased knowledge contribution (hypothesized as Hypothesis 6a and Hypothesis 6b). We speculate that connectivity can create a common language and help accumulate social capital, both of which are critical to knowledge contribution (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Phang et al. 2009). Commitment plays a pivotal role, as it drives motivation to contribute to the OC. We conclude that connectivity and communality strengthen commitment, and thereby influence knowledge contribution.

The hypothesized relationship between individual growth and knowledge contribution (Hypothesis 6d) was not supported. The reason may be the lack of ability to reciprocate the received benefits. Reciprocity is key to increasing knowledge contribution (Faraj et al. 2015, Zhao et al. 2016). In our context, OC members with low levels of knowledge should benefit most from OC knowledge, as it can quickly foster individual growth. Yet, these members are the least capable of reciprocating by contributing knowledge because they lack the knowledge to contribute, which may explain the unsupported Hypothesis 6d.

## Limitations

Our study focused on examining the impacts of media capabilities and not specific system features. Future studies could explore what specific design elements could be used as connective features, thus helping OC users build connections to fellow users who share the same interests. Likewise, future studies could propose and examine design elements that could increase communality (i.e., improve access to a shared knowledge pool).

We collected data from one gaming OC in Taiwan. All contexts provide boundary conditions to generalizing their results, but we see nothing unique in this context that would suggest important boundary conditions for other OCs. Nonetheless, future studies need to examine the applicability of our model to other countries and OCs.

We used a multiple-wave data collection process from multiple sources (i.e., surveys and archives) to reduce common method bias. However, because online surveys use nonprobability samples, they are open to coverage bias and sampling bias. Future studies could use mixed methods (Venkatesh et al. 2016) to collect both qualitative and quantitative data to investigate these issues or use big data analytics (Abbasi et al. 2016) and collect users behavioral data over time to understand how behavio influences subsequent behavior.

## Implications for Research

Implications for OC Research. By answering the two research questions, the overall contributions of this study are as follows: (1) discovering that both connectivity and communality contribute to knowledge contribution in the OC context; (2) clarifying that connectivity exerts stronger total influence than communality on knowledge contribution (with the opposite pattern for individual growth and no differences for community commitment); and (3) identifying four media capabilities (transmission velocity, parallelism, symbol sets, and reprocessability) as means to increase knowledge contribution. These are new to the OC literature. We detail them here.

Our study contributes to OC research by showing how media capabilities influence commitment, benefits external to OCs, and knowledge contribution. Our findings suggest that four media capabilities are particularly important, namely, transmission velocity, parallelism, symbol sets, and reprocessability. These four have large effects on connectivity and communality, which in turn have large effects on community commitment. Stronger commitment has been found to increase users’ repeated visits (Teng 2018) and postings (Bateman et al. 2011). In the OC literature, the identity verification perspective (Ma and Agarwal 2007) and social perspective (Ridings and Wasko 2010) have been adopted to explain users contributions to communities. Our study introduces an additional perspective to explain users’ commitment, that is, media capabilities. This perspective contributes to OC literature by illustrating how media capabilities can be used to encourage users’ contributions.

Our results are unique in identifying media capabilities as novel factors that influence community commitment. Past research has focused on the user characteristics and relationships that influence community commitment, such as leader–member exchange (Oh et al. 2016), congruence (Maruping et al. 2019), and ideological fit (Daniel et al. 2018). Our study shows that the nature of the OC technology itself and how users perceive it (i.e., the four media capabilities) are important factors that influence community commitment. Therefore, in addition to examining user characteristics, it is important to theorize about the nature of the technology used by the OC to understand user commitment.

Our results indicate that parallelism and transmission velocity are important capabilities influencing connectivity, which in turn has direct and indirect effects on commitment, individual growth, and knowledge contribution. Parallelism and transmission velocity are often associated with synchronous communication, not the asynchronous communication that often predominates in OCs. This shows the need for more research on the roles of synchronous and asynchronous communication in OCs, especially as they pertain to the communication goal of connectivity.

We also found that increased reprocessability improved communality but that rehearsability was not related to communality. Communality is easy access to a pool of information, so reprocessability is important. We theorized that rehearsability was also important because it should assist in encoding processes that are part of knowledge contribution, but the findings showed otherwise. The reason may be that capabilities enabling knowledge retrieval are more important to communality than those that enable contribution because most OC users focus on retrieval. More research is needed to better understand the role of rehearsability in OCs.

Our findings show that connectivity had a stronger total effect than communality on knowledge contribution. This finding indicates that connecting those with shared interests more strongly motivates knowledge contribution than ease of access to a knowledge repository. This supports OC research on the importance of social support (Yan and Tan 2014, Kuem et al. 2017, Chen et al. 2019) and social capital (Wasko and Fara 2005, Phang et al. 2009). This finding also joins efforts to explore the importance of connectivity (Phang et al. 2015). This finding demonstrates the relative effects of connectivity and communality, which are new to OC research. More research is needed to better understand this shift in importance from knowledge repositories to user-to-user communication in the OC context.

Implications for ITPGT. Our study uses ITPGT to develop our model. This theory has been used to examine the effects of connectivity and communality on participation quality and quantity (Phang et al. 2014) and on participation intention (Phang et al. 2015). Compared with these studies, our study extends ITPGT by identifying new antecedents of connectivity and communality, that is, four media capabilities. The extension adds value to ITPGT, as these novel antecedents may be applied to various communication media (beyond OCs) (Dennis et al. 2008, table 2), indicating that our research can enhance the impact of ITPGT on future research of varied media.

ITPGT notes that public goods can be enjoyed by all users, without affecting others’ further use. This nature of public goods is important in the OC context. Our research shows that connectivity has a greater total effect than communality on the knowledge contributions that are the lifeblood of ${ \mathrm { O C s } } ,$ whereas communality has a greater effect on individual growth; there was no difference in their effects on commitment to the OC, which is important for the OC’s sustainability (Maruping et al.

2019). Thus, connections with individual OC members drive the contribution of knowledge, which produces large and vibrant pools of knowledge that enable members’ individual growth—and both connectivity and community are important for community commitment. As a result, media capabilities that can promote connection among people with common interest and access to shared knowledge, as well as facilitating collective effort, are important in building and sustaining public goods and the communities that use them.

Implications for MST. Our study contributes to MST by showing the usefulness of the MST media capabilities in influencing knowledge contribution within a large community of users who do not need to reach consensus on a shared course of action. This broadens the reach of MST, which was developed for small group contexts where the communication goal of shared understanding is central (Dennis et al. 2008). Other research has also examined the MST media capabilities in various research contexts (George et al. 2013; Addas and Pinsonneault 2018a, b). Our findings contribute to MST by showing that MST and four of its media capabilities can be adapted to explain the two OC communication goals of connectivity and communality. This broadens our understanding of the usefulness of MST. The MST media capa bilities show robustness in extending beyond the original context in which they were created.

Contexts are very important in developing and extending theory (Johns 2006, 2017; Sarker 2016). Accordingly, not all the media capabilities proposed by MST may be applicable to every context; we found four of the five capabilities to be significant in the OC context. Thus, we need more research examining the applicability of the MST media capabilities in different research contexts.

## Implications for Practice

The setting of this study was a gaming OC platform that hosts many discussion boards focused on different games. This platform has features—such as real-time messaging and discussion boards—in common with many other OC platforms, for example, those on healthcare (Guo et al. 2017, Huang et al. 2019) and questions and answers (Zhao et al. 2016, Huang et al. 2018). Users of these OCs should benefit from knowing friends with similar interests (connectivity) and having easy access to a common knowledge repository (communality). Like gaming OCs, these OCs need knowledge contribution. That is, our study constructs are also important in these OCs. Hence, our findings could be generalized to these OCs.

The findings of our study could help OC managers strengthen users’ intentions to make voluntary contributions. Transmission velocity was positively related to connectivity, showing the importance of message delivery speed in connecting peers with similar interests. To improve message delivery speed, OC managers could aim to maintain the high efficiency and high processing capability of their servers and network facilities. Managers could design system functions to notify message recipients to check their messages when they reached the recipients’ inboxes and when replies were received for previous postings. This could shorten waiting time, assisting message senders in finding peers of similar interests.

We also found that parallelism was positively related to connectivity. The implication here is that OC members should be connected to as many other members as possible without overwhelming them. OC managers could design their systems to use artificial intelligence to identify participants with similar interests and invite them to be online concurrently, which would prevent messages from being missed. Users may discover that many others share their interests and would thus increase their commitment to the community.

Symbol sets were positively related to both connectivity and communality. OC managers could design rich symbol sets in their systems and advise their users how to select appropriate symbols to reach others with similar interests. Symbols, including graphic icons (e.g., emoji), have become common in text messages. OC managers could design attractive symbol sets in the input area, with which participants could enrich their communications, thereby resulting in improved connectivity and communality.

Our findings indicate that reprocessability has a positive impact on communality. That is, media-enabled re-examination could enhance the ease with which a shared pool of information is accessed. Accordingly, the OC should facilitate the rereading of content. For example, OC managers could design system functions that monitor user preferences and recommend pertinent knowledge to them, perhaps reminding them of knowledge they had previously found useful. By making users more aware of the reprocessability capability, they may be more likely to choose to use this feature and obtain access to the existing pool of information, thus improving communality. Although implementing these functions may seem obvious, some leading OC platforms have yet to fully do so. For example, Quora and Stack Overflow recommend posts carrying similar content rather than posts about similar interests. If these OC platforms could offer recommendations based on matched user interests, being made aware of and reading these posts would enable users to enjoy more knowledge, further improving communality.

Although a sizeable pool of shared knowledge (i.e., communality) is often the goal of OC managers, our results suggest that connectivity is also important. Our findings suggest that the ease with which peers with similar interests (i.e., connectivity) can be identified influences the ease with which shared knowledge (i.e., communality) can be accessed. This finding suggests that recommending “potential friends” to those who exhibit similar interests could improve OC success. Although this function is common in social media (e.g., Facebook), it is not typically included in the design of current OC platforms. We suggest that OC managers design tools to enhance connections among members (e.g., people you may know). Enhancing connectivity would help users identify peers with similar interests and thus contribute to the expansion of the knowledge pool.

We found that connectivity and communality could have positive effects on community commitment and individual growth. First, OC managers could recruit moderators to guide new participants with less knowl edge to the relevant knowledge pools. Second, OC man agers could design systems that automatically categorize and visualize knowledge (e.g., in knowledge graphs), increasing the usefulness of knowledge pools. Third, OC managers could design or invest in artificial intelligence based recommendation systems to connect users who share similar interests. For example, TikTok enables video creators to immediately obtain predicted video popularity from consumer interest data (Cote 2021). Such data could be used to show users a list of those they share similar interests with, enabling them to reach these fellow users. We also found that connectivity had a stronger effect than communality on knowledge contribution. Hence, if OCs aim to increase knowledge contribution, a greater focus may be put on enhancing connectivity, in comparison with communality, whereas connectivity could increase knowledge contribution via strengthened community commitment.

## Conclusion

Using the ITPGT and the five media capabilities from MST in the context of OCs, we built a theoretical model that had a medium to large effect in explaining task outcomes in OCs. We found that all media capabilities except rehearsability enhanced the OC communication goals of connectivity or communality and that connectivity had a stronger total effect than communality on knowledge contribution. We conclude that our findings can guide OC managers to strengthen community commitment, offer benefits outside of an OC (i.e., individual growth in the case of a gaming OC), and subsequently increase users’ knowledge contribution to OCs.
