# An Examination of the Dynamics of Crowdsourcing Contests: Role of Feedback Type

Abstract. As more businesses are turning to crowdsourcing platforms for solutions to business problems, determining how to manage the sourcing contests based on their objective has become critically important. Existing research, both theoretical and empirical, studies the impact of a variety of contest and contestant characteristics on the outcomes of these contests. Aside from these static design parameters, a lever organizations (clients) can use to dynamically steer contests toward desirable goals is the feedback offered to the contestants (solvers) during the contest. Although a handful of recent studies focuses on the effects of feedback at a high level (e.g., volume, valence), to the best of our knowledge, none has examined the effects of the information contained in the feedback. Furthermore, the focus of the existing studies is solely on the quality of the submissions and not on other critical contest outcomes, such as the diversity of the submissions, which is found to be significant in the creativity and innovations literature. In this study, first, using the psychology literature on the theory of feedback intervention, we classify client feedback into two types: outcome and process. Second, using data from almost 12,000 design contests, we empirically examine the effects of the two types of feedback on the convergence and diversity of submissions following feedback interventions. We find that process feedback, providing goal-oriented information to solvers, fosters convergent thinking, leading to submissions that are similar. Although outcome feedback lacks the informative value of process feedback, it encourages divergent thinking, which is the ability to produce a variety of solutions to a problem. Furthermore, we find that the effects are strengthened when the feedback is provided earlier in the contest rather than later. Based on our findings, we offer insights on how practitioners can strategically use an appropriate form of feedback to either generate greater diversity of solutions or efficient convergence to an acceptable solution.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdsourcing is increasingly being used as an open innovation mechanism by which organizations (clients) engage an external crowd of potential solution providers (solvers). A defining feature of crowdsourcing is its ability to enlist many individual contributors (often distributed globally) in the development of diverse solutions, dramatically lowering the costs of generating such solutions (Archak and Sundararajan 2009). Over the past decade, crowdsourcing has become an increasingly popular mechanism for generating innovative and efficient solutions for business and societal problems as evidenced by the proliferation of crowdsourcing platforms.

As more organizations open their boundaries and leverage crowdsourcing to access external knowledge, determining how to manage the contests based on their objectives has become critically important. Not surprisingly, studies—both analytical and empirical—explore the effects of numerous contest-related attributes on the outcomes of interest. These studies can be generally categorized into two streams: ones that take a contest design perspective, identifying prespecified contest design parameters (e.g., duration, prize amount, and number of prizes) that may incentivize contestants to generate more effective solutions (e.g., Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Huang et al. 2014, Liu et al. 2014), and others that take a contestant perspective, focusing on contestant characteristics (e.g., gender, cultural and economic background, technical background, prior experience on the platform) that may be conducive to problem-solving in crowdsourcing contests (Bayus 2013, Bockstedt et al. 2015, Hwang et al. 2019)

Recent articles in several business disciplines, including information systems, operations management, and marketing (e.g., Wooten and Ulrich 2017, Camacho et al. 2019, Jian et al. 2019), show that the performance of crowdsourcing contests depends on not only contest design parameters and contestant characteristics, but also the ongoing communication from the client to the solvers during the contest. However, we continue to have a limited understanding of how this dynamic client-provided feedback can influence the solutiongeneration process and, therefore, the outcomes in crowdsourcing platforms. Mihm and Schlapp (2019) recently observed that, although “feedback has been extensively used in practice to improve both the efficiency and the efficacy of contests,” our understanding of “when and how to provide which kind of feedback … is rather limited” (p. 570). Further, the few studies that explore the effects of feedback focus only on numerical rating feedback and high-level constructs, such as directed versus random (Wooten and Ulrich 2017), private versus public (Mihm and Schlapp 2019), full versus none (Jiang et al. 2021), or just the volume of feedback (Jiang and Wang 2020). Whether public or private, the nature of information contained in the feedback is not all the same, but existing studies have not examined those differences and their impact. Recently, Chan et al. (2021) took a step in that direction by classifying feedback based on its valence and analyzing its impact on ideation performance. Our goal is to advance the understanding further by parsing the informational content of client-provided textual feedback to assess its impact on contest outcomes.

The extensive literature on feedback defines feedback intervention as the “actions taken by an external agent to provide information regarding some aspect of one’s task performance” (Kluger and DeNisi 1996, p. 255). According to this literature, manipulating the mere presence or absence or the valence of feedback is not enough to understand the effects of feedback on task performance. What are important in the relationship between goal setting and task performance are the focus and timing of the feedback (e.g., Goodman et al. 2004, Mory 2013). Based on the focus, two forms of feedback are proposed in this literature: outcome feedback, with which the focus is on the accuracy of a response, and process feedback, with which the focus is on describing the reasons that underlie the accuracy (Jacoby et al. 1984). Similarly, based on the timing of feedback, it is classified into two categories: early and late feedback. Following these classifications, we categorize the textual communication from a client in a contest into three types: no feedback, when there is no task-specific information; outcome feedback, which focuses on evaluating the performance of submissions; and process feedback, which offers suggestions for improvements based on the client’s needs. Further, we differentiate between feedback provided in the earlier stages of a contest versus that provided in later stages.

To understand the effects of feedback on the dynamics of contests, we rely on the creativity and innovations literatures, which suggest that innovation requires balancing divergent and convergent forces (Brophy 1998, Anderson et al. 2014, Goldschmidt 2016). Divergent processes are those that generate a wide variety of ideas, thus increasing the solution space, whereas convergent processes are those that involve the selection of several similar ideas, seeking to reduce the size of the possible solution space. Divergent thinking, a key part of the creative process, is often conceived as the ability to explore multiple directions in a thought space from the initial problem or reference point. Convergent thinking, on the other hand, is used to signify thought processes that lead to one best response or the right solution (Cropley 2006). With a focus on finding the right solutions, convergent thinking is sometimes considered as the opposite of creative thinking. Hommel et al. (2011) distinguish between divergent and convergent thinking by conceptualizing the former as a less constrained process and the latter as a more strongly constrained pro cess. Based on this literature, we argue that, although precise guidance in the form of process feedback can lower ambiguity and assist solvers to narrow the search space, the feedback can hinder divergent thinking, leading to premature convergence. Outcome feedback, on the other hand, can expand the search space, thereby facilitating variety or diversity of solutions but poten tially delaying an acceptable solution to the task. Further, immediate feedback can assist in the acquisition of information quickly, bolstering the effects of outcome or process feedback, whereas delayed feedback may slow the reinforcement of learning, thereby hindering the effects of outcome or process feedback.

## 1.1. Research Questions and Contributions

In this study, we explore the primary research question: how do the focus and timing of feedback influence the nature of submissions in a crowdsourcing contest? To answer this question, we empirically examine granular contest data, involving more than 1.4 million submis sions in nearly 12,000 design contests, which we obtained from a leading crowdsourcing platform for creative designs. The platform allows clients to provide textual comments that are publicly announced to all solvers at any time during the contest. The solvers, in turn, can update their designs based on the feedback offered by the client. Our data set consists of detailed information about each contest, including contest design parameters (e.g., award amount and contest duration), the content and timing of public textual comments provided by the client throughout the contest, detailed information of each submitted design (e.g., who submitted the design, the submission file, and the time stamp of when the submission was made), and detailed information about each solver (e.g., all past contests). The solutions sought by the clients in these contests are creative designs, which the solvers sub mit as image files.

For our analyses, first, we classify all client comments into three categories—no, outcome, and process feedback—using a combination of human workers and machine learning techniques (please refer to Section 3 for the methodological details). Second, we use advanced image processing techniques to compute a similarity score for each design submission. Whereas existing research uses subjective measures and proxies (e.g., Wooten and Ulrich 2017, Chan et al. 2021, Koh and Cheung 2021) for assessing the quality of submissions, we leverage an advanced image-comparison algorithm to assess the similarity of each submission to the winning design. Our method recognizes that the design chosen by the client as the winner of a contest is the right solution among all the submissions in the contest; that is, it is the best fit solution for the business problem. The greater the mathematical distance of a submission from the winning design, the lower is its similarity score. We then rely on the mean and variance of the similarity scores to calculate the affinity and variety of solutions, respectively, following feedback interventions. Affinity measures the extent to which the submitted solutions are similar, whereas variety measures how diverse they are. We find that process feedback increases solution affinity leading to solution convergence, and outcome feedback increases submission variety contributing to more diverse solutions. We also find that the positive effect of process feedback on greater affinity is stronger when provided earlier than later in the contest. Delayed feedback hinders swift convergence of desired solutions and may even backfire if provided too late. Further, the convergence effect is stronger for submissions from solvers who made submissions prior to the feedback than for submissions from solvers who make their first submission following a feedback intervention.

We make several theoretical and empirical contributions. First, we use the psychology literature on feedback to categorize textual feedback offered by clients into two types: outcome and process based on the focus of its content and early and late based on timing. Whereas most articles on the effects of feedback on crowdsourcing tasks investigate the effects of the mere presence or absence of feedback, we analyze the informational content of feedback and its timing to evaluate its impact. Furthermore, the previous studies primarily focus on task performance, and we also analyze the effects of feedback on the dynamics of contests, that is, how contests evolve over time as a function of the feedback. Second, we empirically demonstrate that the nature and timing of feedback can be utilized by the client to steer a contest toward faster convergence or a greater variety of solutions, depending on the objective. To conduct our analysis, we leverage an advanced image-comparison algorithm to ascertain the affinity and variety of solutions. We believe the findings will help clients better manage crowdsourcing contests by carefully manipulating the informational content and timing of their comments to contestants. Based on our findings, we offer valuable insights into how crowdsourcing platforms should design guidelines and notification mechanisms for clients to manage their contests based on their objectives. The study should help clients obtain more desirable outcomes from contests, resulting in greater satisfaction with and continual use of the platform.

## 2. Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

## 2.1. Idea Generation in Crowdsourcing Contests

Creative tasks are defined as the production of some thing original and appropriate or novel and useful (Amabile 1983, Hennessey and Amabile 1988). Creative problem solving consists of seeking original ways to reach goals when the means to do so are not readily apparent. The process uses divergent ideation and convergent judgment. Divergent thought from a single starting point generates varied ideas, whereas convergent thought starting from multiple points seeks one most true or useful conclusion. Although there are many different theories of the underlying nature of cre ative processes (see, for example, Kozbelt et al. 2010), our interest is in the dichotomy between divergent and convergent processes. Divergent processes are those that generate a wide variety of ideas, thus increasing the solution space. Convergent processes, on the other hand, are those that involve the selection of a particula number of the best ideas, seeking to reduce ambiguity and the size of the possible solution space. Faludi (1973) distinguishes two different aims of these two processes: the aim in convergence is to “discover the one right answer to a problem set,” whereas the aim in divergence is to “produce a larger number of possible answers” (p. 118). Crowdsourcing contests may foster divergence to creatively generate new ways of framing problems and potential solutions, and sometimes, they may seek convergence to generate a high-quality solu tion relatively quickly.

Girotra et al. (2010) propose a set of variables that govern the underlying statistical process of idea generation: (i) the number of ideas, (ii) the average quality of the ideas, and (iii) the variance in the quality of the ideas. The idea-generation process examined in the social psychology literature (e.g., Diehl and Stroebe 1991, Mullen et al. 1991) also finds the number of ideas and their average quality to be important performance metrics. The quantity and variance of solutions are found to have complementary effects on the quality of the best solutions (Terwiesch and Ulrich 2009). Depending on whether a contest is seeking divergent solutions or quick convergence, the objective in terms of these metrics may differ. For instance, a contest seeking divergent solutions may pursue greater variance, whereas a contest seeking faster convergence may pursue lower variance. Our goal in this paper is to study how the nature of feedback influences these important variables that govern the dynamics and outcomes of contests.

## 2.2. Types of Feedback

Researchers in a variety of disciplines study the role of feedback as well as the effects of different types of feedback on performance and learning (Annett 1969, Balzer and Doherty 1989, Sengupta and Abdel-Hamid 1993, Kluger and DeNisi 1996, Hattie and Timperley 2007). Hattie and Timperley (2007) conceptualize feedback as information provided by an agent regarding aspects of one’s performance or understanding. They suggest that the main purpose of feedback is to reduce discrepancies between current understandings and performance and a goal. According to Sadler (1989), feedback should be aimed at filling the gap between what is understood and what is aimed to be understood. In organizations, feedback is thought to serve both informational and motivational functions, enhancing an individual’s performance (Kopelman 1986). Ilgen et al. (1979) conceptualize the feedback process as a special case of the more general communication process to assist performance in organizations. They categorize the factors influencing feedback effectiveness into three groups: the source of the feedback, its recipient, and the feedback message. In regard to this study, the source of the feedback is the client, and the recipients are the solvers participating in a contest. Thus, our primary objective is to examine the feedback message; specifically, how the informational content of the feedback in terms of its focus influences the behavior of the participants.

Scholars in applied psychology and organizational behavior emphasize the significance of feedback focus as an important dimension influencing the development of an effective task strategy (see, for example, Liden and Mitchell 1985, Goodman et al. 2004). Earley et al. (1990) classify feedback into two categories based on its focus: outcome feedback, which consists of information concerning performance outcomes, and process feedback, which consists of task-related information intended to assist individuals in developing a more goal-oriented strategy. Although outcome feedback (e.g., right or wrong) can identify the need to adjust action, it does not provide much information regarding how to adjust. Process feedback, on the other hand, assists individuals in the development of an effective task strategy. Typically, outcome feedback focuses on the end result, whereas process feedback focuses on the steps to achieve the desired result. For instance, consider a student who has completed an addition problem. Outcome feedback is to just mark the solution right or wrong. However, if the solution is wrong, process feedback consists of pointing out why the solution is wrong and possibly identifying the correct procedure. Thus, although individuals benefit from outcome feedback in simple tasks (Doherty and Balzer 1988), in complex tasks, it is found to be insufficient for decision makers to make efficient decisions (Hammond et al. 1975, Brehmer 1980). Kluger and DeNisi (1996) assert that complex tasks require cognitive feedback on the processes leading to the outcomes. The most effective component of cognitive feedback capable of affecting individual performance is task information (Balzer and Doherty 1989), which enables a decision maker to learn more about the environment. According to Sengupta and Abdel-Hamid (1993), cognitive information enables an individual to obtain greater insight into the individual’s strategy. Note that the focus of feedback, in terms of outcome and process, is a distinct dimension, different from the quantity of information contained in a feedback message (Goodman et al. 2004).

Feedback regarding an individual’s action can be pro vided immediately or with a temporal delay. The cogni tive psychology literature suggests that the timing of feedback can affect task performance (Butler et al. 2007, Mory 2013). Researchers have been reaching opposing conclusions on the effects of feedback timing for decades (Pound and Bailey 1975, Butler et al. 2007); however, Kulik and Kulik (1988), employing a meta-analysis of 53 studies with widely varying methodologies, conclude that although delayed feedback produced better results in some laboratory studies, immediate feedback resulted in better performance in applied studies. A widely accepted theoretical explanation of the superiority of delayed feedback is proposed by Kulhavy and Anderson (1972). The explanation, known as the interference preservation theory, postulates that, during a delay period, originally incorrect actions tend to be forgotten and are, thus, not available to interfere with the learning of correct actions following delayed feedback. Kulik and Kulik (1988) note that delayed feedback helped learning only in special experimental situations in which the information acquisition is significantly separated in time from the feedback presentation; more typically, delayed feedback hindered the acquisition of needed information and hurt performance in dynamic environments (e.g., Sterman 1989). The diminished learning under late feedback conditions is caused by the interference of al ternative response options held in working memory (Opitz et al. 2011).

2.2.1. Feedback in Contests. Although many of the early innovation contests utilized single-submission settings, more and more contests are falling outside this mold with provisions for transparent competitions facilitated by increased client–solver interactions (Wooten and Ulrich 2017). During a crowdsourcing contest, the client often provides feedback to solvers, enabling them to revise their submissions. As the client determines the winning solution based on its preferences, the feedback is expected to mitigate the information asymmetry between the client and the solvers. The feedback can take the form of ratings on a scale (e.g., one to five stars) evaluating the quality of the solution and/or textual comments regarding the quality of the submissions. Feedback provided as textual comments can alleviate several limitations of feedback provided as ratings for each submission. First, a handful of public comments is a far more efficient way of governing a contest than hundreds of individual ratings provided to submissions in a contest. For instance, outcome feedback can be offered on a set of submissions to indicate their quality. Second, textual feedback can offer richer information to the solvers compared with ratings. For instance, a high rating does not clearly convey to the participating solvers what aspect of the submission (e.g., color, font, shape, contrast) the client finds most desirable. Similarly, for the low-rated submissions, the solvers are even more unclear as to what the shortcomings of the designs are and, more importantly, how to improve them. Textual feedback allows a client to more clearly and efficiently delineate the aspects of images that it likes or dislikes, which also motivated us to focus on textual feedback in this study.

Despite the prevalence of feedback in contests, Mihm and Schlapp (2019) note that the “literature on feedback in contests is sparse” (p. 561). They assert that, even though feedback has been extensively used in practice, our understanding of the appropriate nature and timing of feedback to provide solvers is limited. Zheng et al. (2014) express similar sentiments; that is, the interactions between the client and the solvers have not received as much attention in the crowdsourcing literature as have other aspects of the contests, such as the amount of the prize or the number of solvers in the contests and the collaboration among solvers. Recently, Jiang and Wang (2020) lament that the influence of feedback in ideation contests “has largely been ignored” (p. 483) in the literature.

Among the existing studies on client feedback, Mihm and Schlapp (2019) develop theoretical models to examine the effects of feedback policies on contest outcomes. They compare the effects of public and private feedback to contestants, for which the focus was on the incentive effects of feedback. Unlike ours, their study did not consider how feedback can be used to identify areas in which solvers were expected to direct their efforts. Among the empirical studies, Zheng et al. (2014) study the effects of client–solver interactions using both data collected from a crowdsourcing platform as well as surveys of crowdsourcing sponsors. Specifically, the nature of client–solver interactions was measured using three survey questions. They find that greater interactions led to increased solution diversity (measured using a single survey question). Wooten and Ulrich (2017) use field experiments to compare the performance of three types of treatments—no, random, and directed feedback—in open innovation tournaments. They find directed feed back to be associated with increased participation, highe average quality, and lower variance. The feedback on the submissions was delivered once a day using a scale of one to five stars with the directed feedback being highly correlated with the quality of the idea (based on the client’s quality function), whereas the random feed back was generated according to typical rating distributions of platforms. Thus, directed feedback can be considered a form of outcome feedback (albeit not tex tual), which they find to raise the quality of the worst submissions but not the best. We examine directed feedback with a finer lens, splitting it into two catego ries that are firmly grounded in theory.<sup>1</sup> Jiang and Wang (2020) develop a theoretical model in which they endogenize the client feedback to show that feedback volume is chosen strategically based on contest and solver characteristics. We have a related research focus in that we believe feedback can be used strategically based on the contest objective; however, Jiang and Wang (2020) explore the effects of only the volume of feedback, whereas we take a much deeper dive into the type of feedback by systematically analyzing the com ments provided by the client. Chan et al. (2021) study the influence of feedback on ideation performance in non financially incentivized idea-crowdsourcing communities by classifying feedback based on their valence, that is, whether the feedback was positive or negative. They find positive feedback to have a positive effect on idea quality although the effect weakened as the solvers gained expe rience. Jian et al. (2019) also study the effects of comment volume and valence; their focus, however, was on the extent of participation. Recently, using simulations of four different feedback policies (full/early/late/no feed back), Jiang et al. (2021) find that the maximum quality achieved is higher in the full and late feedback policies than in the no and early feedback policies. However, thei findings are based on feedback in the form of ratings only. In summary, none of the existing studies examines the specific informational content of the feedback, which, as we describe, is a critical dimension of feedback. Fur thermore, we focus on the evolution of the solutions rather than just the final solutions.

We classify public textual comments provided by the clients to the solvers into three categories based on the content: (i) no feedback, if the comment includes no assessment of the submissions, for example, “I will select a winner by Sunday, April 3”; (ii) outcome feedback, if the comment evaluates the performance of submissions, for example, “The revisions are not good”; (iii) process feedback, if the comment describes the client’s needs and/or offers suggestions for solvers to revise their submissions, for example, “Black and white should be used minimally. The bronze, brown, and gold should be the focus.” Note that process feedback may contain outcome information too. For instance, a comment such as “I like some of the designs that I have received so far but prefer a green background,” is considered process feedback (as it contains task-related information intended to assist individuals to develop a more goal-oriented strategy), but it also contains outcome information (“I like some of the designs that I have received so far”). Further, both outcome and process feedback may vary in terms of the quantity of information contained in the feedback. For instance, “I do not like the backgrounds” and “I do not like the green backgrounds” are both outcome-oriented but differ in information quantity. Aside from feedback types based on content, we also study the moderating effects of the timing of the feedback.

## 2.3. Research Hypotheses

For design contests to be effective, clients want to attract many participants who are engaged in the design task. All other things being equal, more participants, more submissions, and more revisions often lead to better outcomes (Girotra et al. 2010). Previous research on creativity and organizational behavior reviewed herein suggests that feedback, in addition to monetary incentives, motivates workers to be more engaged (e.g., Ashford and Tsui 1991, Amabile 1998, Eisenberger and Cameron 1998). Researchers also use uncertainty reduction theory (URT) to understand solvers’ submission behavior in crowdsourcing contests. According to URT, the initial interaction between two agents who attempt to build a collaborative relationship is motivated primarily by the objective of reducing uncertainty. Communication between the agents plays a key role in this process as it is through communication that uncertainty is reduced (Gibbs et al. 2011). URT is used to study a number of online platforms, such as online auctions (Ducarroz et al. 2016) and peerto-peer lending (Larrimore et al. 2011). The uncertainty reduction mechanism is relevant for crowdsourcing contests because the mechanisms can be conceptualized as a communication process between clients and solvers, in which clients reveal problem information and contextual information to encourage potential collaboration partners (solvers) to initiate an exchange relationship, contributing to problem solving (Alexy et al. 2013, Lu¨ ttgens et al. 2014). Besides problem-related uncertainties, competition-related uncertainties also have significant effects on solvers’ participation and submission behavior (Boudreau et al. 2011, Jian et al. 2019). In a crowdsourcing contest, typically only the solver who is selected as the winner can get the monetary reward, whereas other solvers who also put great effort in developing solutions receive no compensation. As a result, a solver’s decision to participate and submit a solution involves uncertainties that are related to the potential competition in the contest.

Thus, client feedback is generally found to positively influence solver participation in the crowdsourcing literature (Lakhani et al. 2007, Wooten and Ulrich 2017, Jian et al. 2019). Wooten and Ulrich (2017) find that even random feedback is better than no feedback in increasing solver participation. Jian et al. (2019) suggest that client feedback leads to greater submissions because it reduces both task and behavioral uncertainty; the former refers to the perceived risk of expending effort inappropriately and the latter to the perceived risk of the client not following through in paying the winner. Further, client communication can serve to foster a sense of community, which can also lead to greater engagement (Lakhani et al. 2007). When solvers feel connected, they are more likely to engage in the contest and submit solutions. Furthermore, a client who engages with solvers regularly is likely to be perceived as more reliable and trustworthy, and these are important characteristics in attracting submissions (Wang et al. 2019). Thus, the benefit of feedback may be manifested in not only attracting new solvers to join and make submissions, but also motivating existing solvers to create more solutions. Although none of existing studies analyzes the content of the feedback, we expect feedback—both outcome and process—to result in more submissions.

Hypothesis 1a. Submission quantity is greater with outcome feedback than with no feedback.

Hypothesis 1b. Submission quantity is greater with pro cess feedback than with no feedback.

In complex environments, simply providing outcome feedback generally is insufficient for decision makers to make optimal decisions (Balzer and Doherty 1989, Hogarth et al. 1991). Although individuals benefit from outcome feedback in simple tasks (Doherty and Balzer 1988), in complex tasks, outcome feedback often does not have a positive influence on performance (Hammond et al. 1975, Brehmer 1980). In fact, studies in psychology find outcome feedback to be sometimes negatively correlated with task performance (Hammond et al. 1973, Jacoby et al. 1984). Hammond et al. (1973) show that, when a learning task involves probabilistic relations between the cues and the criterion (as do virtually all learning tasks), outcome feedback can contain noisy in formation, which may result in response inconsistency. Performance in such cases can be adversely affected. In the absence of task information, a decision maker may have very little idea regarding what constitutes a better strategy, which may lead to the perpetuation of flawed strategy (Brehmer 1990). In a design contest, the solvers can be expected to develop a mental model of the client’s needs based on the initial description of a contest.

The solvers make submissions based on this understanding of the client’s ideal design. When the client offers no feedback at all, the solvers have no reason to deviate from their initial belief and, therefore, their initial strategy. However, when outcome feedback is provided, solvers are forced to explore and experiment without a clear direction. When tasks are complex, what is needed is cognitive feedback on the processes leading to the outcomes (Kluger and DeNisi 1996). The most effective component of cognitive feedback capable of affecting individual performance is task information (Balzer and Doherty 1989), which enables a decision maker to learn more about the environment. Process feedback can facilitate a solver’s performance by helping the solver develop an effective task strategy based on the goals of the client. The feedback offers goal-oriented information to solvers, and it fosters convergent thinking, that is, using the existing knowledge to analyze the given information to obtain the best answer (Runco 1994). Thus, process feedback aids solution affinity, whereas outcome feedback impedes it.

Hypothesis 2a. Submission affinity is lower with outcome feedback than with no feedback.

Hypothesis 2b. Submission affinity is greater with process feedback than with no feedback.

Although process feedback can assist solvers in focusing on the expressed preferences of the clients, thereby increasing the affinity of the solutions, it can hinder the solvers’ motivation to explore the solution space. That is, they may suffer from the “stuck in a rut” syndrome. In a design contest, when process feedback identifies correct responses, solvers are less inclined to explore the effects of different designs. Such feedback may make it unnecessary for solvers to engage in exploration and experimentation, which are essential for generating diverse solutions. Outcome feedback identifies a need to adjust an action, but it does not provide any information concerning how to adjust. The information provided to solvers in the form of outcome feedback lacks specific information regarding revising their submissions, and therefore, the recipients must discover suitable solutions through their own explorations. Without clear feedback regarding the solution, solvers try to estimate the target through trial and error. The feedback leads to divergent thinking, which is the ability to produce a variety of answers or different solutions to one question (Michael and Wright 1989). Thus, outcome feedback is expected to increase the variety of submissions, whereas process feedback is expected to limit it.

Hypothesis 3a. Submission variety is greater with outcome feedback than with no feedback.

Hypothesis 3b. Submission variety is lower with process feedback than with no feedback.

Feedback works by reducing the discrepancy between the current and the desired state on the path to achieving a goal (Hattie and Timperley 2007). In dynamic tasks, the timing of feedback influences the performance of agents by moderating how they extract relevant information from the feedback to achieve an objective. The idea originating from behaviorist theories of learning suggests that feedback must be provided early to reinforce correct learning. Late feedback appears to help learning only in special experimental situations, but typ ically, late feedback hinders learning (Kulik and Kulik 1988). With feedback delay, the predictions of potential outcomes are less specific, especially when participants have little a priori information about what response is correct (Opitz et al. 2011). In the context of participation intensity in innovation tournaments, only early feedback is found to have a positive effect; late feedback has no effect (Camacho et al. 2019). The availability of process feedback containing task information in the early stages of a contest allows solvers to develop an understanding of the client’s needs and design solutions that meet those needs, leading to greater affinity. However, such information provided later in the contest may force the solvers to change an existing course of action, leading to lower affinity overall. In addition, process feedback pro vided in the later stages of a contest may lead solvers to perceive more uncertainties associated with the task and feel demotivated. Similarly, when outcome feedback is provided early, solvers are more inclined to explore and experiment, but outcome feedback offered later in the contest may discourage and demotivate solvers from exploring new solution paths. Thus, we believe early feedback reinforces the effects of outcome and process feedback on quantity, affinity, and variety. That is, the moderating effects of feedback timing are stronger with early than with late feedback.

Hypothesis 4a. The effect of feedback on submission quan tity is stronger with early than with late feedback.

Hypothesis 4b. The effect of feedback on submission affinity is stronger with early than with late feedback.

Hypothesis 4c. The effect of feedback on submission variety is stronger with early than with late feedback.

## 3. Data and Empirical Model

To test our hypotheses, we collected data from a large online marketplace, which provides crowdsourced branding and design services in more than 30 categories from designs for new products and apps to posters and logos for companies. More than 200,000 creative free lancers participate in the various crowdsourcing contests held on this platform. Our data set consists of all completed public graphic design contests hosted on the platform between 2009 and 2014. The platform allows clients to post different categories or types of graphic design contests, among which the top three categories are logo design, letterhead and stationary design, and print design. Each contest is assigned to a specific category that best indicates the type of graphic design the client is seeking. A contest typically follows three major steps: (i) Contest specification—the client in need of a creative design posts a contest with specifications, including descriptions of what it needs, the contest’s start and end times, the award amount and structure (e.g., whether only one winner will be awarded or multiple winners will be selected), and the nature of the contest (i.e., whether it is a public contest in which all participants see each other’s submitted design or a private contest in which each participant only sees the participant’s own designs). (ii) Contest submission—any solver interested in participating in the contest can join the contest and submit designs before the contest ends. At this stage, the client may provide public textual comments targeted to all solvers as well as private numerical ratings (one to five) of submissions only visible to the solver of that submission. For textual comments, the client generally observes a set of submissions and then offers feedback intended for all the participating solvers. Even if the client’s feedback is motivated by a specific submission, all solvers can update their beliefs (regarding the client’s desired design) using the public feedback. The effect of this public textual feedback is the focus of our study. In case numerical ratings are provided to submissions in a contest, the distribution of the ratings is made public to all the solvers. The client may also communicate privately with selected solvers. Although we observe the total number of such communications, if any, we do not have access to their texts or timing.<sup>2</sup> (iii) Contest conclusion—at the end of the contest, the client picks the winning design(s), and the prespecified cash prize(s) are awarded to the corresponding solvers.

## 3.1. Empirical Model

The contests in our sample can have multiple public comments, each received from the client at any given time<sup>3</sup> as the contest progresses. As illustrated in Figure 1, for each contest, we consider all feedback chronologically and focus on the characteristics of the submissions between two adjacent feedback interventions.

Because all feedback is publicly available, submissions from solvers between the jth feedback $f _ { i j }$ for contest i and its subsequent feedback $f _ { i j + 1 }$ should be affected by not only $f _ { i j } ,$ but also all prior feedback from $f _ { i 1 }$ to f<sub>ij�1</sub>. Let $y _ { i j }$ denotes a characteristic of interest (i.e., quantity, affinity, or variety) of the submissions, and we have

$$
\begin{array}{c} y _ {i j} = a + \beta_ {j} \times f _ {i j} + \theta_ {j} \times f _ {i j} \times p _ {i j} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {j - 1} \beta_ {k} \times f _ {i k} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {j - 1} \theta_ {k} \times f _ {i k} \\ \times p _ {i k} + c \times j + d \times j ^ {2} + \pi \times X _ {i j} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i j}, \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where $v _ { i }$ denotes contest fixed effect and $X _ { i j }$ denotes time-varying contest characteristics, such as number of received and rated submissions prior to the feedback.<sup>4</sup> Similar to prior studies that assume submissions may naturally change over time (e.g., Jin et al. 2021), we control for the sequence of the feedback j to capture any linear time trend with respect to feedback. We also include its quadratic term to capture any possible nonlinear time trend. In addition, $p _ { i k }$ denotes the progress of the contest when feedback $f _ { i j }$ was provided by the client, and the term $f _ { i k } \times p _ { i k }$ captures the interaction between the feedback and its timing, which allows us to examine whether the effect of early feedback differs from that of late feedback in the contest.

## 3.2. Identifying Feedback Type

To classify all the textual comments provided by the client into one of the three categories (i.e., no, outcome, and process feedback), we adopted a two-step process. In step 1, we employed 160 human subjects to label approximately 5,000 comments. Each subject was asked to label 100 comments. We pretested the survey instrument through pilot tests with business undergraduate students and then deployed it on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), which has become a popular platform for conducting academic research $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , O’Leary et al. 2014, Lowry et al. 2016). Specifically, Steelman et al. (2014) demonstrate that, with adequate controls, AMT samples are comparable in quality to those obtained with traditional methods. After the subjects read the instructions for the task (see Online Appendix B for the instructions), they took a short quiz to ensure they had adequate understanding of the exercise. The questionnaire included attention check questions to make sure that the subjects paid sufficient attention throughout the exercise. If a respondent failed any of the attention check questions, those responses were discarded. Each textual comment was independently labeled by two respondents at first, and if their labels were different, then a third respondent was used to break the tie. Eighty-three percent of the labels were in agreement between the initial two coders. In step 2, using the labeled data obtained with human subjects as the training set, we developed a deep learning–based algorithm to categorize the remaining, nearly 37,000, comments. Two hidden layers of size 50 were used in the model. To mitigate the problem of unbalanced classes, we used the synthetic minority oversampling technique (Chawla et al. 2002) to augment the minority class. With 10-fold cross-validation, the classifier achieved an accuracy of 98% for classifying feedback as outcome or process.

After the type of each comment was identified, two dummy variables were constructed to represent $f _ { i j } -$ $\begin{array} { r } { I f P r o c e s s F B _ { i j } , } \end{array}$ which indicates whether feedback $f _ { i j }$ is process feedback, and $I f O u t c o m e F B _ { i j } ,$ which indicates whether feedback $f _ { i j }$ is outcome feedback as compared with the baseline scenario of no feedback.

## 3.3. Measuring Affinity and Variety

As described earlier, most of the existing literature on crowdsourcing and innovation contests uses proxies or subjective measures for ascertaining the quality of submissions. For instance, Wooten and Ulrich (2017) use a web-based survey to evaluate the quality of each idea in their experimental idea-generation contests. Similarly, Chan et al. (2021) aggregate the up and down votes that an idea received from community members to compute a crowd-voting score for each idea, which they then used as a proxy of its quality. They assert that a higher score represents “the popularity and attractiveness of the idea among the crowd and thus its superior quality” (p. 7). We argue that, for contests sponsored by clients for which they are seeking a solution to a design problem, the clients are the best judge of the quality of the designs, that is, whether the design solves the client’s problem. The absolute value of the quality of a design is not as important as the proximity of the design to what the client is looking to obtain from the contest. Thus, instead of trying to ascertain a quality score for each submission, we computed a similarity score to measure how close the submissions are to the right solution using an advanced image-processing technique that objectively compared each design to the one selected by the client as the winner of a contest.

To compute the scores, first, we used the scale invariant feature transform (SIFT) algorithm (Lowe 1999, Wang et al. 2018), a popular content-based image mining technique, to detect and capture the key feature points of a submitted design with each feature represented as a single 128- dimensional vector. Local features refer to a pattern or distinct structure found in an image, such as a point or an edge, which are usually associated with an image patch that differs from its immediate surroundings by texture, color, or intensity. Unlike human interpretation of images, for SIFT (and other similar algorithms), what the feature actually represents does not matter, just that it is distinct from its surroundings. Second, we evaluated the similarity between each submission and the winning design by examining the average distance among all the key feature points of the two images. During this step, we employed different distance metrics, such as the Euclidean and cosine distance.<sup>5</sup> The similarity score of a submission is between zero and one with higher value indicating greater similarity between the submission and the design selected by the client as the winner of a contest.

After computing the similarity scores, we characterized a pool of submissions following the focal feedback by its (i) quantity, measured as the number of design submissions in the pool; (ii) affinity, measured as the average similarity score of all designs in the pool, and (iii) variety, measured as the variance of the similarity scores of the designs in the pool. A higher affinity is indicative of greater convergence of the submissions in the pool, whereas a higher variety indicates greater diversity of the solutions.

As shown in column (1) of Table 4, the coefficients of $I f P r o c e s s F B _ { i j }$ and $I f O u t c o m e F B _ { i j }$ are both significant and positive, suggesting that both process and outcome feedback provided by the client help increase submissions from solvers.<sup>8</sup> In addition, the coefficients of the interaction terms IfProcess $\ – B _ { i j } \times P r o g r e s s P c t _ { i j }$ and $I f O u t c o m e F B _ { i j }$ $\times P r o g r e s s P c t _ { i j }$ are significant and negative. An analysis of the subsample of feedback prior to 50% contest completion shows that the effects of process and outcome feedback are indeed manifested in the early stage of a contest (see Table D1 in Online Appendix D). Our results suggest that, although both process and outcome feedback can help increase submissions, they may also hurt when delayed.

The negative effect is likely because late feedback makes solvers demotivated as they find it challenging to satisfy the client’s needs. Overall, our results provide partial support for Hypotheses 1a and 1b.

This may be because the hypothesized negative impact of outcome feedback on affinity is offset by the signaling value of the feedback, that is, the presence of outcome feedback signals the client’s active engagement and intention to follow through with the contest, thereby incentivizing solvers to exert more efforts to overcome the noisy information in the outcome feedback.<sup>9</sup> However, we observe a significant and positive effect of process feedback.<sup>10</sup> Meanwhile, the significant and negative coefficient of $I f P r o c e s s F B _ { i j } \times P r o g r e s s P c t _ { i j }$ suggests that the positive effect of client-provided process feedback on submission affinity is stronger when the feedback is given in earlier stages of the contest. Again, this result suggests that process feedback provided too late may lower submission affinity, most likely because of the potential demotivating effect of late feedback. ## 4.3. Robustness Checks

To ensure robustness of our findings, we carried out numerous additional analyses.

4.3.1. Checking the Validity of the Affinity Measure Based on Image Similarity. As noted earlier, in contrast to prior studies, which rely on proxies or subjective measures for ascertaining the quality of submissions, we develop a novel affinity measure that offers several advantages. First, in creative design contests, typically only a small proportion of the submissions are rated by the client. Indeed, in our data, on average, less than 35% of the submissions received a numerical rating score from the client. Our affinity measure allows us to comprehensively evaluate all submissions rather than only focusing on those with a rating score. Second, the rating score based on a raw scale from one to five may fail to capture the finer differences among submissions with the same score and, thus, may not meaningfully speak to the variety of submissions. Our affinity measure, which captures the extent to which the submissions converge to what the client needs, was derived based on analyzing the similarities between submitted designs and the winning design. An underlying assumption in using affinity as a measure of convergence is that a higher similarity score is indicative of closer proximity to the right solution desired by the client. To test the validity of this assumption, we performed two falsification tests.

First, because the client can also rate select submissions on a numerical scale of one to five, a submission with a higher similarity score should also receive a higher rating score given by the client. Therefore, for submissions that received a rating score from the client, we tested if their similarity scores were significantly and positively associated with their rating scores. We regressed rating score on similarity score, controlling for contest and solver fixed effects. The coefficient of similarity score is significant and positive (β � 1.625, $p { = } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , supporting the validity of our measure.

Second, for contests with multiple awards, all the chosen winning designs are ranked. In this case, a higher ranked submission can be considered as better aligned with the client’s desired solution and, thus, should have a higher similarity score. Therefore, we performed a regression of a submission’s awarded position on its similarity score. The coefficient of similarity score is significant and negative (β � �1.768, p � 0.001), indicating that the higher the similarity score, the lower (i.e., better ranked) the awarded position. This result provides further support for the validity of our similarity score measure.

4.3.2. Using Alternative Measures of Affinity and Variety. Although we utilized a submission’s similarity score as a representation of its quality from the client’s perspective, the rating the submission received is an additional indicator of the client’s assessment of submission quality. Therefore, we leveraged submission rating scores to test the robustness of our findings using alternative measures of affinity and variety. Specifically, we reran the model by using the average numerical rating score of submissions (ScoreAffinity<sub>ij</sub>) as a measure of affinity and the variance of numerical rating scores in the submission pool (ScoreVariety ) as a measure of variety. The unrated submissions were ignored. As shown in columns (1) and (2) of Table 6, we obtained largely consistent results.

As a diverse submissions pool is characterized by designs that are different from each other, the average similarity between all pairs of designs should be low. Therefore, we reran the model by using one minus the average pairwise similarity score of submissions (PairwiseVariety<sub>ij</sub>) as a measure of variety.<sup>11</sup> As shown in column (3) of Table 6, our results remain qualitatively consistent. Finally, we report additional robustness checks using other measures of variety, such as entropy and Gini coefficient, in Online Appendix F.

4.3.3. Accounting for Potential Endogeneity of Feedback. In our context, it is possible that the observed feedback provided by the client $f _ { i j }$ may be influenced by the client’s deliberate reactions to the submissions and, thus, may suffer from endogeneity concerns. To strengthen the causal interpretation of our results, we first used the instrumental variables (IV) approach and employed a two-stage least squares estimation with a linear probability model in the first stage, which yields consistent estimators in the case of endogenous binary independent variables (Wooldridge 2002).

When feedback is endogenously given by the client during a contest, it is likely that the client has observed how prior contests were managed by others and follows them to offer process or outcome feedback for its own contest. For example, after a client learns that most clients of logo-design contests offer process feedback when the contest has progressed 50%, it is more likely to also offer process feedback when its own logodesign contest is 50% completed. Constructing IVs based on the behavior of other participants in the same market is used by others, such as Ghose and Han (2014) and Mayya et al. (2021). We employed the percentage of client-provided process feedback $( H i s t P r o c e s s F B P c t _ { i j } )$ and the percentage of client-provided outcome feedback (HistOutcomeFBPct ) in historical graphic design contests of the same category (e.g., logo or print design) at the same stage of contest progress $( P r o g r e s s P c t _ { i j } )$ as an IV for $I f P r o c e s s F B _ { i j }$ and $\begin{array} { r } { I f O u t c o m e s F B _ { i j } , } \end{array}$ respectively. Although these two IVs may be correlated with the type of the focal feedback $f _ { i j } ,$ they should not be directly related to solver submissions in the focal contest.

To assess the validity of the chosen IVs, we employed the Lagrange multiplier (LM) test of under-identification and the Cragg–Donald Wald F test of weak identification. The Kleibergen and Paap rk LM statistic is between

10.815 and 13.624 and significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ for the three estimation equations of quantity, affinity, and variety, suggesting that the minimal canonical correlation between the endogenous variables and the instrument is statistically different from zero, providing evidence of instrument relevance. The Cragg–Donald Wald F statistics for each respecified model with one endogenous variable exceeds the recommended Stock–Yogo critical values for 10% maximal IV size, providing support that the chosen instruments are not weak.

We obtain largely consistent results with the IV estimation as shown in Table 7. For example, the coefficient of $I f P r o c e s s F B _ { i j }$ is significant and positive in the estimation equations of submission quantity and affinity and significant and negative in the estimation of submission variety. In contrast, the coefficient of $I f O u t c o m e F B _ { i j }$ is significant and positive in the estimation equation of quantity, insignificant in the estimation equation of affinity, and significant and negative in the estimation equation of variety.

Our sample has 1,294 repeated clients who posted at least two contests on the platform. It is reasonable to assume that a client who provided more process (outcome) feedback in prior contests provides process (outcome) feedback in the focal contest. Meanwhile, any feedback provided in prior contests should not affect submissions in the focal contest. Therefore, we also used the amount of process (outcome) feedback a repeated client provided in prior contests as the IVs for the type of the focal feedback. In this analysis, the sample was restricted to 2,119 contests by those repeated clients with their first contests excluded. The results are shown in Tables G1 and G2 in Online Appendix G. Indeed, in the first stage estimation, we observe that the amount of process (outcome) feedback in prior contests by a repeated client has a positive and significant effect on ifProcess $F B _ { i j } ( i f O u t c o m e F B _ { i j } )$ in the focal contest. Again, the estimation results based on the new IVs remain largely consistent. We also report additional analyses to strengthen the causal inference, such as propensity score matching and falsification tests of placebo feedback, in Online Appendix G.

4.3.4. Controlling for Information Quantity. To ensure that the observed effects of feedback type (i.e., process versus outcome) are not influenced by the quantity of information contained in the feedback, we conducted a robustness check by controlling for feedback length $( F B L e n g t h _ { i j } ) _ { i }$ , which was measured by the number of characters, in the estimation model. We also included the interaction terms between feedback type and length to account for the possibility that the effect of process or outcome feedback may be stronger when it contains more information. As shown in Table G9 in Online Appendix $G ,$ all coefficients related to $F B L e n g t h _ { i j }$ except for one are insignificant. All major results regarding process and outcome feedback remain significant and qualitatively consistent. These results further corroborate the important and unique role of outcome and process feedback in crowdsourcing contests.

4.3.5. Accounting for the Effects of Private Feedback. In this study, we focus on how the textual public feedback provided by the client affects the pool of submissions. At the same time, it is possible that the client may have provided private feedback to select solvers. As noted in Section $^ { 3 , }$ our data sample contains the total amount of private feedback (NumPrivateFeedback ) provided by the client during a contest, but the details of such feedback are not available to us. To mitigate concerns of possible confounding effects of private feedback, we divided contests into high and low private feedback groups based on the median value of NumPrivateFeedback and then replicated the analyses on the two subsamples. As shown in Table G10 in Online Appendix $G ,$ once again, we obtain largely consistent results across the two groups. This additional analysis provides confidence in the robustness of our results even in the presence of private feedback.

4.3.6. Using Alternative Panel Structure. Our primary analysis at the contest-feedback level allows us to isolate and examine the effect of each feedback. As a further robustness check, we also examined the effect of feedback on submissions received by each contest on each day.<sup>12</sup> In this alternative panel structure, the main independent variables are the accumulated number of process feedback $( \# P r o c e s s F B _ { i j } )$ and amount of outcome feedback (#OutcomeFB<sub>ij</sub>) provided by the client prior to the day. The timing of each type of feedback was measured by the average contest progress of all prior feedback of the same type (i.e., AvgProcessFBProgress $P c t _ { i j }$ and AvgOutcomeFBProgressPct<sub>ij</sub>). As shown in Table G11 in Online Appendix G, we obtain qualitatively similar results, providing further support to the robustness of our findings.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusions

With crowdsourcing contests getting increasingly popular for generating innovative ideas and solutions to business problems, managing the creative processes effectively is becoming critically important. Not surprisingly, there is a growing body of theoretical and empirical literature on designing better sourcing mechanisms by properly calibrating a variety of design parameters (Huang et al. 2014, Hwang et al. 2019, Mihm and Schlapp 2019) Although client feedback can be a powerful tool for dynamically improving the efficacy and efficiency of open contests, the existing studies on feedback in crowdsourcing contests primarily focus on the signaling value of feedback in generating greater engagement with the platform. In contrast, we analyze the informational value of feedback in fostering different creative outcomes based on the goals of the client. Furthermore, we explore the evolution of submissions as a function of feedback rather than just focusing on the final results.

Our study makes several theoretical and empirical contributions to the growing literature on sourcing contests, specifically how the nature of feedback can influence the dynamics and outcomes of the contests. First, we theoretically distinguish the type of feedback provided by the client into outcome and process: outcome feedback provides information on performance outcomes, and process feedback provides information on how the task can be performed. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically analyze the information contained in the client-provided feedback. Through a combination of human workers and a machine learning algorithm, we classify more than 40,000 textual comments provided by the clients in the contests.

Second, using the organizational behavior literature on creativity and innovation, we identify two potentially desirable outcomes in design contests, namely, convergence and divergence. Most of the existing research on contests focus on quality as the sole performance variable of interest, neglecting the importance of diversity of solutions. But previous literature on idea generation affirms that, in innovation tasks, the associative variety of knowledge sharing in crowds affects novel idea generation. In fact, Malhotra and Majchrzak (2019) assert that the accumulated crowd-level knowledge variety can also stimulate others in the crowd to add to the variety of knowledge associations. Thus, in design contests, having the ability to influence the diversity of solutions through appropriate feedback should be considered an important tool in a manager’s toolkit.

Third, to evaluate each solution, we develop a novel image-processing technique, which compares the similarity of each submission to the one picked by the client as the winner. We argue that, in design contests in which the client selects the winning submission, the absolute value of quality (even if it can be computed) may not be the most significant performance variable. The winning solution picked by the client is a better indicator of its preferences. Numerous robustness checks (described in Section 4.3) provide us confidence that the technique we develop in this study can be used to evaluate the submissions of other graphic design contests. The metrics (i.e., affinity and variety) as well as the procedure to compute them can be useful in many other research contexts.

Fourth, and most important, we empirically demonstrate the impact of the two types of feedback on the dynamics of contests. Our results suggest that, although process feedback can be useful in efficiently finding an acceptable solution for the client, it can hinder the exploration of the search space of possible solutions. Outcome feedback, on the other hand, enhances the diversity of solutions proposed by the solvers. Our results also unveil the stronger impact of early as opposed to late feedback and warn clients against the potential adverse effects of delaying feedback in a contest. Although Jiang et al. (2021) show that providing late feedback in the form of numerical ratings may be the best strategy for the client to maximize the number of solvers and high performers, our study sheds new light that it is more advantageous for the client to offer public textual feedback early in the contest. This finding contributes to the crowdsourcing literature by highlighting that the optimal timing of providing feedback by the client may depend on the specific form of feedback (e.g., numerical rating versus textual feedback) because it influences the dynamics of contests differently. In addition, although existing research suggests that client feedback generally has a positive effect on the volume of submissions, sepa rating feedback into outcome and process allows us to demonstrate that the effects are more complex. For instance, process feedback reduces submissions from existing solvers even as it increases submissions from new solvers. Finally, whereas most of the existing re search on contests solely focuses on contest outcomes, this study explores the evolution of the solutions in addition to the eventual aggregate outcomes.

For businesses organizing online crowdsourcing contests, the results of this study offer valuable insights on how to leverage in-contest communication to better manage the contests based on their goals. For instance, if the goal is to find an acceptable solution quickly, then process feedback is most useful. Clearly describing the preferences through textual feedback enables the solvers to narrow down the scope of possible designs. However, as we demonstrate, with process feedback, the crowd may converge too quickly on a few ideas such that the solutions that emerge are only incremental improvements rather than truly innovative solutions. Successful crowdsourcing for innovation involves the identification of a variety of solutions from which businesses can choose. Even in new product developments, for example, projects are successful when they identify a large number of alternative solutions to the problem (Sheremata 2000). Thus, when engendering innovation is the goal, businesses can offer outcome feedback to generate greater diversity of solutions. In addition, as feedback plays a more salient role when offered in the early stages of a contest, businesses should not delay their feedback interventions to steer contests more effectively toward desirable goals.

Our study has several limitations. First, in addition to the public comments made by the client in a contest, which we capture, the platform also allowed clients to communicate with the solvers privately. Our data set contains the volume of such private comments, and as we show in Section 4.3, our results are robust to the volume of those comments. However, our sample does not include the content of the communications, and thus, we are unable to analyze the effects of any feedback information contained in those private communications. Second, it is possible that the effect of feedback may be stronger on the pool of submissions immediately following the feedback than on later submissions. We show that this possibility does not pose a threat to our major findings regarding the role of process and outcome feedback. Future research could leverage a more controlled setting (e.g., a laboratory experiment) to causally investigate whether and how the tempora distance between the submissions and the feedback may moderate the effect of feedback. Third, although we provide evidence that our findings are not biased by potential endogeneity issues of feedback, we do not formally investigate what factors affect the type of feedback provided by a client. An examination of the antecedents of feedback will help further enhance our understanding of feedback in crowdsourcing contests. Fourth, we do not analyze the content of process feedback in this study; it is possible that the information contained in process feedback (e.g., regarding color, font) exerts further influence on the outcomes of contests. Fifth, when we collected the data on completed contests, a small proportion (0.62%) of the submissions were already withdrawn by the solvers and are, thus, missing from our sample. These limitations notwithstanding, our findings offer valuable insights on effectively managing creative solution generation processes using appropriate feedback interventions.
