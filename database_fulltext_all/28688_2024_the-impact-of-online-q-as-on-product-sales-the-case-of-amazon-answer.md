# The Impact of Online Q&As on Product Sales: The Case of Amazon Answer

Abstract. This paper uses data from two online shopping platforms to investigate the impact of questions and answers on product sales. This research question is important as online marketplaces are increasingly incorporating questioning and answering (Q&A) capabilities. However, the economic implications of these Q&A capabilities have yet to be explored and discussed in the literature. We use a difference-in-differences approach to empirically examine the effect of questions and answers that exist on only one platform on the sales of experience goods. Interestingly, we find that answers, particularly the depth of answers, have a positive impact on sales. In addition, the fraction of questions with at least one answer has a positive and significant impact on product sales as well. Our further analyses on textual content suggest that fit-oriented questions and quality-oriented questions impact sales differently and that positive answers and negative answers also impact sale in different ways. Our findings suggest that platform sellers should pay particular attention to questions that their products receive, as answered questions increase product sales.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, online marketplaces have experienced significantly intense competition (Kannan et al. 2022). As a result, many platforms have explored options to incorporate new features to attract consumers and to differentiate themselves. One such feature that has recently gained traction is that of embedding question and answer (Q&A) capabilities into platforms. An example of this feature is Amazon.com’s “Amazon Answer,” which was introduced in 2012 and permits users to ask and/or answer questions related to products on an Amazon product page. In recent years, this Q&A feature has increasingly been adopted by online platforms (Banerjee et al. 2021), such as Newegg, Wayfair, and eBay. This feature is also particularly popular among small and medium-sized e-commerce platforms in Asia, such as Lazada and Shopee. According to a survey by Pew Research Center, 84% of respondents considered the ability to ask questions as either extremely important or somewhat important, whereas online reviews were mentioned by 74% of respondents (Smith and Anderson 2016). Meanwhile, an average product on Amazon receives more than 20 questions and answers, and the vast majority of products (approximately 95%) receive at least one question and answer. However, although Q&A capabilities have become increasingly common, the economic implications of these questions and answers are understudied in the literature. In this paper, we fill this gap by studying the relationships between Q&A and product sales in the context of an online com merce platform.

It is worth noting that online Q&A systems themselves are not completely new to the literature. In fact, at first glance, these online Q&A systems are very similar to discussion thread systems, which have been extensively studied. That said, most papers related to online discussion forums study contexts in which there are no financial implications (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Yan and Tan 2014). Although a few papers have stud ied the economic impact of online discussion platforms (Godes and Mayzlin 2004), discussions within these platforms and discussions within Q&A platforms for online retailers are fundamentally different. Specifically, the former tends to feature more subjective discussions that focus on related experiences and perceptions, whereas the latter tends to ask and answer specific questions about product attributes, features, or applications. More importantly, consumers on e-commerce platforms tend to process Q&A information to help them make purchase decisions, especially when they are in need of specific information (Banerjee et al. 2021).

We also note that Q&As and online reviews both allow users to share information and knowledge. However, prior studies have empirically demonstrated that these two types of content are distinctively different in many aspects. First, Q&A content tends to be specific in nature, as Q&As tend to be related to one or more specific functions or features of a product and do not contain as much information related to personal taste or experience (Liu et al. 2008). Meanwhile, online reviews normally contain more generic content with some degree of personal, customized opinions. As a result, these two types of user-generated content impact consumer decision making differently (Banerjee et al. 2021, Mousavi et al. 2021). Second, online reviews mainly provide oneway communication, whereas Q&As are built mainly on two-way interactions (i.e., users who ask questions or start a conversation and those who provide answers). Third, although both online reviews and Q&As help customers acquire more information about products and reduce their uncertainty with respect to product quality (Sahoo et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2021), Q&A settings predominantly focus on reducing customers’ fit uncertainty (Banerjee et al. 2021) as reflected in the specific and targeted nature of questions and answers. Furthermore, the literature has posited that online reviews and Q&As are complementary to each other (Banerjee et al. 2021). For the reasons we mention previously, insights from the literature studying online Q&As in noncommercial contexts and online reviews in commercial settings might not carry over to the context of our study, which is online Q&As in commercial settings. Importantly, as retailers have increasingly started to implement Q&A features on their respective platforms, it is timely and important to study how Q&As impact product sales.

To operationalize our research agenda, we collect data from Amazon.com and another large online store in the United States between April 2015 and July 2017. Both stores are among the biggest online retailers in the U.S. market and have billions of dollars in annual revenues. Their market share in the product category used in this study is also comparable. However, only Amazon offered both an online review system and a Q&A system on its product pages, whereas the other store only offered a standard online review system during the study period. We take advantage of the fact that consumers who use Q&As on Amazon tend to make purchasing decisions on Amazon (Sarver 2020) by leveraging an empirical model based on the difference-in-differences regression framework that has been widely used in the literature to study similar problems (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Zhu and Zhang 2010).

In our empirical analyses, we begin by investigating the impact of aggregated variables on sales. We find that the depth of answers and fraction of questions with at least one answer positively impact product sales. We then further examine the potential effects of textual content of Q&As. To do so, we take advantage of the approach used by Banerjee et al. (2021) to divide questions into fit oriented and quality-oriented questions. All else being the same, while fit-oriented questions do not appear to have a significant effect on sales, quality-oriented questions have a negative effect on sales. Meanwhile, to assess the impact of answers, we find that it is critical to understand answer sentiment. In summary, positive answers generally improve sales, particularly positive answers to quality-oriented questions, to the extent that two or more positive answers are enough to offset the negative effect of quality-oriented questions. On the other hand, negatively worded answers to quality-oriented questions have a negative effect on sales. With respect to answers to fit-oriented questions, the depth of answers is the key factor that positively affects sales. Additionally, the fraction of fit-oriented questions that have at least one answer has a positive and significant impact on product sales. Therefore, our analysis suggests that the Q&A system should not be utilized without proper planning, primarily because of the potentially negative impact of unanswered questions. In other words, products that have too many quality-oriented questions, especially unanswered questions, may suffer from a decrease in sales.

Our study contributes to both research and practice. We contribute to the economics of information systems literature by being among the first to empirically demonstrate the economic value of questions and answers, which have been widely adopted by e-commerce platforms in recent years. Our findings in this study also pave the way for future researchers to examine related research questions, such as how questions and answers impact consumers’ state of mind when they make purchase decisions and how product experience and questioning and answering behavior are related. In addition, this paper also provides actionable insights for platform sellers regarding the potential impacts of questions and answers and how these elements may affect prod uct sales.

The rest of our paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we survey the literature related to our study. We then explore relevant theories that inform our empirical investigation, as well as formally develop research hypotheses and discuss variables of interest in Section 3. In Section 4, we discuss our data set and describe the details and summary statistics of the data we use in the empirical analysis and describe the nature of the textual content in our data. In Section 5, we describe our empirical model, which is designed to uncover the effect of Q&As on product sales, and corresponding results. Last, in Section 6, we discuss our findings, the implications of this study and its limitations, and potential opportunities for future research.

## 2. Literature Review

In this section, we discuss the literature that is closely related to our paper. Specifically, we survey the literature in information systems and interfaced areas that examine issues related to the economic impact of questions and answers.

There are various forms of online platforms for user-generated content (UGC). Platforms such as Yelp, Reddit, and Wikipedia aggregate and display UGC without explicit commercial purposes. Meanwhile, many e-commerce platforms such as Amazon, Bestbuy, and Newegg have embedded UGC to facilitate consumers purchase decisions. Traditionally, these platforms rely on online consumer reviews as the primary form of UGC. However, in recent years, questions and answers have been increasingly adopted by multiple online platforms. As we seek to study the impact of questions and answers on product sales in this paper, we use this section to review the relevant literature related to different types of platforms such as online discussions and reviews and discuss how our study contributes to the extant literature in this stream of research.

Online reviews have emerged as an important factor in e-commerce settings and have attracted attention from both researchers and practitioners.<sup>1</sup> Generally recognized as a popular form of electronic word of mouth, online reviews play an important role in the growth and success of e-commerce. Pavlou and Dimoka (2006) demonstrate that textual content of online reviews conveys useful information about sellers’ reputations and is thus crucial for building trust. Furthermore, Awad and Ragowsky (2008) provide evidence which shows that online reviews help foster consumer trust and also demonstrates heterogeneity across genders. Moreover, the information in online reviews can also help product owners improve and innovate (Zhang et al. 2021).

The impact of online reviews on product sales in the e-commerce setting has been well studied by researchers. Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006) were among the first to find that the number of reviews, together with price, have a significant impact on online book sales. In addition, Zhu and Zhang (2010) find that the impact of online reviews varies based on product and consumer characteristics. Other review characteristics have also been shown to impact sales including review valence (Chintagunta et al. 2010), review variance (Sun, 2012), and numerical review ratings (Luca 2011). In addition, online reviews can also have spillover effects on sales of covisited products (Kwark et al. 2021), and such effects are still observed after controlling for marketing mix variables such as online coupons (Lu et al. 2013). More recently, many online review platforms also allow for manager or seller responses, in which companies/ sellers can comment on consumers’ posts. Such interactive comments have become a mechanism to not only identify and solve customer problems, but also manage customer relationships (Ravichandran and Deng 2023). Chevalier et al. (2018) have empirically demonstrated that managerial responses can significantly stimulate reviewing activities, particularly negative reviews. Thus, managers must carefully account for reputation effects when responding to consumer reviews (Proserpio and Zervas 2017) because the increased ratings boosted by responses might come at the cost of lengthier, more detailed negative feedback.

In addition to reviews, Armstrong and Hagel (2000) argue that online communities are extremely valuable for online businesses. Consumers use such communities to satisfy needs beyond transaction needs, such as communication, information, and entertainment. Therefore, commercial success in online contexts is greatly affected by whether businesses can effectively organize and make use of communities to meet consumers’ various needs, such that businesses may further reap commercial benefits by building greater consumer loyalty. Social media platforms like Facebook enable companies to build user communities, which offer extra channels for businesses to engage with consumers (Yang et al. 2019). As another interesting example, Last.fm integrates both music consumption and user interactions on one platform. The empirical studies of such communities show that consumers’ willingness to pay for a product related to the community is closely linked with consumers’ community participation (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013). Combating auction fraud is another example that shows the merits of online communities. Although Internet auctions significantly increase exchange efficiency, they also give rise to auction fraud. Scholars have found that online communities can work in concert with for mal authorities to effectively manage the problem of auction fraud (Chua et al. 2007). Communities enable better monitoring of community activities, which yields knowledge to effectively detect potential wrongdoings and exert information control over fraudulent activities. Collectively, these studies confirm the helpful role that online communities and forums play in facilitating ecommerce.

In this study, we examine the economic implications of online Q&A, which are related yet tangential to the setting of online communities that we discussed earlier in this section. Specifically, in contrast to such online communities, Q&A systems are operationalized as embedded platforms in commercial settings. As such, although studies regarding online reviews and online communities have some relevance, it is difficult to extrapolate those findings directly to embedded Q&A systems in commercial contexts.

Online Q&A platforms use a typical knowledge sharing model. Generally, participants are allowed to ask and respond to questions on various topics. Expert Q&A services, which provide professional experts to help resolve problems in a specific domain, are usually offered by various types of organizations (Shi et al. 2021). For instance, JustAnswer (https://www.justanswer.com) connects visitors with verified specialists to offer consultation support in medicine, law, or finance. Although many such expert services are monetized businesses wherein users pay to receive answers, some may provide free advice (e.g., library reference services). Meanwhile, several Q&A platforms rely on crowd participation in which crowds collaboratively answer questions posted by their peers. Typical examples include Quora and Stack Overflow, which have observed rapid growth since their inception. Such peer-based Q&A platforms are relatively more socialized, and the quality of their answers also vary (Liu et al. 2021).

As the purpose of Q&A services is generally to satisfy users’ information needs, numerous studies have focused on predicting answer quality or user satisfaction (Liu et al. 2008). Previous studies have shown that both user attributes and answer characteristics are helpful in signaling whether a particular answer could be chosen as the best answer by the asker (Adamic et al. 2008). Also, preferences for subjectivity and emotion intensity are considered critical factors in answer-seekers’ evaluation process (Kim and Oh 2009). Meanwhile, the politeness degree could bias users’ choices with respect to best answers (Lee et al. 2019). Identifying various methods and criteria to aid the selection of high-quality answers is another popular research stream in computer science (Li et al. 2012). At the same time, scholars in information systems, human-computer interaction, and psychology have focused on investigating user behavior in Q&A communities. One of these primary research streams aims to uncover the motivations behind Q&A activities, of which the main drivers include social interactions (Lou et al. 2013) and self-presentation (Raban 2009). Prior studies also demonstrate that rewards, either in monetary form (Harper et al. 2008) or symbolic form such as badges (Goes et al. 2016), could impact user contributions on Q&A platforms. The insights in these studies are very important in terms of policy design for Q&A websites (Jain et al. 2014).

In this paper, we study a Q&A service that has been integrated into an e-commerce platform to address consumers’ questions and concerns regarding products sold on the platform. Such use of Q&A systems is relatively new in practice, and clear insights are currently lacking in the literature. Because Q&As in a commercial context are significantly different from discussions in noncommercial settings and because the content of Q&As is significantly different from that of other well studied UGC (e.g., online reviews), existing knowledge in the literature may not be directly applicable. One of the few papers that also studies online Q&As in a commercial setting is Banerjee et al. (2021), who study the interactions between Q&As and online reviews. They focus on studying how questions and answers affect subsequent reviews and ratings for a product, whereas we study the impact of Q&As on product sales. Our paper extends the literature by providing important and relevant empirical insights on the economic impli cations of Q&A content. We also present a summary of relevant papers discussed in this section in Table 1. In the next section, we explore relevant theories that underpin the potential impact of Q&As on product sales and formally develop our research hypotheses.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

The core underlying mechanism of our study is that questions and answers help consumers process information (Harper et al. 2008, Shah et al. 2009). More specifically, the interactive nature of Q&As tends to play an important role in stimulating the thought process of users as they navigate uncertainties in the information (Kay and LeSage 2009). In this section, we formally develop our research hypotheses regarding the potential impact of Q&As on product sales. In particular, we draw from theories and empirical findings from the literature to hypothesize the potential impact of Q&As on customer uncertainty parameters, which could subsequently affect consumer utility and product sales. In this regard, we also provide in Section A of the online appendix a simple analytical model that formally establishes the relationship between information uncertainty parameters and consumer utility.

## 3.1. Research Hypotheses

First, we develop our hypothesis on the potential impact of questions. Prior studies have posited that it is primarily potential customers who ask questions on an online retail platform (Mousavi et al. 2021). Specifically, they ask questions when they seek information about a specific topic. As such, previous studies in various disciplines have consistently associated questions with uncertainty (Gajduschek 2003, Marsi and Van Rooden 2007, Patak and Reynolds 2007). In interpersonal communications, uncertainty about the other party is what motivates question-asking, which is considered a necessary process to achieve interpersonal understanding (Knobloch and Solomon 2005). Meanwhile, in a commercial setting, prepurchase uncertainty spurs consumers to search for relevant information about a product, so they may make better purchase decisions (Urbany et al. 1989). Nevertheless, although questions are one major channel for people to seek needed information, they also indicate individuals’ uncertainty with respect to a specific problem or topic (Douglas 1990). As a result, in the context of online retail platforms, since questions are likely driven by customers in need of information about a product, they likely increase the customers’ perceived uncertainty regarding the product,<sup>2</sup> which subsequently decreases consumer utility and negatively impacts product sales.

Although the empirical evidence and theoretical underpinnings we describe here suggest that the impact of questions on product sales is negative, it is worth noting that questions are generally considered to be a form of electronic word of mouth (Mousavi et al. 2021). As such, questions could arguably represent consumer engagement with the product (Park and Kim 2008, Gavilan et al. 2018) and subsequently influence higher product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). Considering these potential positive effects, we frame our hypothesis regarding the overall impact of questions as an open empirical question and accordingly propose the following competing hypotheses.

## Hypothesis 1a. Questions negatively impact product sales.

Hypothesis 1b. Questions positively impact product sales.

Although our first hypothesis focuses on the overall impact of questions on product sales, the competing tensions that underpin the impact allude to the potential heterogeneity impact of questions. In particular, the effect of questions on sales is likely dependent on the type of uncertainty that consumers seek to address. Prior studies have generally categorized consumer uncertainties regarding products in e-commerce contexts as quality uncertainty and fit uncertainty (Chen et al. 2022). In addition, Banerjee et al. (2021) empirically demonstrate that consumers tend to process fit-oriented questions and quality-oriented questions differently. On the one hand, questions about product quality could increase doubt and uncertainty regarding a product, resulting in lower consumer utility that subsequently reduces the likelihood of purchase. On the other hand, questions about product fit could signal interest in a product, resulting in higher consumer utility that subsequently increases the likelihood of purchase. Because it is reasonable to expect fit-oriented questions to primarily impact consumers’ uncertainty about product fit and qualityoriented questions to primarily impact consumers’ uncertainty about product quality, we follow the precedent in Banerjee et al. (2021) and propose the following.

Hypothesis 2. Quality-oriented questions negatively impact product sales, whereas fit-oriented questions positively affect product sales.

We next develop our hypothesis on the impact of answers. Answers can reduce customers’ perceived uncertainty about products, but it is also possible that customers change their beliefs about products when they obtain answers, which generally offer detailed clarifications about product attributes (Gao et al. 2019). Nevertheless, such clarifications can be perceived by consumers as either favorable or unfavorable. When a new answer is provided, it offers a new information signal about the product, and this new signal intuitively reduces uncertainty about the product. On the other hand, since answers might either contain positive or negative signals with respect to product attributes, the impact of new signals on the average perceived uncertainty could be two-fold, giving rise to the two following scenarios:

1. When a consumer receives a positive signal about a product from answers, the consumer’s belief about the product is positively updated. In addition, the information that the answer provides decreases the consumer’s uncertainty about the product. In this case, answers not only decrease information ambiguity but also shift the consumer’s belief about the product in a positive direction. Therefore, the consumer would expect higher util ity from consuming the product in this case.

2. When a consumer receives a negative signal about a product from answers, the consumer’s belief about the product is negatively affected, which should lower the consumer’s expected utility for purchasing and consuming the product. However, a negative signal nevertheless still reduces uncertainty about the product. As a result, there are two competing drivers that impact the consumer’s utility and purchase probability in opposite directions.

In summary, the impact of answers on consumer utility and subsequently product sales can be in either direction, depending on the nature and the information content of answers on a platform. Accordingly, we propose the following competing hypotheses to test empirically.

## Hypothesis 3a. Answers negatively impact product sales.

## Hypothesis 3b. Answers positively impact product sales.

Our competing hypotheses regarding the potential impact of answers on product sales propose competing tensions that allude to the potential heterogeneity of the impact. In particular, the potential heterogeneity is potentially driven by how consumers process the content of answers (Shah et al. 2009). In that regard, Zeng et al. (2021) suggest that the underlying force that could underpin the competing tensions is the sentiment of answers because readers tend to process positive answers and negative answers differently: positive answers are gener ally linked with positive perceptions, whereas negative answers are generally perceived negatively (Reese 2006, Libkin 2016). Extending these established patterns to the context of our study, it is reasonable to expect that positive answers tend to improve consumer perceptions, resulting in higher consumer utility and subsequently increasing product sales, whereas it is reasonable to expect that the impact of negative answers is negative. Formally, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4. Positive answers impact product sales positively, whereas negative answers impact product sales negatively.

Thus far, we focused on the potential impact of questions and answers independently. However, these hypotheses do not explicitly address the interactions between questions and answers. In other words, it is also important to examine the effect of a question that is answered compared with that of an unanswered question. Earlier studies have demonstrated that answers that provide specific solutions to users’ queries could improve user satisfaction (Liu et al. 2008). Relatedly, in commercial settings, consumers have increasingly demanded support tools that allow them to examine specific attributes of a product, which many platforms offer through a Q&A feature; the answers provided in this system, whether those answers come from sellers or peer consumers, could help resolve consumers’ concerns directly. As a result, consumers’ uncertainty regarding the product could decrease, and they would better understand if the product would likely suit their needs. In addition, such detailed attribute-level information can also benefit other consumers besides those actually asking the questions, as the Q&A system in commercial settings is usually implemented in a public space that broadcasts information to all consumers. For example, a recent study empirically demonstrated that a Q&A system could help reduce consumer-product fit mismatch among consumers by addressing product-relevant questions (Banerjee et al. 2021). Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that the uncertainty that consumers might have about the products themselves and their quality is reduced once consumers’ specific concerns are addressed.

In addition, a Q&A system also possesses several unique characteristics that could affect the uncertainty associated with consumers’ purchase decisions. For instance, a Q&A system tends to offer product information interactively, unlike consumer reviews that are generally perceived to be more didactic. In this regard, studies in the education literature have shown that students tend to be more attentive and achieve better academic performance when an interactive audience response system is embedded in lectures, because the interactive system motivates students to understand concepts more deeply (Kay and LeSage 2009). In our commercial retail context, the interactions between consumers and the Q&A system may encourage consumers to perceive that they understand and evaluate product-relevant information more clearly, particularly for attributes that are of specific interest to them. This enhanced perception about how a product may fit with consumers’ needs and expectations could affect consu mers’ purchase decisions. In addition, Q&A is also an effective method to trigger relevant memories of consumers. Previous studies have demonstrated that such a mechanism serves as another channel that enhances conceptual learning and changes in audience responsebased learning (i.e., changes to prior conceptions and personal beliefs) (Poulis et al. 1998, Penuel et al. 2006). Therefore, Q&A systems could also help enhance consumers’ perceptions of knowledge repositories relevant to a given focal product. Accordingly, with respect to consumers’ greater confidence in terms of product knowledge and evaluation, we expect that Q&As will impact consumers’ purchase process, especially by reducing product fit uncertainty. In sum, it is clear that perceived uncertainty in product fit and quality is reduced for consumers once their questions receive answers, which positively affects consumers’ expected consump tion utility. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 5. Answered questions positively impact produc sales.

In summary, our hypotheses suggest that, although questions signal an increase in uncertainty about a prod uct that consumers consider purchasing, they may also suggest more consumer engagement. That said, once their questions are answered, the uncertainty regarding a given product decreases. However, the effect of answers can be two-fold, depending on whether the answers addressing consumers’ questions are positive or negative. Meanwhile, the effect of questions depends on whether questions are fit oriented or quality oriented. Next, we discuss the variables of interest based on the proposed hypotheses.

## 3.2. Variables of Interest

In this section, we describe the Q&A-related variables and measures that we use to investigate our research hypotheses.

The first variable of interest is the volume of questions. In general, volume is an important element as it captures the capacity of the variable or the number of signals. In addition, it has been demonstrated in the literature that the volume of other word-of-mouth-related variables (e.g., volume of online reviews) has a significant impact on product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). It also represents a high-level measure with respect to consumers’ questions: If the impact of this variable is statistically significant, then it could imply that consumers use Q&A systems to obtain signals about products that will help them in their decision-making process. Next, the volume of answers is our second variable of interest. Similar to the volume of questions, the volume of answers is used as a high-level measure that captures the number of information signals relayed by answers. Additionally, we use an approach similar to Banerjee et al.

(2021) whereby we classify questions as either fit oriented or quality oriented and then construct high-level measures of the total number of fit-oriented and the total number of quality-oriented questions.

In addition, we use the average question word count as our third variable of interest to measure the impact of questions from a different perspective. Specifically, we use average word count as a high-level proxy to measure the average amount of information contained in the questions. As shown by Mudambi and Schuff (2010), the average word count is a suitable proxy for the depth of textual content in the context of user-generated content related to experience goods. Because the depth of content has been shown to have a strong impact on product sales in other related contexts (Chen et al. 2014, Mudambi and Schuff 2010), it is important to study whether such a connection is salient for questions. In addition, if this measure does indeed have a significant effect, it could imply that consumers consider questions carefully and pay attention to related details. Our fourth variable of interest is the depth of answers, which we operationalize by using the average answer word count. We use this variable as a measure to test whether consumers pay attention to details of answers and study their content closely. Although questions are typically neutral in terms of their sentiment, answers could convey positive or negative sentiments. To further investigate the impact of textual content, we perform a sentiment analysis of all answers, so we may assign each answer a sentiment score.

Our final variable of interest captures whether questions are answered or not. Specifically, we are interested in how the fraction of questions with at least one answer affects product sales. This variable represents how much of the potential uncertainty raised by other customers has already been addressed through the Q&A system.

## 4. Empirical Context and Exploratory Analysis

In this section, we describe our research context and the data used in the study, including the nature and textual content of questions and answers.

## 4.1. Empirical Context

We collect data from two major American online retailers, both of which provide platforms for their customers to exchange opinions about products. The primary platform we study is Amazon.com, which offers both an online review system as well as a Q&A system (“Amazon Answer”) for each product. Henceforth, we refer to Amazon.com as “platform A.” To identify the effect of questions and answers on product sales on platform A, we also collect data from a second online retailer (henceforth, “platform B”) that offers an online review and rating system for each product but not a Q&A system. We use data from platform B to control for unobserved product and time characteristics that might influence product sales. As we collect historical data for the same products on both platforms, we can leverage our difference-in-differences approach to eliminate any product-platform effects that do not change over time. This practice has been widely used to study the economic impact of user-generated content, specifi cally online reviews (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Zhu and Zhang 2010, Sun 2012).

Platform B is a large retailer with more than a billion dollars in revenue over each of the last five years. This retailer, which has both a brick and mortar and online presence, operates in several countries, including the United States. We collect data from its main website, which primarily serves customers in the United States. Although platform B’s U.S.-based website is considered a direct competitor of platform A, platform B’s website only offers a standard online review system. Hence, we are able to use the data from platform B to control for the effect of online reviews, ratings, and external shocks common to products on both platforms when studying the impact of Q&As on product sales.

We use video games as the focal product for our study for the following reasons.<sup>3</sup> First, video games serve as a good example of experience goods. The literature has argued that unlike the case with search goods, evaluating the features of experience goods is not easy without consuming the products themselves (Nelson 1970). Information provided by other customers about their experience with such goods through online reviews and Q&As helps reduce uncertainty customers may experience before making a purchase. Therefore, user-generated content regarding experience goods is generally more important to customers compared with that regarding search goods (Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). Second, most video games have unique identifiers. Hence, matching products across two platforms to perform difference-in-differences analysis can be executed more accurately and efficiently. Third, video games typically have a reasonably long product life cycle, so the data set contains products with different ages, unlike some other categories of experience goods (e.g., laptop computers). For these reasons, many studies have chosen video games as their focal product category (Zhu and Zhang 2010, Kim et al. 2021).

We use an automated script to collect our data and obtain a list of products in the video game category on the websites from both retailers. Then, for each product, the script collects product characteristics, review variables, and Q&A variables (the Q&A is only available on platform A). In addition, for each product, the script also acquires time-varying product details, such as sales ranks and prices on a daily basis. ## 5. Empirical Model and Results

In this section, we investigate the relationship between Q&As and product sales. We first present our empirical model specification in Section 5.1. This model specification is widely used in the literature facing identification settings similar to ours (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Sun 2012). We then empirically investigate Hypotheses 1a, 1b, 3a, 3b, and 5 using the main specification. Thereafter, we present the results of a series of extended empirical analyses to investigate Hypotheses 2 and 4, so we may better understand the mechanisms through which Q&A impact sales.

## 5.1. Model Specification

The primary objective of this study is to examine the impact of Q&As on product sales. In this subsection, we specify an econometric model that allows us to identify such relationships. It is important to note that the econometric model used in this study must address the issue of endogeneity due to omitted variables (i.e., some product characteristics such as product quality are typically unobservable to researchers but could be correlated with sales), simultaneity (i.e., product sales may concurrently affect Q&A variables), and reverse causality (i.e., product sales may influence Q&A variables). To address these identification issues, we adopt an empirical model based on a difference-in-differences framework, which is widely used in previous studies that face similar identification problems and use similar data sets (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Sun 2012). The specification of the model is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Delta \Delta l n (s _ {j t}) & = \beta_ {1} \Delta l n (P r i c e _ {j t} ^ {A}) + \beta_ {2} \Delta l n (P r i c e _ {j t} ^ {B}) + \beta_ {3} \Delta (R _ {j, t - 1} ^ {A}) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {4} \Delta (R _ {j, t - 1} ^ {B}) + \beta_ {5} \Delta (Q A _ {j, t - 1} ^ {A}) + \epsilon_ {j t}, \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

in which $s _ { j t }$ is the sales of product j at time t. Also, $P r i c e _ { j t }$ is the price of the product, $R _ { j , t - 1 }$ is the set of review variables, and $Q A _ { j , t - 1 }$ is the set of Q&A variables for product j at time t. Meanwhile, A and B denote the platform that corresponds to these variables. The dependent variable (sales) is differenced across both platforms and time, whereas the independent variables are differenced across time only. Details regarding the micro foundation of Equation (1) are provided in Section B of the online appendix.

This model is developed using the data on both platforms to establish casual inference. For instance, there could be unobservable variables such as product popu larity that influence both product sales and Q&A vari ables. However, such product-time effects should be similar across the two platforms simultaneously, and hence we eliminate them in the model (i.e., the increase in sales based on these factors is similar in both plat forms A and B and hence will even out when differenced across platforms). Therefore, this feature helps address endogeneity concerns in the identification process. In addition, we eliminate product-platform fixed effects (i.e., product characteristics that vary across platforms but remain the same over time for the same product) by differencing the data across time. Review variables $R _ { j , t - 1 }$ and Q&A variables $Q A _ { j , t - 1 }$ are lagged to alleviate concerns about simultaneity and reverse causality (i.e., sales at time t are unlikely to influence review and Q&A variables at time $t - 1 ) . ^ { 5 }$

5.3.1. Answer Sentiment. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that the sentiment of textual content can impact readers’ thought processes (Singh et al. 2015, Mahi et al. 2020). Nevertheless, we note in Section 4.2.2 that the questions in platform A are overwhelmingly neutral. Meanwhile, unlike the case with the questions, we observe more variation in the sentiment of the answers. As such, we use an approach used in the text mining literature (Gonc¸alves et al. 2013, Wang and Cardie 2016) in which the sentiment of each answer is used as a proxy to measure whether the answer provides a positive or negative signal, a construct that is related to our theoretical framework regarding consumers’ perceived uncertainty. Doing so enables us to empirically test Hypothesis 4 by separately measuring the effect of answers with positive and negative sentiment.

We then separately estimate two regression models based on the specification in Equation (1): one for answers with positive or neutral sentiments and one for answers with negative sentiments. We report our results in Table 10, where model (1) includes positive and neutral answers and model (2) includes negative answers. ## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

In recent years, many online marketplaces such as New-Egg in the United States and Lazada in Asia have offered a feature to allow consumers to ask and answer questions regarding products directly on product pages. The characteristics of Q&A in these commercial settings are significantly different from those of Q&A in noncommercial settings, and the nature of Q&A is also different from that of online reviews. As a result, academics and platform managers currently lack empirical evidence that allows them to clearly and confidently establish potential economic implications of Q&A. Motivated by this gap in the literature, we use an econometric model to identify the impact of Q&As on product sales in the context of an online commerce platform.

Through our empirical analysis, we find many interesting results regarding the effect of Q&A systems on sales. For instance, we find that, although a larger number of quality-oriented questions negatively affects sales, an increase in the number of positive answers to such questions actually positively affects sales, whereas negative answers exacerbate the negative effect. In terms of fit-oriented questions, detailed, longer answers help consumers understand product fit and reduce fit uncertainty, as consumers pay attention to the details of such answers rather than rely solely on overall statistics. Furthermore, all other Q&A values being the same, an increase in the fraction of fit-oriented questions with answers (questions that receive at least one answer) significantly increases sales.

Although the primary dependent variable of this study is sales rank (due to data limitations), the estimated influence of Q&As can be quantified by extrapolating the relationship between sales rank and product quantity sold on Amazon, as established in Brynjolfsson et al. (2003). Based on Equation (12), for an average product in our data set, the coefficient of the fraction of questions with answers from Table 9 can be extrapolated as an increase of approximately 1.59 units sold per a 1% increase in the fraction of questions with answers for that product. Similarly, for the coefficient of average answer word count, the effect size can be estimated as an increase of approximately 1.64 units sold per one additional answer word count for a given product.

Our study offers relevant and practical managerial insights. As many emerging online marketplaces have started to adopt a Q&A feature, we demonstrate that such a system has direct economic implications for sellers. For example, our results caution platform managers to be mindful of potential unintended consequences of a Q&A system, as we find that unanswered qualityoriented questions can in fact negatively impact product sales. Therefore, a Q&A system on an online shopping platform must be carefully planned and executed, not only to support users who answer questions, but also to encourage users (i.e., through appropriate nudging and incentive mechanisms) to answer questions, especially those with no answers. In addition, sellers must also be prudent in responding to questions on the platform, so they may alleviate potential negative effects from unanswered questions. Sellers are likely able to reduce buyer uncertainty by providing clear, well-structured answers to customer questions. Furthermore, the positive impact of Q&As on product sales at the individual product level could imply that platforms as a whole also benefit from well-designed and implemented Q&A systems. For instance, in an experiment to efficiently display questions and answers, platform managers could prioritize displaying fit-related questions with sufficient positive answers to potential buyers. Overall, our study also contributes to the literature on the economics of information systems, specifically in the contexts of online discussions and e-commerce, since we are among the first to offer empirical evidence for the economic value of an online Q&A system.

We conclude our work by discussing some limitations and potential avenues for future research. Our study uses sales rank as a proxy for actual product sales as it is available to the public. Although the literature has established that sales rank tends to have a log-linear relationship with sales, it would be ideal to use product sales as the main outcome variable. Second, another valuable opportunity for future research would be to study the behavioral side of Q&A systems, (i.e., studies that develop consumer behavior theories on how consumers incorporate questions and answers into their purchase decision processes). Finally, we study the impact of Q&As on sales for experience goods; while we find qualitatively similar results for electronics as examples of search goods without control data, conducting an in-depth analysis of the impact of Q&As on sales for search goods using control data could offer additional insights.
