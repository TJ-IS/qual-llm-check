# Attending to Customer Attention: A Novel Deep Learning Method for Leveraging Multimodal Online Reviews to Enhance Sales Prediction

Abstract. Although the impacts of helpful online reviews on customers’ purchase decisions and product sales have been widely investigated, review helpfulness has been commonly measured relying on quantitative indicators at the review level. Helpful reviews qualified by such simple indicators, however, may not necessarily yield accurate sales predictions, owing to the ever-evolving review information quality, customer demand, and product attributes. Positing that reviews with higher customer attention should be more influential to customers purchase intention and product sales, we propose to leverage customer attention to better realize the potential of multimodal reviews for sales prediction. We conceptualize customer attention at the holistic review set, review subset, individual review, and review element levels, respectively, and induce four indicators of customer attention, that is, timeliness, semantic diversity, voting awareness, and varying multimodal interaction. We then propose a deep learning method, which incorporates these customer attention indicators using neural network attention mechanisms specifically designed for multimodal review–based sales prediction. Empirical evaluation based on a large data set in a case study predicting hotel sales (specifically, monthly occupancy rate) shows that, in terms of both prediction performance and representation learning performance, our proposed method outperformed benchmarked state-of-the-art deep learning methods.

## 1. Introduction

Online reviews (reviews for short herein) have become one of the most effectual information channels that bridge customers and their targeted products (services) (Yin et al. 2014). Many studies have provided theoretical and empirical evidence that reviews affect customers purchase intention (Forman et al. 2008, Li et al. 2019). Such an indispensable role of reviews for customers endows reviews with promising potential for revealing future sales (Zhu and Zhang 2010). Sales prediction, as a focal problem of operational researchers and practitioners, is conducive to retailers’ product operation and business value creation (Yu et al. 2012). Unlike conventional quantitative features for sales prediction, such as a product’s internal attributes (e.g., product type and historical sales) and environmental factors (e.g., market situation and holiday effect) (Tsoumakas 2019), reviews open up an avenue for unraveling the future trend of sales from the perspective of customers’ attitude toward and demand for the product.

As many studies have shown substantial impact of reviews on product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Forman et al. 2008, Zhu and Zhang 2010, van Nguyen et al. 2020), a straightforward way to realize reviewbased sales prediction is to incorporate all available review information with respect to a product, such as quantitative indicators (e.g., ratings and votes) and textual descriptions, into the prediction model. This, how ever, will inevitably incur predictive bias as only a small portion of reviews can be actually perceived and internalized by customers within a certain period (Li and Hitt 2008, Moe and Schweidel 2012), let alone some useless reviews (Qahri-Saremi and Montazemi 2019). On the other hand, although prior research has made several attempts at review-based sales prediction (Yu et al. 2012, van Nguyen et al. 2020), their provided solution approaches suffered serious deficiencies. From the data perspective, they commonly used unstructured reviews in quantitative ways. Textual reviews’ valence, extremity, and helpfulness votes have all been extensively investigated (Sahoo et al. 2018), whereas the predictive values of the semantic information conveyed in review texts and the visual information embedded in review images remain largely under-investigated. From the model perspective, original reviews were taken by a prediction model without effective differentiation, impeding the proper play of their predictive role.

Indeed, sales performance is inherently rooted in customers’ purchase intention, which is sensitive to customer’ past experiences and opinions conveyed by reviews (Sahoo et al. 2018). Furthermore, customers are more likely to be affected by the reviews they read carefully, whereas reviews that receive little scrutiny are less likely to affect customers’ purchase intention. Therefore, reviews that attract intensive customer attention<sup>1</sup> can be naturally expected to be more influential to customers purchase decisions and have more predictive capabilities on sales. In this vein, it is sensible to explore what kinds of reviews attract more customer attention and whether such reviews are more useful for sales prediction.

The value of reviews to customers has been assessed by information quality (Wang and Strong 1996), argument quality (Liu et al. 2020), and review helpfulness (Forman et al. 2008). There is a consensus that more helpful reviews tend to be perceived to have higher quality and exert greater impact on customers’ purchase intention (Filieri et al. 2018, Yin et al. 2020). Helpful reviews qualified by content-based indicators (e.g., valence, objectivity, and intelligibility), albeit with their considerable diagnostic values for customers’ product evaluation (Filieri 2015), may not necessarily be the most influential of the purchase intention of the customer base. This is because customers with heterogeneous preferences naturally favor reviews with different content characteristics (Moe and Schweidel 2012), and thus it is difficult to ascertain which content styles will receive customer attention steadily. The same review content can be differently or even conversely evaluated due to customers’ differential characteristics. There have been conflicting findings regarding review helpfulness; for example, negative reviews can exert both positive and hurtful effects on sales (Lee et al. 2008, Berger et al. 2010), and longer reviews can be both more and less helpful (Fink et al. 2018).

Customers’ attention paid to multimodal reviews is reflected in their browsing behaviors, which can be naturally characterized along the temporal and spatial dimensions. That is, the two dimensions coordinately depict the transitions of customer’s browsing attention over granularity levels of multimodal reviews and dif ferent stages. From a process perspective, customers attention and evaluation efforts paid to reviews can be understood over two stages of information processing, according to the message learning (Riley 1954) and infor mation processing (McGuire 1968) theories. Specifically, given a set of reviews, customers would first decide what kinds of reviews to pay attention to (i.e., the recep tion stage) and then comprehend the ones they heed to reach their product evaluation (i.e., the yielding stage). Although the aforementioned content-based helpful ness indicators indeed partially account for the yielding stage (Weathers et al. 2015), with the expectation that review characteristics good for diagnosticity will be more favorably evaluated (Kuan et al. 2015), they do not capture customers’ initial attention to reviews in the reception stage. In this regard, we posit that when faced with a vast number of reviews, customers are unlikely to evaluate all reviews by their diagnostic values. Rather, in practical scenarios, customers tend to only attend to a review subset to fulfill product evaluation while forgoing the others regardless of their helpfulness. This posit is consistent with the previous finding that review readers, with their limited attention spans, are unlikely to go through every review (Kuan et al. 2015). Therefore, we propose that customers’ initial attention to reviews, as an important prerequisite to their follow-up evaluation, should be attended to in sales prediction.

From a spatial perspective, we, conforming to customers’ browsing behaviors along a top-down (or bottom-up) roadmap, decompose their attention into fou granularity levels, that is, the holistic review set, review subset (along the temporal or topic dimension), individual review, and review element (modality) levels, respectively. For example, the process that customers drill down from the holistic review set to useful elementary review information (i.e., the top-down case) may span granularity levels from the holistic review set level all the way to the review element level. Given the reviews of a product, it is also possible that a customer would start at a certain information type of a review (e.g., release date, number of votes, text, or image) and then expand from it to more reviews successively (i.e., the bottom-up case). Overall, we strive to unravel customer attention with a process-granularity framework (elaborated in Section 3) and delve into our first research question (RQ1): What kinds of reviews conform to customer attention paid at different granularity levels in the purchase decision process?

On the ground of theoretical analysis (presented in Section 3), we posit that customers are likely to pay their timeliness attention, semantic diversity attention, votingawareness attention, and attention for multimodal interaction at the four granularity levels (from the holistic review set level to the review element level). At the holistic review set level, we, drawing on the dual-process theory (Groves and Thompson 1970), posit that customer attention is likely to dynamically focus on a set of timely reviews rather than being time invariant. At the topic level, studies concerning selection effect show that customers with heterogeneous preferences focus on diverse product attributes, causing their actually attended and perceived helpful reviews to differ from each other (Li and Hitt 2008, Moe and Schweidel 2012). At the review level, we consider the number of helpfulness votes, one of the most prevalent review helpfulness indicators that are likely to attract customers’ attention and comprehension efforts strongly (Mudambi and Schuff 2010, Kuan et al. 2015, Salehan and Kim 2016). At the review element level, we focus on the interactive role of heterogeneous review modalities in dispersing customer attention because typical reviews simultaneously contain textual descriptions and images. Based on the elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986), we posit that when customers fortify their purchase decisions by virtue of reviews, they may pay asymmetrical attention to texts versus images.

Overall, our induced indicators of customer attention include (1) timeliness, (2) semantic diversity, (3) review helpfulness, and (4) varying multimodal interaction.<sup>2</sup> Furthermore, given the prevalence and ubiquity of multimodal reviews<sup>3</sup> nowadays, an even more imminent research question (RQ2) is as follows: How can we leverage customer attention to enhance the effectiveness of multimodal reviews for sales prediction? In response to RQ2, we propose a deep learning method named DTV-AMI (for DTV attention-enhanced deep learning with adaptive attention for multimodal interaction) specifically designed for multimodal review–based sales prediction. DTV-AMI draws its strength from two components, that is, DTV (for semantic diversity, timeliness, and voting awareness attention mechanisms) and AMI (for adaptive attention mechanism for multimodal interaction), which are constructed based on four neural network attention mechanisms corresponding to the four customer attention indicators.

Specifically, our proposed DTV allows the prediction model to attend more to timely, semantically diverse, and voted reviews. Given a sequence of reviews for sales prediction, DTV deduces customer attention by recursively taking each review as a potential starting point of customers’ browsing behaviors, in response to the fact that customers arrive successively rather than at one stroke. While scanning the reviews dynamically, DTV attends more to timely, semantically diverse, and voted reviews. The proposed AMI further empowers the prediction model to make an adaptive tradeoff (i.e., weighting) between review text and images (if any) at each review point and differentiate such adaptive tradeoffs across different reviews, conforming to customers heterogeneous browsing behaviors. Overall, DTV-AMI incorporates our induced customer attention indicators to capitalize on multimodal reviews’ due predictive power on sales. Experiment results in a case study of sales<sup>4</sup> (specifically, monthly occupancy rate) prediction in the hotel industry with 2,685 hotels and their 1.2 million review texts and 197 thousand review images show that DTV-AMI outperformed benchmarked deep learning methods, demonstrating the value of customer attention for better leveraging multimodal reviews for sales prediction.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Impact of Multimodal Reviews on Product Sales

The reason reviews can impact product sales lies in that customers’ purchase decisions are considerably affected by a wealth of product-specific opinions conveyed by reviews (Wang and Strong 1996, Kang and Zhou 2017). However, the beneficial role of reviews for customers does not necessarily hold due to the heterogeneous information quality of reviews (Mudambi and Schuff 2010). One key factor to unravel the influential mechanism of reviews on product sales is review helpfulness, which plays a mediating role between the impact of reviews on customers’ purchase intention (Filieri et al. 2018). The helpfulness of a review is contingent on the extent to which it can be perceived by customers to facil itate their purchase decision process (Yin et al. 2014). Therefore, helpful reviews are commonly expected to have higher impact on customers’ demand and product sales (Berger et al. 2010, Sahoo et al. 2018).

The proliferation of reviews has stimulated lots of research interests around review helpfulness, such as determinants of review helpfulness (Mudambi and Schuff 2010, Kuan et al. 2015) and review helpfulness prediction (Lee et al. 2018, Ma et al. 2018). Forman et al. (2008) examined the relationship between reviews and sales by evaluating review helpfulness and concluded that reviews’ source credibility is positively associated with their helpfulness and product sales. Zhu and Zhang (2010) demonstrated that the impact of reviews on product sales is moderated by the characteristics of the product and customers, indicating the inadequacy of solely relying on simple helpfulness indicators for sales prediction. Kuan et al. (2015) demonstrated that voted reviews signify remarkable diagnostic values (helpfulness) but are negatively correlated with sales.

In contrast to numeric helpfulness indicators, textual reviews generally provide rich details for customers to evaluate whether the specific product attributes match their own preferences (Buschken and Allenby 2016). Especially when the ratings across products are similar, the role of textual reviews for revealing customers’ purchase decisions and sales trend becomes even stronger (Sahoo et al. 2018). To uncover the impact of review text on product returns, Sahoo et al. (2018) quantified textual reviews by text length and fraction of reviews voted helpful. Berger et al. (2010), after investigating the valence of textual reviews, argued that negative reviews can increase purchase likelihood and sales by improving customers’ product awareness. Archak et al. (2011) identified textual reviews as an important determinant of customers’ choices and placed a higher premium on their impact on sales compared with numeric helpfulness indicators.

As is well recognized, “a picture is worth a thousand words” because it can provide informational, aesthetic, and self-enhancement values simultaneously (Li and Xie 2020). Likewise, image reviews offer customers a visual information channel to evaluate products more vividly (Zhang and Mao 2012). Karimi and Wang (2017) examined the impact of reviewers’ profile images on review helpfulness and reported that the visual decoration offered by profile images enhances customers’ perception and evaluation of review helpfulness. Ma et al. (2018) explored the effect of customer-generated images on review helpfulness and verified their values for indicating review helpfulness. They also claimed that images alone do not provide sufficient cues for customers to make effective judgements about the product. Recently, Wu et al. (2021) confirmed that images add more diagnostic value to reviews and hybrid reviews (i.e., reviews with both textual information and images) can be perceived more helpful than text-only reviews.

## 2.2. Review-Based Sales Prediction

Sales has been conventionally predicted with data from the perspectives of retailers and the macro-market, such as retailers’ historical sales trend, product promotion, and market environmental factors (e.g., competition environment and government policy) (Tsoumakas 2019). Such data certainly shed light on products’ future sales, but they only reflect customers’ demand indirectly without enough regularity, limiting their predictive capabilities for sales (Wu and Brynjolfsson 2015). Reviews, with their indispensable diagnostic values, exert substantial impact on customers’ purchase intention and thus can be leveraged to reveal product sales from the perspective of customers’ demand. Yu et al. (2010) explored reviews for predicting sales performance and found that the quality of and sentiment in textual reviews have significant impact on sales. Chen et al. (2017) performed sentiment analysis using review texts for sales prediction and found that textual reviews produce better summary of customer opinions than rating scores. Archak et al. (2011) argued that quantitative features are insufficient to capture the multifaceted values of review information, whereas textual reviews enable the learning of customers’ preferences for different product features and therefore can be used to enhance sales prediction.

Zhao et al. (2019) predicted the customer base’s satisfaction using textual reviews, confirming reviews impact on customers’ purchase intention. van Nguyen et al. (2020) used review sentiment and helpfulness votes for product demand prediction and revealed that sales performance is indeed a sign of customers demand. Li et al. (2019) empirically demonstrated the usefulness of both numeric review indicators and textual reviews for sales prediction. Especially, they found that numeric review indicators and textual reviews do interplay while shaping product sales. Besides, while predicting sales, some studies also suggested the moderating effect of review helpfulness on the relationship between review information and sales (Lee and Choeh 2020).

There have also been some studies that realized the dynamic effects of reviews on sales. Scholz et al. (2018) noticed that the effect of user-generated content on customer purchase behavior changes as other items are newly submitted and proposed a hierarchical model to capture such dynamics. Lu et al. (2018), from a dynamic perspective, argued that the effects of review characteristics on review helpfulness change temporally, whereas high-quality reviews are extremely influential. When leveraging reviews for sales prediction, Yu et al. (2012) adopted time series analysis to capture the temporal relationships among reviews.

## 2.3. Attention Mechanism in Deep Learning Methods

Attention mechanism, an important component of neural networks nowadays, has recently set off an upsurge in diverse research areas, such as natural language processing and computer vision (Vaswani et al. 2017, Chaud hari et al. 2019). It was initially introduced for machine translation tasks (Bahdanau et al. 2014). It is essentially a weight learning scheme that aims to assign and optimize a set of weights corresponding to the input sequence to endow a neural network with the ability to attend more to those salient elements (Xu et al. 2015). Such a mecha nism provides insights into the issue of how to leverage customer attention to enhance sales prediction (RQ2). First, the attention mechanism allows the model to automatically search for parts of its source inputs that are relevant to the prediction task (Galassi et al. 2019). This aligns well with customers’ review browsing behaviors; that is, customers focus selectively on certain reviews they need while neglecting others in their purchase decision process. Second, the attention mechanism offers an interpret ability advantage for the neural networks, in that the way neurons are weighted by attention is just like how fea tures’ importance scores are estimated by a regression model. By various explainable attention compatibility functions, such as similarity (Graves et al. 2014), scaled multiplicative (Vaswani et al. 2017), and additive (Bahdanau et al. 2014) functions, we are aware of which kinds of inputs and how these inputs will be attended to by the model. This notable benefit allows us to design new attention mechanisms specifically tailored to customer attention in our focal context.

Moreover, given the stream nature of multimodal reviews, attention mechanism is powerful and computationally efficient to capture the sequential dependencies by considering the locality among the elements of the input sequence from different perspectives, such as space, time, and semantics (Li et al. 2018). Attention mechanism, with both considerable interpretability and predictive capability, may serve as an effective solution method for modeling customer attention in our context. Customer attention is distinct from the attention mechanism in a neural network. We conceptualize customer attention to reviews, which serves as the motivation for our design of the attention mechanism in DTV-AMI.

## 2.4. Research Gaps

Our review of related studies pinpoints their deficiencies for leveraging customer attention to enhance multimodal review–based sales prediction. First, although prior studies promised great potential of multimodal reviews for revealing product sales (Forman et al. 2008, Berger et al. 2010, Kuan et al. 2015), the predictive capabilities of unstructured textual and (especially) image reviews for sales remain largely under-investigated, and multimodal review–based sales prediction remains an open problem despite its great potential. Second, existing review-based sales prediction methods commonly use textual reviews in quantitative ways and fail to differentiate reviews while using reviews to predict sales. Although recent studies have disentangled the causal impact of review content dimensions on sales conversion (Liu et al. 2019), it is still challenging to leverage richer multimodal review information to enhance sales prediction. To manifest the significance of our proposed DTV-AMI in filling these gaps in the literature, we compare it with major related studies in Table A1 (Online Appendix A).

Third, the compatibility functions of existing attention mechanisms are obviously incompatible with our induced customer attention indicators (presented in Section 3). For example, existing compatibility functions primarily rely on affinity-based strategies,<sup>5</sup> such as similarity (Graves et al. 2014) and dot-product (Luong et al. 2015) functions, and thus tend to attach more importance to those keys that are highly correlated with the target query. As a result, existing attention mechanisms cannot effectively leverage multimodal reviews in ways customers receive and process them for sales prediction, thus calling for novel attention mechanisms conforming to customer attention specifically in this context. Specifically, semantic diversity in customer attention requires nonredundant keys that are incompatible with (complementary to) the target query to be more highlighted. Customers’ timeliness attention and voting awareness attention require the prediction model to focus more on timely and voted keys (i.e., reviews). Customers’ adaptive attention for multimodal reviews urges the prediction model to make an adaptive tradeoff (i.e., weighting) between review text and images. Resolving these meth odological requirements and challenges constitutes the contribution of our designed DTV-AMI. To differentiate our proposed DTV-AMI from popular existing attention mechanisms, we compare them in Table A2 (Online Appendix A).

## 3. Positioning, Design Rationales, and Methodological Challenges

Our aim in this study is to better leverage multimodal reviews in ways customers receive and process them for sales prediction. As this study falls in the design science paradigm (Hevner et al. 2004), a sound design rationale, as a foundation for artifact design and evaluation, is important for establishing a design science contribution (Gregor and Hevner 2013). The design rationales for our attention mechanisms are four customer attention indicators, which stem from a process-granularity framework. By expounding the process-granularity framework, we endeavor to address three fundamental questions: (1) how to induce the indicators, (2) why they are adequate for revealing customers’ attention paid to multimodal reviews, and (3) how they guide the design of attention mechanisms accordingly.

Figure 1 shows the process-granularity framework, outlining customers’ browsing behaviors regarding multimodal reviews along the process and granularity di mensions. From a process perspective, we draw on the message learning (Riley 1954) and information processing (McGuire 1968) theories and posit that customers process reviews as information through two stages, that is, determining what kind of reviews to attend to in the reception stage and then evaluating the attended information to decide whether to accept it in the yielding stage (Kuan et al. 2015). On this premise, customers’ attention, in the reception stage, is key and fundamental for leveraging those influential reviews, which have really affected customers’ purchase intention, to enhance sales prediction.

From a granularity perspective, the procedures by which multimodal reviews are delivered to customers involve successive levels, from the holistic review set level to review element (text or image) level and vice versa. Specifically, when customers begin with a certain product among others, they are actually anchored in a holistic review set (i.e., holistic review set granularity) attached to this product. Besides, while they click in the product page, they are first presented with a recent review subset (i.e., review subset granularity along the temporal dimension) due to the limited space of the page. Then, customers disperse to drill down to their preferred review topics (i.e., review subset granularity along the topic dimension) via the overall review categorization labels. In this way, they come to a list of qualified multimodal reviews and decide which ones (i.e., single review granularity) to go through in detail. Furthermore, when clicking on a specific review, they place their tendentious focus on either the review text or the review images (i.e., review element granularity). All these steps occur successively before concrete review content can be scrutinized, and thus the customer attention they attract is largely content free.

Although a considerable number of content-based indicators (e.g., accuracy, length, valence, and objectivity) have been induced for quantifying the usefulness of review information to customers in the yielding stage (Wang and Strong 1996, Liu et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2021), we give particular consideration to content-free customer attention in the reception stage. This is because customers with heterogeneous preferences may attend to various content styles, for example, long versus short, positive versus negative, and subjective versus objective. It is difficult to affirm which content styles will receive customer attention steadily. Instead, our induced content-free indicators are in conformity with customers’ attention in the first place and thus can be brought into a unified attention framework to position influential reviews. At the same time, review contents can be leveraged by standard deep learning methods naturally. We expound the four customer attention indicators, along with the correspond ing design choices, as follows.

(1) Timeliness Attention. The dual-process theory (Groves and Thompson 1970) posits that customers’ decision making is accomplished through the joint action of two kinds of processes, namely, the fast and automatic process (recollection) that focuses on simple cues related to environmental factors, such as information quantity and information completeness (Luo et al. 2018), regardless of contextual details, and the slow and conscious process (familiarity) that scrutinizes a selected subset of relevant information. This theory essentially stresses the threshold of cognition for customers’ decisions (Wixted 2007). Faced with the holistic review set, it is rational to expect that outdated reviews will be delivered to the fast and automatic process while the latest reviews will be more attended to by customers. Therefore, we incorporate review timeliness in indicating customer attention.

The timeliness attention of customers guides our design of the timeliness attention mechanism. Customers do not always start at the latest review. Rather, each review point from the review sequence can potentially act as a target query, raising the need for a global attention mechanism (i.e., allowing the query to match the keys globally). The timeliness customer attention implies a time-decaying effect on a review’s utility. As time goes on, the review timeliness changes accordingly, so it is necessary to weight reviews by their chronological order, conforming to the ever-evolving information query requirements of customers. Hence, we design an attention mechanism that can dynamically attach larger weights to more recent reviews and generate decaying weights for reviews as time goes on in response to customers’ timeliness attention.

(2) Semantic Diversity Attention. Prior research has confirmed the existence of selection effect in customers posting and reading behaviors regarding reviews (Li and Hitt 2008). Both reviewers and customer readers hold heterogeneous preferences, which are subject to differentiation effect (Moe and Schweidel 2012). As reviewers highlight their preferred product attributes respectively, they produce a semantically diverse set of reviews. As customer readers take advantage of reviews they prefer, they jointly highlight a set of reviews that are semantically diverse and collectively give a comprehensive assessment on the product. Hence, the semantic diversity within a set of reviews should be an indispensable part of customer attention.

The semantic diversity attention of customers guides our design of the semantic diversity attention mechanism. Existing attention mechanisms are primarily equipped with affinity-based compatibility functions (as shown in Table A2 in the online appendix), which tend to attach more importance to mutually correlated reviews and thus result in semantic redundancy among attended reviews. The semantic diversity customer attention requires each query<sup>6</sup> to attend more to reviews that are incompatible with (complementary to) the query. Hence, we design an attention mechanism that can guide the prediction model to assimilate semantically diverse reviews in response to customers’ semantic diversity attention.

(3) Voting Awareness Attention. From the perspective of a single review, helpful reviews, with their higher diagnostic values and persuasiveness, are likely to attract more customer attention and exert greater impact on customers’ purchase decisions during the yielding stage. The number of helpfulness votes on a review, one of the most ubiquitous indicators for review helpfulness (Mudambi and Schuff 2010; Yin et al. 2014, 2021), is a good signal for customer attention at the individual review level. In line with prior studies (Kuan et al. 2015, Gang and Taeho 2019), we posit that voted reviews may draw more customer attention.

The voting awareness attention of customers guides our design of the voting awareness attention mechanism. The voting awareness customer attention requires reviews with more votes to be more intensively attended to. Accordingly, we design an attention mechanism that encourages the model to give higher weights to voted reviews in response to customers’ voting awareness attention.

(4) Varying Attention for Text-Image Interaction. Review text and images inherently play different roles in influencing customers’ purchase decisions. The descriptions offered by textual reviews provide rich insights into customers’ opinions toward the product (Yu et al. 2012, Yin et al. 2021), whereas the visual cues from review images can vividly depict the product within various real scenarios (Ma et al. 2018, Li and Xie 2020). The elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986) inspires us that, depending on the elaboration degree, there are two routes for customers to engage in the elaboration of multimodal reviews, that is, the central and peripheral routes. The central route is always achieved by customers’ thoughtful examination, whereas the peripheral route is often resulted from customers low involvement, for example, using simple decision rules for information evaluation (O’Keefe 2008). Having noted both the substitution (Cook and Lalljee 1972) and complementary (Muller et al. 2004) effects of text-image interaction, we posit that the text and images in a review are possibly processed in either the central route or the peripheral route, depending on whether (and to what extent) the specific content conveyed by the text (or images) is preferred by customers. Such preferences between the text and images are likely to differ across reviews. Therefore, the varying text-image interaction should be a core influential factor of customer attention at the review element level.

The varying attention for test-image interaction of customers guides our design of the adaptive attention mechanism for multimodal interaction. Customers’ attentions over text versus images should be adaptively varied across reviews to reflect the continuous variation likeli hood of the relative importance threshold between the text and images of a single review. Therefore, we design an attention mechanism that can adaptively assign differential weights to review text and images over reviews, conforming to customers’ adaptive attention to multimodal reviews. AMI is designed to combine modalities at the time-step level to conform to customer attention and is hence fundamentally different from common multimodal deep learning methods (Ramachandram and Tay lor 2017), which are developed for multimodal fusion and combine modalities at the modality level.

In summary, leveraging customer attention for multi modal reviews to enhance sales prediction calls for the design of specialized attention mechanisms. Table 1 summarizes our induced customer attention indicators, in terms of their theoretical rationales, methodological challenges, and our proposed solutions. Although some of our induced customer attention indicators (e.g., timeliness and voting awareness) have been conceptually investigated in previous behavioral studies (Kuan et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2020), the attention mechanisms we design based on the customer attention indicators constitute the core design science contribution of this paper.

## 4. Proposed Method

The theoretical rationales of customer attention and the methodological challenges (Table 1) motivate us to design a deep learning method with two mechanisms (DTV and AMI) to effectively leverage customer attention for multimodal review–based sales prediction. The proposed DTV-AMI consolidates (1) timeliness attention, (2) semantic diversity attention, (3) voting awareness attention, and (4) adaptive attention for multimodal interaction within a multimodal deep learning framework.

## 4.1. Overview of DTV-AMI

Figure 2 outlines the framework of DTV-AMI. DTV-AMI first preprocesses texts and images of reviews and extracts multimodal review embeddings using suitable pretrained neural networks. Then, in view of the stream nature of review data, it constructs a multimodal deep learning model with two parallel gated recurrent unit neural networks (i.e., GRUs),<sup>7</sup> for producing the text embedding sequence and the image embedding sequence, respectively. Next, it incorporates two mechanisms, that is, DTV and AMI, into the multimodal deep learning model. Although DTV-AMI outputs predictions directly, its learned multimodal representation features can also serve as inputs in further building downstream, potentially even more accurate prediction models.

## 4.2. Multimodal Review Embedding

4.2.1. Data Collection and Preprocessing. For a more intuitive presentation of the proposed method, without loss of generality, we illustrate its components in the context of a case study, in which we collaborated with a leading hotel $\mathrm { \ g r o u p } ^ { 8 ^ { \cdot } }$ in China and were tasked to predict the sales (specifically, occupancy rate) of each chained hotel in each month based on multimodal review data. Given a list of hotels, we collect and clean the data as follows. First, we collect the hotels’ multimodal (i.e., quantitative, textual, and image) review data. Then, for each review, we collect the review text along with its images and quantitative indicators (number of votes and release date). Besides, we discard the reviews whose text length (number of words) is shorter than five. If a review only contains images (text), we take it as a pure-image (plain-text) review, which can also be incorporated by our method. Next, for each hotel-month, we sort its valid reviews within a certain time span $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ a quarter, half a year, or a year) prior to the predicted month in chronological order and thereby obtain a review sequence, with three possibilities, that is, plain-text, pure-image, and text with images, for each data point. Finally, we filter out the hotel-months (prediction instances) whose review sequence length (number of reviews) is shorter than ten or whose occupancy is zero.

4.2.2. Bilevel Sequence Padding Mask. Padding mask is an operation commonly used in deep learning models, such as attention mechanism (Vaswani et al. 2017) and BERT (bidirectional encoder representations from transformers) (Devlin et al. 2018), to shape the input sequences into the same length and dimensionality. It generally proceeds in two stages. In the first (padding) stage, all input sequences are padded to a fixed length, by truncating the sequence elements beyond the fixed length and padding the sequence positions shorter than the fixed length with zeroes (Dwarampudi and Reddy 2019). With the mask strategy in the second stage, the manually padded values will be excluded from training and testing (Song et al. 2020).

To better present the padding mask operation specific to our model context, we first introduce the standard selfattention mechanism (Vaswani et al. 2017). Consider an input (text or image) review sequence $\mathbf { c } = [ \mathbf { c } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { c } _ { 2 } , \ldots ,$ $\mathbf { c } _ { L } \mathbf { \dot { l } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \dot { d } _ { c } \times L }$ , where L and $d _ { c }$ are the sequence length and feature space dimensionality, respectively. Let $\mathbf { m } = [ m _ { 1 } , m _ { 2 } , \ldots , m _ { L _ { F } } ] ^ { T } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { L _ { F } }$ be the L -dimensional mask template vector, where $L _ { F }$ is the fixed sequence length. Denote the keys, values, and queries (the three basic components in attention mechanisms corresponding to sequence c) as $\mathbf { K } = [ \mathbf { k } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { k } _ { 2 } , \ldots , \mathbf { k } _ { L _ { F } } ] \in$ $\mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { k } \times L _ { F } }$ ${ \bf V } = [ { \bf v } _ { 1 } , { \bf v } _ { 2 } , \ldots , { \bf v } _ { L _ { F } } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { v } \times L _ { F } }$ , and $\mathbf { Q } = [ \mathbf { q } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { q } _ { 2 } , \dots ,$ $\mathbf { q } _ { L _ { F } } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { k } \times L _ { F } } .$ , with equal feature space dimensionality for k and q. Then, the standard self-attention mechanism makes an adaptive use of the input sequence ${ \mathfrak { c } } ^ { 9 }$ by dynamically learning a weight vector $\mathbf { \Psi } \equiv \mathbb { R } ^ { L _ { F } }$ over time step (sequence position) iterations. At the jth step iteration, self-attention derives its corresponding weight vector $\pmb { \alpha } _ { j } = [ \alpha _ { 1 } ^ { j } , \alpha _ { 2 } ^ { j } , \dots , \alpha _ { L _ { F } } ^ { j } ]$ by the scaled-multiplicative compatibility function $f ( q _ { j } , { \bf K } ) = { \bf q } _ { j } ^ { T } { \bf K } / \sqrt { d _ { k } } ;$

$$
\pmb {\alpha} _ {j} = \mathrm{softmax} \left(\frac {\mathbf {q} _ {j} ^ {T} \mathbf {K}}{\sqrt {d _ {k}}}\right),\tag{1}
$$

$$
\alpha_ {i} ^ {j} = \frac {e ^ {\mathbf {q} _ {j} ^ {T} \mathbf {k} _ {i} / \sqrt {d _ {k}}}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {L _ {F}} e ^ {\mathbf {q} _ {j} ^ {T} \mathbf {k} _ {i} / \sqrt {d _ {k}}}}.\tag{2}
$$

Furthermore, by incorporating the predefined mask template vector m into the softmax operation, the impact of the padded sequence elements on model predictions will be effectively eliminated. That is,

$$
\tilde {\alpha} _ {i} ^ {j} = \frac {e ^ {\mathbf {q} _ {j} ^ {T} \mathbf {k} _ {i} / \sqrt {d _ {k}} + m _ {i}}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {L _ {F}} e ^ {\mathbf {q} _ {j} ^ {T} \mathbf {k} _ {i} / \sqrt {d _ {k}} + m _ {i}}},\tag{3}
$$

where $m _ { i } = \left\{ \begin{array} { c } { { - \infty , i f p a d d e d { \bf c } _ { i } } } \\ { { 0 , o t h e r w i s e } } \end{array} \right.$ , such that the weights of padded inputs will be zeroed while the weights of the others can be trained normally.

In our multimodal review–based sales prediction context, we perform a bilevel sequence padding mask. At the individual review level, in case of single-type reviews, we perform the padding mask operation on the bimodal (text and image) review sequences to tackle the modality missing (i.e., sequence positions with either pure image or plain text) to adapt them to the subsequent multimodal deep learning. At the hotel-month level, we perform the padding mask operation on instances<sup>10</sup> (corresponding to hotel-months) in view of their unequal sequence lengths. After preprocessing and pad ding mask, we obtain textual $( \bar { \mathbf { X } ^ { ( D ) } } = \{ x _ { 1 } ^ { ( D ) } , x _ { 2 } ^ { ( \bar { D } ) } , \dots , \bar { x _ { n } ^ { ( D ) } } \} )$ and image $( \mathbf { X } ^ { ( M ) } = \{ x _ { 1 } ^ { ( M ) } , x _ { 2 } ^ { ( M ) } , \dots , x _ { n } ^ { ( M ) } \} )$ ) review sequences.

## 4.3. Model Construction

To better coordinate the text and image sequences for review-based sales prediction, we unify them within a constructed multimodal deep learning model. Because review texts and images have been respectively embedded by their suitable neural networks, we focus on their common time sequence property and use GRU (Cho et al. 2014) as our base model. Specifically, we construct a bimodal GRU (a combination of text GRU and image GRU) corresponding to the bimodal review sequence. Each GRU takes a kind of embedding sequence (each embedding feature in the sequence corresponds to a time step) as its inputs and is trained through recursive time step iterations. At the (T � 1)th time step, the $( T - 1 ) \mathrm { t h }$ text GRU (image GRU) cell converts the input ${ \bf x } _ { T - 1 } ^ { ( D ) } ~ ( { \bf x } _ { T - 1 } ^ { ( M ) } )$ into its last hidden cell states ${ \bf h } _ { T - 1 } ^ { ( D ) } ~ ( { \bf h } _ { T - 1 } ^ { ( M ) } )$ to make a prediction for the current step, using its reset gate, update gate, and output gate. Overall, by training the constructed bimodal GRU and outputting their hidden states, we obtain two parallel sequences of GRU embeddings, namely, the text GRU embedding ${ \bf H } ^ { ( D ) } =$ $[ { \pmb h } _ { 1 } ^ { ( D ) } , { \pmb h } _ { 2 } ^ { ( D ) } , \dots , { \pmb h } _ { L _ { F } } ^ { ( D ) } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { h } \times L _ { F } }$ and the image GRU embedding ${ \bf H } ^ { ( M ) } = [ { \pmb h } _ { 1 } ^ { ( M ) } , { \pmb h } _ { 2 } ^ { ( M ) } , \dots , { \pmb h } _ { L _ { F } } ^ { ( M ) } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { h } \times L _ { F } }$

4.4. DTV-AMI–Based Multimodal Deep Learning Given the bimodal GRU, DTV-AMI synergizes its two neural networks by training them coordinately with the DTV and AMI mechanisms. These mechanisms are designed specifically in response to the methodological challenges (Table 1). DTV (Figure 3) is designed to incorporate customers’ timeliness attention, semantic diversity attention, and voting awareness attention into the learning process of the bimodal GRU to appropriately deliver the multimodal review sequences to sales prediction. DTV takes the multimodal review sequences as its inputs, including the textual $( \mathbf { H } ^ { ( D ) } )$ and image $( \mathbf { H } ^ { ( M ) } )$ ) embedding sequences from the bimodal GRU and the quantitative sequences for review release date $\mathbf { \tau } ( \mathbf { t } = \left[ t _ { 1 } , \right.$ $\hat { t _ { 2 } } , \ldots , t _ { T } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { L _ { F } } )$ and number of votes $( \mathbf { s } = [ s _ { 1 } , s _ { 2 } , \dots ,$ $s _ { L _ { F } } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { L _ { F } } )$

4.4.1. Semantic Diversity Attention. Unlike common attention mechanisms (Vaswani et al. 2017) that match the query with each of the keys directly and indiscriminately, DTV characterizes the semantic diversity based on keys’ interrelationships and their complementarities with the target query, in view of the key-vector’s unique representative role for the input element in the sequence. To this end, we design the semantic diversity attention mechanism based on the L2-norm distance metric, which is usually used to measure the diversity between two vectors (Tian et al. 2017). Through the semantic diversity attention mechanism, the model will attend more intensively to a review that is less redundant with other reviews.

For the ith time step, the semantic diversity attention mechanism can be described as

$$
\overline {{r}} _ {j} = \frac {1}{T - 1} \sum_ {u = 1, u \neq j} ^ {T} \frac {\mathbf {k} _ {j} ^ {T} \mathbf {k} _ {u}}{\sqrt {d _ {k}}}, \overline {{r}} _ {j} \subseteq \overline {{\mathbf {r}}} = [ \overline {{r}} _ {1}, \overline {{r}} _ {2}, \ldots , \overline {{r}} _ {T} ] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {L _ {F}},\tag{4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \beta_ {j} ^ {i} = \frac {e ^ {- \overline {{r}} _ {j}}}{\sum_ {u = 1} ^ {T} e ^ {- \overline {{r}} _ {j}}} \frac {\| \mathbf {q} _ {i} - \mathbf {w} _ {j} \mathbf {k} _ {j} \| _ {2} ^ {2} + \lambda \| \mathbf {w} _ {j} \| _ {2}}{\sqrt {d _ {k}}}, \\ \beta_ {j} ^ {i} \subseteq \boldsymbol {\beta} ^ {i} = [ \beta_ {1} ^ {i}, \beta_ {2} ^ {i}, \ldots , \beta_ {T} ^ {i} ] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {L _ {F}}, \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where $\overline { { r } } _ { j }$ is the average redundancy between the jth key and other $T - 1$ keys, measuring the extent to which the information represented by $\mathbf { k } _ { j }$ can be inferred from other keys. The term $e ^ { - \overline { r } _ { j } } / { \sum _ { u = 1 } ^ { T } e ^ { - \overline { r } _ { j } } }$ in Equation (5) is set to normalize the redundancy vector ${ \overline { { r } } } ,$ whereas $( \| q _ { i } -$ ${ w _ { j } } k _ { j } \lvert | _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } + \lambda \rvert | { w _ { j } } \lvert | _ { 2 } ) / \sqrt { d _ { k } }$ is the L2-norm-based (inverse matching) compatibility function, which helps to highlight the keys complementary to the target query. We introduce $\mathbf { w } _ { j }$ to induce an adaptive query upon the keys for the jth step and use λ to regulate $\| \mathbf { w } _ { j } \| _ { 2 }$ to alleviate overfitting. Note that $f ( \mathbf { q } , \mathbf { k } )$ itself is insufficient to yield a diverse query over keys, given the potential interkey redundancies. Therefore, we adjust $f ( \mathbf { q } , \mathbf { k } )$ with r. Overall, $\pmb { \beta } ^ { i }$ indicates a diversity score distribution over keys, geared to the query $\mathbf { q } _ { i } .$ . That is, candidate keys that are both complementary to the target query and less redundant with each other will receive larger weights.

4.4.2. Timeliness Attention. To realize the timeliness attention mechanism, DTV introduces a time-decaying term to adjust the model’s attention assigned to each review. We construct the timeliness attention mechanism based on an exponential decay function, which is effective in and generally applied for fitting time-varying user behaviors, such as dynamic user interest (Ahmed et al. 2011) and sequential user behavior (Chen et al. 2009). Additionally, we set a parameter η to account for the varying intensity of the effect of the time-decaying term on the model’s attention. Specifically, we add the time-decaying term and multiply it with the normalized diversity-weighted score $\begin{array} { r } { e ^ { \beta _ { j } ^ { i } } / ( \sum _ { u = 1 } ^ { T } e ^ { \beta _ { u } ^ { i } } ) } \end{array}$ as follows:

\tilde {\alpha} _ {j} ^ {i} = \eta e ^ {\frac {\Delta t _ {j}}{\Delta t _ {m a x}}} \frac {e ^ {\beta_ {j} ^ {i}}}{\sum_ {u = 1} ^ {T} e ^ {\beta_ {u} ^ {i}}} \subseteq \tilde {\boldsymbol {\alpha}} ^ {i} = [ \tilde {\alpha} _ {1} ^ {i}, \tilde {\alpha} _ {2} ^ {i}, \dots , \tilde {\alpha} _ {T} ^ {i} ] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {L _ {F}}.\tag{6}
$$

In Equation (6), $\Delta t _ { j } = t _ { j } - t _ { 1 }$ and $\Delta t _ { m a x } = t _ { T } - t _ { 1 }$ , where $t _ { 1 , } t _ { j } ,$ and $t _ { T }$ denote the release dates of the first, $j \mathrm { t h } ,$ , and last (latest) reviews in the given time span, respectively. With the timeliness attention mechanism, more recent reviews can receive higher attention weights and the weights of the review sequence along the temporal dimension display a decaying effect.

4.4.3. Voting Awareness Attention. We also use an exponential function with a parameter $\gamma$ to realize the voting awareness attention mechanism, so that the mechanism can focus the model on voted reviews in a nonlinear way. Specifically, we add an exponential decay term to the time-decaying term and multiply their sum with the normalized diversity-weighted score as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\tilde {\alpha} _ {j} ^ {i} = \left(\eta e ^ {\frac {\Delta t _ {j}}{\Delta t _ {m a x}}} + \gamma e ^ {\frac {s _ {j}}{s _ {m a x}}}\right) \frac {e ^ {\beta_ {j} ^ {i}}}{\sum_ {u = 1} ^ {T} e ^ {\beta_ {u} ^ {i}}} \subseteq \tilde {\boldsymbol {\alpha}} ^ {i}} \\ & {\qquad = [ \tilde {\alpha} _ {1} ^ {i}, \tilde {\alpha} _ {2} ^ {i}, \ldots , \tilde {\alpha} _ {T} ^ {i} ] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {L _ {F}}.} \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

In Equation $( 7 ) , s _ { j }$ is the number of votes received by the jth review, and $s _ { m a x }$ is the maximum among the votes of all reviews in the given time span, so $s _ { j } / s _ { m a x }$ measures the relative popularity of the jth review. With the voting-awareness attention mechanism, higher attention weights will be assigned to reviews with more votes.

Parameters η and γ adjust the intensity of the effects of the time-decaying and voting awareness terms (and their interplay) on the model’s attention. Practically, reviews’ helpfulness votes and timeliness do interact. Reviews of lower timeliness tend to accompany larger cumulative votes, whereas voted reviews, with their remarkable attractiveness, tend to resist the time-decaying effect on their helpfulness. DTV well accommodates such interplay.

Finally, we get $\hat { \pmb { \alpha } } ^ { i }$ (the normalized $\tilde { \pmb { \alpha } } ^ { i } ) _ { . }$ , a comprehensive review weighting scheme that takes into account the semantic diversity, timeliness, and voting awareness attentions. Weighting the value matrix V by $\tilde { \pmb { \alpha } } ^ { i } ,$ , we can derive the representations $\mathbf { z } _ { i }$ at the ith time step, that is, $\mathbf { z } _ { i } = \mathbf { V } \hat { \pmb { \alpha } } ^ { i } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \bar { d } _ { v } }$

4.4.4. Adaptive AMI. After obtaining multimodal sequence representations ${ \bf Z } ^ { ( D ) } = [ { \bf z } _ { 1 } ^ { ( D ) } , { \bf \bar { z } } _ { 2 } ^ { ( D ) } , \ldots , { \bf z } _ { T } ^ { ( D ) } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { v } \times L _ { F } }$ (for the textual modality) and $\mathbf { Z } ^ { ( M ) } = [ \mathbf { z } _ { 1 } ^ { ( M ) } , \mathbf { z } _ { 2 } ^ { ( M ) } , \ldots , \mathbf { z } _ { T } ^ { ( M ) } ] \in$ $\mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { v } \times L _ { F } }$ (for the image modality), AMI aims to adaptively integrate them to output the predictions $\begin{array} { r } { \tilde { \bf y } = [ \tilde { y } _ { 1 } , \bar { \tilde { y } } _ { 2 } , \dots , } \end{array}$ $\tilde { y } _ { T } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { L _ { F } }$ . To this end, we set adaptive weights to determine the varying relative importance threshold between text and images across reviews, conforming to the requirement of customers’ adaptive attention for multimodal interaction. Specifically,

$$
\tilde {y} ^ {i} = (\xi_ {i} \pmb {z} _ {i} ^ {(D)} + (1 - \xi_ {i}) \pmb {z} _ {i} ^ {(M)}) ^ {T} \pmb {\theta} _ {i},\tag{8}
$$

where ${ \pmb \theta } _ { i }$ is the weight vector of the output layer at the ith time step. $\pmb { \xi } = [ \xi _ { 1 } , \xi _ { 2 } , \ldots , \xi _ { T } ] ^ { T }$ is an adaptive weight vector, where $\xi _ { i }$ reconciles the tradeoff between the utilities of textual modality and image modality at the ith step. With the adaptive attention mechanism for multimodal interaction, the roles of the text and images of a single review for sales prediction will be weighted adaptively.

Finally, DTV-AMI takes a global average over all time steps, that is, $\textstyle { \bar { y } } = \left( \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { T } { \tilde { y } } _ { i } \right) / T$ , as the model’s prediction result and optimizes the multimodal model with the MSE (mean squared error) loss function. Unlike common attention-based recurrent neural network methods that conduct average pooling on multiple attention representations before prediction (i.e., ex ante fusion) (Wang et al. 2016, Lei et al. 2018), y ¯ herein averages multiple predictions resulted by attention representations from all time steps $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ ex post fusion), thus incurring less information loss. This also resembles the real scenario that reviews are posted and customer readers arrive intermittently, whereas each review (time step) in a given time span can potentially act as a starting point (target query) of customers’ browsing behaviors (forward or backward).

## 4.5. Optimization Algorithm

Figure C1 (in Online Appendix C) outlines the training process of the proposed optimization algorithm for DTV-AMI. First, we preprocess the collected multimodal review data to obtain textual review sequence $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { x } } ^ { ( D ) }$ , image review sequence $\mathbf { X } ^ { ( M ) }$ , and sequences of their corresponding release date t and number of votes s, along with the sales to predict $\mathbf { y } .$ Then, we extract multimodal embeddings with BERT and VGG-16 and input them into two GRU networks, respectively. Next, for each GRU network, we add a DTV layer on its hidden states. Subsequently, we train the DTV-enhanced bimodal GRU model while taking AMI as an adaptive modality fusion strategy. DTV-AMI derives its prediction result by averaging the prediction outputs from all time steps. During the back propagation optimization of DTV-AMI, neuron weights, attention weights, and $\xi$ are all updated by computing their batch-wise gradients as usual.

## 5.2. Main Experiments

Although our main objective in this study is to effectively leverage multimodal reviews for sales prediction, we also examined the integrated prediction effect of multimodal reviews in conjunction with some internal (i.e., hotel-related) and external (i.e., environmental) factors considering their substantial impact on sales performance. Because sales prediction is essentially a time series prediction task that entails dynamic features, we selected historical sales and the month indicator $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ a variable that indicates the month of the predicted sales) as representatives of the dynamic influencing factors of hotel sales. Specifically, historical sales, as a typical kind of features for time series prediction (Doganis et al. 2006), comprehensively reflects the temporal variation pattern of the internal operation status of the hotel and the external market environment, whereas the month indicator accommodates the seasonal and holiday eff ects, which exert substantial impact on sales performance (Chu and Zhang 2003), and implies the temporal variation pattern of the external market size. The effects of static factors, internal (e.g., size, location, price tier, and amenities of the hotel) or external (e.g., local competitors, convenience, and attractions), on sales are largely static and can be implicitly captured through the incorporation of historical sales. Overall, we conducted four experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method for leveraging customer attention to enhance multimodal review–based sales prediction (RQ2). We tested three time spans, that is, a quarter, half a year, and a year prior to the predicted month, respectively, yielding three data sets with 51,344 (quarter), 61,203 (half year), and 66,470 (year) instances (corresponding to hotel-months), respectively.

First, through two experiments, we compared DTV-AMI against state-of-the-art attention-based deep learning methods, in terms of both prediction performance (in Experiment 1) and representation learning performance (in Experiment 2). We used GRU as the baseline and added different attention mechanisms to it (i.e., attentionbased GRUs) to enhance the prediction performance. We identified two typical compatibility functions in attention mechanisms: scaled multiplicative attention (Vaswani et al. 2017) and activated attention (Ma et al. 2017). In view of the number of heads (one set of K, V, and Q for one head) in attention mechanisms, we incorporated both self-attention (Chaudhari et al. 2019) and multihead attention (Galass et al. 2019) strategies with each attention mechanism. We compared the two versions of our proposed method, that is, DTV-AMI-self (DTV-AMI self-attention) and DTV-AMI-mh (DTV-AMI multihead attention), against the baseline, that is, (1) GRU, and eight benchmarks. Specifically, we first included four attention mechanisms: (2) MPL-self (scaled multiplicative self-attention), (3) MPL-mh (scaled multiplicative multihead attention), (4) AC-sel (activated self-attention), and (5) AC-mh (activated multihead attention). In view of the multimodality of reviews, we further identified four state-of-the-art attention-based multimodal deep learning methods as benchmarks: (6) DIIA (dynamic inter- and intramodality attention) (Huang et al. 2021), which learns the inter- and intrarelationships between modalities using attention mechanisms, (7) CEN (channel-exchanging network) (Wang et al. 2020), which conducts multimodal fusion by dynamically exchanging channels between subnetworks of different modalities, (8) MDL-CW (multimodal deep learning with cross weights) (Liu et al. 2018), which exploits the cross weights between representations of modalities and tries to learn interactions of the modalities, and (9) MCL (multiplicative combination layer) (Rastegar et al. 2016), which conducts multimodal fusion by multiplicatively combining information from different source modalities.

In Experiment 1, we compared DTV-AMI against the baseline and benchmarks in terms of prediction performance. We tested them with textual reviews, image reviews, and multimodal reviews, respectively. In case of multimodal reviews, the baseline and benchmarks— except the multimodal benchmarks (6–9) that have their special fusion strategies—fused multimodal representations via average pooling, a common embedding fusion strategy used in deep neural networks (Wang et al. 2016, Lei et al. 2018).

Besides the evaluation of prediction performance, DTV-AMI can also be evaluated in terms of its produced representation features because one of the prominent advantages of multimodal deep learning methods is their powerful representation learning abilities on multimodal data (i.e., multimodal representation learning). In this vein, representation features learned by DTV-AMI can act as inputs of downstream prediction models, which potentially further improve prediction performance. Therefore, in Experiment 2, we took the representation features produced by DTV-AMI, baseline, and benchmarks as inputs and further trained downstream prediction models using three methods, that is, support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and XGBoost, to see which deep learning method produces more effective multimodal review representations for sales prediction. For the deep learning models with multistep representation features, we conducted average pooling across their representations from all time steps.<sup>12</sup> In view of the high dimensionality of the representation feature space, we conducted feature selection using lasso before prediction.

In Experiment 3, we examined the prediction performance of the historical sales time series (within the three time spans, respectively). We used GRU and ARIMA (autoregressive integrated moving average) for sales prediction based on historical sales time series. For GRU, we input the historical sales time series into a GRU model where each time step of the sales time series corresponds to a cell in GRU (Yamak et al. 2019). The GRU model then processed the time series forward and optimized its cells backward recursively and output the final predicted sales result by its last cell.

In Experiment 4, we combined the representation features of historical sales by the time-series-based GRU model (i.e., the hidden features of historical sales output by the last cell of the trained GRU in Experiment 3), the multimodal review representation features (derived in Experiment 2), and the month indicator to further en hance the sales prediction performance.

For each setting in the four experiments, we estimated the prediction performance through five independent runs of 10-fold cross validation, resulting in 50 estimates. We used RMSE (root mean square error) and MAE (mean absolute error), which have been widely used to gauge model performance in continuous value prediction tasks (Lei et al. 2018), as performance metrics. The experiment results under MAE are available in Online Appendix E. Online Appendix F describes the method implementation and experimentation.

## 5.4. Exploratory Analysis

After being trained with the real sales data, the parameters related to our designed attention mechanisms provide us hints for understanding and uncovering, given multimodal reviews, what customers tend to attend to at different granularity levels (RQ1). As we theoretically analyzed before, better prediction results can be associated with more underlying customer attention. To this end, we further performed exploratory analysis with respect to each of the four attention mechanisms on top of the experiment results (some additional exploratory analyses are included in Online Appendix H).

We derived the weights of the four attention mechanisms from the trained model based on the training set. Then, for visualization purpose, we averaged the weights over all instances for each attention mechanism. When visualizing the timeliness, semantic diversity, and voting awareness attention mechanisms, we sorted the reviews from all time steps in descending order of timeliness, semantic diversity, and number of votes, respectively. Besides, DTV-AMI’s attention weights for some examples of multimodal reviews are available in Online Appendix H.4.

5.4.1. Analysis of the Timeliness Attention Mechanism. Figure 4 shows the variation patterns of the timeliness attention mechanism versus self-attention under different time spans. The fluctuations in Figure 4 indicate that reviews with higher timeliness tend to receive larger weights, demonstrating the effectiveness of our designed timeliness attention mechanism in conforming to customers’ timeliness attention. That is, there is a timedecaying effect of customer attention paid to reviews. Besides, as the time span expands, there emerges a long tail effect, corroborating that outdated reviews have little impact on customers’ purchase decision making and thus are obsolete for sales prediction. Moreover, the contrastive patterns between the timeliness attention– generated weights and the self-attention–generated weights indicate the advantageous capability of the timeliness attention mechanism in attending to timely reviews, which contributes to more effective multi modal review–based sales prediction.

5.4.2. Analysis of the Semantic Diversity Attention Mechanism. To visualize the semantic diversity attention mechanism, for each time step, we calculated the similarities of the review with others. Then, we averaged such similarities as the average redundancy of the review. Next, we sorted the reviews from all time steps in ascending (descending) order of redundancy (diversity). Figure 5 shows the variation patterns of the semantic diversity attention mechanism versus selfattention under different time spans. The fluctuations in Figure 5 indicate that reviews that are less redundant with each other tend to be more extensively valued by the DTV-AMI model, whereas self-attention tends to weight reviews in discrepant semantic diversities indiscriminately. This manifests the superiority of our designed semantic diversity attention mechanism in focusing more on semantically diverse reviews, which is in conformity with customers’ heterogeneous information demands. The long tail in the year span implies that reviews within a longer span are more likely to be redundant with each other.

## 5.5. Ablation Analysis

To disentangle the predictive value of each of the four attention mechanisms we designed for DTV-AMI (RQ2), we conducted an ablation analysis. We tested the prediction performance of DTV-AMI-mh on multimodal reviews by removing each of its core components (i.e., semantic diversity, timeliness, voting awareness, and AMI) one at a time, respectively. This yielded four variants of DTV-AMI, that is, DTV-AMI without semantic diversity attention (TV-AMI), timeliness attention (DV-AMI), voting awareness attention (DT-AMI), or AMI (DTV), respectively. The results are available in Online Appendix I.

## 6. Contributions and Implications

This study makes several contributions to the literature. First, it conceptualizes customer attention to multimodal reviews for sales prediction. It decomposes customer attention into indicators of timeliness attention, semantic diversity attention, voting awareness attention, and adaptive attention for multimodal interaction. This broadens our outlook about review helpfulness from the individual review level to multiple granularity levels (review element, review, and retailer). Meanwhile, it opens up new possibilities for leveraging customer attention to foster multimodal-review-based sales prediction. Second, methodologically, this study proposes a multimodal deep learning method (DTV-AMI), which draws strengths from four attention mechanisms, to incorporate customer attention indicators for multimodal review–based sales prediction appropriately. To the research streams of review mining and product operation, DTV-AMI offers a methodological tool to deliver the facilitating role of multimodal reviews for customer demand assessment and product value creation. For the design science paradigm of information systems research, DTV-AMI enriches its collection of methodological tools with a new artifact, expanding the boundary of multimodal reviews’ enabling role for business decision making. Third, the empirical results based on a large-scale multimodal review data set in a real case deepen our knowledge about how customer attention to reviews varies over time, semantic diversity, and helpfulness votes; how customers attend to review texts and images differently; and how the timeliness and helpfulness votes of a review interact.

First, as multimodal reviews, such as image reviews, audio reviews, and buyers’ video shows in addition to textual reviews, become increasingly prevalent and conveniently accessible, our proposed method serves as a practically valuable tool for adequately leveraging such multimodal data to support business decision making. ## 7. Conclusion

Whereas many studies have concentrated on review helpfulness from the perspective of quantitative review characteristics, we endeavored to reveal the value of multimoda reviews for sales prediction by virtue of customer attention at various granularity levels (review element, individual review, review subset, and holistic review set). We conceptualized four indicators of customer attention to pinpoint those reviews that are most influential of customers’ purchase intention. To leverage customer attention for enhanced multimodal review–based sales prediction, we proposed a multimodal deep learning method (DTV-AMI), which incorporates two mechanisms, that is, DTV for modeling customers’ semantic diversity, timeliness, and voting awareness attentions and AMI for reflecting customers’ self-adaptation between review text and images. Empirical evaluation results on a large-scale multimodal review data set demonstrate DTV-AMI’s superior capabilities over existing alternatives.

Our work has some limitations, which may be addressed in future research. First, although we empirically evaluated DTV-AMI in a case study in the hotel industry, its generalizability needs to be validated in a wider range of review-based prediction contexts. In this regard, the deployment of DTV-AMI has certain requirements on the multimodal review data. For example, realizing the timeliness and voting awareness attention mechanisms entails a sufficient span of reviews where some should have received votes. Besides, realizing the adaptive attention mechanism for multimodal interaction entails a sequence of multimodal reviews where textual reviews are expected to be accompanied by image reviews. Second, as DTV-AMI models the reviews of a focal hotel, reviews of its competitors, which are susceptible to the competitors actions (e.g., different promotions offered at different times) and may change over time (Jabr and Zheng 2014), also tend to affect the sales of the focal hotel and thus may also be leveraged for further improved sales prediction. Third, the roles of our constructed customer attention indicators for sales prediction are inherently domain specific and need to be adapted to the focal domain as the sales context varies, for example, tuning λ, η, γ, and ξ to adjust the predictive roles of semantic diversity, timeliness, voting awareness, and multimodal interaction, respectively. Fourth, as we extracted fixed feature embeddings for textual and image review sequences to reduce computational complexity, exploring finergrained customer attentions, for example, word-level and image-region-level attentions, also has promising potential.
