The usability of voice interaction is critical for consumers to have a satisfactory experience when interacting with voice-controlled smart products. However, voice interaction from the user’s perspective is underresearched and there are no existing scales to measure voice-interaction usability. This study uses a mixed methods approach to explore the construct of voice-interaction usability and establish a scale that can be used for measuring it. We first developed voice-interaction usability dimensions based on user reviews by using a coding technique that stems from grounded theory and then set up a classification of voice-interaction usability with five dimensions and 13 subdimensions based on cooperative principle theory. After developing the multilevel dimensions, we collected and examined multiple rounds of survey data to develop and validate a voice-interaction usability scale. This study enhances cooperative principle theory by extending the four-principle framework, developing the subdimensions of the framework in the human-machine voice-interaction context, and operationalizing the dimension concepts by developing the associated scales. The voice-interaction usability scale not only contributes to research on users’ behavior and experience with voice-controlled smart products but also provides insights to improve the design of voice-interaction functions. 

Keywords: Usability, smart product, voice interaction, scale development, cooperative principle theory 

## Introduction

With the development of natural language processing technology, voice-controlled smart products are becoming more common, and voice interaction has gradually become the main way in which users interact with smart products (Luger & Sellen, 2016). Through voice interaction, smart products can realize their functionality and help users get their expected information and content. The attribute of voice interaction enables users to perceive the intelligence and personality of smart products (Luger & Sellen, 2016). The intelligence of smart products is conveyed through voice interaction, and users’ attitudes toward smart products strongly depend on voice-interaction quality (Lee et al., 2017). Voice interaction encourages users to anthropomorphize smart products, and the use of voice can create social cues. Research on human-computer interaction (HCI) shows that humans often treat the information technology (IT) devices they are interacting with as social actors. Once users detect social cues from IT devices, they will give them a social response (Langer, 1992; Nass & Moon, 2000). Since natural language interaction allows voice-controlled smart products to provide more social cues, it is reasonable to expect that users will want to talk with smart products in an interpersonal way. 

However, it is challenging to understand human-smart product voice interaction in the same way that we understand interpersonal conversation. The reason lies in the differences between human-smart product voice interaction and interpersonal conversation: (1) smart products cannot propose a clear communication goal and do not typically initiate communication (Mavridis, 2015); (2) smart products rely on a stimulus-output system for data input and module output, while interpersonal communication is often based on social experience and social identification; (3) smart products lack self-awareness, unlike humans who understand their roles; (4) smart products cannot experience emotions and can only imitate emotions, whereas humans can generate and express emotions due to empathy (Wirtz et al., 2018); and (5) smart products cannot perceive changes in the environment that may occur during a conversation and are therefore unable to flexibly adjust the content of the dialogue (Mavridis, 2015). As a result, the theories for explaining interpersonal conversation need to be further developed in the humanmachine voice-interaction context. 

We argue that the concept of usability can well reflect users evaluation of their voice interaction with voice-controlled smart products. Drawing upon the definition of usability (Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006, p. 182), voice-interaction usability means the extent to which users perceive that they can achieve a certain goal effectively and efficiently when they use voice to interact with a smart product. High usability can lower users’ physical or mental effort in using products (Allen et al., 2018) and determines users’ satisfaction with smart products. Therefore, identifying the elements that contribute to voice-interaction usability and developing their relevant measures are critical in understanding smart product adoption. Establishing a scale for voice-interaction usability can also help organizations design satisfactory voicecontrolled smart products. 

Usability, as an important concept for evaluating the use of information systems (IS), has been studied in various contexts, such as website usability (Venkatesh and Ramesh, 2006), mobile device usability (Kim et al., 2012), and mobile application usability (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). Researchers have developed scales for usability based on the uniqueness of their research objects, such as the Microsoft Usability Guidelines (MUG) scale developed for website usability (Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006) and the mobile application usability scale that consists of multiple dimensions (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). However, existing usability dimensions and scales cannot fully capture human-smart product voice-interaction usability because of contextual differences. For example, users input information using a mouse, keyboard, or finger for websites or mobile applications. For voice interaction with smart products, however, users input information by speaking to them in a manner similar to verbal communication with other human beings. In terms of output, users mainly rely on vision when obtaining information from websites or mobile applications (Reicherts et al., 2022). 

The interface presents a variety of information at the same time, and users can scan, read, or compare them simultaneously. When acquiring information from voice interaction, users rely on hearing. Information is presented one piece at a time, and multiple pieces of information are presented in chronological order. Moreover, natural language interaction with voice-controlled smart products imitates human conversation (Cowan et al., 2015) and provides a stronger anthropomorphic experience for users. As a result, users will experience more humanlike emotions through the smart product’s timbre, pitch, and tone of voice. Although some general usability attributes in prior studies can partially be applied to study voice-interaction usability, the contextspecific characteristics need to be further explored. 

Thus, it is important to develop a context-specific scale for voice-interaction usability. Development of such a scale is required to help researchers better understand the adoption and use of smart products and to help practitioners better design smart products. Voice-interaction usability determines whether the voice interaction can meet its intended users’ needs and preferences through human-smart product communication. 

Considering the unique characteristics of human-smart product voice interaction, the importance of voiceinteraction usability for user satisfaction, and the theoretical gap concerning the conceptualization and measurement of voice-interaction usability, the two research questions of this study are: (1) What are the dimensions of voice-interaction usability and how can they be measured? (2) How well does our voice-interaction usability model predict user behavioral outcomes compared with existing models? 

We employed a sequential exploratory mixed methods approach (Creswell, 2003) and conducted qualitative research followed by a quantitative study. In the first phase, we used a qualitative research approach to code users reviews of smart products. Using open coding followed by axial coding, we identified 13 concepts associated with the voice-interaction usability of smart products. We then classified the 13 concepts into five dimensions by drawing on cooperative principle theory (CPT) (Grice, 1975). 

CPT is a linguistic theory that was originally used to study interpersonal conversation. CPT presents certain principles that need to be met for an effective conversation. We argue that CPT is a good starting point and theoretical basis for studying human-machine voice interaction because humanmachine voice interaction resembles interpersonal communication. Specifically, the user and the machine interact with natural language. Because of the revolutionary nature of voice-controlled smart products supported by natural language processing technology, smart products are more humanlike. Users also tend to anthropomorphize the smart product (Langer, 1992) and talk to it as if it were a human being. The voice interaction between users and machines often has a conversational goal that needs to be effectively achieved. Through a process by which we iteratively compared our coding results with the CPT principles and maxims, we found that CPT can be applied to the context of human-machine voice interaction but that it also needs to be further developed to better fit this new context. In the second phase, we followed a quantitative approach to develop a measurement scale for the voiceinteraction usability construct. After obtaining a reliable scale, we tested its nomological validity. 

Our study contributes to research and practice in three ways. First, we conceptualized voice-interaction usability in the context of smart products by developing five dimensions and 13 subdimensions. Further, we developed and validated a measurement instrument for the construct that can be used in future research. Second, we found unique features (e.g., access to rich linked resources and humanlike voice) of human-smart product communication and thereby extend 

CPT to the human-machine voice-interaction context. Third, the voice-interaction usability instrument we developed can serve as a tool that helps firms design and improve the usability of smart products and enhance users’ voiceinteraction experience. 

## Theoretical Background

## Usability in Voice Interaction and Other Contexts

Usability is a well-established concept that refers to “the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use” (Karat, 1997, p. 34). Usability is context dependent and shaped by the relationship between objects, tasks, and users (Phang et al., 2009). It has received great attention in the HCI and IS fields (Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006). Prior research has explored the dimensions of usability in various contexts as shown in Table 1. 

Based on Table 1, we can see that the existing usability scales are not well suited for the human-machine voiceinteraction context. First, the research objects of these studies do not involve voice interaction. Most of these context-based usability classifications reflect the characteristics of specific contexts. For example, some of the MUG usability dimensions (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002) describe characteristics that are somewhat unique to organizations’ websites. Another example is mobile application usability, which contains user interface structure, graphics, output and input, and application utility and design (i.e., aspects that embody the features of mobile computing) (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). However, the context of human-machine voice interaction has its own specific characteristics. Existing usability scales do not cover the unique features related to voice interaction, such as the content of the conversation as well as voice-, and timbrerelated characteristics. Furthermore, some attributes of existing usability scales such as those relating to multimedia (Nielsen, 1993), media use, community (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002), navigation/organization (Palmer, 2002), and vision-related factors (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015) simply do not fit the voice-interaction context. In sum, there is no context-specific dimensional framework or scale for voice-interaction usability. Given the growing importance of smart products and the voice interfaces that they present to users, conceptualizing and measuring voice-interaction usability is increasingly important.

Although some usability aspects covered in prior studies may be applicable to studying voice-interaction usability, such as interaction styles and response time (Nielsen, 1993, 2000), significant problems still exist when applying the current usability dimensions to the smart product voiceinteraction context. In terms of breadth, there is no single dimensional system that can comprehensively reflect the characteristics of the smart product voice interaction. In terms of depth, the existing dimensions cannot accurately reflect voice-interaction usability. For example, the content dimension in MUG could be used to evaluate the content that users obtain from a voice interaction; however, there are differences between the content that users obtain from a website versus a voice-controlled smart product. In humansmart product voice interaction, the voice output of a smart product takes two forms: (1) direct responses to the user’s voice commands and (2) content provided by linking to a third-party platform. Thus, MUG items cannot be used directly to measure voice interaction accurately. 

With the popularization of voice-controlled smart products in recent years, some studies have explored the usability or usability-related attributes of smart voice interaction. Scholars have conceptualized and described smart voice interaction differently based on various scenarios, such as the usability of voice-interaction products for blind people (Abdolrahmani & Branham, 2018), voice-interaction products in cars (Hua & Ng, 2010; Larsen et al., 2020), and intelligent voice assistants (Bogers et al., 2019; López et al., 2017). Table 2 summarizes previous work on humanmachine voice interaction and the existing usability-related attributes. Based on the literature review, we identified three major gaps in the existing body of knowledge on voiceinteraction usability. First, existing conceptualizations and instruments for measuring voice-interaction usability are incomplete or inadequate. Many dimensions identified in the literature only focus on certain aspects of voice interaction, such as correctness and naturalness (López et al., 2017). A comprehensive perspective is lacking for characterizing voice-interaction usability. This situation leads to two critical problems: incompleteness in classification and dimensional overlap. Second, some studies adopt traditional usability dimensions or general concepts to examine voiceinteraction features, such as efficiency, effectiveness, and satisfaction (Larsen & Scheel, 2020; Kouroupetroglou & Spiliotopoulos, 2009). These dimensions are too general and cannot accurately reflect users’ experience through voice interaction. Third, most of the prior studies on voiceinteraction usability are not empirically based. Thus, there is no appropriate context-specific scale to evaluate voiceinteraction usability, and the dimensions of voice-interaction usability have not been empirically validated. 

## CPT

CPT (Grice, 1975) provides a theoretical lens that may be useful in characterizing the dimensions of voice-interaction usability. CPT suggests that people have a commonly accepted purpose in a conversation. A specific conversation may consist of several stages, each with a specific purpose. The basic conversational cooperation principle is: “make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged” (Grice, 1975, p. 45). Based on this theory, complying with the cooperative principles provides the basis for a conversation to proceed effectively. The principles include manner, relation, quality, and quantity. Each principle has specific maxims, which are presented in Table 3. If both parties of the dialogue abide by these four principles in a conversation, they can communicate effectively. 

CPT has received extensive attention in a variety of disciplines, such as marketing (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987), psychology (Schwarz, 1999), and law (Sunstein & Thaler, 2003). Previous studies have found that when people use IT devices (e.g., computers), they tend to mindlessly apply the social rules of interpersonal interaction to their interaction with the IT device (Langer, 1992). Thus, people will respond to the IT device in a social manner, even though they realize that they are interacting with a machine (Nass & Moon, 2000). Conversations between humans and smart products are, at least in some ways, similar to interpersonal conversations. For example, both types of conversations involve cooperation and a specific purpose or direction. Therefore, CPT can be a useful theoretical lens for thinking about voice interaction between humans and smart products. 

In the context of human-smart product interaction, conversations are generally initiated by a human user in the form of instructions. After the user issues an instruction, the smart product attempts to provide feedback that matches the user’s needs as much as possible and does not intentionally violate the principles of cooperation. Hence, CPT provides a good perspective to help us understand the voice interaction between people and smart products. To achieve the aim of a conversation, the principles of quality, quantity, relevance, and manner need to be met. Obviously, humans and smart products differ significantly in their nature. However, although smart products are essentially manufactured, voicecontrolled smart products display many social cues—for example, they can “hear” and “speak,” have names, characters, and personalities, and are made to be humanlike (Feine et al., 2019). In the process of voice interaction, users perceive and pay attention to the anthropomorphic characteristics of smart products. While anthropomorphism is not relevant to interpersonal conversation, it is a critical factor in human-machine voice interaction because it helps maintain an environment where users can engage with smart products almost as if they were human. Hence, although CPT is a good starting point from which to explore the usability of human-machine voice interaction, the theory and its associated principles need to be extended to fit this new context of human-smart product voice interaction. 

## Mixed Methods Approach

## Research Design

This study used a mixed methods (qualitative and quantitative) approach to address the research questions. Identifying the dimensions of voice-interaction usability required a qualitative approach. Developing and validating a means of measuring voice-interaction usability and determining how well the construct can predict user behavioral outcomes required a quantitative approach. A mixed methods approach offers several advantages (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016). This approach supports generating and evaluating theory and facilitates complementary qualitative and quantitative approaches. In our case, we used the qualitative approach to generate dimensions and the quantitative approach to test the validity of our voice-interaction usability instrument.

The mixed methods approach involved two phases. In Phase 1, we obtained 30,834 user reviews of smart speakers and developed voice-interaction usability dimensions based on the reviews related to voice interaction by using coding techniques from grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). The coding process involved three steps: In Step 1, we conducted a pretest of open coding by randomly taking a 10% sample. In Step 2, we conducted open coding of the full sample. Based on the open codes that we obtained in Step 2, we performed axial coding in Step 3. After axial coding, we further clustered the axial codes in selective codes by drawing on CPT. Through this process, we conceptualized voice-interaction usability as a multidimensional construct with five second-order dimensions (extended from CPT) and 13 first-order dimensions (drawn from our coding of the data). 

In Phase 2, we developed a measurement instrument for voice-interaction usability. In this phase, we followed three stages. In Stage 1, we created an initial scale by generating an item pool based on our open codes and previous studies and then evaluated it for content and face validity. In Stage 2, we obtained survey data in two rounds, refined our scales by examining their validities and reliability, and deleted weak scale items. In Stage 3, we reexamined our instrument by collecting a third round of survey data to confirm that our measures were reliable and valid. Based on Stages 2 and 3, we refined the scales and reduced the number of indicators from 58 to 45. Finally, we conducted a nomological validity test by testing a structural model. Then, we compared the predictive validity of our structural model with some of the classical models (e.g., unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT)-based model, technology acceptance model (TAM), and MUG-based model). ## Phase 1: Develop Voice-Interaction Usability Construct Using Coding Technique

Data collection: This study leverages text-based data in the form of online user reviews of five smart speakers (Tmall Genie from Alibaba, Xiaodu smart speaker from Baidu, Xiaoai smart speaker from Xiaomi, Dingdong smart speaker from Jingdong, and Xiaoya smart speaker from Himalaya) to conduct coding analysis. The smart speakers we chose are the leading brands of smart speakers (produced by Alibaba, Jingdong, Baidu, Xiaomi, and Himalaya) in China (iiMedia Research, 2019). In terms of product category, we focus on smart speakers for three reasons. First, smart speakers are one of the most common voice-controlled smart products. The number of users of smart speakers is expanding rapidly in the global market, and smart speakers have become the fastestgrowing internet-of-things device category in the world (Deloitte, 2019). Second, smart speakers often serve as an entry point for people to obtain personalized smart services (Gartner, 2017). Thus, their use frequency of use is relatively high. Third, using these products requires voice interaction between humans and technology, and much of the user review content focuses on the voice-interaction experience. Hence, based on user reviews, we can identify the aspects of voice interaction that users are most concerned about. We obtained 30,834 user reviews related to voice interaction over a range of three years (2017-2019) from the Tmall and JD platforms, which are the two largest e-commerce platforms in China. Because we were specifically interested in user perceptions of voice-interaction usability, we did not code reviews focusing on aspects unrelated to voice interaction (e.g., price, logistics, and packaging). We reached theoretical saturation after coding approximately 21,000 reviews. Although we continued coding the rest of the user reviews, we did not produce any new open codes, showing that the size of our dataset met the saturation criteria (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

Coding procedure: We analyzed and coded user reviews line by line. After applying the open codes, we analyzed and extracted concepts from the open codes and clustered them into axial codes. Then, we classified the axial codes into five dimensions by drawing on CPT. We used the qualitative analysis software NVivo 11.0 for the entire coding process (Hutchison et al., 2010). Figure 1 shows the coding process. 

The coding process involved three steps and four researchers. The first author and an IS Ph.D. candidate conducted independent double-blind coding throughout the entire process. Two other authors examined and analyzed the coding and then clustered the results in the final step. Appendix A describes the coding scheme development process. 

Step 1: pretest of open coding. To ensure the reliability of the coding results, we randomly selected 10% of the reviews to work with before open coding the whole sample. Two coders read the 10% sample of user reviews word-for-word, conducted open coding independently, and compared the results of their coding. The two coders discussed any coding inconsistencies and sought to further clarify the concepts corresponding to the content of the text.

Step 2: open coding. Open coding the entire sample took three months, and the qualitative textual material was coded by two coders working independently. The Cohen’s kappa coefficient, which was used to calculate intercoder reliability, was 91% (the reliability equals the number of codes agreed upon by both parties divided by the number of all codes), exceeding the recommended threshold of 70% (Boyatzis, 1998) and similar to reliability values reported in previous research (Curtis et al., 2017). After careful comparison and the deletion of open codes that were duplicates or used less than five times, we obtained 34 purified open codes, which were used as a basis for clustering. Tables 5 to 9 show the purified open codes. 

Step 3: axial coding and selective coding. The two coders independently analyzed and clustered the 34 open codes into axial codes. After two coders independently performed the clustering, two senior experts discussed and evaluated the codes until they reached a consensus. In the end, we developed 13 axial codes. Tables 5 to 9 show examples of user reviews and the process by which we derived the open and axial codes. 

Coding results: After developing the open and axial codes, we further summarized the axial codes to develop our framework of smart product voice-interaction usability. In the process of axial code generalization, we iteratively compared the axial codes with the four principles of CPT to determine the extent to which this theory might be applicable to the domain of human-machine voice interaction. We found that the essence of an effective conversation does not change in the human-machine voice-interaction context— i.e., humans are still concerned about the manner, relation, quality, and quantity of the conversation. Nine of the 13 axial codes fit the meaning of the four principles of CPT. There were four axial codes that did not fit the CPT principles: humanlike voice, verbal richness, personality, and empathy. We defined the four constructs based on corresponding open codes and user reviews (the definitions are shown in Table 10). Then, we referred back to the voice-controlled smart product literature. We identified anthropomorphism as a unique characteristic of voice-controlled smart products that significantly differentiates interpersonal conversation from human-machine voice interaction (Xiao & Kummar, 2021; Yam et al., 2021). We further compared the definition of anthropomorphism with the four axial codes and found that they were closely associated with anthropomorphism. Anthropomorphism in voice interaction constitutes a facet of voice-interaction usability. The user reviews revealed that users perceive anthropomorphism (encompassing humanlike voice, verbal richness, personality, and empathy) in voice interaction as an essential feature of effective, efficient, and satisfactory interactive human-machine experiences. Thus, in total, we identified five second-order dimensions (quantity, quality, relation, manner, and anthropomorphism).

We took two measures to ensure the fit between the axial and selective codes (first- and second-order dimensions). First, after completing coding and identifying the 13 axial codes, we carefully defined each construct based on the open codes. The definitions were then examined to ensure there was no significant overlap between them. Then, we defined the second-order constructs drawing on CPT and the previous literature (Grice, 1975; Epley et al., 2007). The definitions of the second-order constructs are presented in the existing literature. 

Next, we recruited 20 students to classify the 13 firstorder constructs using the Q-sorting method in order to evaluate whether the first-order constructs could be fitted to the second-order constructs. Specifically, we (1) gave the students the definitions of each first- and second-order construct and asked them questions to check whether they understood the definitions, (2) provided them with five larger cards with the names and definitions of the secondorder constructs and 13 smaller cards with the names and definitions of the first-order constructs, and (3) asked them to match the first-order construct names (smaller cards) to the appropriate second-order construct (larger cards). All 20 students placed the cards correctly, indicating that the constructs could be clearly distinguished based on their definitions and that the firstand second-order dimensions matched. The five secondorder dimensions are described below. 

## Quantity Dimension

Based on Grice’s (1975) definition of quantity and its associated maxims, the quantity of human-smart product voice interaction relates to the quantity of information that the smart product provides, including the responses of the smart product and the linked content it provides. While information is obtained in a different way through voice interaction, as opposed to browsing content on a website or using an app, the definition of quantity still fits the context of human-smart product voice interaction. For example, when using voice-controlled smart products, users can seek answers by asking them questions or asking them to play programs from third-party platforms. Regarding the former case, as shown in Reviews A1-A3 in Table 5, users expressed that the smart product seemed to know everything, like an encyclopedia or a knowledgeable person. These comments reflect that the smart product’s answer is informative. For the latter case, we found that users are concerned about whether smart products can access rich resources and whether they can play different types of content (see Reviews B1-B3 in Table 5). 

## Quality Dimension

The quality dimension of interpersonal conversation involves trying “to make your contribution one that is true”, and the two maxims associated with quality are “do not say what you believe to be false” and “do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence” (Grice, 1975, p. 46). In the human-smart product voice-interaction context, since they lack consciousness, machines will not intend to say something that is false or unsupported by evidence; however, the humans who obtain information from them may nevertheless evaluate the accuracy of the answer. For example, users might assess whether the information provided in response to a query about the weather forecast is accurate. Since smart products provide content from third parties, users might also evaluate source reliability. Through content analysis, we found that users’ perception of information quality is consistent with previous studies; that is, regardless of whether the information is acquired by auditory or visual means, users are concerned about the accuracy and reliability of information (Aladwani & Palvia, 2002). In the context of human-smart product voice interaction, quality includes the response accuracy of the smart product and the content reliability of its third-party source resources. The codes and examples of user reviews pertaining to the quality dimension are shown in Table 6. 

## Relation Dimension

The relation dimension refers to whether the content of the dialogue is related to the subject and purpose of the conversation (Grice, 1975). In the context of human-smart product voice interaction, the relation dimension is associated with the functional usage of the smart product. The user reviews indicated that the most common types of functional usage include asking smart products questions to obtain information, chatting with them for fun, and asking them to execute tasks such as turning lights on or off. Relation is important to users because they evaluate whether the smart product is understanding their instructions based on the relation between input and output. Indeed, the user’s experience of a voice-controlled smart product is largely based on the smart product’s ability to recognize and understand the user’s speech (D’Mello et al., 2010). Over the last few decades, scholars have devoted themselves to natural language recognition research, design, and development (Yilmazyildiz et al., 2016). The higher the degree of a smart product’s language recognition and ability to understand users, the stronger the relation will be between a smart product’s response and the user’s voice input. In a question-and-answer (Q&A) or chatting context, users evaluate the relation not only in a single Q&A but also in multiple rounds of Q&A sessions. When asking a smart product to perform a certain task, users assess the relation by whether the smart product has performed the corresponding task correctly. Table 7 shows the codes and examples of user reviews under the relation dimension. 

## Manner Dimension

In interpersonal interactions, the manner dimension focuses on whether people speak in a concise and orderly fashion that avoids ambiguity (Grice, 1975). In user reviews, we found that in addition to the quantity, quality, and relation of the feedback content, users were also concerned about the manner (easy to understand, succinct answers, and orderly presentation) of smart products’ language expression. Compared with traditional electronic products, the point of distinction for voice-controlled smart products is that they should be easy to use because users can operate and perform functions using natural language. Therefore, users of voicecontrolled smart products do not need to have extensive experience or knowledge to use such products. The user reviews indicated that users of smart products also include children; for children to interact effectively with smart products, it is important that these products understand children’s voice inputs and provide voice outputs that are easy to understand. As shown in Table 8, users’ satisfaction with smart products depends on whether their responses are easy to understand, even for children, and whether their answers are concise and focused. In addition, smart products are often used as a tool to answer difficult questions, akin to a speaking Wikipedia. In this regard, the reviews indicated that users are concerned with the logic of the content expressed by smart products. Information presented by sound is different from the content presented visually when a user browses a web page. When outputting information visually, multiple pieces of content are presented to the user at the same time, and the user can look back or skip to browse the information. However, content presented by voice must follow a strict chronological order. Listeners must hear a sentence before they can receive the next message. Therefore, logical clarity is a key element of the manner dimension. Table 8 shows the axial codes, open codes, and examples of user reviews corresponding to the manner dimension. 

## Anthropomorphism Dimension

We observed a large number of user reviews relating to the anthropomorphism of smart products. Based on the definition of anthropomorphism (Epley et al., 2007), anthropomorphizing human-smart product voice interaction refers to imbuing the language expressions of the smart product with humanlike characteristics, motivations, intentions, and emotions. Users first perceive smart products’ humanlike nature from their voices. It is technically possible for smart products to speak with highly realistic human voices instead of voices that sound machine generated. Highly realistic human voices reflect a timbre, tone, and sound that are full of emotion. According to Reviews J1-J3 in Table 9, these voice characteristics make users feel that they are talking to a real person, and this creates a pleasant feeling. 

Reviews K1-K3 in Table 9 show that users also perceive the verbal richness of smart products’ responses. Smart products can use different language expressions, words, and sentences to express or describe things with the same meaning. Verbal richness makes users perceive that the smart product is vivid and humanlike, unlike a stereotypical machine, which would respond in a rigid way with fixed replies. Verbal richness makes users feel that the smart product is not a rigid and programmed machine but a lively human being talking to them. 

We also found many user reviews that focused on the personality of smart products, describing them as lovely, gentle, obedient, competent, humorous, rigorous, and lively. Users have different perceptions of the personality of smart products that seem to be brand dependent. For example, as shown in Reviews L1-L6 of Table 9, users described Xiaoya as gentle, Tmall Genie as strict, and Xiaoai as obedient and capable. The review showed that many users seriously evaluate the personality of smart products, just as they would evaluate a human being’s personality, which reflects the user’s tendency to anthropomorphize smart products. The reviews also included many comments about the empathy of smart products, suggesting that they understand and care about their feelings and are attentive to their needs. Empathy is one of the important dimensions of service quality (Pitt et al., 2006). The reviews indicated that when smart products help users’ complete tasks or provide services for them, users perceive them as empathetic. The existing literature has explored empathy in the context of artificial intelligence (AI), suggesting that it can play an important role in shaping users’ experience of interacting with an AI (Yalçın, 2020) and may also affect users’ trust in the AI (Cheng et al., 2021). Table 9 presents the axial codes, open codes, and examples of user reviews corresponding to the anthropomorphism dimension.

## Phase 2: Develop and Validate an Instrument for Measuring Voice-Interaction Usability

## Stage 1: Setting Up an Initial Scale

Moving on to scale development, the produced scale items should capture the essential aspects of the focal constructs. According to Mackenzie et al. (2011), the items can come from various sources. In this study, item creation was based on three sources: (1) deduction from the theoretical definition of the constructs, (2) users’ reviews and the open codes derived from them, and (3) suggestions from IS experts. Following Mackenzie et al. (2011), we took care to ensure that (1) item wording was simple and precise, (2) double-barreled items were avoided, (3) items did not contain ambiguous or unfamiliar language, and (4) items did not induce social desirability bias. In this way, 62 items were generated. 

Once the initial scales were developed, we recruited four IS researchers (two Ph.D. candidates and two M.S. students) to test the face and content validities. The aim of the face validity test was to make sure the expression of each indicator was clear and concise. After each researcher examined the face validity independently, we discussed and compared the results. In order to make the indicators clearer, we revised the wording where necessary. Through this process, we deleted three unclear items and obtained 59 scale items. The content validity test confirmed that each indicator accurately reflected the meaning of the corresponding constructs. As a further test, we recruited 20 students and conducted a confirmatory Q-sort to examine the content validity (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). We made cards with items and construct definitions. Then, we asked participants to choose the most appropriate definitions of 13 constructs for each item. The results show that the definition of the constructs could be clearly differentiated and that the items reflected the meaning of the intended construct. The initial item pool is shown in Table B1 in Appendix B. After testing for face and content validities, we collected survey data to examine the measurement model. We used SPSS 22.0 and SmartPLS 3.3.3 to analyze the measurement model for scale refinement and perform the nomological validity test. 

## Stage 2: Scale Purification and Refinement

We collected the data (first-round) on www.wjx.com, which is the largest questionnaire distribution platform in China. We chose to collect data from smart speaker users to study voice-interaction usability, as smart speakers are the most common voice-controlled smart product and have a large number of users. Users chat with smart speakers, or issue voice commands to make them perform functions. To ensure that the users we surveyed had voice-interaction experience with smart speakers, we took three measures: (1) We set clear guidelines before the questionnaire, highlighting that only respondents with experiences interacting with smart speakers were eligible to answer the questions. (2) We included a question asking respondents when they most recently interacted with a smart speaker. If the respondent answered “never,” the survey was terminated. (3) We asked the platform to screen qualified respondents and paid an extra fee for this additional work. We paid a total of 20 RMB for each completed questionnaire. A total of 718 questionnaires were received and then screened. The wjx platform sets a minimum time required per page such that each respondent had to spend at least one minute answering questions on a page before proceeding to the next page. The response times met the time requirement, and there were no cases involving missing answers. We deleted surveys in which respondents entered the same score for all items, leaving us with 678 usable questionnaires. Table C1 in Appendix C shows the demographics. 

We examined Bartlett’s test of sphericity (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin [KMO] statistic = 0.969, p > 0.01) and then used factor analysis to identify problematic scale items (Kaiser, 1958). The results indicate that the sample data had sufficient interitem correlations to perform factor analysis. Thus, we performed exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using varimax rotation. Based on the result of the rotated component matrix, we extracted 13 factors and deleted nine indicators (ARLR5, RACC1, FBR3, ETU4, ETU5, HLV5, VERN1, VERN2, EMP1) because their factor loadings were less than 0.5. We then calculated the factor loadings again without dropped scale items. All items loaded on their corresponding construct at levels that were above 0.5. These preliminary results demonstrated adequate convergent and discriminant validities of the scales. After deleting the weak items, we further conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the reliability and the convergent and discriminant validities of the scale. The values of composite reliability (CR) and the Cronbach’s α of each variable exceeded 0.7, and the values of the Dijkstra-Henseler Rho_A (ρ<sub>A</sub>), which represents an approximately exact measure of construct reliability, were between 0.6 and 0.9. The results of the three indexes indicate strong scale reliability (Hair et al., 2019). Each construct’s average variance extracted (AVE) value exceeded 0.5, suggesting adequate convergent validity (see Table C2 for details) (Gefen et al., 2000; Gefen & Detmar, 2011). Next, we measured the scale’s discriminant validity. We presented the data in steps similar to those of Hoehle and Venkatesh (2015), where discriminant validity is determined by comparing the AVEs for each construct and the squared correlation of the construct with other constructs. The results in Table C3 provide evidence of adequate discriminant validity. The heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) values were below 0.85 (Hair et al., 2019), and the standard root mean square residual (SRMR) value was 0.037 (≤0.08), indicating good model fit. We tested for common method bias (CMB) by using marker variable analysis (Malhotra et al., 2006), using health consciousness as a marker variable, which is unrelated to any variables in the measurement model. The results suggest an insignificant CMB because the original correlations were not significantly different from their CMB-adjusted values. 

Next, we collected data in a second round on wjx. After screening the data, we obtained 684 valid questionnaires. Table C4 shows the demographics. In this round, we further purified the scale by repeating the first-round data examination steps. Then, we tested the relationship between second- and first-order constructs by calculating their path coefficients and t-values. The results of the EFA test show five items (RINF1, ARLR4, CER3, CER5, and FBR2) with factor loading values below 0.5. After removing these weak items, we reexamined EFA and CFA; the scale properties are shown in Table C5. The discriminant validity result is shown in Table $\mathrm { C 6 , }$ and the HTMT values are below 0.85. These results show that the scale had acceptable reliability and validity after deleting the weak items. The SRMR value of 0.042 shows good model fit. We investigated the relationships between second- and first-order constructs and modeled these relationships as formative for the following reasons: (1) All the first-order constructs are clearly defined and can be clearly distinguished, reflecting a specific aspect of their corresponding second-order construct, which cannot be replaced or deleted (Petter et al., 2007), and (2) there are causal relationships between the two levels of constructs (Petter et al., 2007). We examined the variance inflation factor (VIF) values and path coefficients of these relationships. The VIF value of each first-order construct was less than 3.3 (Diamantopoulos et al., 2006), and the relationships between the second- and first-order constructs were positive and significant. Then, we tested for CMB by using marker variable analysis (Malhotra et al., 2006), again using health consciousness as a marker variable. The results indicate an insignificant CMB. The results in Table C7 suggest that the formative relationships between the secondand first-order constructs are supported by the data analysis (Petter et al., 2007). 

## Stage 3: Scale Reexamination

During Stage 3, we (1) examined the purified scales from Stage 2 and checked whether there were any weak items, (2) examined the reliability and validity of the scales, (3) 

examined the goodness of fit indices, (4) investigated the relationships between second- and first-order constructs, (5) constructed a structural model and perform a nomological validity test, and (6) compared our model with the competing models. The aim of the comparison was to evaluate whether the context-specific variables we proposed and the associated scales had stronger explanatory power in the human-smart product voice interaction domain than competing models. We collected another new sample of data on wjx and paid 30 RMB for each questionnaire. We received 667 valid responses. The demographics are shown in Table D1. 

First, we validated the measurement model. We first conducted an EFA. The rotated component results show that all the factor loadings were greater than 0.7. Then, we conducted a CFA. Table D2 indicates that all AVE values exceeded 0.5, the Cronbach’s α and CR values exceeded 0.7, and values of $\rho _ { \mathrm { A } }$ were between 0.89 and 0.98. The discriminant validity of the scale was also acceptable (Table D3). The results of HTMT were below 0.85, and the SRMR value of 0.046 revealed good model fit. Then, we used marker variable analysis to test CMB. The results indicate an insignificant CMB. Next, we tested the relationships between the second- and first-order constructs. We measured the second-order constructs using two methods. The first method involved using global items for the second-order constructs. We developed the global items based on the definitions of the second-order constructs and followed the same instrument development procedure. The results indicate that no weak indicators needed to be dropped and the scales for the second-order constructs had good validity and reliability. Table 11 presents the second-order construct items. 

The second method involved summing the factor scores for the first-order constructs to create a single-item composite index for each second-order construct (Petter et al., 2007). For example, we obtained two composite items for quantity by summing the factor scores of response informativeness and access to rich linked resources. In this way, we obtained two measurement results, which are shown in Table D4. Each first-order construct’s VIF value was less than 3.3, and the relationships between the second- and first-order constructs were positive and significant. These results indicate that our scales had good validity. After three rounds of scale development and testing, we had reduced the 58 indicators of the initial scale to $4 5$ indicators and obtained a reliable and valid voice-interaction usability scale, which is shown in Table 12.

Next, we constructed a structural model for a nomological validity test and compared it with classical models, including TAM, UTAUT, and MUG-based models. These models have been previously validated for explaining the antecedents of IS usage. As this study contributes to the use behavior research, we used intention to use and use behavior as dependent variables. In IS research, these two dependent variables are commonly studied when examining individual technology acceptance (Venkatesh et al., 2003). They have been theoretically and empirically validated as the outcomes of usability or usability-related attributes (Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2013, Venkatesh & Ramesh, 2006, Davis, 1989). We measured intention to use and use behavior by adapting the validated scales of Venkatesh et al. (2003, 2012). The four models that we compared are shown in Figure 3 and the scales of Models 2, 3, and 4 are shown in Table D5 in Appendix D. We also used smart product-related characteristics (e.g., response time and consistency) and user-related characteristics (e.g., smart product self-efficacy, usage experience, age, gender, and education) as control variables. 

We examined both the measurement and structural models in Figure 3 using the data collected in the third round. We measured the five dimensions (as the second-order constructs) using the corresponding items of the first-order constructs by calculating the factor scores for each first-order construct based on the items. Table 13 presents the structural model examination results. For the control variables, only consistency was significantly related to intention to use and use behavior. The influences of other control variables were insignificant. The scales of the variables in Models 2, 3, and 4 were all adapted from the original literature (Table D5). The EFA and CFA results show that the scales in the four models exhibited acceptable reliability and validity. We used the PLSPredict algorithm to conduct predictive model assessment for all four models. The predictive relevance (Q<sup>2</sup>) value of each model was greater than 0, implying that each model outperformed the most naive benchmark, defined as the indicator means from the analysis sample (Hair et al., 2019). Moreover, the results show that none of the indicators have higher root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) values when compared to the naive linear regression model (LM) benchmark, indicating the models’ high predictive power (Hair et al., 2019).

Given that anthropomorphism was not included in the original CPT framework, we examined its explanatory power by adding it to the three competing models to understand the impact of anthropomorphism on these models (Models 2, 3, and 4 in Figure 3) and by deleting it from the voice-interaction usability model. When we added anthropomorphism to the three models, the adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ of the two dependent variables increased by over 6.5% in all competing models. When we deleted it from the voice-interaction usability model, the adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ decreased by 1.7% and 2.9%, respectively. We also tested the independent effect of anthropomorphism and found that it explained 17.1% and 18.5% of the variance for intention to use and use behavior, respectively. These results indicate that anthropomorphism had a statistically significant effect on the two dependent variables. 

With respect to the role of anthropomorphism in Model 1, we contend that anthropomorphism is not a prerequisite factor but a dimension of voice-interaction usability. For example, the quantity of voice interaction is not dependent on the level of anthropomorphism of voice interaction. Moreover, if it were a prerequisite factor, anthropomorphism could have served as a boundary condition that might have changed the effectiveness of the quantity, quality, relation, and manner dimensions. Therefore, we tested the moderating effect of anthropomorphism on the relationships between the dimensions of quantity, quality, relation, and manner and the two dependent variables. No significant moderating effects were detected, suggesting that anthropomorphism did not serve as a boundary condition. Taken together, these results indicate that anthropomorphism is a significant and independent dimension of voice-interaction usability. 

## Discussion and Implications

This research was motivated by the increasing application of voice-controlled smart products in consumers’ daily lives. We conceptualized voice-interaction usability and developed and validated its associated scale. We used a mixed methods approach and conducted the study in two phases. In the first phase, we collected and analyzed 30,834 user reviews on voice-controlled smart products and developed the multilevel dimensions of voice-interaction usability, which included 13 first-order and five second-order constructs. The two-layer dimensional system we developed considers the contextbased features of human-machine interaction, building on and extending CPT. Based on the results of text coding, we developed an additional dimension (i.e., anthropomorphism) for voice-interaction usability, thus extending CPT in the context of voice-controlled smart products. In the second phase, we developed the associated scale following the scale development method of Mackenzie et al. (2011). After the initial item generation and content validity test, we specified the measurement models and obtained survey data from more than 1900 consumers of voice-controlled smart products (i.e., smart speakers in this study) in three rounds. We conducted EFA and CFA for each round of survey data. In the nomological validity test, we found that our scale for measuring voice-interaction usability is significantly associated with intention to use and use behavior. Finally, we benchmarked our model against three classical models, including TAM, UTAUT, and MUG-based models. The results show that our voice-interaction usability-based model explained more variance than the three competing models. 

## Theoretical Implications

This study has several theoretical implications. First, we contribute to the usability literature by conceptualizing and developing the underlying dimensions of voice-interaction usability. We conceptualize voice-interaction usability as consisting of five second-order dimensions of quantity, quality, relation, manner, and anthropomorphism and 13 first-order dimensions. Differing from past research on the usability of non-AI related and voice-based AI technology/products, our conceptualization of voice-interaction usability leverages the unique contextual characteristics of human-smart product voice interaction. The subdimensions reflect users’ functional use of voice-controlled interaction. For example, we found that when users chat with smart products, ask them questions, or search for information, they value response informativeness, response accuracy, feedback relevance, succinct and easily understood answers, a humanlike voice, verbal richness, personality, and empathy. When asking smart products to provide external resources, users are concerned about access to rich linked resources, third-party content reliability, and orderly presentation. The dimensions we developed reflect the attributes of content and manner of voice interaction. The subdimensions of anthropomorphic features also capture the difference between human-computer voice interaction and interpersonal communication. In sum, the voice-interaction usability dimensions we developed enrich the usability literature, complementing the studies on technology-, information system-, and product-related usability. Our study and its findings respond to the call for research on developing context-specific theory (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Hong et al., 2014). 

Second, we contribute to the AI-related use behavior research by developing a measurement scale for voice-interaction usability. Our scale consists of 45 items for 13 constructs and 20 items for the five second-order constructs, which were refined and validated through three rounds of rigorous instrument development and testing. Compared with the scales of previous literature related to voice interaction (Hoffman & Novak, 2018; Yam et al., 2021), we identified many new constructs and related measures based on user reviews of voiceinteraction experience. These constructs, including response informativeness, access to rich linked resources, command execution relevance, orderly presentation, and verbal richness, are unique to the context of human-machine voice interaction. Overall, we provide a new instrument for assessing voiceinteraction usability in the context of smart products. The instrument we developed can be used in future empirical studies associated with voice-interaction usability. For example, the scales could be used to investigate the effect of such usability on users’ attitudes and behavior. 

Third, our research extends CPT by adding a new contextspecific dimension to the existing theory and developing the subdimensions of each principle in the context of the voiceinteraction usability of smart products. Specifically, we identified a new dimension, anthropomorphism, which is a unique and significant characteristic of human-machine voice interaction and represents an extension of CPT, and summarized users’ perceived anthropomorphic components associated with voice interaction, including humanlike voice, verbal richness, personality, and empathy. Although it is not a significant factor in the interpersonal communication context, anthropomorphism is a key element that users are concerned about in the human-smart product voice-interaction context. In addition to adding anthropomorphism to extend CPT, we enrich CPT in another way by developing context-specific subdimensions of the four principles and operationalizing the related constructs in the context of voice interaction. Our findings advance the previous work on CPT by extending it from the human-human context to the human-machine context. 

Fourth, we contribute to the voice-interaction usability literature by exploring and validating the effect of the five dimensions of voice-interaction usability on use behavior in relation to smart products. Our findings indicate that quantity, quality, relation, manner, and anthropomorphism are all positively related to intention to use and use behavior. Compared with the classical models of TAM, UTAUT, and MUG, we empirically show that these five dimensions of voice-interaction usability can better explain the variance in user intentions and behavior in relation to smart products, thus contributing to our understanding of usability in the voiceinteraction context. 

## Practical Implications

This study has important implications for practitioners. First, since the voice-interaction usability of smart products includes quantity, quality, relation, manner, and anthropomorphism, managers could optimize the design and functions of voicecontrolled smart products to improve usability. For example, based on the quantity principle, since consumers want access to rich linked resources, organizations should actively expand third-party platform audio resources. In terms of quality, improving machine learning algorithms so that smart products can better learn the usage habits of users and produce personalized responses could be beneficial. Improving users perceived accuracy of smart product responses is also important. Product developers should improve the contextual awareness of the smart product so that it can better understand users’ voice input. The relation dimension is associated with the enhancement of natural language processing technology. Managers should test whether the voice output of the smart product is easy to understand and succinct and whether it presents information in an orderly fashion when replying to complex queries. Managers should also consider the anthropomorphic factors that users might perceive during voice interactions, including tone, emotional expression, verbal richness, and empathetic verbal expression. 

Second, our voice-interaction usability scale can be used by both managers and users to evaluate smart products. Managers could use the scale as a theoretical reference to design and develop voice-interaction functions, evaluate the usability of voice interactions, and investigate consumers attitudes toward voice-interaction usability. Our scale could also be used to improve the usability of products and compare a product’s usability relative to a competitor’s product. Users could employ our scale to choose voicecontrolled smart products with high usability before they make purchase decisions. Our scale could also be used in smart product co-creation projects to provide insights and unleash creativity in voice-interaction design. 

Third, our results show that all five aspects of voiceinteraction usability are positively associated with the intention to use and use behavior of smart products. Thus, our findings suggest that managers could increase the use of smart products by improving the quantity, quality, relation, manner, and anthropomorphism attributes of smart products. Given that these dimensions reflect the aspects of voice interaction that users are concerned about, managers could design advertisements and promote their products based on these aspects to persuade consumers to try or purchase smart products. 

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

The development of our voice-interaction scale is based on the coding results in Phase 1, which may represent a limitation. Some existing usability-related concepts (e.g., response time) that may be applicable to our research context were not found in our qualitative data. Future research could incorporate more data from other sources to validate our dimensions. In addition, the user reviews we analyzed were based on five smart speakers manufactured and used in China and designed to communicate primarily in Mandarin Chinese. The expression of Mandarin Chinese may be different from that of other languages. Future work could validate our scales using smart products employing other languages in other countries. Smart products are being rapidly developed, and new smart products are currently or will soon be emerging in various contexts—for example, driverless cars. It is likely that for smart products that are used in specific contexts, the usability of voice interaction may include context-related content. Future research could examine voice interaction in specific contexts and which dimensions of voice-interaction usability are most critical in different circumstances. For example, in specific contexts, such as the use of social robots in elder healthcare or the use of voice interaction in self-driving cars, the dimensions of usability may be different and contain context-specific attributes. In summary, it is our hope that the dimensions and measurement instrument we developed will provide a theoretical foundation for future research in the important area of voice-interaction usability.

## Table A1. The Coding Scheme Development Process

In developing the coding scheme, we first identified existing dimensions or categories of usability (see Tables 1 and 2 in the main text). This step provided us with insights that helped us in understanding the qualitative data. Then, the first author and another coder randomly selected 10% of the reviews to work with before open coding the whole sample. In the precoding process, two coders compared the qualitative data with the existing categories of usability. The coders found that there were four aspects needing attention when compiling a coding scheme. First, many existing categories cannot be applied to the human-smart product voice-interaction context. Second, some categories are general and can be further explored, such as the dimension of content. User reviews on voice interaction cover multiple aspects of content (e.g., knowledgeable, access to rich copyrighted content, reliable content, and content with high accuracy). Third, some open codes of user reviews related to voice interaction cannot fit with any categories in the existing studies (e.g., multiple linked resource types, plenty of linked resources, access to rich copyrighted content, and the execution result is related to the command). Fourth, users express similar content using different words. Following the open coding process and iterative comparative techniques (Corbin & Strauss, 1990), in the coding scheme, we removed the codes not relevant to research and aggregated the open codes that reflect similar insights. The coding scheme is shown in Tables 5 to 9 in the main text. When coding the whole sample, two coders coded the qualitative data using the coding scheme. We coded all the reviews containing information related to voice interaction. The two coders put open codes that reveal similar insights into the same category. The coders also examined whether any new open codes related to voice interaction emerged and added them into the coding scheme. After the coders independently coded all quantitative data, developed the open codes, and aggregated them, we came back to the theory (CPT) and compared the codes with the theory. We found that some codes fit this theory well. The remaining codes related to anthropomorphism, and thus CPT can be applied and extended in this research context.
