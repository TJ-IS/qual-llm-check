## Abstract

Most contemporary studies on information security focus on largely static phenomena in examining security-related behaviours. We take a more dynamic, situational and interactionist approach that proposes that security-related behaviours result from an interaction between the person and the perception of a threatening situation. We derive and define situational information security awareness based on situation awareness literature, and examine how individuallevel (innate traits, experience) and system-level factors (design variations, warning signal) influence awareness, and how it influences subsequent threat and coping appraisals, and ultimately security-related behaviours in a multi-method phishing experiment including eye tracking and survey components with 107 employees. The results underscore the importance of situational information security awareness and show that past experience with phishing and a security warning increase awareness, while phishing emails' contextual relevance and misplaced salience decrease awareness. Situational information security awareness, in turn, increases perceived threat and perceived coping efficacy and, ultimately, actual behavioural responses to phishing attacks.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Phishing emails have been common for decades and are considered a top security threat by both security researchers and governmental authorities, who expect the problem to get even worse (Erb, 2018; Landewe, 2019). The reason is quite simple: the majority of phishing-related incidents are attributed to the often unaware behaviour of individuals who regularly fall prey to phishing attacks (Hong, 2012). Many of the most devastating security attack in history, such as the Locky ransomware attacks, had their roots in a phishing email (Mathews, 2017). In this case, a single email opened by a single user with sufficient access to an organization's server enabled cyber attackers to hold large volumes of encrypted data for ransom. Consequently, cybersecurity researchers (Kaspersky Lab, 2019; Palmer, 2016) and governmental authorities (Chanthadavong, 2017; Erb, 2018) have issued warnings about thi increasing threat to corporate and public sector networks. Because the sole reliance on security software (eg, spam filter) to prevent phishing attacks does not provide sufficient protection, the burden of detecting and coping with phishing remains on the shoulders of the individual user (Wang, Li, & Rao, 2017)

The dominant assumption in information security research is that individuals are rational actors who deliberatel plan their behaviours in advance and are aware of the benefits and costs of their responses (Dennis & Minas, 2018) For example, information security awareness, defined as general knowledge about information security and cogni zance of one's organizations security policies, represents a key element in the formation of beliefs, attitudes and intentions towards policy compliance (Bulgurcu, Cavusoglu, & Benbasat, 2010). Appendix A provides additional con ceptualizations of information security awareness. While these conceptualizations differ according to their stud context, they have the common denominator of being considered as a product, that is, a state of knowledge. While the assumption of the aware user may hold true in some information security contexts, such static, pre-conceived notions of awareness are less appropriate to capture the role that a situation plays in influencing awareness and sub sequent behaviours. This is because static awareness concepts are most often formed before individuals actually per form security-related behaviours and thus do not reflect the situation-specific facets of the process individual follow while performing them. For example, users may generally be aware about the security threat of phishing but may still click on unknown links or attachments due to a lack of situation-specific awareness of the threat (Alsharnouby, Alaca, & Chiasson, 2015; Dhamija, Tygar, & Hearst, 2006; Moody, Galletta, & Dunn, 2017)

Accordingly, the assumption of the aware user is challenged by studies on the behavioural aspects of phishing, which try to ascertain why phishing works, and how it can be detected and prevented. Questions about why phish ing works are fundamentally questions about awareness: when individuals fall for a phishing email, did they deliber ately assess the situation (eg, check URL in the link) or did they click on it without much deliberate thought (Dennis & Minas, 2018)? To answer this question, studies have mainly investigated the impact of email recipients individual characteristics (eg, demographics, personality traits, experience, knowledge), and the characteristics of the email (eg, sender address, gain or loss-framed messages) itself. Appendix B summarizes the results of previous research in greater detail. However, we posit that this question cannot be fully answered by assessing awareness through general knowledge and experience constructs (Algarni, Xu, & Chan, 2017; Moody et al., 2017), through pro ficiency tests (Wright & Marett, 2010), or asking participants about their attention towards security-related cues after the experiment (Vishwanath, Herath, Chen, Wang, & Rao, 2011; Wang, Herath, Chen, Vishwanath, & Rao, 2012). How people respond to queries is only an indirect reflection of their phenomenological awareness, becaus they are removed from the situation. In a similar vein, some experimental studies reminded their participants that they were about to judge phishing emails (Pattinson, Jerram, Parsons, McCormac, & Butavicius, 2012; Wang et al. 2017). Yet, priming participants into a phishing situation may be too removed from reality to be ecologically valid (D. Straub & Gefen, 2004), because it artificially raises their awareness.

As a departure from prior studies that focus on static aspects of awareness as a product detached from th actual situation, we take an approach that proposes that security-related behaviours result from an interactio between the person and the perception of the situation. Thus, our basic premise is that security-related behaviours are grounded within the context of the environment in which they occur and requires assessing the encountered situation. To conceptualize individual's actual awareness in a security-related situation in, we introduce the construct of situational information security awareness. It is derived from the perceptual cycle model (PCM) of situation aware ness (Smith, Hancock, & Peter, 1995), which considers both the product (ie, state of knowledge) and process (how that knowledge is created through interaction with the environment) of situation awareness. Our research aims to answer the following two research questions:

RQ1. What determines users' situational information security awareness?

## RQ2. How does users' situational information security awareness influence security-related behaviour?

To address the research questions, we develop and empirically test a model of situational information security awareness by drawing on prior research on situation awareness, phishing literature, and protection motivation theory (PMT). Specifically, our model integrates key factors that draw on the interaction between the individual and his or her system environment in achieving awareness. This includes factors at the individual level (e.g., innate traits, expe rience) and system-level (e.g., email design, warning signal). Furthermore, once an event or situation is noted, prio coping and protection motivation literature argue that individuals evaluate how threatening the situation is and the resources at their disposal to cope with it (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Rogers, 1983). Situational information security awareness, which provides the contextual knowledge basis of the situation required for the appraisal process, is thus instrumental in explaining how users actually cope with a threatening situation. We carried out a multi-method phishing experiment including the use of eye tracking and a post-experimental survey with 107 employees. Our results suggest that phishing experience and the receipt of a security warning increase situational information security awareness, while phishing emails' contextual relevance and misplaced salience decrease it. Situational informa tion security awareness, in turn, increases perceived threat and perceived coping efficacy, fear, protection motivation and ultimately security-related behaviours.

The richness of experimental (eye tracking and screen recording) and survey data collected enabled us to capture the process of how users collect security-related information cues of phishing and their responses to phishing emails which we could not have done by using a survey only. Shedding light on which security-related information cues users view or do not view and how this influences threat and coping appraisals and, ultimately, behaviour responds to calls from information security researchers demanding further insights into the complex interaction between infor mation processing and decision making by using cognitive neuroscience and its associated neurophysiological mea sures, such as eye tracking, applied to information security issues (Anderson, Vance, Kirwan, Eargle, & Jenkins, 2016) Our study also contributes to practice. If users fail to recognize a threat and consequently do not appraise it as bein one, their own and their organization's information security posture is weakened. Accordingly, this study also point to useful training and design recommendations to increase users' situational information security awareness.

The paper proceeds as follows. First, we provide the theoretical background for situational information securit awareness and its key antecedents and consequences. We then develop the hypotheses of our research model. The subsequent sections present the research methodology, data analysis and results. Finally, we discuss the theoretica contributions and practical implications of our study, and conclude by addressing limitations and offering recommen dations for future research.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Situational information security awareness

We derive and define situational information security awareness based on research into awareness rooted in humar factors (Adams, Tenney, & Pew, 1995; Smith et al., 1995). In this research stream, scholars examine what they cal situation awareness, which describes the level of awareness that an individual has of a situation (Salmon et al., 2008). Prior research refers to situation awareness (Salmon et al., 2008) either as the product of awareness (ie knowledge that one can make use of; Endsley, 1995) or the process of gaining awareness (ie, how that knowledge i created through interaction with the environment; Fracker, 1991), or as a combination of the two (Adams et al., 1995; Smith et al., 1995). Although there is no universal definition of situation awareness (Salmon et al., 2008), it ha been argued that the construct demands an understanding of both the product and the process (Salmon et al., 2008 Stanton, Chambers, & Piggott, 2001)

In this regard, perceptual cycle models (PCM) of situation awareness (Adams et al., 1995; Smith et al., 1995) seem to offer the most comprehensive description of how awareness is achieved and maintained. These models have solid underpinning theory based on Neisser's (1976) original PCM and are comprehensive in that they refer to the continuous cycle of awareness acquisition and maintenance, encompassing both the product and the process of awareness (Salmon et al., 2008). According to Neisser (1976), knowledge in the form of schemata leads to the antici pation of certain information that directs exploratory movements and increases an individual's receptivity to particu lar aspects of the available information. As the schema absorbs information that an individual picks up from th environment, it serves in turn to modify the information that the schema is prepared to receive next further guiding exploration. Thus, the perceptual cycle combines both product and process aspects of awareness: ‘as product, it is the state of the active schema—the conceptual frame or context that governs the selection and interpretation of events. As process, it is the state of the perceptual cycle at any given moment. As process and product, it is the cycli cal resetting of each by the other’ (Adams et al. (1995, p. 89).

Transferred to information security, when users enter the system environment to do a particular task, such a read an email, their initial awareness level (ie, the state of active schema) directs what they look for, such as onl reading the message or also attending to security-related information cues (eg, sender address, URL link). The infor mation users collect from the system environment is interpreted in consideration of their existing knowledge to hel them determine what is happening and also to help them to anticipate future states in which they might find them selves, for instance, if they click on a link or attachment. These anticipations further refine their perceptual sensitiv ity, as when the expectation of seeing one security-related information cue sensitizes a user to subtle variations o other cues in the email. Thus, users demonstrate their situational information security awareness by attending to more security-related information cues (eg, sender address, URL link), because their state informs them about th legitimacy of an email and thus also about future phishing risks

Thus, we define situational information security awareness as a user's knowledge of particular security threat transported by security-related information cues captured in a situational process in the immediate system environ ment (Adams et al., 1995; Smith et al., 1995). We will describe the way we measured situational information securit awareness by using eye tracking within the research method section

## 2.2 | Antecedents of situational information security awareness

PCM emphasizes the interaction between the individual and his or her environment in achieving and maintaining sit uation awareness and integrates top-down and bottom-up information processing. The former being schema-driven processing, in which an individual's schema directs perceptual exploration of available environmental information and the latter being environmentally-driven processing, in which certain environmental cues trigger perceptual explo ration. Accordingly, several factors related to both the individual as well as the system environment that influenc the key mechanisms of PCM have been identified in previous research approaches (Adams et al., 1995; Endsley 1995; Smith et al., 1995). These approaches share many commonalities that indicate key mechanisms that are also important for situational information security awareness.

Individuals differ in their ability to achieve situation awareness, given the same information input from the envi ronment. Experience as well as innate traits and abilities are hypothesized to influence an individual's informationprocessing mechanisms in the development of awareness (Endsley, 1995). Because schemas are developed over time with experience, more experienced individuals are better at pattern matching, that is, matching the patterns of a cur rent situation with a schema, to achieve situation awareness (Endsley & Jones, 2012). In addition, a person's predis position to be more trusting in general than others has been shown to lead to lower situation awareness, as they also tend to place too much trust in the displayed information in a system and are less capable of detecting system failures when they occur (Aydogan, Sharpanskykh, & Lo, 2015; Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2008). In ou study, we consider experience as past encounters with phishing emails and websites as well as the personality trai of agreeableness due to the more trusting nature of agreeable individuals

The degree to which any individual can achieve situation awareness is greatly affected by the environment and by the design of the system they use (Endsley, 1995). Since situation awareness depends on the information that i available to the individual in the environment according to PCM, contextual relevance of information, which characterizes the pertinence of information to the particular situation, task or problem at hand (Wilson, 1973), is fundamen tal. Accordingly, system capabilities and interface design need to manage and present critical information in a meaningful and easy to process format (Endsley, 1995). Since phishing attackers can take advantage of which infor mation they want to display to the individual in their email message, our study considers contextual relevance of mail as the pertinence of the email message premise to the recipient, such as the alignment to his or her work contex (Hong, 2012; Steves, Greene, & Theofanos, 2019).

Although individuals will generally attempt seek out information relevant to their goals (top-down informatio processing), their attention will be caught by information that is highly salient and trigger bottom-up processing. In many systems, there is a proliferation of signals drawing individual's attention, often either misleading or overwhelm ing them. Misplaced salience refers to the overuse of prominent visual features that overwhelm and misdirect individ uals' attention, thereby negatively influencing situation awareness (Endsley & Jones, 2012). For example, th overuse of colours, buttons, company logos or pictures of a person can be used by phishing attackers to distract the individual from more important information that may reveal the email's true nature.

The critical cues used for activating an individual's mental models and schemata could be also made salient by a security warning as a trigger (Endsley & Jones, 2012). Security warnings are designed to call users' attentio to important information that they may be unaware of because they are distracted, or they lack vigilance Whereas designers' philosophy behind security warnings is that this is the only information that users need to decide what to do, in reality the warning is just one information cue that is processed in addition to all othe information available in the situation. Thus, users often search for confirming evidence to determine whether the warning indicates a real problem before responding to it (Endsley & Jones, 2012). In our study, we consider a security warning as discrete communication that is designed to persuade individuals to improve their information security posture (Anderson et al., 2016). Specifically, a red pop-up identifying a website as malicious, describing the threats related to the site and offering different coping mechanisms (exit the website, retrieving more infor mation or ignore the warning).

## 2.3 | Consequences of situational information security awareness

To study the particular consequences of situational information security awareness, we draw on protection motiva tion theory (PMT, Rogers, 1975, 1983). PMT describes a process that starts with the receipt of information (sources of information), which leads to an assessment of the information by the recipient of the information (cognitive medi ating process), and ultimately to the use of this information to take action (coping mode). The use of situational infor mation security awareness as sources of information is reasonable because it describes a generative process of knowledge creation and informed action taking, specifically how individuals acquire contextual knowledge which is directly derived, in part, from the perceptual information presented to individuals in their immediate situation (Smith et al., 1995). As per coping theory upon which PMT was, in part, originally based, such processes inform the development of the cognitive appraisals. For example, the clear distinction between contextual knowledge and appraisal, and the dependence of appraisal upon knowledge, is emphasized in Lazarus and Smith (1988).

What information is gathered in the situation produces specific effects on decision making, but how this process unfolds depends on the way the individual evaluates and interprets the different elements of the situation, namel the threat element and the coping response as conveyed by security-related cues. This interpretative process results in threat and coping appraisals. In this sense, situation awareness can be used to derive both the threat component and the coping response provided by a situation. PMT, on the other hand, is particularly effective in describing the underlying mechanisms through which the security-related cues in a situation produce their effects. Taken together we believe that situation awareness can help answering what questions, whereas PMT can provide further answers to how questions. This distinction between contextual knowledge and appraisal enables seamless theoretical integra tion. Thus, in our study, we integrate situational information security awareness as a key information source influenc ing threat and coping appraisals in a security-related context. The appropriateness of integrating situation awarenes and PMT is further elaborated in Appendix C.

During threat appraisal, individuals form beliefs regarding the degree of threat existing in a situation. They gen erate a subjective evaluation of the threat in that situation, which is perceived threat. Perceived threat has two dimensions, namely perceived threat severity (ie, individuals' beliefs about the seriousness of the threat's conse quences) and perceived threat vulnerability (ie, individuals' beliefs about the likelihood that they will experience the threat). Together, perceived threat severity and perceived threat vulnerability determine the magnitude of perceive threat (Wang et al., 2017). Thus, it is theoretically reasonable to consider perceived threat as a formative second order construct comprising both perceived threat severity and perceived threat vulnerability (Wang et al., 2017) Once a threat is perceived, fear, a negatively valenced emotion occurs (Floyd, Prentice-Dunn, & Rogers, 2000). Dur ing coping appraisal, individuals form beliefs regarding their ability to control the threats. The appraisal allows generation of a subjective evaluation of coping capability in the situation, that is perceived coping efficacy. Perceived coping efficacy has two dimensions as well, namely perceived response efficacy (ie, individuals' beliefs that the recommended behaviour will effectively reduce the threat) and perceived self-efficacy (individuals' beliefs in their ability to successfully perform the recommended behaviour). We also include both components to give a more holistic pic ture of perceived coping efficacy and consider perceived coping efficacy as a formative second-order construct according to the literature (Floyd et al., 2000; Popova, 2012; Witte & Allen, 2000). Perceived threat, fear and perceived coping efficacy increase protection motivation, that is, individuals' intention to protect themselves from a potential threat. Protection motivation, in turn, results in protective behaviours (Witte & Allen, 2000).

## 3.1 | Antecedents of situational information security awareness

Based on situation awareness literature, an individual's awareness is influenced by individual factors (eg, innate abili ties and experience) and system-related factors (eg, interface design and warning signals; Endsley, 1995; Salmon et al., 2008). Initially, we focus on characteristics of the individual, including relevant information-processing mechanisms and components that play a role in achieving situational information security awareness. In particular we propose that experience with phishing and the personality trait of agreeableness are important factors that influ ence situational information security awareness. Then, we focus on system-level attributes and propose that contex tualized relevance of and misplaced salience in a phishing email, as well as the receipt of a security warning influenc situational information security awareness.

Over time, users may encounter different security-related situations, based on which they will develop a menta library of prototypical situations, or schemata (Rumelhart, 1984). By matching the patterns of the current situatio with a schema, users can quickly recognize known classes of situations (Sarter & Woods, 1991), such as having seen an email like the one at hand before. Schemata are formed by direct experience or indirectly through training or sto rytelling (Fiske & Taylor, 1991). A key feature of schemata is that new situations do not have to be exactly like prior situations; rather, only a few critical cues may be needed to achieve a match. The crucial factors for achieving awareness here is the recognition of the critical cues used for pattern matching and having a good stock of such schemata in memory (Endsley & Jones, 2012).

Schemata are very useful in the process of developing situational information security awareness (Mogford, 1997: Sarter & Woods. 1991) in the context of phishing. They provide knowledge about which components in ar email message are relevant, and which critical information cues are significant (eg, a sender's address). This knowl edge is crucial for directing attention in gathering and classifying information in the schema-driven perception process of PCM (ie, top-down information processing; Endsley & Jones, 2012). Just as experienced drivers dem onstrate their situation awareness by scanning the lane of traffic around them more extensively than inexperi enced drivers (Underwood, Ngai, & Underwood, 2013), users with prior phishing experience are expected to demonstrate their situational information security awareness by attending to more security-related information cues (eg, sender address, URL link), because their state is informative about the authenticity of an email. Thus we hypothesize:

## H1. Prior phishing experience positively impacts situational information security awareness.

Another important individual-level characteristic is the match between the phishing email and the recipient' personality (Dennis & Minas, 2018). Agreeableness as one of the Big Five personality traits is considered to b especially relevant in a phishing context (Dennis & Minas, 2018). Agreeableness is cooperation and harmony wit others (Barrick & Mount, 1991). Agreeable individuals are more optimistic, trusting, and willing to compromise wit others (Barrick & Mount, 1991). They believe that individuals with whom they interact are usually honest and hav good intentions (Rolland, 2002). Because they are more trusting, they are also more susceptible to phishing (Cho Cam, & Oltramari, 2016).

In examining the mechanisms that make agreeable individuals to be more susceptible to phishing, one explanation could be that their levels of situation information security awareness are lower than that of less agreeable indi viduals. Agreeableness is a genuinely social-interpersonal domain (Costa, McCrae, & Robert, 1992). Since email contain social information, it is likely that agreeable individuals show distinct attention patterns when confronte with social stimuli (Rauthmann, Seubert, Sachse, & Furtner, 2012). In particular, we would expect that when asked to click on a link or download an attachment, their trusting nature drives them to be less suspicious. While, individuals low on agreeableness are more skeptical and less likely to trust an email (Cho et al., 2016), agreeable individuals pay less attention to security-related information. Consequently, their situation information security awareness suffer by being less attentive to more security-related cues (eg, sender address, URL link) that would increase their suspi cions. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H2. Agreeableness negatively impacts situational information security awareness.

To perform their tasks, individuals search for specific information in the system environment, such as task relevant information in an email (top-down processing of PCM). Phishing attackers can take advantage of this and tailor phishing mails to the individual's context. Contextual relevance of phishing emails characterizes the pertinenc of the email message premise to the recipient, such as the alignment to his or her work context (Hong, 2012; Steve et al., 2019). Recent research proposed that the greater contextual relevance of a phishing email, the less likely an individual is to notice, attend to, and think deeply about suspicious cues (Steves et al., 2019). For example, when an employee is working in the office and the phishing email is purportedly from HR. In contrast, when the phishing mai premise is not well aligned to an individual's work context, they are more likely to attend suspicious cues. In this case if the employee receives a phishing email purportedly from his or her private bank. Consequently, if the alignment of the phishing mail's premise and employee context affects which cues the employee finds to be salient, then impor tant security-related cues might not be attended to if other cues manipulated by the phisher are fitted to th employee's context, thereby undermining situational information security awareness. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H3. Contextual relevance negatively impacts situational information security awareness.

Many types of information compete for an individuals' attention, and individuals will usually try to seek out information that is pertinent to their goals. For example, an employee may search for a particular task instruction in an email among competing side information and design elements such as buttons, company logos, or pictures. B doing so, the employee's attention will be caught by highly salient information, which triggers bottom-up processing of PCM. Salience refers to the compellingness of certain forms of information and is mostly determined by its physi cal characteristics (Endsley & Jones, 2012). The human perceptual system is more sensitive to certain signal charac teristics than others (Endsley & Jones, 2012)

Salient properties can be also used by phishers to hinder the development of situational information security awareness. For example, buttons, company logos or pictures of a person can be used to distract the individua from more important information that may reveal the email's true nature. This creates the so-called Las Vegas Strip phenomenon (Endsley & Jones, 2012). If too much information draw attention, it is difficult to process any of it well. The brain tries to block out all competing signals to give attention to the desired information, which uses up considerable cognitive resources. In phishing emails, misplaced salience, that is, the overuse of prominent visual features in an email, could work to draw users' attention and either misleading or overwhelming them. We expect that in an email with salient design elements as opposed to an email with plain text employees are less attentive to security-related cues, thereby undermining their awareness of the situation. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4. Misplaced salience negatively impacts situational information security awareness.

Mental models and schemata are key features used for achieving higher levels of awareness. The critical cues used for activating these mechanisms need not only be part of an email's characteristics but could be also made salient by a security warning as a trigger condition for bottom-up processing in the PCM. The characteristics of a security warning may affect the degree to which a warning supports situational information security awareness. Security warnings can be classified along three primary dimensions: immediacy, relevancy and complexity (Anderson et al., 2016). Immediacy describes the degree to which a security warning can be deferred. An immediate warning demands attention to information cues because users need to develop an understanding of the situation, such a why the warning occurred and what they should do next. Relevancy refers to the applicability of a security warning to a user's present task (Anderson et al., 2016). Users more likely process security warnings that are clearly applicabl to the tasks they are currently engaged in (Vredenburgh & Zackowitz, 2006). Complexity concerns the required men tal effort to process the message (Anderson et al., 2016). The more complex the message, the more users need to gather additional information cues to arrive at a decision as what to do when they have different options (eg, leaving the website, searching for reasons for the warning, or ignoring the warning). When a warning does not display information in a way that allows individuals to quickly assess whether the warning is indicative of a real problem and choose a response, they will seek other information to confirm or deny it (Endsley & Jones, 2012).

Consider the situation of users who click on a link in an email. They will check whether the evolving situatio conforms to their expectations, such as whether the content of the website matches what is promised in the email However, a red warning dialog pops up that marks the website they are trying to access as malicious. Per PCM, such an unexpected event prompts bottom-up information processing, that is, further search and explanation of the envi ronmental information that is received. So, instead of responding to this warning right away, the users may first attend to other information cues by for instance returning to the email to check the email sender's address or other cues. The information the users pick up from this unexpected event and other information in the environment in turn modifies or updates the users' existing schema (Smith et al., 1995). Consequently, we expect that a security warning is one important information cue that serves to prompt further search and explanation in the sense that users atten to more security-related information cues to develop an awareness of the situation. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5. An immediate, relevant, and complex security warning positively impacts situational information security awareness.

## 3.2 | Consequences of situational information security awareness

While impacted by individual and system-related factors, situational information security awareness itself influences the threat appraisal and coping appraisal processes. Lazarus and Folkman (1984) maintain that individuals do not react to stressful situations if they do not even notice them. In other words, individuals do not respond to negativ situations if they are unaware of the impact these situations have on them. Yet, once the situation is noted, then individuals evaluate how threatening the situation is and the resources at their disposal to cope with it. Thus, situa tional information security awareness provides the contextual knowledge basis required for the appraisal process which consists of an evaluation of whether and how the event is personally threatening. In other words, knowledge has to do with the facts of the situation and is directly derived in part from the perceptual information presented to the individual in the situation, whereas appraisal defines the personal significance of this situation for the individual' well-being (Lazarus & Smith, 1988).

Phishing emails serve as an indicator for users that sensitive information will be gathered, identity theft will b committed, or data will be corrupted. Being more aware about this particular security threat of a phishing attack which is transported by security-related information cues in emails, will lead to more accurate assessments of th likelihood of falling prey to phishing attacks (ie, perceived threat vulnerability) and the negative consequences caused by being a victim of them (ie, perceived threat severity). When users believe that they are vulnerable to phishing attacks and that the consequences of being victimized are serious, perceived threat of phishing attacks will result (Wang et al., 2017). Conversely, individuals who are less aware of the security-related situation will underestimate the risks associated with it and fail to perceive phishing as a threat. At the same time, if individuals are aware of security-related situation, they will evoke the coping appraisal process to find the appropriate resources at their dis posal to cope with it. In phishing attacks, coping resources at their disposal could range from technical countermeasures (eg, security software) to procedural countermeasures (eg, not clicking on a potentially malicious link, not downloading an .exe file, deleting the email or archiving it in the spam folder, or informing the IT about the email) Knowing what is going on allows users to identify what countermeasures they can employ and whether these coun termeasures are effective. Having an up-to-the-minute understanding of the situation, they will feel more confident to take relevant actions to avoid or mitigate the threat of phishing (ie, perceived self-efficacy) and perceive that thei course of action is effective in reducing the threat (ie, perceived response efficacy), resulting in a higher perceive coping efficacy. We argue that situational information security awareness will have a positive effect on both per ceived threat and perceived coping efficacy. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H7. Situational information security awareness positively impacts perceived coping efficacy.

For theoretical cohesiveness, we also test the consequent relationships between constructs of the PMT nomology, including fear, protection motivation and actual security-related behaviours (Boss et al., 2015). Perceived threat and perceived coping efficacy induces an individual's protection motivation to deal with the threat (Boss et al. 2015; Floyd et al., 2000). Thus, when individuals have appraised phishing, including its consequences, as threatening as well as believe they can take the appropriate response and consider it effective, they will be more motivated to protect themselves (Herath et al., 2014; Lai, Li, & Hsieh, 2012). In addition, perceived threat of phishing increases fear of phishing, which is a negatively valenced emotion with regard to the risk of being victimized by phishing attacks (Wang et al., 2017). When users exhibit fear related to phishing attacks, they are more motivated to protect themselves from such a threat (Boss et al., 2015). Protection motivation, in turn, results in security-related behaviours (Boss et al., 2015), such as actual behaviours of protecting oneself from phishing by applying technical countermeasures (eg, use of security software) or procedural countermeasures (eg, not clicking on a potentially maliciou link, not downloading an .exe file, deleting the email or archiving it in the spam folder, and/or informing the IT department about the email).

## 4 | RESEARCH METHOD

To test our research model, we followed a multi-method approach consisting of a phishing experiment with employees in organizations of different industries using eye tracker technology together with a post-experimenta survey. Employees were invited via email sent by our contact person at the respective participating organization Descriptions of the study indicated that we wanted to observe email and Internet use behaviour in organizations; as in previous phishing studies ( Moody et al., 2017) participants were not told about the true purpose of the study prior to it but were debriefed after the study. We also clarified that the purpose of the study was academic researc and that independent university scholars would conduct the study and analyse the results. Participation was volun tary, anonymous and unrewarded and participants could withdraw from the study at any time without consequences. We also consulted the organizations' data protection officers who gave their approval prior to the study. In total, 378 invitation emails were sent and 107 employees in organizations of 10 sectors located in Western Europ followed the invitation and completed the study (response rate: 28%). 55% of participants were male and 45% were female. The mean age of the subjects was 40 years (SD 10 years). Table D1 in Appendix D summarizes the demo graphic characteristics of our sample. In the following, we will provide details on the study design, describe our tech nical setup and data collection methods, and explain our measures.

## 4.1 | Study design

The study design is illustrated in Figure 2.

First, the experimenter welcomed the participant in a separate and quiet office room provided by the respective participating company. Although participants were not at their usual workstation, the rooms were set up to simulat a normal office environment. Afterwards, the consent form with experiment instructions was presented to the par ticipant. It suggested that the purpose of this study is to observe email and Internet use behaviour, as noted in th previous section. The participant was to take the role of an employee in a business department at a fictional com pany with the task to read and process 20 emails made available in the inbox of a webmail server similar to the inbox they use at their organization. For tasks within emails that prompted to access a website with usernames and passwords, usable accounts were provided. The participants were prompted to treat such information ‘as if they wer their own . These instructions were intended to make the accounts appear worthy of protection without overl emphasizing information security itself. Further, files needed for the tasks were created and located on the desktop of the computer. In case of questions during the task, an email address for the IT helpdesk was also provided. Afte giving consent, the participant was seated at a desktop PC and the eye tracker was calibrated to the participant Then, the task of reading and processing 20 emails started and the eye tracker began recording the participant's eye movements and screen activities. To avoid distracting participants, the experimenter left the room during the task Upon completion of the task, the participant was presented with a questionnaire. The questionnaire was postexperimental to ensure that participants' behaviour during the task would not be biased. Finally, the participant wa debriefed, told about the phishing emails and given recommendations on how to avoid being a victim of future rea phishing attacks (Resnik & Finn, 2018). On average, the whole study took 60 minutes.

The set of emails consisted of 14 legitimate emails with legitimate attachments or links leading to legitimate websites and six real-world phishing examples in which URL links to websites were fraudulent and aimed to lure indi viduals into submitting personal information or downloading malicious attachments. Appendix E provides a detailed overview of features of the phishing emails. To design our email tasks in our study setting as appropriate as possible for the involved employees, we gathered anonymous examples of internal email exchange within the participating organizations. The 14 legitimate emails contained ordinary requests such as sending information to a colleague's business email, accessing a supplier's website, setting up a meeting, approving an order or invoice and the like. Since reading and processing emails is among the most common daily routines in many white-collar jobs, we used them to ensure a setting that is as realistic as possible

## 4.2 | Technical setup and data collection methods

The technical setup consisted of three main components: the eye tracker, the web client and the webserver wit experiment-specific web content. The three components were applied as follows. First, we used the Tobii Pro X2-30 eye tracker, which is a small stand-alone eye tracker that can be attached to the bottom of the screen and is designed to capture data at 30 Hz. This unobtrusive eye tracker is primarily designed for research of natural behaviour outside the lab (Tobii, 2015), which suited specifically our needs to conduct our experiments in the participating organizations. The system latency is 50 to 70 ms and it has an immediate gaze recovery time for blinks (Tobii, 2015). The eye tracker has a gaze accuracy, which describes the angular average distance from the actual gaze point to the one measured by the eye tracker, of $0 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ and a gaze precision, which describes the spatial variation between individ ual gaze samples, of $0 . 3 2 ^ { \circ }$ (Tobii, 2015). In a comparison of 12 different eye trackers, the average gaze accuracy ranges from $0 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ to $2 ^ { \circ }$ and precision from 0.005 to $0 . 5 ^ { \circ }$ , indicating that data quality can vary considerably across eye trackers (Holmqvist et al., 2015). The Tobii eye tracker has been previously used in academic research to evalu ate marketing stimuli (Khushaba et al., 2013), understanding learning processes (Shareghi Najar, Mitrovic, & Neshatian, 2015) and identifying key user interface features of ransomware attacks (Arief, Periam, Cetin, & Hernandez-Castro, 2020). During the experiment, all screen activities were recorded for analysis using Tobii Pro Stu dio eye tracking software (Tobii, 2016), which tracks the participants' eye fixations, saccades, pupil dilations and also all behavioural actions on the screen.

Second, the web client ran on a PC with Windows 7 and all participants used the same browser (Firefox) to ensure that the types of visible security-related information cues were identical for all users. Third, while participants were able to access pages normally for links in non-phishing emails, links from phishing emails led to phishin websites hosted on our webserver. These spoofed websites mirrored regular pages and allowed the entry of logins of the user data provided for the experiment. To make our pages appear legitimate, we employed common technica deception measures used by phishers by including a mix of text-based subdomains in our URLs. ## 4.3 | Measures

Table F1 in Appendix F presents the questionnaire items for the latent constructs in the research model. The mea sures on the PMT constructs in our study were adopted from prior PMT literature successfully employed in the information security domain and modified to fit the constructs described in the research model in the context o phishing. Fear, perceived response efficacy and protection motivation were adapted from Boss et al. (2015), while perceived threat severity, perceived threat vulnerability, perceived self-efficacy and phishing experience were adapted from Zahedi, Abbasi, and Chen (2015). Agreeableness was taken from the 10-item Big Five Inventory (Rammstedt & John 2007), which is an abbreviated version of the well-established Big Five Inventory (John, Donahue, & Kentle, 1991).

As requested by prior research (Boss et al., 2015; Crossler et al., 2013) we were able to measure participants actual security-related behaviours as well as situational information security awareness using eye gaze and scree recording data collected by the eye tracking software.

Following prior research approaches in coding security-related behaviour in the realm of phishing (Wright & Marett, 2010), this study also included strict coding of the actions performed in response to our six phishing emails

Since the phishing efforts used in this study also consisted of accompanying web sites and attachments and thu considered the ‘bait-and-hook’ strategy used commonly by phishers, we initially focused on the ‘hook’ part in coding behaviour, as it is difficult to rationalize coding anyone as acting secure who (a) submitted their system login on a phishing website or (b) executed a malicious .exe-File in an attachment (Email #3). For these two actions, security related behaviour was coded ‘0’. Inaction, that is, not acting upon having read a phishing email but proceeding to the next email, was also coded as ‘0’, as it indicates users' lack of motivation to take protective action and maintaining the status quo (Chen, 2017). Since phishing attacks aim to receive a response, users are able to protect themselve by not clicking on fraudulent links or downloading malicious attachments, and by immediately reporting the phishing email to the proper organization authorities. Thus, security-related behaviour was coded as ‘1’ when the participant took protective action by either (a) deleting the email or archiving it into the spam folder or (b) notifying someon else about the phishing email (IT helpdesk, boss or colleague whose email addresses were provided for the experi ment). We then calculated the mean score for all six phishing emails. An overall descriptive view of the behavioura reactions to our six phishing emails is shown in Appendix G.

To recap, situational information security awareness is reflected in both participants' cognitive state of mind and the collection process in gathering security-related information cues in emails. In measuring awareness, we postulate that eye movements reflect the extent to which a user exerts a grip on the current environment (de Winter, Eisma Cabrall, Hancock, & Stanton, 2018; Peißl, Wickens, & Baruah, 2018). Eye tracking has also received wide adoption i reading research as it allows the inference of moment to moment cognitive processes during reading (Rayner, 1998) The eye tracking technology can help better understand what users actually look at when assessing the legitimacy of an email, the pattern of their eye movements, the areas they chose to fixate their gaze on, and many other metric not obtainable in traditional self-reporting methods. Users' control of eye movements and choice to focus their gaze onto a certain region is driven generally by their need to divert their attention. Therefore the data generated by the eye tracking method not only tells about the pattern of eye movements on a certain region but also informs about user's visual attention and perception (Poole & Ball, 2006; Rayner, 1998). Recent eye tracking research (de Winte et al., 2018; Underwood et al., 2013) further showed that situation awareness can be assessed by examining eye movements to so-called hazard precursors. Precursors are visual cues that put critical demands on the individual's understanding of a situation (de Winter et al., 2018). Security-related information cues in an email are similar to these hazard precursors because they inform about the authenticity of an email or the ‘hazard’ of a phishing email.

Thus, in order to measure a user's awareness, we first have to establish critical features in the email environment (eg, sender address), and whether the user has directed their attention to these features. To clarify, eye movements to security-related information cues may signal high awareness (because the user scans these task-relevant objects) whereas no eye movements to these cues may signal low awareness. Accordingly, we first pinpointed important areas of interest (AOI) in each email to capture processed security-related information cues (Rayner, 1998). As determined b prior research on phishing detection, these information cues included (a) subject line, (b) email source (‘from’ address) (c) form of greeting (personal vs impersonal), (d) signature, (e) privacy/security information below signature, (f) link URL and (g) file extension of attachment (Dhamija et al., 2006; Pfeiffer, Kauer, & Roeth, 2014; Whalen & Inkpen, 2005).

The component of users' cognitive state of mind was applied in our experimental setting by looking at partici pants' fixations on security-related information cues in emails as described above. This is based on the eye-mind hypothesis (Just & Carpenter, 1980), which proposes that the location of the eye fixation shows what the participant is thinking about or attending to at the time. Table G2 in Appendix G shows the amount and percentage of partici pants that fixated within the respective AOI at least once. The process component of awareness was captured by the number of viewed and successfully processed security-related information cues in the email. In this way, we postulate that each information cue provides additional evidence about whether an email is phishing (Molinaro & Bolton, 2018). Based on that, we finally measured situational information security awareness by the number of security-related information cues that were fixated in relation to the overall number of available security-relate information cues. The higher the ratio, the higher the situational information security awareness of the respective participant.

Contextual relevance characterizes the pertinence of the email message premise to the recipient, which we attempt to capture with the alignment with recipients' work responsibilities and business practice plausibility (Hong, 2012; Steves et al., 2019). Three categories to characterize the alignment are used: high, medium and low. For high contextual relevance, the email should match with the recipient's work responsibilities within the company, shoul be highly plausible, and/or strongly align with a business-relevant event. The alignment of phishing emails 1 and 2 i categorized as high. Email 1 contained a request to log in to the company's new HR website with the recipient's sys tem login in order to access a performance review. Email 2 contained a final reminder to finish a compliance training using the link provided. If the recipient failed to do so, their supervisor would be informed. Medium alignment is achieved with either case: (a) the email is allegedly internal but has weak alignment with the recipient's work respon sibilities or (b) the email is allegedly external but has strong alignment with the recipient's work responsibilities. Th alignment of phishing emails 3 and 4 is categorized as medium. Email 3 appeared to be sent from LinkedIn, thus external to the recipient's company, but contained a request to add a colleague from the same organization. Email 4 included a purportedly internal network activity report as attachment, but a better suited recipient would have been someone in the IT department rather than business department. Low alignment is achieved when the premise relates to a topic that is less relevant or plausible to the recipient's business context. The alignment of phishing emails 5 and 6 is categorized as low. Both emails appeared to come from outside the organization and their premis does not align with a business context but a more private one. Both emails requested to update personal information on either eBay (email 5) or a banking website (email 6). Finally, contextual relevance was encoded as a three-level var iable with low levels coded as 0 (email 5 and 6), medium levels coded as 1 (emails 3 and 4) and high levels coded as 2 (emails 1 and 2).

Misplaced salience was encoded as a binary variable with the phishing emails who had salient design feature including use of coloured, bold and underlined text as well as company logos, buttons and pictures as 1 (emails 1, 3 and 5) and the phishing emails with plain text and without any further design elements as 0 (emails 2, 4 and 6).

To ensure that our phishing mails accurately represent the respective levels of misplaced salience and contextua relevance, we asked 15 PhD students and scholars in the IS discipline to perform closed card sorting. Specifically, we asked them to assign each email to our levels of high, medium, or low contextual relevance and high or low misplaced salience. We then computed two metrics: the proportion of substantive agreement and the substantive-validity coef ficient (J. C. Anderson & Gerbing, 1991). Overall, the ratios were well above 80%, indicating that most respondent sorted the majority of emails into their posited levels.

Security warning was encoded as a binary variable (1 = received a security warning, n = 54; 0 = did not receive a security warning, n = 53). The security warning that we used (see Appendix I) was a red pop-up identifying a websit as malicious, describing the threats related to the site and offering different coping mechanisms (exit the website retrieving more information or ignore the warning). In line with the taxonomy of security messages (Anderson et al., 2016), our warning can be classified on the high spectrum of the binary, high/low classification of the dimensions of immediacy, relevancy and complexity. We also asked participants to rate the security warning's immediacy ( Th security warning interrupted my task ), relevancy ( The security warning was relevant to the task I was engaged in and complexity (‘Understanding the security warning was mentally demanding’) on Likert scales ranging from completely disagree (1) to completely agree (7). The mean scores were 6.1 (SD 1.2), 5.3 (SD 1.3) and 5.1 (SD 1.4) respectively, which provides reasonable assurance that our security warning ranged rather on the higher spectrum of the three dimensions of immediacy, relevancy and complexity (Anderson et al., 2016)

Corresponding with PMT and IS behaviour literature we also use control variables, including age (D'Arcy Hovav, & Galletta, 2009), gender (Herath & Rao, 2009), education (Zahedi et al., 2015), job experience (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010), computer use both at work and home (D'Arcy et al., 2009) and security habits (Zahedi et al., 2015).

## 5 | DATA ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

Given our within-subject differences for contextual relevance (H4) and misplaced salience (H5), we needed to split hypothesis testing of our proposed model in Figure 1 into two parts. First, antecedents of situational information security awareness (H1-H5) were analysed by means of a generalized form of the standard linear model tha accounts for both fixed and random effects (McLean, Sanders, & Stroup, 1991). We used a linear mixed effect mode instead of other common technigues. such as repeated measures analysis of variance, due to its advantages fo modelling individual change, including more flexibility regarding its data requirements (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002) Here, situational information security awareness is analysed per phishing mail scenario to capture the differences fo our email design of contextual relevance and misplaced salience. Second, to analyse consequences of situationa information security awareness (H6 and H7), we used partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modelling (Chin, 1998). Here, situational information security awareness is analysed using the averaged score across all email scenar ios. We chose PLS to deal with the complexity of second-order constructs and the different measurement types (Gefen, Rigdon, & Straub, 2011). We used the SmartPLS 3.0 software (Ringle, M, Wende, & Becker, 2015) for analyz ing both measurement and structural model.

## 5.1 | Hypotheses test part 1

Before testing the hypotheses related to H1 to H5, that is, the influence of phishing experience, agreeableness, mis placed salience, contextual relevance and security warning on situational information security awareness, we conducted preliminary tests to ensure the reliability and validity of the responses to phishing experience and agreeableness. The Cronbach alpha values of experience and agreeableness are 0.89 and 0.77, respectively, wel above 0.70, the generally acceptable level for adequate internal consistency. The same applies to composite reliabil ity values for experience and agreeableness of 0.93 and 0.89, respectively. The results of a principal component anal ysis to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the items indicate that the scales were valid (Bollen & Lennox, 1991). Regarding descriptive statistics, the average reported phishing experience was 6.03 (SD = 1.04) and the average reported agreeableness was 2.52 (SD = 1.01) on scales from 1 to 7, indicating that the respondents gen erally believed their previous encounters with phishing to be high and to be less agreeable

Next, we calculated two linear mixed-effect models with situational information security awareness as th dependent variable. One control variable model that would establish baseline fit statistics and our theoretical mode including these control variables that would need to improve on the baseline fit statistics to demonstrate predictive ability (Ramsey & Schafer, 2013). Several control variables were used in the analysis: (a) email type (the email seen by the participant); (b) age; (c) gender; (d) education; (e) work experience; (f) computer use at work; (g) computer use at home; and (h) security habit. To obtain a control variable model with optimum fit statistics non-significant control variables were dropped from the model. The final control variable model (see Figure 4 returned fit statistics of −133.623 for Akaike's information criterion (AIC) and −129.734 for Schwarz's Bayesian information criterion (BIC). These values established baseline fit statistics that our theoretical model needed to improve on with significantly lower AIC and BIC values (Ramsey & Schafer, 2013). Next, the theoretical vari ables were added to the model (see Figure 4). A likelihood ratio test to compare the full model's fit statistic (AIC = −145.257; BIC = −141.410) with the control variable model (AIC = −133.623; BIC = −129.734) confirms a significant improvement (P < .05), thereby providing significantly better predictability than the control variable model (Carte & Russell, 2003).

Our testing of the theoretical model shows that situational information security awareness increases with phish ing experience and the receipt of a security warning, supporting H1 and H5, and decreases with misplaced salienc and contextual relevance, supporting H3 and H4. We did not find a significant relationship between agreeablenes and situational information security awareness. Thus, H2 is not supported.

Effect sizes for our theoretical variables are calculated using Cohen's d (Brysbaert & Stevens, 2018; Cohen, 1988). We find effect sizes of 0.40 for experience, 0.06 for agreeableness, 0.25 for misplaced salience, 0.49 for contextual relevance and 0.57 for security warning. Following Cohen, effect sizes of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 are considered small, medium, and large, respectively (Cohen, 1988). By this standard, the sizes of the effects, except for agreeable ness, can be considered medium. We ran a post-hoc power analysis for all relationships based on the observed effect sizes using G\*Power 3.1 software (Faul, Erdfelder, Lang, & Buchner, 2007). The observed statistical power for all sig nificant relationships was well above 0.8 and therefore appropriate to identify potential effects (Cohen, 1992 Marcoulides & Saunders, 2006).

Finally, we conducted a post-hoc analysis on potential habituation (Vance, Jenkins, Anderson, Bjornn, & Kirwan 2018) by analyzing changes in situation information security awareness across the viewings of all 20 emails and plot ting gaze plots to evaluate if participants actually read the security warning (see Appendix I). As the results in Appen dix I indicate, we can conclude that habituation had not occurred.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

## 6.1 | Summary of findings

With the help of an elaborated experiment in the context of phishing, we examined the role of situational informa tion security awareness in influencing security-related behaviour. We also investigated several individual level and system-level antecedents to awareness and the consequent impacts of threat and coping appraisals on the extent of fear, protection motivation, and users' actual security-related behaviours

At the individual level, we show that the user characteristic of past experience with phishing plays a significant role in achieving situational information security awareness. Experienced users demonstrate their awareness by attending to more security-related information cues, which indicates that experience enables the development of schemata and recognizing the critical cues that activate pattern matching in the process of forming awareness. Con trary to our expectations, the personality trait agreeableness did not significantly influence situational information security awareness. Prior empirical work has also produced mixed results on the contribution of personality traits to phishing susceptibility with the majority reporting insignificant correlations to agreeableness (Sommestad & Karlzén 2019). In our case, being more agreeable did not translate in paying less attention to security-related cues, but w cannot rule out that agreeable individuals could still fall for a phishing email by being influenced by compelling parts of the email text rather than the cues

At the system-level, we find that situational information security awareness is negatively impacted by contextua relevance and misplaced salience. Users pay attention to fewer information security-related cues in phishing mes sages when the premise of the phishing email is aligned with their work context, compared to when it is misaligned In addition, phishing emails with salient design elements (eg, logos, images of individuals) draw user's attention mor away from security-related cues than phishing emails with plain text. Further, we find that a security warning posi tively impacts situational information security awareness. Users who received a security warning as a trigger condi tion attended to more information cues, which indicates that warnings can serve as a critical cue to activate th mechanisms of pattern matching and could be integrated in users' existing schemata

With regard to consequences of situational information security awareness, we find that it influences both the development of threat and coping appraisals. Concerning threat appraisal, our findings indicate that situational information security awareness is a significant determinant for perceived threat (perceived vulnerability and perceived severity). Scrutinizing emails for security indicators offers an informational basis that the threat of phishing indeed applies to users' circumstances and that there is a high probability of occurrence, and also provides information on how phishing may cause consequential harm. Further, whereas we do not find support for a direct relationship between perceived threat and protection motivation, our findings show an indirect impact via fear of phishing. In other words, when users perceive phishing as threatening, the fear of phishing is generated as an outcome, whic strongly invokes the motivation to take protective actions against phishing. In terms of coping appraisal following the reasoning that the overall amount of coping ability users experience is the combination of self-efficacy and response efficacy, our findings indicate that users who are aware of the security-related situation also feel more con fident to take relevant actions and perceive that these actions are effective, thus exhibit a higher perceived coping efficacy, which in turn also positively impacts protection motivation. Last but not least, we find a significant relation ship between protection motivation and actual security-related behaviour, which addresses one of the main goals o information security research, that is, changing actual behaviour and not just intentions (Crossler et al., 2013)

## 6.2 | Implications for research

Our study contributes to research on various levels, from broadly proposing a dynamic, situational, and interactionist approach to security behaviours in the form of situational information security awareness to providing specific con tributions in terms of empirical findings. The theoretical contributions of our overall model are further evaluated in Appendix C2, following recommendations of evaluating and developing theories in IS following the approach of Weber (2012).

The key theoretical contributions of the study are threefold. First, studies in the area of information securit awareness have typically focused on static, pre-conceived aspects of awareness (see Appendix A and B). Yet research untangling users' actual information security awareness in a security-related situation has been sparse. Our model proposes a dynamic, situational and interactionist approach to security behaviours, in the form of situationa information security awareness, which combines both an individual's state of knowledge and how that state is achieved through interaction with the system environment. Due to this perspective on how security behaviours are shaped through person-situation interaction, new avenues of research could emerge, as phenomena associated with a person-situation interaction need to be considered from a broader, ecological perspective rather than a narrow individualistic perspective.

Second, the study examined antecedents of situational information security awareness, an area that has not received sufficient attention in the extant information security awareness literature (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). At the individual level, we identified prior experience, which enables the development of schemata to support patter matching in the process of forming awareness. Our empirical results support the importance of experience fo directing attention in taking in and classifying information in the perception process. The insight that experienced users attend to more security-related information cues indicates that their mental models of phishing may be mor complex with more links between concepts related to the characteristics of phishing attacks than the mental model of phishing of inexperienced users. This does not only mean that they are more likely to start the pattern matching process, but also that they may know more about the interconnectedness of information cues. This insight provides a basis for future research on how mental models could be used to determine susceptibility to security attacks such as phishing and the effectiveness of training.

We also looked at factors related to the system and task environment as they affect the user's achievement of situational information security awareness. In studying sender characteristics, prior studies have found that users ar more likely to be caught by emails from known sources such as friends or familiar institutions (Jagatic, Johnson, Jakobsson, & Menczer, 2007; Moody et al., 2017; Parsons, McCormac, Pattinson, Butavicius, & Jerram, 2015). Ou study explores differences in awareness depending to the degree a phishing email matches the context in which it is read. In doing so, we show that awareness is not only driven by whether a sender is known or unknown but also whether an email is contextually relevant. This provides empirical evidence for the proposition that the alignment of the phishing email's premise and employee context affects which cues the employee finds to be salient (Steve et al., 2019).

In addition, prior research on phishing susceptibility finds that influence techniques within such emails (eg, an urgent deadline) distract individual's limited attentional resources away from important security-related cues (eg, th accuracy of the sender address; Vishwanath et al., 2011). Within our research, we find the same to be true for salient design elements in phishing emails, which led users to misplace their attention and reduced their situational informa tion security awareness. Future research could extend this finding by moving beyond the email message and salient design elements and consider system interfaces (eg, email clients or browsers) that vary in feature complexity of their security-related cues (eg, lock icon, https, colours, highlighting the domain name, etc.). Beyond salient design ele ments in a phishing mail, complexity of cues would also be expected to influence situational information securit awareness.

Since awareness is derived from various sources of information, we hypothesized and empirically found that cues that trigger awareness may also be received from security warnings. It is important to note that whil researchers tend to focus on manipulating the information provided through the security warning (eg, a fear appeal) this is not the only source of information users draw on. It is important, therefore, that approaches on how users per ceive and respond to security warnings (Anderson et al., 2016) also take into account information users derive from other sources in the immediate system environment to test the effectiveness of security warnings. By showing that security warning prompts further attention to other security-related cues that are used to achieve a cohesive view of the situation, we extend prior literature focusing on the isolated effect of a warning itself.

Further, our results indicate that situational information security awareness serves as an initiator of the cognitive mediating processes that are the focus of PMT, meaning that situational information security awareness can act as the source of information needed for threat and coping appraisal. This has also some theoretical implications for other research fields in the IS discipline applying coping theory (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Although it has been investigated for more than a decade in IS research, it has until now been unclear how the appearance of an unex pected IT event (Guinea & Webster, 2013) leads to cognitive appraisals on behalf of users. By extending the basi coping process through the addition of situational information security awareness we are now able to provide a first answer to the pressing question ‘why a particular cue is more or less salient for an individual user’ (Stein, Newell Wagner, & Galliers, 2015, p. 389), as users are situationally aware of these respective cues

Third, in addition to the hypotheses tested in our research model, we extend prior research on information security awareness, which based its measurement of awareness solely on subjective user perceptions (Bulgurcu et al. 2010), by proposing an objective measurement of awareness using eye gaze data. Specifically, we inferred situationa information security awareness from eye fixations on different security-related information cues to understand th processes that individuals use to gather information within their environment and to determine whether attention i given to specific cues that are relevant for developing situational information security awareness. Understandin what the user is viewing (or neglecting to view) when handling incoming emails in a working environment provided rich insights into user awareness. Using eye tracking to objectively measure awareness as it occurred also helped in mitigating challenges of self-reported measures of awareness, which may be subject to social desirability bias and subjectivity bias (Anderson et al., 2016).

## 6.3 | Implications for practice

As phishing attacks persistently threaten both the organization and its employees, our study's findings have signifi cant practical implications for information security management. We find that users with prior phishing experienc attend to more security-related information cues. This result may indicate that their mental models of phishing ar more complex and contain more links between concepts related to the characteristics of phishing attacks than th mental models of phishing with less prior phishing experience. Training programs should thus be designed to provid information regarding the interconnectedness of information cues, such as how an impersonal greeting may be con nected to an unknown sender email address, which in turn could be connected to a malicious file or a fraudulent link leading to malicious website.

In addition, our findings regarding the influence of contextual relevance on situational information securit awareness emphasize the importance of varying phishing exercises suitably and challenge employees with contex tually relevant phishing emails to provide training on new scams. Training implementers must understand and cat egorize the relevancy of a phishing email for portions of the target audience. For example, employees ar differently exposed to phishing emails at work due to the extent they receive external mails. Work groups who regularly deal with external third parties are increasingly exposed that could lead to higher susceptibility in suc groups. However, it could also lead them to acquire situational information security awareness more easily due to the regular requirement of matching the patterns of emails with their mental library of prototypical emails to make decisions with regard to their legitimacy. To respond to these different abilities in acquiring situationa information security awareness the development of a set of phishing emails that vary in their detection difficult based on the number of security cues being manipulated may be required. For instance, more aware users may benefit from a variation of more difficult phishing emails that update and enhance their mental models of phish ing and counter possible stereotypes that may develop by being repeatedly exposed to similar phishing emails Conversely, less aware users may respond more favorably to more simple phishing emails with fewer cues being manipulated to help them develop a mental library of prototypical phishing emails. By training them in matchin the patterns of the current situation with their schema, less aware users can then quickly recognize known clas ses of phishing emails.

Regarding the potential design of email systems and protection mechanisms, our descriptive statistics on the gaze results for AOIs indicate that only a low proportion of participants fixated on two key security indicators in emails, the sender address and the real URL link. One plausible recommendation would be to improve the design of both indicators by, for instance, changing colour, size or position to make the cues more salient for users. In addition, to control for misplaced salience negatively affecting situational information security awareness, organizational IT security should assess the viability of disallowing automatic loading of images or certain design elements in an email for different work groups.

While our descriptive results on behavioural reactions show that in about 38% of all cases, the phishing email were deleted or archived in the spam folder, such phishing emails were forwarded to the IT helpdesk, disturbingly less than 8% of the time. If IT departments rely on reports from their employees about current phishing emails to update their security filters, they must not only motivate participants to protect themselves from phishing, but also encourage them to inform the IT department about phishing emails they receive

## 6.4 | Limitations and future research

When interpreting our results some limitations need to be considered. Although we designed our experimental sce nario of reading and processing 20 emails in a work environment as realistically and contextually relevant as possible such as by gathering examples of internal email exchange within the organizations and using real-life phishing exam ples, it is difficult to assess the extent to which experimental realism was maintained. Since participants' behaviou was recorded using an eye tracker, we need to address the issue that participant's behaviour in the experiment ma differ from natural behaviour. Yet, one potential indicator supporting the realism of our experiment is that our results on phishing click rates are in line with both industry experiences and peer literature (see Appendix G). This researc included strict coding of security-related behaviours in response to a phishing email, similar to prior approache (Wright & Marett, 2010). Although the underlying screen recording data of actions performed by the participant helps reduce potential subjectivity bias that otherwise could arise in self-reported scales, we do not rule out the pos sibility this might also lead to a different kind of bias.

Prior literature on information security awareness has primarily relied on subjective self-assessments in evaluating users' awareness levels (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; D'Arcy et al., 2009; Dinev & Hu, 2007). We consider the analysis of eye gaze data as a first step in trying to reflect and assess information security awareness objectively. We, however acknowledge that further insights are needed. One interesting avenue would be to identify scan path differences (eg transitions from sender address to text vs text to sender) using eye tracking pattern analysis. Another enhancement for measuring situational information security awareness could be to identify whether security-related information cues in emails need to be differently weighted. Since several security-related cues in emails can be manipulated by phishers such as content of the email, there might be some cues that are more important than others in evaluating whether an email is legitimate or a phish, such as the sender address or server-domain of URLs, which are harder to manipulate

## APPENDIX B: PREVIOUS EMPIRICAL STUDIES ON PHISHING

To understand individuals' susceptibility to phishing attacks, two primary angles of antecedents have been consid ered in prior research: the characteristics of the recipient of the message and the characteristics of the message itself.

Researchers have examined how users' characteristics affect their susceptibility to phishing attacks. Demographic characteristics, including gender and age, have impacts on phishing susceptibility. Women were found to be more susceptible than men and younger participants between the ages of 18 and 25 more than other age groups (Sheng, Holbrook, Kumaraguru, Cranor, & Downs, 2010). Other dispositional factors, including personality traits of curiosity, risk propensity, and trust, also increase phishing susceptibility (Moody et al., 2017). Users' overconfidence in phishing email detection increases due to variability in attention allocation and dispositional optimism (Wang et al. 2016). On the other hand, experiential factors, including computer self-efficacy, Internet experience, and securit knowledge can decrease susceptibility (Moody et al., 2017; Wright & Marett, 2010). Detection accuracy also increases with users' appropriate coping adaptiveness, which results from perceived phishing threat, perceived detection efficacy and phishing anxiety (Wang et al., 2017). Mindfulness, or the receptive attention to current surroundings and experiences, enables individuals to avoid phishing attacks (Jensen, Dinger, Wright, & Thatcher, 2017).

Beyond individual characteristics, research has recognized that users' vulnerability to phishing attacks also depends on email characteristics. Users are more susceptible when an email comes from a known source or when an unknown sourc is perceived as sincere, competent, attractive and worthy (Algarni et al., 2017; Moody et al., 2017). They are more vulnera ble to gain or loss-framed messages, and less susceptible to messages that rely on fictitious prior shared experience (Goel Williams, & Dincelli, 2017; Wright, Jensen, Thatcher, Dinger, & Marett, 2014). Beyond message characteristics, the numbe of emails received in a day also increases likelihood to respond a phishing email (Vishwanath et al., 2011)

## APPENDIX C: THEORETICAL AND CONTRIBUTORY JUSTIFICATIONS

## Philosophical justifications for integrating situation awareness and PMT

The combination of different theoretical lenses into a conceptual whole requires important considerations to ensure that the combination is possible. To justify the combination of situation awareness and PMT, we draw upon th works of Okhuysen and Bonardi (2011) and Murray, Evers, and Janda (1995) who provide important considerations when combining theories (Lowry et al., 2019).

Okhuysen and Bonardi (2011) suggest to consider the proximity and compatibility between underlying assump tions between the two theories. Proximity refers to the conceptual distance that exists between the phenomen that the lenses address in their original conception (Okhuysen & Bonardi, 2011, p. 7). For example, as Okhuysen and Bonardi (2011) note, ‘microeconomics and psychology are both partly concerned with explaining how and why indi viduals act in particular ways. Their combination, then, might help enrich our understanding of individual behaviour (p. 7). Similarly, both situation awareness models and PMT are theories to explain individual behaviour and both are used as psychological theories in their respective fields. In Ergonomics, individualistic models of situation awareness such as the perceptual cycle model (PCM) of situation awareness by Smith et al. (1995), are rooted in cognitive psy chology and seek to explain the processes underpinning the awareness held in the minds of individuals and their interaction with the environment (Stanton et al., 2017). This focus incorporates the psychological processes involved (eg, mental models, schema, perception) and the nature of situation awareness itself (eg, the person's situation awareness comprising knowledge about something). Conversely, PMT is a major health psychology theory aimed at explaining the cognitive mediation process of behavioural change in terms of threat and coping appraisal based on processing of information from environmental and intrapersonal sources (Maddux & Rogers, 1983). Thus, althoug they have been applied in different research fields, PCM of situation awareness and PMT are proximal theories and as such, it is appropriate to combine situation awareness and PMT from this perspective.

The degree of compatibility of assumptions refers to how theories ‘rely on similar or dissimilar individua decision-making processes, […], or other properties in the development of their explanations’ (Okhuysen & Bonardi 2011, p. 7). One defining characteristic regarding decision-making processes is whether models are based on, or incorporate single or dual information processing and decision-making pathway assumptions. Single models of cogni tion specify that information processing and decision-making follow cognitive, analytic, and rational processes of deliberative and systematic reasoning. Single models are analogous to conscious processes that an individual can be aware of, intentionally initiate and guide, and have control over. Dual-processing models of cognition include non conscious processes that operate in parallel to the conscious analytic decision-making process typical of singleprocess models (Gerrard, Gibbons, Houlihan, Stock, & Pomery, 2008; Levesque, Copeland, & Sutcliffe, 2008). PMT is a single-processing model, which relies on an expectancy value perspective (ie, analytic processing) for decision mak ing (Rogers, 1975). Similarly, questions about situation awareness are fundamentally questions about consciousness (Dekker, Hummerdal, & Smith, 2010). Based on PCM, which is an information processing model, Smith et al. (1995 describe situation awareness as a generative process of knowledge creation and informed action taking (p. 138) Thus, both PCM of situation awareness and PMT rely on similar information processing and decision-making pathways.

Combing the arguments regarding the proximity and compatibility of assumptions lead us to be confident that integrating situation awareness and PMT, as per Okhuysen and Bonardi (2011) is appropriate.

The appropriateness of integrating situation awareness and PMT is also supported by the work of Murray et al. (1995), who recommend investigating ontological, epistemological and axiological consistency while integrating theories. The philosophical assumptions are closely related to the level of focus or approach with respect to an individual, his or her social context, and surrounding environment. PMT as a social cognitive theory incorporates the individual, their behaviour, and their environment in a principle of triadic reciprocal causation or triadic reciprocality: Environmental events, inner personal factors (cognition, emotion and biological events), and behaviour are mutually interacting influences (Maddux & Rogers, 1983). Similarly, situation awareness based on the PCM (Smith et al., 1995) focuses on the ecological level, which highlights the individual's interactions with their envi ronment, wherein the individual shapes their environment, and is shaped by it vice versa. Thus, PMT and situation awareness share a fundamental ontological stance: both recognize that cognition is extended beyond the individual because perception, decisions and actions are grounded within the context of the environment in which they occur.

PMT and situation awareness are also consistent epistemologically. As noted earlier, both PCM of situation awareness and PMT can be situated within the realm of psychological theories. Situation awareness is a psychologi cal construct that evolves through an individuals' interaction with their environment, while PMT is ultimately an indi vidual's psychological/cognitive reaction to environmental stimuli. Thus, epistemologically, they are the same type of theory drawing largely upon comparable understandings of human psychology.

Finally, axiological compatibility between theories involves understanding whether the theories are compatibl from ethical standpoints (Kock, Gallivan, & DeLuca, 2008), which is primarily a normative consideration (Chatterjee & Sarker, 2013). Both PCM of situation awareness and PMT are non-normative theories. They are primarily useful in explaining behaviours and do not have a normative component. Therefore, both situation awareness and PMT ar axiologically compatible.

## APPENDIX I: POST-HOC ANALYSIS ON HABITUATION

Due to recent studies on habituation in information security (Anderson et al., 2016; Vance et al., 2018), we also need to discuss the potential issue that situational information security awareness could be negatively influenced by automaticity of cognitive processes due to a reduction in receptiveness to novel stimuli (Endsley & Jones 2012). Individuals may not attend to information that is outside the routinized sequence. Thus, situational infor mation security awareness may suffer when that information is important. As opposed to the view presented in our paper, where security-related cues are interpreted to form awareness, leading to the cognitive appraisals and then security-related actions, automaticity would be seen to short circuit that process. A more direct stimulus response pairing would be prompted, and conscious awareness and decision making would be very low (Endsley & Jones, 2012).

To assess habituation in our study, we first analysed changes in situation information security awareness levels across all 20 email viewings following prior literature (see Vance et al., 2018). A decrease in awareness levels woul indicate that habituation had occurred. The mean and standard deviations of situational information security awareness per email are shown in Table I1. In a linear mixed-effects model, we included situational information securit awareness as the dependent variable, the participant ID as random factor and the email presentation number as fixe factor. The email number beta was not significant, indicating that habituation had not occurred: $\chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 , \mathsf { N } = 2 1 4 0 ) =$ $0 . 0 4 , P > . 0 5 , \beta = . 0 0 8$

Second, we added potential direct relationship between situational information security awareness and pro tection motivation, which would bypass the appraisal processes, in our original PLS model (Figure 5). We find a non-significant direct effec $( \beta ~ = ~ - . 1 7 , ~ t ~ = ~ 1 . 5 9 )$ and total effect $( \beta = - . 0 9 , t = 0 . 8 2 )$ , but a significant indirect effect (β = .09, t = 1.97). This indicates that awareness alone does not trigger an immediate decision to protect oneself from a threat, but rather individuals initially go through the appraisal processes as hypothesized.

Third, to determine if the security warning message was actually read or ignored by our participants, we defined the textual part of our warning page as an area of interest (AOI) following prior research (Neupane, Rahman Saxena, & Hirshfield, 2015). We computed the average number of fixations and average gaze duration inside th AOI. Table I2 shows that participants spent almost 6 seconds inside the message area on average with 19 fixations (Table I2). Thus, participants' primary focus was inside the warning dialog

Next, we plotted the fixations of all participants in a scatterplot overlaid on top of the warning (shown in Figure I1; Neupane et al., 2015). There are more fixation points in the middle of the scatterplot, representing dense gaze points inside the warning area. Participants spent most of their time inside the warning area, spread consistentl across the sentences in the warning message. Additionally, the fixation points flow along the sentences (as shown b a sample participant in Figure I2), demonstrating the warning reading flow. Participants were not just fixating insid the warning window but actually read the warning message (Neupane et al., 2015).

However, we concede that by having only one security warning, we cannot exclude the possibility that users may become habituated with more than one warning. In other words, while our warning might have been effectiv in the short-term, it may get less effective in the long-term if the same warning is repeated, as demonstrated by prior literature (Vance et al., 2018).
