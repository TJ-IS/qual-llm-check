########## RADAR_Background ##########


Four major areas of research are examined. First, we review the information systems (IS) literature on defending IT infrastructure. Second, we explore the theory of robust optimization (RO) as the overarching framework for improving the robustness of cyber defense AI agents. Third, we review AAE to examine effective approaches to automatically emulating adversarial attacks against IT infrastructure. Lastly, we identify state-of-the-art RL methods for operationalizing AAE in cyber defense. Based on the review, we identify research gaps and formalize our research questions.

## Defending IT Infrastructure

Recent IS research on defending IT infrastructure can be classified into three major streams: (1) AI-enabled security research, which focuses on developing new IT artifacts based on AI as guided by computational design science (Abbasi et al., 2010; Rai, 2017), (2) behavioral security research that aims to model organizational/individual behavior, policy compliance, and security risk for defending IT infrastructure (Hui et al., 2018), and (3) economic security research focusing on modeling the impact of investment, market effects, and other economic factors on the security of IT infrastructure (Hui et al., 2018). Table 1 summarizes recent IS studies based on their focus, research stream, AI agent’s task (if applicable), and whether they assume an adversarial environment. The AIenabled security research stream often focuses on leveraging AI to design artifacts that learn from human security analysts and automate selected cybersecurity tasks (Ampel et al., 2024; Benjamin et al., 2016, 2019; Ebrahimi et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016; Samtani et al., 2017; Yin et al., 2019; Ebrahimi et al., 2022). However, these artifacts are often not designed to be deployed in adversarial environments, where the adversary can generate adversarial attacks to mislead the artifact. As a result, the security of these IT artifacts can be compromised, which is detrimental to the effectiveness of modern AIenabled cyber defense (Tolido et al., 2019).

On the contrary, behavioral and economic security studies have provided theoretical justification for an inherently adversarial environment (Burns et al., 2019; Karhu et al., 2018). These studies have incorporated the adversary’s behavior into their modeling of security phenomena. For instance, Burns et al. (2019) adopted this view in studying the impact of insiders’ behavior on information security. Similarly, Karhu et al. (2018) considered the adversarial role of hostile firms in protecting software resources from exploitation. However, there is a lack of research on how to strengthen the robustness of cyber defense AI agents to adversarial inputs (Goodfellow et al., 2018). We examine the theory of robust optimization as an overarching methodological framework for strengthening the robustness of AI agents in the presence of adversarial inputs.


########## RADAR_RO ##########


Traditional AI agents aim to learn from benign training inputs via minimizing a cost (loss) function without considering the scenario in which an adversary could manipulate the inputs. RO expands the optimization function to account for the adversary, which leads to learning a robust AI agent (Madry et al., 2018). RO offers a rigorous, unifying approach that encompasses solving two optimization problems simultaneously: (1) emulating an adversary that aims to mislead an AI agent by maximizing a cost function (known as inner maximization), and (2) learning an AI agent that reduces incorrect defense decisions by minimizing the cost function (known as outer minimization). Given an input ??, the inner maximization finds an adversarial input ??+?? that achieves a high cost for the defender, thus increasing the chance of evading the cyber defense AI agent. Accordingly, RO’s objective is formulated as a minimax optimization featuring competition between the adversary and the AI agent:

$$
\min _ {\phi} \left(\mathbb {E} _ {(x, y) \sim \mathcal {D}} \left[ \max _ {\delta \in \varDelta} \ell (h _ {\phi} (x + \delta), y) \right]\right)\tag{1}
$$

In this formulation, $h _ { \phi }$ represents the model associated with the cyber defense AI agent. ?? and ?? denote the original input (e.g., a malware file) and class label (e.g., benign vs. malicious), respectively. ?? is the sampling distribution, and $\delta \in \varDelta$ represents a perturbation selected from the set of permissible perturbations (e.g., modifications to the malware file). While the adversary aims to mislead the agent by maximizing the loss (the inner maximization in Equation 1), the AI agent aims to minimize the expected loss ℓ (the outer minimization in Equation 1). This formulation suggests that strengthening the robustness of the AI agent requires effective emulation of the adversarial inputs (i.e., attacks) (Kolter & Madry, 2018). As such, we next examine nascent methods for emulating adversarial attacks against IT infrastructure.

########## RADAR_Gaps ##########


Our review of defending IT infrastructure suggests a vital need for cyber defense AI agents that are robust to adversarial attacks. While AAE is an active research area, there lacks a framework that considers both attack and defense to model the game between the adversary and the AI agent. Also, only a few AAE methods comply with a realistic threat model (i.e., binary black box AAE). Furthermore, many AAE methods are mainly additive and thus limited in their coverage of generated attacks. In addition, most AAE methods are limited in their ability to model the adversary’s sequential decision-making process in interacting with the cyber defense AI agent. As such, using deep RL to model the sequence of actions and RO to enhance defense could address these gaps. However, state-of-the-art deep RL methods designed for continuous environments need enhancement for discrete actions in cyber attacks. Additionally, it is unclear how RO can be extended to incorporate effective RL-crafted attacks. Given these gaps, the following research questions are posed: (1) How can the RO theory be leveraged in cybersecurity to strengthen the robustness of cyber defense AI agents? (2) How can effective adversarial attacks be emulated with deep RL to establish RO? (3) What mitigation strategies can be learned from utilizing RO and RL to guide the design of more robust cyber defense AI agents?


########## ARText_PA ##########


Predictive analytics leveraging SML models are becoming prevalent in various business contexts, including spam detection [3, 33], fraud detection [54, 57], credit approval [8, 59], student admission [8, 59], marketing [19, 22], and crowdsourcing [56]. Most predictive analytics applications draw upon the statistical learning theory to utilize or develop SML models. The statistical learning theory formalizes the predictive task as choosing a model $f : X \to Y ;$ , where X is the input space and Y is the output space, such that for a given input $x \in X , f$ predicts the corresponding output $y \in Y$ [53]. For example, detecting review manipulation could be formulated as identifying a model that predicts the spam review label given the review text and reviewer attributes [33]. The optimal model is chosen by applying the empirical risk minimization (ERM) principle on a training set containing l samples of input-output pairs. Specifically, the ERM principle finds the function that minimizes the loss $\mathcal { L } ( f ( x ) , y )$ between the output y to a given input x and the prediction f ðxÞ made by the function on the training set: $\operatorname* { m i n } _ { f } { \frac { 1 } { l } } \sum _ { i = i } ^ { l } { \mathcal { L } } ( f ( x _ { i } ) , y _ { i } )$ . The loss function could be the squared error $\left( y _ { i } - f ( x _ { i } ) \right) ^ { 2 }$ for regression tasks where $\gamma \mathrm { { s } }$ are continuous outputs, and the misclassification indicator function $1 ( y _ { i } = f ( x _ { i } ) )$ for classification tasks, where $\gamma \mathbf { \bar { s } }$ are discrete outputs.

The key assumption underlying the ERM principle is that all inputs x, including training inputs and unseen inputs from the actual application setting, are drawn independently from the same fixed distribution $P ( x )$ . Nonetheless, this assumption could be challenged in certain real-world application domains where the input that predictive analytics relies on to make a decision is provided by the individual who is immediately afected by the decision [13]. In such application domains, adversaries can meticulously manipulate the input data to trick SML models into favorable decisions and subsequently benefit from them [8, 60].

Examples of such application domains include including spam detection [33], credit approval [8, 59], employee hiring [8, 59], and student admission [8, 59]. Specifically, review spammers could meticulously write spam review content to bypass SML-based spam review detection systems. Similarly, spam emails could be deliberately designed to deceive SMLbased spam email filtering systems. Credit card applicants might provide fraudulent information to get approval from automated credit application processing systems. Job candidates might fake their background attributes in job applications to deceive human resources automation, and student candidates might alter their information to get admitted by automated student admission management systems. While these systems attempt to mitigate the adversarial exploitation by employing fraud detection systems, recent systems have extensively leveraged SML [1, 3, 33, 54, 57] and are, therefore, vulnerable to adversarial exploitation as well. Such threats against the SML model underlying predictive analytics necessitate the consideration of adversarial robustness when applying SML models.

However, the existing evaluation of predictive analytics primarily focuses on predictive performance, that is, the model’s ability to predict new observations accurately on testing datasets resembling the training datasets [48]. Such an evaluation procedure is insuficient for assessing the adversarial robustness of SML models in real-world application domains, where inputs can be perturbed to mislead SML models. For example, predictive analytics for classification tasks, such as detecting spam reviews and phishing websites, primarily use accuracy, precision, recall, F-measure (F1), and area under the receiver operating curve (ROC AUC) [3, 33]; predictive analytics for regression tasks, such as predicting sales, widely adopt mean absolute percentage error and root mean square of errors [22]; and predictive analytics for ranking tasks, such as recommender systems, often utilize top-k precision and rank correlation coeficient [19, 38]. These metrics cannot directly measure adversarial robustness. Hence, it is imperative to enrich the predictive analytics literature by developing a systematic adversarial robustness assessment model.


########## ARText_Defense ##########


The IS literature has examined the defense of exploratory adversarial attacks from the perspective of the principal-agent model, where the principal leverages an SML model (e.g., a classifier) to make a decision (e.g., fraud, admission, credit approval) based on the data provided by the agent [7]. The adversarial agent has the incentive to strategically perturb the data provided to the principal in order to attain a favorable decision. In anticipation of the possible strategic perturbation, the principal can defend the SML model by incorporating the perturbation into the model optimization. Specifically, an enhanced support vector machine (SVM) capable of prohibiting adversaries constrained by a certain perturbation range has been developed by solving a Nash equilibrium between the principal and the adversarial agent through mixed integer programming [8]. Since numerically solving the mixed integer program can be computationally intensive, the genetic algorithm has been utilized to expedite the process by approximating the solution [10]. The genetic algorithm is further extended to address adversarial attacks where the inputs are composed of only binary variables [9]. Relatedly, the particular perturbation type of hiding data has been studied and the defensive measure of data imputation methods has been proposed for SVM [59] and support vector regression [60].

We seek to complement the existing IS literature on defense against adversarial attacks in several ways. First, while prior research extensively studies SVM and develops defensive measures with analytical solutions specific to this type of SML model, it is unclear whether defending other types of SML models (e.g., models with non-linear discriminant functions) can be solved with analytical solutions. Many predictive analytics applications leverage SML models other than SVM as their underlying SML models, and therefore we aim to develop a generalized approach for enhancing the SML models in these predictive analytics applications.

Second, past literature mostly studies the white-box exploratory attack which assumes the adversaries to have the full knowledge of the target SML model. We extend the literature by investigating the black-box exploratory attack where limited knowledge of the target SML model other than its outputs is available to adversaries. The black-box exploratory attack is common to numerous domains, such as fraud detection, spam detection, credit approval, employee hiring, and student admission. Adversaries targeting these domains often have little knowledge about the SML models but can repetitively access the model outputs given inputs.

Third, existing research based on the principal-agent model assumes all individuals interacting with predictive analytics applications are rational and have incentives to perturb the input data for favorable output. Our research allows for benign agents who truthfully report the input data. This is reasonable in several use-cases such as online review platforms, credit card applications, employee hiring, and student admission, where not all individuals intend to commit fraud.

In summary, this research seeks to address the black-box adversarial attack, where the predictive analytics application makes decisions about individuals based on the input provided by them, and adversaries aim to mislead the predictive analytics application into favorable decisions. In particular, the predictive analytics application makes decisions by minimizing the loss caused by incorrect decisions in anticipation of adversarial perturbation in inputs and the adversary has repetitive access to predictive analytics outputs but limited information about the underlying SML model specifications.


########## Ampel_IS ##########


IS scholars have made remarkable advances in cybersecurity analytics research in recent years. We summarize selected recent cybersecurity analytics literature published in prevailing IS journals in Table 2. For each study, we summarize the year of publication, author(s), cybersecurity focus, whether the approach was multi-dataset or not, analytical method(s), and if a system or UI was built to present the analytical results.

Most prior cybersecurity analytics studies have examined hacker forums to proactively identify, detect, and mitigate cyber threats (Benjamin et al., 2019; Ebrahimi et al., 2020; Samtani et al., 2017; Yin et al., 2019; Yue et al., 2019). Earlier cybersecurity analytics traditionally relied on classical machine learning (ML) or text mining methods (Benaroch, 2018; Karhu et al., 2018; Samtani et al., 2017; Sen et al., 2020; Yin et al., 2019), while more recent studies have leveraged the DL-based BiLSTM to automatically extract feature representations (i.e., embeddings) from the inputted hacker forum post content (Ebrahimi et al., 2022; Samtani et al., 2022). Despite providing timely contributions to our understanding of hacker forum content, most studies do not conduct the critical CTI task of assigning a specific label to each exploit within hacker forums. Additionally, researchers rarely embed their algorithm(s) into a system or UI. Consequently, CTI researchers or practitioners may have difficulty leveraging a cybersecurity analytics IT artifact for their cyber-defense workflow and CTI tasks (Samtani et al., 2017; Silic & Lowry, 2020).

Designing a novel IT artifact for a high-impact societal application (e.g., hacker exploit labeling) requires a principled approach. Past IS cybersecurity analytics literature has leveraged the computational design science paradigm (Hevner et al., 2004; Rai, 2017) to guide and ground their work. The computational design science paradigm provides IS scholars with three concrete guidelines to design and evaluate novel algorithms, computational models, and systems for advanced data analytics applications (Rai, 2017). First, the IT artifact's design can be inspired by key domain requirements or data characteristics. In a recent example within extant cybersecurity analytics literature, the webpage structure from DNMs guided the design of a novel transductive support vector machine (SVM) (Ebrahimi et al., 2020). Second, IS scholars should demonstrate the novelty of their design by comparing the quantitative performance (e.g., accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score) of their proposed IT artifact against well-established baseline methods. Finally, the IT artifact should contribute back to the IS knowledge base. Contributions can include a situated implementation of the IT artifact (e.g., user interface) and/or nascent design theory (e.g., design principles) (Rai, 2017). Executing each guideline requires understanding the application for which the artifact is being developed. For this study, we identify what data characteristics have been used to analyze hacker forum exploits.

Most hacker-forum exploit analytics studies have analyzed the post content, which often contains significant jargon, surrounding an exploit's source code to categorize posts into broad categories (e.g., botnets, keyloggers, malware). Similar to IS cybersecurity analytics literature, earlier studies (2017-2018) often used classical ML (e.g., SVM) based on their tasks (Deliu et al., 2018; Samtani et al., 2017). Since classical ML approaches rely on a manually defined set of features (which is labor intensive and time-consuming to construct), scholars in recent years have relied on the DL-based LSTM or BiLSTM for their tasks (Goyal et al., 2018; Williams et al., 2018). Irrespective of the analytical method, past studies have not examined how to assign a specific label to each exploit in hacker forums based on its source code or the metadata (e.g., exploit titles) in exploit DNMs or public exploit repositories.


########## Ampel_Gaps ##########


We identified several research gaps from prior literature. First, past studies analyzing exploits in hacker forums primarily analyze post content and often omit source code. However, source code contains significant, often precise, syntax and information (e.g., function names, exploit actions, etc.) that is not in natural language (Nuñez-Varela et al., 2017). Second, hacker-forum exploit source code is nontrivial to categorize, as it often lacks clear exploit labels and metadata (e.g., informative titles). Additionally, prevailing DL-based methods (e.g., LSTM, BiLSTM) used for hacker forum analysis were designed for natural language and often struggle to capture long semantic relationships (e.g., dependencies) within exploit code. Finally, despite containing professionally vetted exploits, rich metadata, and overlapping hacker content, exploit DNMs and public exploit repositories have not been leveraged in a multi-dataset model to help label hacker forum exploits. Based on these research gaps, we pose the following research questions:

How can we extend a BiLSTM model to capture the long semantic dependencies found within exploit source code?

How can we transfer the knowledge from metadata-rich exploit DNMs and public exploit repositories to help label exploits in hacker forums based on their source code?


########## DSDL_21 ##########


Advances in social media and artificial intelligence technologies promote the emergence of omnichannel retailing, which entails various online channels to realize seamless customer-to-business interaction (Song et al. 2020b). Typical channels for customer-to-business interaction include social media, email, short message, web portal, and telephony, which can be categorized into two types according to the data form they involve, that is, text and voice based. A common goal shared by the two interaction types is to provide high-quality service so as to boost customer satisfaction (Chen et al. 2018). With an online chatbot, customers can interact with a retailer through text chat without temporal and geographical boundaries such that their requirements, such as information searching and complaint, can be satisfied automatically (Radziwill and Benton 2017, Chung et al. 2020). Call centers, another prevalent channel, resolve customer queries through voice chat (Kumar and Telang 2012). More recently, the use of an intelligent conversational bot, which provides online live voice chat service by imitating human service representatives, has achieved great success in many domains, including digital banking (Kaur et al. 2020), intelligent education (Edwards et al. 2019), interactive marketing (Pradana et al. 2018), and healthcare (Denecke et al. 2019). Given the ubiquity of online chat–based service, research is underway to leverage rich customer interaction information to provide customized goods, services, and experiences so as to create more customer value (Cai and Lo 2020). Because high-quality experience during the purchase decision-making process can boost customer loyalty, business revenue, and business agility (Klaus and Maklan 2013, Shi et al. 2020), much research is devoted to customer experience evaluation.

Prior studies investigate factors influencing online customer experience. Novak et al. (2000) measure customer experience in online environments and reveal a set of factors (e.g., customer type and interaction speed) that influence the forming of online experience. van Dolen et al. (2007) provide evidence for the impact of customers’ characteristics on their perceived satisfactions from online group chat and highlight the importance of the communication style of the advisor, who facilitates interaction to help customers attain their objectives, for customer satisfaction. Rose et al. (2011) review the online experience literature to unravel the antecedents and consequences of customer experience in the purchase context and summarize the differences between online and off-line contexts, such as the degree of personal contact and the manner in which information is provided. Stein and Ramaseshan (2016) uncover that customer experience is mainly determined by touch points and episodes encountered during the service delivery process. This rationalizes the voice chat–based customer response prediction in our study because almost all of the customer experience touch-point elements identified by Stein and Ramaseshan (2016), including atmospheric, communicative, process, employee–customer interaction, and product interaction elements, are wellcovered by the voice chat. From an omnichannel customer experience view, Shi et al. (2020) conclude that perceived compatibility is positively related to customers’ omnichannel shopping intention. Overall, these studies regarding online experience lay a sound foundation for using customers’ voice chat with an agent to predict their response behavior.


########## DSDL_24 ##########


Our review of existing related studies reveals their incompetence for voice chat–based customer response prediction. First, existing response prediction methods have been developed predominantly by relying on quantitative features and textual data, failing to model the complex data patterns embedded in richer multimodal information, such as audio information. Second, synthesizing theory-driven constructs and data-driven deep learning methods may lead to improved prediction performance but has not been explored.

Our proposed method (i.e., DSDL) aims to fill these gaps in the literature. First, unlike prior studies that evaluate online customer experience by virtue of rich personal information (van Dolen et al. 2007, Stein and Ramaseshan 2016), DSDL allows personalized customer preferences to be estimated in the absence of explicit personal information, better satisfying today’s imperative requirements of privacy protection and data security. Besides, instead of investigating customer satisfaction statically (Novak et al. 2000, van Dolen et al. 2007), DSDL measures dynamic customer satisfaction in an interlaced way. Second, unlike existing methods that use telemarketing data in quantitative ways (Moro et al. 2014,

Apampa 2016) and predict customers’ responses with traditional machine learning methods (Chun 2012, Chapelle et al. 2015), DSDL extracts multiview features and constructs a deep learning model. Third, the theory-driven satisfaction generated by DSDL not only enables more rational model training, but also enhances predictions.


########## DSDL_3 ##########


Extant research shows that customers’ satisfactory experiences during their purchase decision making positively influence their purchase intentions, so the overall satisfaction can be regarded as an important antecedent for actual purchase behavior (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010, Lankton et al. 2016). Such evidence inspires us to capitalize on satisfaction to predict whether a customer will make an off-line response after being invited by an agent via online voice chat. When modeling customer satisfaction, Spreng et al. (1996) identify two kinds of satisfaction, information and attribute satisfaction. Informa tion satisfaction is contingent on the informational content and the way it is delivered (McKinney et al. 2002). The more the agent-provided information conforms to customers’ requirements, the more satisfied the customers are. Furthermore, more acceptable and intelligible delivery (e.g., appropriate tone, intonation, and speech rate of the voice and exquisite speechcraft) by the agent tends to elicit higher customer satisfaction. Attribute satisfaction is fundamentally determined by some core attributes associated with the product as these attributes are indispensable in forming customers’ preferences (Szymanski and Henard 2001, Ofek and Srinivasan 2002). Product attributes used to model attribute satisfaction should be explicitly tailored to customers preferences.

Given the determinants of customer satisfaction, another important issue is how customer satisfaction is achieved. The expectation-disconfirmation theory (Oliver 1977, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010) elucidates the mechanism of customer satisfaction commendably. The theory asserts that the rationale behind satisfaction is rooted in disconfirmation, that is, the discrepancy between prior expectation and actual experience (Oliver 1977). Expectation is a set of customers’ preexposure beliefs about a product, whereas actual experience is the actual performance of the product as perceived by customers (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Experience exceeding expectation yields positive disconfirmation (i.e., satisfaction), whereas expectation exceeding experience yields negative disconfirmation (i.e., dissatisfaction) (McKinney et al. 2002, Lankton et al. 2016). Satisfaction increases as the degree of positive disconfirmation increases (Fan and Suh 2014). These conclusions motivate us to capture the causal relationship between customers off-line responses and the agent’s online invitations by modeling two antecedents—expectation and experience— and their disconfirmation.

The expectation-disconfirmation theory entails complex dynamics, whereas final satisfaction is a cumulative function of prior disconfirmation (Lankton and McKnight 2012). Prior research demonstrates that the belief and attitude toward a product vary over time as customers gain new experiences continually (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004). Given the sequence nature of voice chat, it is reasonable to differentiate disconfirmations resulted from different fragments of a dialogue. After the agent starts a dialogue with a customer, both interact dynamically when discussing multiple topics, and different topics with their related contexts (i.e., questions and answers) tend to impact disconfirmation differently. The multiple fragments of the dialogue inevitably give rise to fluctuation in disconfirmation, thus precipitating the variance of the overall satisfaction.

In summary, the aforementioned prior studies jointly reveal that satisfaction is a focal point in forming customers’ response intention and, thus, can be used as a strong predictor of actual response behavior. Additionally, the response intention reflected by disconfirmation and satisfaction holds considerable continuance (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010), so the impact of a moderate time lag between the predicted off-line response behavior and the online invitation on prediction is negligible. Furthermore, the expectation-disconfirmation literature highlights the predictive roles of expectation, experience, and disconfirmation for satisfaction (Oliver 1977, Brown et al. 2008). In this sense, the task of predicting customer satisfaction can boil down to (1) modeling the dynamic expectation, experience, and disconfirmation implied in the voice chat context and (2) aggregating them into cumulative satisfaction.

However, solving this problem is methodologically challenging. First, as indicated by the aforementioned satisfaction determinants, modeling customer satisfaction should take account of informational content and its delivery mechanism simultaneously. To achieve this, we extract multiview features from voice chat data with (1) context-based textual features to capture semantic information and (2) narrative patterns and voice signal–based audio features to depict the delivery of informational content. Besides, identifying key product attributes conforming to customers’ preferences is also challenging because the agent knows little about the customers before inviting them to the store. To this end, we propose a key-attribute extraction scheme, which can cover important attributes tailored to customers preferences. Furthermore, considering that customers inherently hold heterogeneous willingness to pay for different attributes (Ofek and Srinivasan 2002), we model customers’ potential preferences via text analysis based on the voice chat content and embed them into keyattribute spaces (i.e., preference templates).

Second, modeling the expectation, experience, and disconfirmation is a particularly challenging problem in the absence of customers’ personal information. To deal with this difficulty, we learn fragment-wise expectations based on preference templates as customers form their expectations by projecting future product performance into their preferred product attributes (Lankton et al. 2014). Besides, we propose a cross-view attention mechanism for capturing attribute-wise experiences of each dialogue fragment. The rationale behind this is that each voice chat is indeed a question–answer session, where the agent’s answers to customers’ questions tend to determine the degree of customer satisfaction whereas the customers’ answers to the agent’s questions tend to reflect the intensity of their willingness to participate. Thus, the actual experience can be measured by attending to customer–agent dialogue fragments alternately. To distinguish between positive and negative disconfirmation, we construct a class-wise contrastive learning objective. That is, we regard customer response prediction as a binary classification task, that is, whether a customer responds to an online invitation, with responsive customers as ones of satisfaction and unresponsive customers as ones of dissatisfaction.

Third, given that the formation of customer satisfaction is a dynamic process in which the disconfirmation between expectation and experience evolves over dialogue fragments, we model the expectation, experience, and disconfirmation at the granularity level of dialogue fragment and aggregate the fragment-wise satisfactions into the cumulative satisfaction. When estimating the cumulative satisfaction, we impose bidirectional (i.e., attribute and fragment) sparsity constraints on the contrastive learning objective, considering that the role of a certain attribute-wise or fragment-wise satisfaction for forming the cumulative satisfaction varies across customers because of their personalized preferences (Ofek and Srinivasan 2002). For example, if a satisfied customer experiences negative disconfirmation at some time step or attribute, this time step or attribute is inappropriate for modeling the customer’s positive contrastive (i.e., disconfirmation) pattern and, thus, should be eliminated. With the bidirectional sparsity constraints, the finally derived cumulative satisfaction is more robust to attribute-wise satisfaction variations and fragmentwise satisfaction fluctuations. Table 1 summarizes our induced satisfaction perspectives, along with theoretical foundations, methodological challenges, and our proposed solutions.

The reason reviews can impact product sales lies in that customers’ purchase decisions are considerably affected by a wealth of product-specific opinions conveyed by reviews (Wang and Strong 1996, Kang and Zhou 2017). However, the beneficial role of reviews for customers does not necessarily hold due to the heterogeneous information quality of reviews (Mudambi and Schuff 2010). One key factor to unravel the influential mechanism of reviews on product sales is review helpfulness, which plays a mediating role between the impact of reviews on customers’ purchase intention (Filieri et al. 2018). The helpfulness of a review is contingent on the extent to which it can be perceived by customers to facil itate their purchase decision process (Yin et al. 2014). Therefore, helpful reviews are commonly expected to have higher impact on customers’ demand and product sales (Berger et al. 2010, Sahoo et al. 2018).

The proliferation of reviews has stimulated lots of research interests around review helpfulness, such as determinants of review helpfulness (Mudambi and Schuff 2010, Kuan et al. 2015) and review helpfulness prediction (Lee et al. 2018, Ma et al. 2018). Forman et al. (2008) examined the relationship between reviews and sales by evaluating review helpfulness and concluded that reviews’ source credibility is positively associated with their helpfulness and product sales. Zhu and Zhang (2010) demonstrated that the impact of reviews on product sales is moderated by the characteristics of the product and customers, indicating the inadequacy of solely relying on simple helpfulness indicators for sales prediction. Kuan et al. (2015) demonstrated that voted reviews signify remarkable diagnostic values (helpfulness) but are negatively correlated with sales.

In contrast to numeric helpfulness indicators, textual reviews generally provide rich details for customers to evaluate whether the specific product attributes match their own preferences (Buschken and Allenby 2016). Especially when the ratings across products are similar, the role of textual reviews for revealing customers’ purchase decisions and sales trend becomes even stronger (Sahoo et al. 2018). To uncover the impact of review text on product returns, Sahoo et al. (2018) quantified textual reviews by text length and fraction of reviews voted helpful. Berger et al. (2010), after investigating the valence of textual reviews, argued that negative reviews can increase purchase likelihood and sales by improving customers’ product awareness. Archak et al. (2011) identified textual reviews as an important determinant of customers’ choices and placed a higher premium on their impact on sales compared with numeric helpfulness indicators.

As is well recognized, “a picture is worth a thousand words” because it can provide informational, aesthetic, and self-enhancement values simultaneously (Li and Xie 2020). Likewise, image reviews offer customers a visual information channel to evaluate products more vividly (Zhang and Mao 2012). Karimi and Wang (2017) examined the impact of reviewers’ profile images on review helpfulness and reported that the visual decoration offered by profile images enhances customers’ perception and evaluation of review helpfulness. Ma et al. (2018) explored the effect of customer-generated images on review helpfulness and verified their values for indicating review helpfulness. They also claimed that images alone do not provide sufficient cues for customers to make effective judgements about the product. Recently, Wu et al. (2021) confirmed that images add more diagnostic value to reviews and hybrid reviews (i.e., reviews with both textual information and images) can be perceived more helpful than text-only reviews.

########## ACAA_3 ##########


Our aim in this study is to better leverage multimodal reviews in ways customers receive and process them for sales prediction. As this study falls in the design science paradigm (Hevner et al. 2004), a sound design rationale, as a foundation for artifact design and evaluation, is important for establishing a design science contribution (Gregor and Hevner 2013). The design rationales for our attention mechanisms are four customer attention indicators, which stem from a process-granularity framework. By expounding the process-granularity framework, we endeavor to address three fundamental questions: (1) how to induce the indicators, (2) why they are adequate for revealing customers’ attention paid to multimodal reviews, and (3) how they guide the design of attention mechanisms accordingly.

Figure 1 shows the process-granularity framework, outlining customers’ browsing behaviors regarding multimodal reviews along the process and granularity di mensions. From a process perspective, we draw on the message learning (Riley 1954) and information processing (McGuire 1968) theories and posit that customers process reviews as information through two stages, that is, determining what kind of reviews to attend to in the reception stage and then evaluating the attended information to decide whether to accept it in the yielding stage (Kuan et al. 2015). On this premise, customers’ attention, in the reception stage, is key and fundamental for leveraging those influential reviews, which have really affected customers’ purchase intention, to enhance sales prediction.

From a granularity perspective, the procedures by which multimodal reviews are delivered to customers involve successive levels, from the holistic review set level to review element (text or image) level and vice versa. Specifically, when customers begin with a certain product among others, they are actually anchored in a holistic review set (i.e., holistic review set granularity) attached to this product. Besides, while they click in the product page, they are first presented with a recent review subset (i.e., review subset granularity along the temporal dimension) due to the limited space of the page. Then, customers disperse to drill down to their preferred review topics (i.e., review subset granularity along the topic dimension) via the overall review categorization labels. In this way, they come to a list of qualified multimodal reviews and decide which ones (i.e., single review granularity) to go through in detail. Furthermore, when clicking on a specific review, they place their tendentious focus on either the review text or the review images (i.e., review element granularity). All these steps occur successively before concrete review content can be scrutinized, and thus the customer attention they attract is largely content free.

Although a considerable number of content-based indicators (e.g., accuracy, length, valence, and objectivity) have been induced for quantifying the usefulness of review information to customers in the yielding stage (Wang and Strong 1996, Liu et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2021), we give particular consideration to content-free customer attention in the reception stage. This is because customers with heterogeneous preferences may attend to various content styles, for example, long versus short, positive versus negative, and subjective versus objective. It is difficult to affirm which content styles will receive customer attention steadily. Instead, our induced content-free indicators are in conformity with customers’ attention in the first place and thus can be brought into a unified attention framework to position influential reviews. At the same time, review contents can be leveraged by standard deep learning methods naturally. We expound the four customer attention indicators, along with the correspond ing design choices, as follows.

(1) Timeliness Attention. The dual-process theory (Groves and Thompson 1970) posits that customers’ decision making is accomplished through the joint action of two kinds of processes, namely, the fast and automatic process (recollection) that focuses on simple cues related to environmental factors, such as information quantity and information completeness (Luo et al. 2018), regardless of contextual details, and the slow and conscious process (familiarity) that scrutinizes a selected subset of relevant information. This theory essentially stresses the threshold of cognition for customers’ decisions (Wixted 2007). Faced with the holistic review set, it is rational to expect that outdated reviews will be delivered to the fast and automatic process while the latest reviews will be more attended to by customers. Therefore, we incorporate review timeliness in indicating customer attention.

The timeliness attention of customers guides our design of the timeliness attention mechanism. Customers do not always start at the latest review. Rather, each review point from the review sequence can potentially act as a target query, raising the need for a global attention mechanism (i.e., allowing the query to match the keys globally). The timeliness customer attention implies a time-decaying effect on a review’s utility. As time goes on, the review timeliness changes accordingly, so it is necessary to weight reviews by their chronological order, conforming to the ever-evolving information query requirements of customers. Hence, we design an attention mechanism that can dynamically attach larger weights to more recent reviews and generate decaying weights for reviews as time goes on in response to customers’ timeliness attention.

(2) Semantic Diversity Attention. Prior research has confirmed the existence of selection effect in customers posting and reading behaviors regarding reviews (Li and Hitt 2008). Both reviewers and customer readers hold heterogeneous preferences, which are subject to differentiation effect (Moe and Schweidel 2012). As reviewers highlight their preferred product attributes respectively, they produce a semantically diverse set of reviews. As customer readers take advantage of reviews they prefer, they jointly highlight a set of reviews that are semantically diverse and collectively give a comprehensive assessment on the product. Hence, the semantic diversity within a set of reviews should be an indispensable part of customer attention.

The semantic diversity attention of customers guides our design of the semantic diversity attention mechanism. Existing attention mechanisms are primarily equipped with affinity-based compatibility functions (as shown in Table A2 in the online appendix), which tend to attach more importance to mutually correlated reviews and thus result in semantic redundancy among attended reviews. The semantic diversity customer attention requires each query<sup>6</sup> to attend more to reviews that are incompatible with (complementary to) the query. Hence, we design an attention mechanism that can guide the prediction model to assimilate semantically diverse reviews in response to customers’ semantic diversity attention.

(3) Voting Awareness Attention. From the perspective of a single review, helpful reviews, with their higher diagnostic values and persuasiveness, are likely to attract more customer attention and exert greater impact on customers’ purchase decisions during the yielding stage. The number of helpfulness votes on a review, one of the most ubiquitous indicators for review helpfulness (Mudambi and Schuff 2010; Yin et al. 2014, 2021), is a good signal for customer attention at the individual review level. In line with prior studies (Kuan et al. 2015, Gang and Taeho 2019), we posit that voted reviews may draw more customer attention.

The voting awareness attention of customers guides our design of the voting awareness attention mechanism. The voting awareness customer attention requires reviews with more votes to be more intensively attended to. Accordingly, we design an attention mechanism that encourages the model to give higher weights to voted reviews in response to customers’ voting awareness attention.

(4) Varying Attention for Text-Image Interaction. Review text and images inherently play different roles in influencing customers’ purchase decisions. The descriptions offered by textual reviews provide rich insights into customers’ opinions toward the product (Yu et al. 2012, Yin et al. 2021), whereas the visual cues from review images can vividly depict the product within various real scenarios (Ma et al. 2018, Li and Xie 2020). The elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986) inspires us that, depending on the elaboration degree, there are two routes for customers to engage in the elaboration of multimodal reviews, that is, the central and peripheral routes. The central route is always achieved by customers’ thoughtful examination, whereas the peripheral route is often resulted from customers low involvement, for example, using simple decision rules for information evaluation (O’Keefe 2008). Having noted both the substitution (Cook and Lalljee 1972) and complementary (Muller et al. 2004) effects of text-image interaction, we posit that the text and images in a review are possibly processed in either the central route or the peripheral route, depending on whether (and to what extent) the specific content conveyed by the text (or images) is preferred by customers. Such preferences between the text and images are likely to differ across reviews. Therefore, the varying text-image interaction should be a core influential factor of customer attention at the review element level.

The varying attention for test-image interaction of customers guides our design of the adaptive attention mechanism for multimodal interaction. Customers’ attentions over text versus images should be adaptively varied across reviews to reflect the continuous variation likeli hood of the relative importance threshold between the text and images of a single review. Therefore, we design an attention mechanism that can adaptively assign differential weights to review text and images over reviews, conforming to customers’ adaptive attention to multimodal reviews. AMI is designed to combine modalities at the time-step level to conform to customer attention and is hence fundamentally different from common multimodal deep learning methods (Ramachandram and Tay lor 2017), which are developed for multimodal fusion and combine modalities at the modality level.

In summary, leveraging customer attention for multi modal reviews to enhance sales prediction calls for the design of specialized attention mechanisms. Table 1 summarizes our induced customer attention indicators, in terms of their theoretical rationales, methodological challenges, and our proposed solutions. Although some of our induced customer attention indicators (e.g., timeliness and voting awareness) have been conceptually investigated in previous behavioral studies (Kuan et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2020), the attention mechanisms we design based on the customer attention indicators constitute the core design science contribution of this paper.

########## Wolf_21 ##########


Misconduct in financial markets directly harms investors and deteriorates market participants’ trust in the financial system. Households and investors that are less willing to participate in financial markets often fail to achieve returns sufficient for retirement plans and other financial goals. The reluctance to use financial markets also increases companies’ cost of capital because capital becomes scarce, which ultimately reduces economic growth. Consequently, building and preserving trust in financial markets represents a major societal challenge.

Financial misconduct is widely regarded as being both common and costly (Dyck, Morse, & Zingales, 2010). Retail investors suffer damages amounting to billions of dollars each year. The Council of Economic Advisors (2015) estimates that the aggregate annual cost of conflicted advice in individual retirement accounts (i.e., investment advice where conflicts of interest are present because of high commissions for intermediaries) amounts to USD 17 billion.

There are various types of misconduct committed by brokers or investment advisors. One type of misconduct is providing false and misleading information to customers. This type of misconduct can be characterized as a principal-agent problem, where the broker benefits at the expense of the client or the market (Cumming, Johan, & Li, 2011). One example is a broker or investment advisor breaching the “suitability rule,” <sup>2</sup> meaning that transactions or investments in securities are not in accordance with the client’s investment profile. Further examples include brokers or investment advisors charging exaggerated fees or failing to obtain the best price for a client in securities transactions.

Other types of misconduct affecting financial markets include front running, scalping, and churning. Front running refers to brokers making use of their private information about a client’s order by buying or selling a security in advance of the client’s trade, allowing them to profit from a price movement that may be caused by the client’s (potentially large) trade (Cataldo & Killough, 2003). Churning describes the excessive buying and selling of securities on a client’s account without the consent of the client and disregarding the client’s interests in order to generate higher commissions for the intermediary (Cumming & Johan, 2008). Scalping refers to the practice of investment advisors purchasing a security before recommending it to a client without disclosing the benefit that they may derive from a potentially higher price should the customer follow their recommendation (Hazen, 2010). A more detailed overview of financial market manipulations performed by intermediaries is presented by Siering et al. (2017).

Brokers and investment advisors in the US are subject to a comprehensive system of regulations. FINRA, the responsible competent authority, mandates the disclosure of material facts about every broker and investment advisor, including any allegations or instances of wrongdoing (Lazaro, 2014). Using public and nonpublic regulatory data provided by FINRA, several studies have already proposed approaches to detect misconduct based on past intermediary misconduct (Egan, Matvos, & Seru, 2019; Qureshi & Sokobin, 2015). However, McCann, Qin, and Yan (2017) point out that the publicly available information provided via FINRA’s website BrokerCheck is not sufficient to identify brokers who are likely to commit misconduct and does not help investors protect themselves. Therefore, this paper presents different classifiers to detect misconduct based on self-disclosed information of financial intermediaries subjected to different levels of external verification.


########## Wolf_23 ##########


Millions of users routinely self-disclose personal information by participating in social media networks (Bazarova & Choi, 2014). Jourard (1971, p. 2) defines self-disclosure as “the act of revealing personal information to others.” Users of social media networks primarily self-disclose information to attract attention (Hollenbaugh & Ferris, 2014) and to maintain and develop relationships (Krasnova et al., 2010). Financial intermediaries and other professionals also disclose profile information on business networks to reach or interact with potential customers.

For the purpose of automatically identifying financial intermediary misconduct, we base our feature selection on two important theoretical streams in the context of self-disclosure in social media networks and fraud detection. First, we rely on information manipulation theory (McCornack, 1992) to explain why individuals who commit misconduct communicate differently, compared to honest individuals. Second, warranting theory (Walther et al., 2009) provides the theoretical basis for explaining why the level of external verification can influence the utility of different feature sets.

According to information manipulation theory, deceivers violate four key communication principles (McCornack, 1992). First, deceivers exaggerate or understate the quantity of information in order to conceal or misrepresent information. Second, deceivers tend to alter the quality of information or simply lie to disguise facts. Third, deceivers mislead receivers by providing information that is out of context. Fourth, deceivers may purposely communicate information in an ambiguous manner in order to confuse the receiver. Information manipulation theory has been empirically tested in the context of financial reporting fraud. In this context, researchers demonstrated that writers of misleading financial statements actually use techniques posited in information manipulation theory to deceive (Glancy & Yadav, 2011; Humpherys et al., 2011). Also, previous research has shown that classifiers that account for feature sets based on information manipulation theory can identify fraudulent projects in the context of crowdfunding campaigns (Siering, Koch, & Deokar, 2016).

Even if deceivers try to make their profiles look similar to those of truth-tellers, as suggested by interpersonal deception theory (Buller & Burgoon, 1996), their fabricated profiles will necessarily be less detailed and precise than authentic ones. However, interpersonal deception theory does not fit our context because it builds on repeated communication exchanges between the sender and receiver (Buller & Burgoon, 1996) whereas the LinkedIn profiles analyzed in this study represent static information as a “monolog” of the intermediary. In conclusion, we assume that financial intermediaries’ self-disclosed information on the business network LinkedIn differs between intermediaries who are likely to commit misconduct and those who are not likely to commit misconduct.

Warranting theory (Walther et al., 2009) proposes that individuals’ self-disclosed information is more valuable if it cannot be easily manipulated by the individuals themselves. This theory postulates that information that is harder to manipulate is more plausible or trustworthy than information that is easier to manipulate. Self-disclosed information on social media network profiles is subject to different levels of external verification. Other social media network users can confirm information provided by the individual profiles. Furthermore, self-disclosed information can be counterchecked with data provided by known and reliable external sources, which provides an even stronger source of verification than that provided by possibly unknown third parties. In the case of financial intermediaries, publicly available data provided by the regulatory authority FINRA’s website BrokerCheck can be used as a reliable external source that is difficult to manipulate.
