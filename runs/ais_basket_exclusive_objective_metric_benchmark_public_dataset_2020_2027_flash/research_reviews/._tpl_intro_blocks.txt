[P1] The sustained development of digitalization, big data, and artificial intelligence has spawned various realtime interaction–based online commercial modes, exemplified by e-commerce live streaming (Fei et al. 2021), social network–based marketing (Lien et al. 2017), and robocaller-based telemarketing (Azad et al. 2020). A striking advantage of these emerging commercial modes is that they enable closer customer–retailer interaction. Besides, multiform communication channels, such as voice and video, allow retailers to interact with their customers directly as in a traditional off-line face-to-face sales scenario. This offers enormous commercial opportunities to retailers because closer customer–retailer interaction implies deeper customer engagement, which is positively related to sales (Morgan et al. 2018). As cus tomers interact intensively with retailers, improving customers’ satisfaction during the interaction to enhance their purchase intention is attracting widespread interest (Ba and Johansson 2008).

[P2] Some off-line retailers, such as retailers of fixed assets (e.g., houses and cars) and experiential services (e.g., dining and entertainment), have to sell their products in brick-and-mortar stores but may also benefit from real-time interactions with customers. In particular, inviting potential customers online for off-line product experience and sales (i.e., invite online and experience in store)—the focus of this study—has emerged as a cutting-edge marketing tool for off-line retailers. In a typical invite-online-and-experience-in store mode, potential customers browse products online and may further conduct a simple registration on the platform—either a third-party platform or the retailer’s platform—to learn more product information. The offline retailer entrusts a caller agent (hereafter referred to as agent) service provider to invite potential customers to the store and pays the service provider for the number of actual customer arrivals. An agent, acting on behalf of the retailer, calls potential customers several times in succession to invite them to brick-and-mortar stores. Unlike those online commercial modes that wait for customers passively, the invite-online-and-experience-instore mode identifies and invites customers actively. Besides, the use of an agent service provider centralizes customer–retailer interactions, easing the operational management of customer satisfaction (Radziwill and Benton 2017, Chung et al. 2020).

[P3] Effectively predicting customers’ off-line responses to online invitations, that is, customer response prediction, is key to the success of the invite-online-and-experiencein-store mode. On the one hand, there is a significant discrepancy between the huge potential customer market and the limited marketing capability of the agent service provider. Success in attracting customers to the store depends on widespread outbound calls to and long-term follow-ups with potential customers. However, because of limited time and capacity, the agent service provider may not be able to implement a point-to-point precision marketing strategy for all potential customers. On the other hand, if customers’ willingness to respond can be predicted accurately in time (e.g., once the first call has ended), the agent can subsequently focus on customers with high response willingness, thereby not only improving the success rate of online invitation, but also enhancing the marketing efficiency.

[P4] Customer response prediction for online invitation, albeit with great importance, is fraught with challenges. One major challenge lies in the lack of personal information. Different from conventional telemarketing modes, which target existing customers whose personal details are well-recorded (Moro et al. 2014, Apampa 2016), and standard online/off-line commercial modes, in which detailed customer information is conveniently available once customers have placed orders (van Dolen et al. 2007, Stein and Ramaseshan 2016), little customer information is accessible to the agent before the first call in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode. During the interaction with customers through voice chat, the agent is tasked to proactively promote candidate products to customers, learn customers’ preferences and demands, and respond to customers’ concerns. Thus, the voice chat well-accommodates the product, customer, and interaction factors that are verified as strong indicators of customer satisfaction (Stein and Ramaseshan 2016, McLean and Osei-Frimpong 2017). In this sense, the voice chat should be a valuable data source that can be leveraged for customer response prediction. Besides, the limited capacity and high running cost of the agent urge customer response to be accurately unraveled as soon as possible. Therefore, customer response prediction based on the voice chat is of great importance in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode.

[P5] Prior research studies prediction tasks similar to online invitation–based customer response prediction, such as marketing response prediction (Apampa 2016), telemarketing success prediction (Moro et al. 2014), and response prediction for advertising (Chapelle et al. 2015). However, the methods developed for these tasks cannot deal with voice chat–based customer response prediction. First, these methods target regular customers and, thus, are established upon rich customer information, whereas the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode targets potential new customers, whose personal information is extremely difficult to obtain in advance. Second, these methods are developed largely for quantitative features, such as customer attributes, product attributes, and trans action data (Chun 2012, Olson and Chae 2012), whereas voice chat data are unstructured and more complex.

[P6] Extant research provides theoretical guidance for customer response prediction. There is a consensus that satisfaction is a decisive factor for customers’ purchase intention (Kuo et al. 2009, Fang et al. 2014). Specifically in the context of online customer–retailer interaction, researchers further find that customer satisfaction de pends predominantly on online experience (Novak et al. 2000, Rose et al. 2011), which is a holistic process made up of a customer’s online interaction with an organization and can be derived from a set of touch points (McLean and Wilson 2016). Studies employing the expectation-disconfirmation theory show that, in addition to online experience, customer satisfaction is also affected by expectation (Oliver 1977, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). The discrepancy between expectation and actual experience constitutes disconfirmation with positive disconfirmation associated with satisfaction and negative disconfirmation associated with dissatisfaction (McKinney et al. 2002).

[P7] In light of such a theoretical foundation, it is sensible to conduct customer response prediction for online invitation by modeling customers’ experiences and expectations with the voice chat data. The voice chat is well suited for deriving customers’ experiences as it covers those crucial touch points, including factors related to customers’ behavior, the retailer’s performance, and customer–retailer interaction, which are influential to the formation of actual experience (Rose et al. 2011, Klaus and Maklan 2013). However, it is an extraordinary challenge to capture customers’ expectations without their personal information, not to mention that customers adjust their expectations ceaselessly as their actual experiences evolve (Fan and Suh 2014, Lankton et al. 2016). Furthermore, customers’ expectations are inherently rooted in their preferences for certain product attributes, and such preferences may differ across customers (Lemon and Verhoef 2016). To overcome these difficulties, we pose our first research question (RQ1): how to learn customers’ differential preferences and dynamic expectations in the absence of their personal information. In response to RQ1, we propose an embedding strategy named attribute-frequency-and-order-based preference embedding to construct a personalized preference template for each customer. Then, we split the voice chat into multiple dialogue fragments according to the customer’s and agent’s pauses and further transform each preference template into an attribute–fragment matrix. In doing so, we accommodate the dynamics of the expectation as attribute–fragment expectations allow distinct expectations toward various attributes over time steps (i.e., fragments).

[P8] As for customer experience, we model it from two aspects: information satisfaction, which is affected by the quality of information used for purchase decision making, and attribute satisfaction, which indicates the level of contentment of the customer with certain product attributes (Spreng et al. 1996). In this sense, both textual and audio (multiview)<sup>1</sup> information from the voice chat should be subsumed when measuring customers’ experiences. Textual information describes the key product attributes, whereas audio information reflects the way the informational content is delivered, and the two types of information synergistically determine customers’ perceived information quality (Lee et al. 2002). With a voice chat, it is natural to expect that the customer experience evolves as the conversation goes on. In line with the dynamic expectation, we also capture the evolution of customer experience. Additionally, we notice that the voice chat is essentially a flow of question–answer pairs between the customer and agent. During different fragments of the dialogue, the customer and agent discuss different topics, resulting in differential experiences. Specifically, the agent’s replies to the customer’s questions tend to affect the customer’s satisfaction, whereas the customer’s responses to the agent’s questions tend to reflect the customer’s product preference and purchase intention. This indicates that customers’ experiences for the voice chat–based online invitation are formed and affected in an interlaced way. Given these requirements for modeling customers’ experiences, we pose another research question (RQ2): how to learn customers’ dynamic experiences from the multiview voice chat information. To this end, we design a multiview deep learning method named cross-view attention–based deep learning, which learns customers’ dynamic experiences in an interlaced way from the multiview voice chat information.

[P9] Given the constructed customer experience and expectation, we have two options to achieve customer response prediction. One is to characterize the disconfirmation by the discrepancy between experience and expectation as inspired by the expectation-disconfirmation theory (Oliver 1977, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010) such that customers’ satisfaction and willingness to visit a store can be causally revealed. However, it is difficult to optimize the parameters associated with customers’ expectations and experiences directly because of the lack of groundtruth labels. Alternatively, we can liberate the predictive power of dynamic expectation and experience features by reconstructing them in hidden spaces with deep learning methods in response to the complexity of the multiview voice data. As deep learning models are well-known as “black boxes” (Adadi and Berrada 2018), we propose to learn theory-driven satisfaction to enhance deep learning–based customer response prediction. Hence, we investigate our third research question (RQ3): how to leverage theory-driven dynamic customer satisfaction to enhance the perform ance of voice chat–based customer response prediction. To tackle RQ3, we synthesize the attribute-frequencyand-order-based preference embedding strategy and the cross-view attention–based deep learning model into a bimodal sequential neural network structure and optimize them coordinately with a learning objective named bidirectional sparsity-based contrastive learning, which accommodates the dynamic expectation and experience for theory-driven satisfaction generation and response prediction.

[P10] Overall, we propose a theory-driven deep learning method named dynamic satisfaction–empowered deep learning (DSDL) for voice chat–based customer response prediction in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode. By synergizing its three core components (i.e., attribute-frequency-and-order-based preference embedding, cross-view attention–based deep learning, and bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning), DSDL leverages theory-driven satisfaction to enhance the performance of deep learning–based customer response prediction. Experiment results in the context of agent-based car sales show that DSDL outperforms relevant, state-ofthe-art deep learning methods in prediction and representation learning performance. Explanatory analysis results render insights into DSDL’s predictive advantages and its learned customer satisfaction.

[P1] The recent advancement in machine learning and the increasing availability of big data have encouraged organizations to apply predictive analytics to inform mission-critical decisionmaking [14, 25, 32, 48]. Such predictive analytics applications often leverage a supervised machine learning (SML) model to make empirical predictions based on the patterns captured from the data [48]. For example, review platforms leverage text classification to detect spam reviews [33]; email service providers leverage classification models to filter out spam emails [17]; mobile app platforms utilize text mining and image processing to detect fraudulent apps based on app descriptions and icons [57]; financial institutions predict default rate by leveraging borrower information [8]; human resources use SML to assist job candidate selection [60]; healthcare practitioner discover treatment barriers from patient communities [58]; and autonomous vehicles leverage sensory data to predict energy eficiency [50].

[P2] Despite its enormous potential, predictive analytics is not without its pitfalls. One emerging threat to predictive analytics has proven to be particularly problematic: adversarial attacks [23]. As SML-enabled predictive analytics become an integral part of organizational decision-making, adversaries are incentivized to exploit the vulnerabilities of SML models underlying predictive analytics applications and mislead these applications into making erroneous decisions favoring the adversaries. In particular, such adversarial attacks often involve meticulously perturbing the input to the target SML model for achieving the desired output. This could be concerning for predictive analytics applied in the domains where the input is provided by the individual who is immediately afected by the decision made by the SML model [8, 13, 60]. Examples of such application domains include spam detection [33], fraud detection [1], credit approval [8, 59], employee hiring [8, 59], and student admission [8, 59]. In particular, a spam review could be meticulously modified to bypass the spam review detection system and spam emails could be deliberately designed to deceive SML-based spam email filtering systems. Such adversarial attacks on SML models could undermine the decision-making enabled by predictive analytics, leading to potential reputation damages and financial losses.

[P3] The vulnerability of SML models is not new and has received increasing attention from the research communities [8, 13, 18, 23, 26]. Nonetheless, it warrants further investigation in the emerging applications of predictive analytics for three reasons. First, organizations have increasingly applied SML-enabled predictive analytics into their mission-critical business processes [11, 20]; however, predictive analytics in information system (IS) research has not fully acknowledged the potential security threat against the vulnerabilities of the underlying SML models. There is limited understanding and awareness of the issue in the predictive analytics literature to provide guidelines for organizations utilizing predictive analytics taking inputs from potentially unreliable sources. Second, the IS literature focuses the evaluation of predictive analytics primarily on the models’ predictive performance, such as accuracy [34, 48]. The systematic assessment of predictive analytics applications’ robustness to adversarial exploitation has been generally understudied. As organizations decision-making about predictive analytics is informed by its evaluation, there is a need for adversarial robustness assessment techniques to complement the existing practice of evaluating predictive analytics applications. Third, the limited awareness of adversarial attacks and the lack of robustness assessment have resulted in the deployment of predictive analytics applications that are potentially vulnerable to adversarial attacks. The exploitation of such applications could lead to financial losses, reputation damages, and health and safety risks, impeding the realization of predictive analytics’ enormous potential. As such, there is an urgent need for enhancing the adversarial robustness of these deployed predictive analytics applications to adversarial exploitation.

[P4] In this paper, we seek to address two critical research questions relating to the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications: (1) How to assess the robustness of predictive analytics applications to adversarial attacks? (2) How to improve the robustness of predictive analytics applications in an adversarial context? Drawing upon the technology threat avoidance theory, we propose a research framework for assessing and enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications. The research framework seeks to provide a principled approach for improving the adversarial robustness of existing predictive analytics applications. We further instantiate the proposed framework by developing a robust text classification system. Leveraging the technology threat avoidance theory as a kernel theory, our proposed framework comprises a novel assessment model for evaluating the adversarial robustness of a predictive analytics application, and design principles for enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications. We demonstrate the efectiveness of our framework in the context of text classification tasks. In particular, we develop the ARText system, which jointly incorporates ensemble learning and adversarial retraining to improve its robustness to adversarial attacks. To rigorously evaluate our proposed system, we conducted two sets of evaluations on the testbeds of spam review detection and spam email detection. The first set of evaluations examined the utility of our proposed adversarial robustness assessment. The second set of evaluations gauged the eficacy of our proposed system in enhancing adversarial robustness. Overall, the ARText system was able to outperform baseline models in detecting adversarial spam reviews, indicating the efectiveness of our proposed design framework.

[P1] Online reviews (reviews for short herein) have become one of the most effectual information channels that bridge customers and their targeted products (services) (Yin et al. 2014). Many studies have provided theoretical and empirical evidence that reviews affect customers purchase intention (Forman et al. 2008, Li et al. 2019). Such an indispensable role of reviews for customers endows reviews with promising potential for revealing future sales (Zhu and Zhang 2010). Sales prediction, as a focal problem of operational researchers and practitioners, is conducive to retailers’ product operation and business value creation (Yu et al. 2012). Unlike conventional quantitative features for sales prediction, such as a product’s internal attributes (e.g., product type and historical sales) and environmental factors (e.g., market situation and holiday effect) (Tsoumakas 2019), reviews open up an avenue for unraveling the future trend of sales from the perspective of customers’ attitude toward and demand for the product.

[P2] As many studies have shown substantial impact of reviews on product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Forman et al. 2008, Zhu and Zhang 2010, van Nguyen et al. 2020), a straightforward way to realize reviewbased sales prediction is to incorporate all available review information with respect to a product, such as quantitative indicators (e.g., ratings and votes) and textual descriptions, into the prediction model. This, how ever, will inevitably incur predictive bias as only a small portion of reviews can be actually perceived and internalized by customers within a certain period (Li and Hitt 2008, Moe and Schweidel 2012), let alone some useless reviews (Qahri-Saremi and Montazemi 2019). On the other hand, although prior research has made several attempts at review-based sales prediction (Yu et al. 2012, van Nguyen et al. 2020), their provided solution approaches suffered serious deficiencies. From the data perspective, they commonly used unstructured reviews in quantitative ways. Textual reviews’ valence, extremity, and helpfulness votes have all been extensively investigated (Sahoo et al. 2018), whereas the predictive values of the semantic information conveyed in review texts and the visual information embedded in review images remain largely under-investigated. From the model perspective, original reviews were taken by a prediction model without effective differentiation, impeding the proper play of their predictive role.

[P3] Indeed, sales performance is inherently rooted in customers’ purchase intention, which is sensitive to customer’ past experiences and opinions conveyed by reviews (Sahoo et al. 2018). Furthermore, customers are more likely to be affected by the reviews they read carefully, whereas reviews that receive little scrutiny are less likely to affect customers’ purchase intention. Therefore, reviews that attract intensive customer attention<sup>1</sup> can be naturally expected to be more influential to customers purchase decisions and have more predictive capabilities on sales. In this vein, it is sensible to explore what kinds of reviews attract more customer attention and whether such reviews are more useful for sales prediction.

[P4] The value of reviews to customers has been assessed by information quality (Wang and Strong 1996), argument quality (Liu et al. 2020), and review helpfulness (Forman et al. 2008). There is a consensus that more helpful reviews tend to be perceived to have higher quality and exert greater impact on customers’ purchase intention (Filieri et al. 2018, Yin et al. 2020). Helpful reviews qualified by content-based indicators (e.g., valence, objectivity, and intelligibility), albeit with their considerable diagnostic values for customers’ product evaluation (Filieri 2015), may not necessarily be the most influential of the purchase intention of the customer base. This is because customers with heterogeneous preferences naturally favor reviews with different content characteristics (Moe and Schweidel 2012), and thus it is difficult to ascertain which content styles will receive customer attention steadily. The same review content can be differently or even conversely evaluated due to customers’ differential characteristics. There have been conflicting findings regarding review helpfulness; for example, negative reviews can exert both positive and hurtful effects on sales (Lee et al. 2008, Berger et al. 2010), and longer reviews can be both more and less helpful (Fink et al. 2018).

[P5] Customers’ attention paid to multimodal reviews is reflected in their browsing behaviors, which can be naturally characterized along the temporal and spatial dimensions. That is, the two dimensions coordinately depict the transitions of customer’s browsing attention over granularity levels of multimodal reviews and dif ferent stages. From a process perspective, customers attention and evaluation efforts paid to reviews can be understood over two stages of information processing, according to the message learning (Riley 1954) and infor mation processing (McGuire 1968) theories. Specifically, given a set of reviews, customers would first decide what kinds of reviews to pay attention to (i.e., the recep tion stage) and then comprehend the ones they heed to reach their product evaluation (i.e., the yielding stage). Although the aforementioned content-based helpful ness indicators indeed partially account for the yielding stage (Weathers et al. 2015), with the expectation that review characteristics good for diagnosticity will be more favorably evaluated (Kuan et al. 2015), they do not capture customers’ initial attention to reviews in the reception stage. In this regard, we posit that when faced with a vast number of reviews, customers are unlikely to evaluate all reviews by their diagnostic values. Rather, in practical scenarios, customers tend to only attend to a review subset to fulfill product evaluation while forgoing the others regardless of their helpfulness. This posit is consistent with the previous finding that review readers, with their limited attention spans, are unlikely to go through every review (Kuan et al. 2015). Therefore, we propose that customers’ initial attention to reviews, as an important prerequisite to their follow-up evaluation, should be attended to in sales prediction.

[P6] From a spatial perspective, we, conforming to customers’ browsing behaviors along a top-down (or bottom-up) roadmap, decompose their attention into fou granularity levels, that is, the holistic review set, review subset (along the temporal or topic dimension), individual review, and review element (modality) levels, respectively. For example, the process that customers drill down from the holistic review set to useful elementary review information (i.e., the top-down case) may span granularity levels from the holistic review set level all the way to the review element level. Given the reviews of a product, it is also possible that a customer would start at a certain information type of a review (e.g., release date, number of votes, text, or image) and then expand from it to more reviews successively (i.e., the bottom-up case). Overall, we strive to unravel customer attention with a process-granularity framework (elaborated in Section 3) and delve into our first research question (RQ1): What kinds of reviews conform to customer attention paid at different granularity levels in the purchase decision process?

[P7] On the ground of theoretical analysis (presented in Section 3), we posit that customers are likely to pay their timeliness attention, semantic diversity attention, votingawareness attention, and attention for multimodal interaction at the four granularity levels (from the holistic review set level to the review element level). At the holistic review set level, we, drawing on the dual-process theory (Groves and Thompson 1970), posit that customer attention is likely to dynamically focus on a set of timely reviews rather than being time invariant. At the topic level, studies concerning selection effect show that customers with heterogeneous preferences focus on diverse product attributes, causing their actually attended and perceived helpful reviews to differ from each other (Li and Hitt 2008, Moe and Schweidel 2012). At the review level, we consider the number of helpfulness votes, one of the most prevalent review helpfulness indicators that are likely to attract customers’ attention and comprehension efforts strongly (Mudambi and Schuff 2010, Kuan et al. 2015, Salehan and Kim 2016). At the review element level, we focus on the interactive role of heterogeneous review modalities in dispersing customer attention because typical reviews simultaneously contain textual descriptions and images. Based on the elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986), we posit that when customers fortify their purchase decisions by virtue of reviews, they may pay asymmetrical attention to texts versus images.

[P8] Overall, our induced indicators of customer attention include (1) timeliness, (2) semantic diversity, (3) review helpfulness, and (4) varying multimodal interaction.<sup>2</sup> Furthermore, given the prevalence and ubiquity of multimodal reviews<sup>3</sup> nowadays, an even more imminent research question (RQ2) is as follows: How can we leverage customer attention to enhance the effectiveness of multimodal reviews for sales prediction? In response to RQ2, we propose a deep learning method named DTV-AMI (for DTV attention-enhanced deep learning with adaptive attention for multimodal interaction) specifically designed for multimodal review–based sales prediction. DTV-AMI draws its strength from two components, that is, DTV (for semantic diversity, timeliness, and voting awareness attention mechanisms) and AMI (for adaptive attention mechanism for multimodal interaction), which are constructed based on four neural network attention mechanisms corresponding to the four customer attention indicators.

[P9] Specifically, our proposed DTV allows the prediction model to attend more to timely, semantically diverse, and voted reviews. Given a sequence of reviews for sales prediction, DTV deduces customer attention by recursively taking each review as a potential starting point of customers’ browsing behaviors, in response to the fact that customers arrive successively rather than at one stroke. While scanning the reviews dynamically, DTV attends more to timely, semantically diverse, and voted reviews. The proposed AMI further empowers the prediction model to make an adaptive tradeoff (i.e., weighting) between review text and images (if any) at each review point and differentiate such adaptive tradeoffs across different reviews, conforming to customers heterogeneous browsing behaviors. Overall, DTV-AMI incorporates our induced customer attention indicators to capitalize on multimodal reviews’ due predictive power on sales. Experiment results in a case study of sales<sup>4</sup> (specifically, monthly occupancy rate) prediction in the hotel industry with 2,685 hotels and their 1.2 million review texts and 197 thousand review images show that DTV-AMI outperformed benchmarked deep learning methods, demonstrating the value of customer attention for better leveraging multimodal reviews for sales prediction.

[P1] The rapid proliferation of new information technology (IT) in recent years has created significant benefits for modern society. Despite its usefulness, IT often possesses numerous vulnerabilities that can allow unauthorized users unfettered access to an organization's networks, systems, private data, and other critical assets. Sophisticated hackers often develop exploits (e.g., SQL injections, cross-site scripting, etc.) in plain-text source code to execute cyber breaches (Samtani et al., 2017). Each cyber breach is estimated to cost an average of \$7,910,000 to an organization (Sun et al., 2020). This cost underscores the importance for organizations to proactively identify exploits. To this end, organizations are increasingly investing in cyber threat intelligence (CTI) capabilities to detect emerging exploits (Wagner et al., 2019).

[P2] To develop proactive CTI, academia and industry are increasingly examining openly accessible exploits in major international hacker forums, exploit darknet markets (DNMs), and public exploit repositories (Samtani et al., 2020). Hacker forums are large discussion boards that provide freely accessible exploits developed by the large and evolving international hacker community. These forums often contain millions of user-generated posts and span multiple geopolitical countries and regions such as Russia, the United States, the Middle East, and others. Exploit DNMs allow hackers to share and sell highly specialized exploits (e.g., 0-days). While less common than in hacker forums, the exploits found within exploit DNMs are more sophisticated than those found in hacker forums. Public exploit repositories are collections of exploits manually labeled and vetted by industry professionals and CTI experts. These repositories are more common than exploit DNMs and provide more exploit details (e.g., attack type, common vulnerabilities and exposures, etc.) than hacker forums. Sample exploits from a hacker forum, an exploit DNM, and a public exploit repository are shown in Figure 1.

[P3] Hacker forum posts with exploit source code (top left of Figure 1) often lack a clear exploit label (i.e., category). In contrast, exploit DNMs (top right of Figure 1) and public exploit repositories (bottom middle of Figure 1) offer a rich set of metadata and clear exploit labels (e.g., local, web). A recent CTI report from the renowned SANS Institute indicated that cyber analysts at leading CTI companies require assistance in identifying, collecting, and analyzing unknown exploits from hacker communities to proactively develop knowledge about hacker capabilities (Brown & Lee, 2021; Newman, 2020). This is partly in response to the Mirai malware that caused widespread damage across the United States. The source code for Mirai was posted on a hacker community platform two months before its large-scale usage (Yue et al., 2019). These attacks show that hacker forum posts can be a valuable source for identifying precursors to attacks and require up-to-date monitoring. However, most of the exploits found in hacker forums are unlabeled (Ampel et al., 2020). Unlabeled exploits can often prevent cyber analysts from identifying the specific tactics and strategies of hackers and stymie the production of timely and relevant CTI (Tounsi & Rais, 2018). Extant procedures to label exploits are currently manual, which is a time-consuming and nontrivial process (Wagner et al., 2019). Cyber analysts frequently cite these manual processes and high workloads as their primary challenges, leading to burnout and turnover (Agyepong et al., 2020). Automating the exploit labeling process can reduce manual processes and provide cyber analysts with tactical, strategic, and operational CTI capabilities. We present selected benefits that exploit labeling can provide to each CTI type in Table 1.

[P4] Tactical CTI is the techniques, tactics, and procedures used by threat actors (Wagner et al., 2019). Providing clear labels to hacker forum source code allows cyber analysts to quickly organize discovered exploits, operationalize them, and discover the capabilities of threat actors. For example, once a cyber analyst knows a set of exploits are SQL injections (as opposed to other exploits), the analyst can build a secure copy of their IT (e.g., a virtual machine with an SQL server), conduct targeted penetration testing with the labeled exploits, and identify suitable mitigations and cyber-defenses against the successfully executed exploits. This hacker emulation is known as red teaming and is essential to modern cybersecurity practices (Alomar et al., 2020). From an operational CTI perspective, labeled exploits can help cyber analysts monitor new exploits posted on hacker forums and choose relevant exploits to investigate further in a targeted fashion (Tounsi & Rais, 2018). This operational CTI can be combined with tactical CTI and external resources (e.g., SANS CTI reports) to strengthen cybersecurity systems against specific attacks (e.g., Mirai malware). For example, SANS CTI reports may link web application exploits to a specific advanced persistent threat group. An analyst can use the combined information of newly labeled exploits and known strategies employed by that APT group to select the appropriate system-hardening strategies. From a strategic perspective, exploit labels allow senior officials to identify attack trends (e.g., how exploits rise or fall over time) in a timely fashion such that they can make the appropriate security investments (Tounsi & Rais, 2018). Moreover, such information can be integrated into cyber-risk assessment models to create proactive, preventive, and dynamic insights that facilitate effective cybersecurity investments (Shin & Lowry, 2020).

[P5] In this study, we adopted the computational design science paradigm (Rai, 2017) to develop a novel deep transfer learning exploit labeler (DTL-EL) framework that leverages the rich metadata and large quantities from exploit DNMs and public exploit repositories (source domain) to automatically label each exploit's source code found in hacker forums (target domain). The proposed framework is based on deep transfer learning (DTL), a prevailing approach in cybersecurity analytics and CTI literature in IS. DTL can transfer knowledge from a large and labeled source domain to improve model performance when the target domain is not large or rich enough to create a fit model (Zhuang et al., 2020). DTL has particular value in CTI applications that do not often have rich labeled datasets, such as hacker forum analysis (Samtani et al., 2020).

[P9] Moreover, recent information systems (IS) cybersecurity research has shown the applicability of deep transfer learning (DTL) for CTI, where a model trained on a larger labeled source domain (English darknet market postings) provided significant benefits to three smaller target domain models (Russian, French, and Italian darknet market postings) (Ebrahimi et al., 2022). In addition to DTL, the proposed DTL-EL framework also incorporates several state-of-the-art methods in deep learning (DL) and attention mechanisms that were carefully designed for exploit labeling. First, DTL-EL includes a bidirectional long shortterm memory (BiLSTM) with its hidden and cell states pre-initialized with the titles of professionally vetted exploits in exploit DNMs and public exploit repositories. The pre-initialization design emulates how a cybersecurity professional reads the title of an exploit before examining its source code. Second, DTL-EL transfers the pre-initialized BiLSTM model's learned representation of the source domain dataset (i.e., exploit DNMs and public exploit repositories) to help label exploit source code in hacker forums (target domain). Third, the DTL-EL incorporates a self-attention mechanism to identify and capture semantic and long sequential dependencies within the exploit source code to improve DTL-EL's performance. Consistent with the guidelines of the computational design science paradigm, we rigorously evaluated the proposed DTL-EL with a series of benchmark experiments (Rai, 2017). The DTL-EL framework can help future IS researchers execute targeted cybersecurity analytics on exploit labeling, trend analysis, real-time monitoring, and other critical CTI tasks.

[P10] This paper is organized as follows. First, we review IS cybersecurity analytics research, computational design science guidelines, and hacker-forum exploit analysis literature. Second, we identify research gaps from prior literature and pose research questions. Third, we present DTL-EL's methodological foundation by reviewing the BiLSTM with attention mechanism and DTL. Fourth, we describe the proposed DTL-EL. Fifth, we present the results of our experiments. Sixth, we summarize our proposed DTL-EL's practical implications and contributions to the IS knowledge base. Finally, we conclude this research and discuss potential future directions.
