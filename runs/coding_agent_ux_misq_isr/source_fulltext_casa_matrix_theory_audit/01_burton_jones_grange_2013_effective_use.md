## 1. Introduction

As Orlikowski (2000, p. 425) notes, “Technology per se can’t increase or decrease the productivity of workers’ performance, only use of it can.” This simple, yet powerful idea, underpins one of the largest bodies of research in our field: the study of information technology (IT) acceptance and use (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2003). That work has been enormously beneficial in helping us learn how to get users to use systems, but system use alone is not sufficient to obtain benefits (Seddon 1997); the use must be effective. At first glance, this notion of “effective use” might seem obvious. It is, after all, part of this journal’s own mission:

The mission of ISR remains unchanged: to advance knowledge about the effective and efficient utilization of information technology by individuals, groups, organizations, society, and nations for the improvement of economic and social welfare (Agarwal 2011, p. 1)

In this article, we show that the concept of effective use is, in fact, quite complex. Moreover, it is extremely under researched. Marcolin et al. (2000, p. 53) identified this problem a decade ago and predicted a shift “from the study of use 0 0 0 to the study of effective use,” but their prediction has not yet been fulfilled. We believe it is important to take that step.<sup>1</sup> The purpose of this paper is to present a theory of effective use that we hope can support and motivate such research.

The way we developed our theory was driven by a second motivation: to consider the unique nature of information systems (IS). That is, we do not wish to propose a theory to explain the effective use of any artifact, such as a theory of human motivations or tool affordances. Rather, we ask what is an information system and what does this imply for what effective use involves, and what drives it? Answering this question requires us to take a specific view on what constitutes information systems and to derive specific implications from this view. As we will explain, we chose a theory known as representation theory that offers a particular view of information systems, and we drew a number of implications from it. Other views of information systems could have, and hopefully will be taken, and other implications could have, and hopefully will be drawn. The work we present is simply an initial attempt to develop a platform for research on effective use, and to develop a theory of a specific IS phenomena (here, effective use) from a general theory of IS (here, representation theory<sup>2</sup>).

We begin by defining key terms and reviewing the relevant literature on effective use and representation theory. We provide an especially detailed overview of representation theory in that section to provide a clear foundation for how we use it in the paper. After providing this review, we present our theory of effective use. This involves presenting a general framework, applying the framework, and deriving two models from it. We then discuss the implications of our theory and conclude the paper.

## 2. Background

In this section we define effective use and summarize the two bodies of research that inform our theory: research on effective use, and representation theory.

## 2.1. Defining Effective Use

We define effective use as using a system in a way that helps attain the goals for using the system. Our definition is adapted from Burton-Jones and Straub (2006), who defined system use in terms of a user, system, and task, and defined a task as a “goal-directed activity” (p. 231). To move from use to effective use, we simply shifted the emphasis from using the system to perform a goal-directed activity to using it in a way that helps attain the relevant goal. Four assumptions underlie our definition. First, we recognize that “use” can occur at any level of analysis, but we focus our initial theory on the individual level for reasons of scope. As we show, even at this level it is quite complex. Second, we assume that systems are never used just to use them; rather, they are used to achieve other goals (Gasser 1986). As a goal is a “cognitive representation of a desired end point” (Fishbach and Ferguson 2007, p. 491), we assume that the relevant goal for effective use is simply whatever end point the system is used to attain. Third, we assume that goal attainment has objective qualities; it may be hard to evaluate in some cases, but it is not completely subjective. Operationally, it is assessed in terms of performance (Sonnentag 2002). Thus, we view effective use and performance to be relatively objective notions. Finally, we recognize that different stakeholders (e.g., designers, users, managers) may have different views on the goals for using a system. Rather than taking just one of them, we will explain how researchers can study effective use from different stakeholder perspectives.

Our definition of effective use may initially appear similar to the concept of perceived usefulness (or performance expectancy), which is the degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her better attain significant rewards (Venkatesh et al. 2003, p. 23). However, the constructs differ in scope because effective use focuses on rewards that stem from the way a system is used, whereas perceived usefulness focuses more broadly on rewards that stem from use, not just the way it is used (e.g., it could include rewards that stem from the context in which an IS is used). The constructs also differ in terms of raters, as perceived usefulness refers to a user’s expectation or perception (i.e., it resides in the user’s mind), whereas we view effective use objectively (i.e., it is an observable behavior).

## 2.2. Past Research on Effective Use

After a detailed review of the literature, we found no studies on the nature or drivers of effective use for systems in general. However, three small streams of research inform this area. First, there are studies that have not studied effective use explicitly, but have done so implicitly by studying outcomes that stem from how systems are used, e.g., if users exploit their knowledge of an IS when using it (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006) or apply and adapt their knowledge of systems (Barki et al. 2007). Such studies often draw on task technology fit theory (Goodhue and Thompson 1995), arguing that benefits stem from using the right features for the task at hand (e.g., Ahearne et al. 2008, Devaraj and Kohli 2003).

Second, there are studies that examine concepts that are similar to effective use, but that deviate sufficiently in spirit or content to be considered differently. For instance, the concept of “faithful appropriation” (Chin et al. 1997, DeSanctis and Poole 1994) is closely related to effective use in that it relates to outcomes that stem from use, but it differs in that it traditionally focuses on whether or not the system is used in a manner consistent with the designer’s intentions. Likewise, the concept of “infusion” emanated from organizational-level research examining how fully systems are integrated into organizations (Saga and Zmud 1994). Although defined in terms of performance outcomes, it differs from effective use in that it refers to how extensively the system is integrated into the work and how fully it is used. Studies operationalizing infusion at the individual level have similarly focused on this notion of extensive (Meister and Compeau 2002) or full and sophisticated use (Jain and Kanungo 2005), notions that are absent from our definition of effective use. There are also studies, both in our field and others, that have examined factors that may support effective use, but are not effective use per se, such as the competence of users or the usability of systems (Desjardins et al. 2005, Xie and Wolfram 2002).

Finally, there are studies that explicitly studied effective use, or synonyms thereof. It turns out that there are very few such studies. To be as sure of this as possible, we conducted two literature reviews. The first was a detailed but informal review based on our own knowledge of the IS literature. This yielded three articles published in a range of outlets (shown in the bottom rows of Table 1). Concurrently, we conducted a formal literature review of four fields that study IT use: human computer interaction and computer supported cooperative work (HCI/CSCW), health informatics, information science, and information systems. For each field, we carried out a structured review of all articles published in three highly regarded and diverse outlets during the years 1990–2010. The 12 outlets we reviewed were:

—Human computer interaction and computer supported cooperative work: ACM Transactions on Computer Human Interaction (TOCHI); ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI); and ACM Conference on Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)

—Health informatics: Journal of the American Society of Medical Informatics (JAMIA); International Journal of Medical Informatics (IJMI); and Journal of Medical Internet Research (JMIR)

—Information science: Journal of the American Society of Information Science and Technology (JASIST); Information Processing and Management (IPM); and Journal of Documentation (JOD)

—Information systems: MIS Quarterly (MISQ); Information Systems Research (ISR); and Information and Organization (I&O)

We split the 12 outlets into three groups of four outlets, with each group containing one outlet from each of the four fields. We then hired six research assistants, formed them into three pairs, and assigned each pair to a group of outlets. Each pair then searched online for articles in these outlets using key words such as “effective use” and synonyms thereof. Each pair was first to identify articles that mentioned effective use, then classify in how much detail it was studied (high, some, low), and for those that studied it in detail, to identify whether and, if so how, the authors defined it, and what its dimensions and drivers were. Each pair worked until they had 100% agreement. Each pair was then asked to check a randomly selected 30% of another pair’s codes. Finally, we hired a seventh assistant to check a randomly selected 30% of all the codes from all the pairs. After each iteration, pairs worked to resolve coding differences until 100% agreement was reached. In the end, the coders found 491 articles that mentioned effective use, roughly equally divided among the four fields. Clearly, the concept is part of our common lexicon. Yet, surprisingly, our review yielded only one article per field that studied effective use in substantial detail (e.g., defining it and describing drivers of it). Overall, Table 1 shows the results from this formal review and from our informal review.

It is difficult for any literature review to be complete, but we feel that Table 1 presents a fair picture of past research. As the table shows, we could only find seven detailed studies of effective use and these studies offer different definitions, examine different systems at different levels of analysis, and propose different drivers and dimensions. The literature is clearly still at a nascent stage of development. We wish to build on and complement this pioneering body of work by providing a theory of effective use that can apply across information systems in general. The next section explains the particular view of information systems we adopted to build this theory and its appropriateness for our research.

## 2.3. Representation Theory

The nature and drivers of effective use must relate in some way to the three elements of use, e.g., to the competencies and motivations of users, the nature and purpose of systems, and the characteristics of tasks (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). Because we wish to focus on the unique nature of information systems, our starting point is the system element and we begin by making some basic assumptions about information systems, much like researchers in other sciences build discipline-specific theories by making basic assumptions, like in economics, assuming that humans are rational, or in psychology, that humans are symbol processors. That is, to build a “native” IS theory, we take the view that some assumptions about an IS must be taken as given to provide leverage in understanding the specific IS phenomena that concern us, much as researchers in economics and psychology take certain assumptions of people as given, not because they are perfectly true, but because they give them leverage in understanding human behavior.

Our most basic assumption relates to the nature and purpose of an information system because the effective use of any artifact must relate, at least in part, to its nature and purpose. To provide a plausible basis for our assumptions, we need a theory that describes the nature and purpose of information systems. Working against this is the reality that many types of systems exist with different purposes, and even single systems can have multiple, and conflicting, purposes (Orlikowski 1991). Thus, very few theories address this issue. One exception is representation theory (Wand and Weber 1990, 1995). Because we draw on it extensively, and readers may not know it well, we describe it in substantial depth.

The theory’s basic premise is that information systems exist because “it is the human condition to seek better ways to understand and to represent the world” (Weber 1997, p. 59). That is, although information systems are used for many task-specific reasons, the core purpose of all such systems is to help people understand the states of some real-world systems that are relevant to them, such as the states of their mind (represented, for example, in a word processing system), states of their business (represented, for example, in a payroll system), or states of the organization’s environment (represented, for example, in an environmental scanning system). Implicit in the theory is the notion that representations are enabling in some sense, at the most general level, enabling users to act in the world. Weber (2003, p. viii) writes

0 0 0 “representation” [is] the essence of all information systems. The raison d’etre for information systems [is] that they track states of and state changes in other systems. By observing the behavior of an information system, we obviate the need to observe the behavior of the system it represents 0 0 0 0 For example, with an order-entry information system, we track states of and state changes in customers, which means that we do not have to consult with each customer individually to determine the goods or services they wish to purchase. Moreover, in some cases an information system provides us with the only means we have available to observe the behavior of the represented system. For example, in a simulation, the represented system may not exist, except in our minds.

To some readers, it may seem odd to say that the purpose of an IS is to represent a domain. To clarify this idea, the basic reasoning is the same as that underlying the theory of the firm in economics. That is, people can create firms for many specific reasons (to sell one good or another), but the core reason, which may not even be conscious to the person founding the firm, is that a firm is a more efficient vehicle for contracting than the market (Coase 1937). Just as the theory of the firm states that this is the reason why firms are formed, representation theory states that information systems are used because they serve as efficient vehicles for representing domains. Much like the theory of the firm, it also argues that this core purpose supports the specific purpose that a user thinks about when using the system (e.g., our conscious purpose in using an inventory system may be to manage a firm’s inventory, and the representational function of an inventory system indeed helps us do so).

Building on this logic, representation theory describes the nature and purpose of an IS as follows. First, in terms of nature, it states that information systems consist of three structures (Weber 1997, pp. 78–80). Deep structure is the specification of a domain offered by an information system, such as the specification in an inventory system that a warehouse contains goods. Surface structure refers to the facilities that allow users to access and interact with the representations, such as the inventory system’s user interface, including its screens, menus, and report layouts. Physical structure refers to the machinery that supports the other structures, such as the devices used for input (keyboards), output (monitors), storage (disks), transportation (networks), and computation (circuits). Second, representation theory states that the purpose of an information system is to faithfully represent a real world domain, such as the state of a firm’s inventory (Weber 1997, p. 73).

The idea that systems provide representations is not unique to this theory. In IS, it can be seen in studies of IT-impacts (Ruhleder 1994), knowledge management (Walsham 2005), virtual work (Overby 2008, Robey et al. 2003), and organizational design (Yoo et al. 2006). This idea is also important in several of our sister fields such as database (Kent 2000), human computer interaction (Bodker 1998, Suchman 1995), and artifical intelligence (Davis et al. 1993). The unique aspect of representation theory is its focus on faithful representations (Weber 1997, p. 59). The theory assumes that people use systems to interact with its representations—the other structures of a system are used simply as a means to that end—and they desire representations that faithfully represent some domain, because they provide a more informed basis for action than unfaithful representations do. For example, inventory managers desire systems that provide an accurate account of what is in a warehouse because that enables them to make better purchasing decisions.

Of the three structures of an IS, the most complex is deep structure. The key idea is that deep structure conveys the meaning of a representation.<sup>3</sup> For example, assume that a designer creates a database to represent a firm’s inventory domain as perceived by stakeholders during the requirements elicitation stage of IS development. Assume that one of the fields in the database is ProductID. The business meaning of this field is that certain product instances exist that are unique compared to other products. Once the system is in operation, the database will be populated with tokens (data) during use. These tokens reflect instances of the concepts defined in the deep structure (Parsons and Wand 2008). Thus, an entry such as BT74-J inherits a specific meaning if entered as a ProductID. In addition to such straightforward cases, more complex manifestations of deep structure can occur because of the many ways in which users can engage in IS development and use:

• Creation. It is common in many organizations for users to create spreadsheets to record data or run routines not supported by a database system. Often, users define the meaning of tokens/data in those sheets themselves and thereby create the relevant deep structure, e.g., when describing columns and fields in those sheets. If they do not do this clearly, the meaning of tokens/data in those sheets may be difficult to understand, especially for other users.

• Change. Boudreau and Robey (2005, p. 13) describe how users of an ERP system decided to “use [one] field (the statistical code) to capture information of another nature (a credit card payment).” When users agree to such changes, the meaning of data in such fields, and thus the deep structure, are changed, at least from the users’ perspective. Of course, if the system designers are not advised, the deep structure would remain unchanged from their perspective.<sup>4</sup>

• Leverage. Consider systems that contain what introductory IS texts call unstructured data. Users typically convey meaning in such systems by taking advantage of (hence “leveraging”) deep structure that already exists in the environment surrounding one’s use of the IS. For instance, when we write Table 2 in this paper, we take advantage of deep structure in the English language and in norms for writing research papers that enable us to convey the meaning we intend.

Deep structure can also be complex because of its potentially multilayered nature. This is especially true for general purpose software like spreadsheets and word processors. For instance, word processors allow users to enter and manipulate many types of symbols in many different ways. At one level, their deep structure reflects the “universe” of these symbols and possible manipulations, independent of their meaning to users. The user brings in a different deep structure—that of the domain for which the user is using the word processor. Moreover, any given user will only leverage a small portion of the potential deep structure available in such general purpose systems (e.g., we are not using the deep structure underlying the mail-merging features of our system). The relevant deep structure in any instance of use is that which the user leverages when he or she uses the system for a given task, a point we return to later.<sup>5</sup> Although these ideas are complex, the main point we wish to emphasize is that in all information systems, the representation they offer when they are in use is provided by the combination of the deep structure and the data/tokens that populate it. For this reason, our paper focuses on the surface structure, physical structure, and representations provided by an IS rather than just the surface, physical, and deep structures alone.

In summary, representation theory allows us to assume that (1) every IS has three components (its representations, surface structure, and physical structure); (2) representations are accessed through a system’s surface and physical structure; and (3) more faithful representations of a domain are desired. Before describing our theory, we should clarify three further issues about representation theory: the nature of a faithful representation; whether we are using representation theory as is or extending it; and whether representation theory is consistent with theories of artifacts in general.

On the first point, the faithfulness of a representation is a product of the underlying deep structure and the tokens that populate it. For example, if an inventory system does not have a table in which to record aspects of inventory important to a particular firm, then it will provide an unfaithful representation of that firm’s inventory. However, even if an inventory system is installed with all the tables required to provide a faithful representation, the system will still provide an unfaithful representation if inventory data is not entered into the system correctly (Wand and Wang 1996). The same applies to systems with unstructured data. If the words on the screen of our word processor do not reflect what we mean to say in this research paper, then it does not provide a faithful representation of our ideas.

Another aspect of faithfulness is that one can judge it universally (does the system represent all elements in the domain in all their detail?) or in a manner that is specific to users’ needs (does the system represent all elements in the domain that are relevant to the user’s needs?). This reflects the distinction between assessing a representation in terms of semantics (is a sign correct?) or in terms of both pragmatics and semantics (is a sign as correct as it needs to be?) (Chandler 2002). We adopt the latter view (combining semantics and pragmatics) because it accords with the purpose of an IS in representation theory, which is to provide representations that meet users’ needs. It is this aspect of the theory, in fact, that allows us to account for different stakeholders’ views (as we noted earlier) because each stakeholder (designers, users, managers) may have a different perspective on the representations that users require. A user may feel that one set of representations is needed, for example, but his or her manager might have a different view.

On the second point, we should clarify that we are extending representation theory beyond its traditional use. Representation theory has existed for many years (Wand and Weber 1988), with the ideas spread over many papers. In their early work, Wand and Weber (1990, 1995) described the whole domain to be explained, which they referred to as involving both an internal view and an external view, but, based on a belief that the core of the IS discipline lay in the study of IS artifacts themselves, independent of their use or social context (Weber 1987), they focused their research efforts on the internal view alone. They wrote

0 0 0 we focus only on the internal view. Specifically1 0 0 0 we proceed using the following perspective: An information system is an object that can be studied in its own right, independently of the way it is developed and deployed in its organizational and social context. In other words, in our models we are not concerned with the way an information system is managed in organizations, nor with the characteristics of its users, nor with the way it is used, nor with the impact it has 0 0 0 in organizations. Instead, we are concerned only with information systems as independent artifacts that are built to achieve certain requirements (Wand and Weber 1995, p. 205).

Figure 1 distinguishes between the scope of how we use representation theory and the scope nearly all other researchers have used. The figure shows the system that an IS represents, the IS itself, and the use of the IS. It also distinguishes two parts of the represented system: a real-world domain (such as an inventory warehouse) and a perception of it (how someone perceives the warehouse). Because researchers traditionally used representation theory to study the internal view alone, the focus was solely on creating a faithful representation of how users perceived a domain—the inner dashed box in Figure 1— typically studied in terms of conceptual modeling (Weber 1997). Excluded were the distinction between reality and peoples’ perceptions of it, the surface and physical structures, and how and why systems are used. We consider all the issues—the outer dashed box—because all are necessary to understand effective use.

To date, only a few studies have applied representation theory to topics other than conceptual modeling, specifically data quality (Wand and Wang 1996), fit (Davern 1996, Strong and Volkoff 2010), maintenance (Heales 2002), and alignment (Rosemann et al. 2004, Sia and Soh 2007). To apply it to effective use, we had to expand what is generally considered within its scope, as Figure 1 shows. Our reason for doing so is that we do not share Wand and Weber’s view that the core of the IS discipline lies in the study of IS artifacts independent of their use or social context. Rather, we believe that theories of IS artifacts like representation theory (or indeed other theories of IS artifacts), can be valuable precisely for studying IS artifacts in use in their social context, thus providing a broader core to the field.

The third and final issue we noted above was whether representation theory is consistent with theories of artifacts in general. This is relevant because any theory of specific artifacts, such as IS artifacts, should still be compatible with theories of artifacts in general. In this light, one theory regarding artifacts in general is the theory of affordances (Gibson 1977), which has attracted significant attention in HCI and IS research (Hartson 2003, Markus and Silver 2008, Norman 1999, Zammuto et al. 2007). Although a detailed integration of representation theory and affordances awaits future research, our initial analysis would suggest that these two theories are very compatible. According to Hartson (2003), for instance, an affordance is simply what an artifact offers someone, and four types of affordances can be distinguished:

1. Sensory are those that enable users to sense, such as see or feel.

2. Physical are those that enable users to physically do something.

3. Cognitive are those that enable users to think or know something.

4. Functional are those that enable users to accomplish a goal.

In Figure 2, we illustrate how these four types of affordance relate to the ideas in representation theory.

As we show in Figure 2, affordances can be nested because to leverage an artifact’s cognitive and functional affordances, one must be able to sense and physically interact with it (Hartson 2003). For instance, to have any chance at hammering a nail, one must be able to see (sense) the hammer and physically wield it. The same applies in representation theory: to obtain representations from an IS, one has to be able to access them through a system’s surface and physical structure. From an affordance perspective, being able to see a hammer and physically wield it is not enough however; one must cognitively understand what to do with it to achieve the intended function (hammering the nail in the wall). Likewise, representation theory assumes that people use systems to obtain representations, and they want representations precisely because cognitively understanding a domain enables them to function in it. As Dennis (1996) pointed out, users must often do something with the information they receive to attain their goals. What users do with their information is more likely to be successful if the representations are faithful. For example, if an inventory system lists the correct values of inventory in the warehouse, managers can make better purchasing decisions. In short, representation theory and affordances theory seem to link quite naturally, providing us with confidence in its appropriateness for our work.

Overall, we have described representation theory in substantial detail to provide a clear foundation for how we use it in the paper. We recognize that some readers might still question Wand and Weber’s (1988) claim that representation theory applies to all information systems, or might argue that even if it can apply to all systems, it would be more useful to build a theory tailored to specific systems and tasks, or classes of them. Others may even reject representational views (Barad 2003, Klein and Hirschheim 2006, p. 312). We recognize these criticisms, but believe that exploring the implications of representation theory is worthwhile. All theories have limitations and within the scope of this paper, it is not possible to explore the implications of multiple general theories, nor the implications of both a general theory and a specific one. Thus, we simply take the assumptions of representation theory as given. As the title of our paper implies, representation theory offers one perspective. We encourage researchers to pursue others too.

## 3. A Theory of Effective Use

To build the theory, we followed a three-step approach suggested by Ostrom (2005, pp. 27–29), a noted theorist: (1) propose a high-level framework that defines the kinds of variables to be included in the theory and its associated metatheoretical assumptions, (2) apply the framework to a specific phenomenon, and (3) propose empirically testable models. We draw on representation theory in each stage.

## 3.1. Framework

The first step was to identify a high-level framework that would reflect the metatheoretical assumptions of representation theory and that could help us analyze effective use. The main metatheoretical assumptions of representation theory are critical realism, which it uses to explain the nature of representations, and teleology, which it uses to explain their purpose.

Critical realism says that the world exists independent of our perceptions but that we know the world only through our perceptions, which are partial and fallible (Weber 1997, p. 174). The implication of adopting critical realism is that representation theory assumes that the representations provided by information systems are fallible, and that their fallibility may be difficult, even impossible, to determine (Weber 1997, p. 175). Teleology is the view that human behavior is goal driven. This can be seen in the theory’s position that information systems are created and used to meet basic human needs:

Our ability to survive in the world is often associated with how well we conceive the world and how we then use information to represent the world 0 0 0 0 And nothing is more certain than these efforts will continue. It is the human condition 0 0 0 ! (Weber 1997, p. 59).

The fact that representation theory adopts both critical realism and teleology has an interesting implication. Specifically, it implies that people will try to improve how they create and use systems because they benefit from having better representations. However, because representations are fallible, there is always room for improvement, and because fallibility is difficult to determine, there is always the potential that users may overinvest, underinvest, or misdirect their efforts in creating and using systems. In sum, these assumptions imply that although the creation and use of information systems can improve over time, the process is likely to be never ending and error prone.

Upon identifying this implication, we set out to identify a framework that could reflect it. One such framework is the basic cybernetic control framework (Weiner 1948), long used to analyze and understand natural and social systems (Carver and Scheier 1998). Figure 3 illustrates the framework; for identical versions elsewhere, see Carver and Scheier (1998) in psychology and Liang and Xue (2009) in IS. Using Figure 3, representation theory can be explained as follows: people take actions to create and use information systems so that they can obtain better representations than available elsewhere, such as manual systems (link 1); they then perceive the consequences of creating and using them (link 2); if the perceived consequences do not match their goals (links 3a and 3b), they conduct actions to improve their design and use (link 4). Finally, disturbances can affect consequences at any stage (link 5). In systems theory, disturbances reflect the effects of uncontrollable or unpredictable factors in the environment on the criterion, e.g., changes in the business environment could mean that an IS no longer provides a valid representation of the domain, necessitating further corrective action. Because this framework can be used to describe the arguments of representation theory quite well, and is consistent with its metatheoretical assumptions, we used it as the basis for our theory. That is, we use this framework to define the kinds of variables to be included in our theory (per Ostrom 2005), as opposed to picking types of variables (whether process oriented or variance oriented, subjective or objective) arbitrarily.

## 3.2. Applying the Framework

Following Ostrom’s (2005) approach, our second step was to apply this high-level framework to the context of effective use and performance. The letters “EU” and $\prime \prime \mathrm { P } ^ { \prime \prime }$ in Figure 3 illustrate how we did this. They reflect the concept of a goal hierarchy, which is that people can have goals at different levels that contribute to each other (Carver and Scheier 1998). This flows from the assumption we mentioned earlier that systems are always used to achieve some other goal, i.e., they are used as a means to an end rather than for their own sake (Gasser 1986). Because any means can also be considered a goal (Vallacher and Wegner 1987), we propose two goals in our theory— effective use and performance—where effective use is the lower-level goal (the means) and performance is the higher-level goal (the desired end). Thus, by including the words “performance” and “effective use” in each box in Figure 3, we mean that each box can be viewed in terms of both aspects, i.e., people can have goals for both, conduct actions to improve both, perceive consequences for both, compare consequences to their goals for both, and so on.

Although the framework in Figure 3 is set at a high level, it accounts for several complexities in studying effective use, only some of which have been noted in past work. For example, like task-technology-fit theory (Goodhue and Thompson 1995), it shows that users can adjust their use of an IS based on feedback. However, it also allows us to account for the fact that using an IS might not be the only or even the best way to improve performance (Haas and Hansen 2005). Overall, it suggests that

• users can take several actions to improve their performance; they are not limited to improving it only through effective use of a system;

• users can attend to feedback from effective use as well as from performance; and

• when responding to feedback, users can take actions to improve their use of the system, their performance, or both.

In light of recent discussions (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009), it is worth noting that teleology assumes that users can seek goals consciously and/or unconsciously (Austin and Vancouver 1996). Thus, Figure 3 does not suggest that users continually and consciously monitor feedback and take corrective action. It just suggests that improving effective use and performance is a cyclical, error-prone process influenced by the self-regulatory mechanisms of the user and external disturbances.

Overall, Figure 3 contributes by providing an integrative perspective on effective use and performance, but it is not specific enough to provide concrete explanations. It simply provides a high-level explanation that flows from the assumptions of representation theory (critical realism and teleology). To facilitate our next step—developing testable models—we took a closer look at one part of the framework: Link 1. Figure 4 provides a closer view of this link. It shows that users can take actions to improve both effective use and performance and that these have consequences for both effective use and performance, with these consequences contributing to each other in a meansend relationship (such that effective use improves performance). Whereas Figure 3 shows the global influence of all actions taken to improve effective use and performance (in Link 1), Figure 4 shows that the models we will propose focus on those actions taken to improve effective use (Link 1a), together with the means-end link from effective use to performance (Link 1b), because these are the links that relate directly to our study’s objective: to understand the nature and drivers of effective use. We therefore leave the other links for future research.<sup>6</sup>

## 3.3. Models

According to Ostrom (2005, pp. 27–29), good theories are generative in that many models can be generated from them. In this section, we derive two, one focused on the nature of effective use, and one focused on its drivers.

3.3.1. The Nature of Effective Use. Link 1b in Figure 4 shows how effective use improves performance. Figure 5 provides a testable model of this link, identifying several dimensions of each construct. The dimensions are important because without them, the link between effective use and performance is a tautology (because we defined effective use as using a system in a way that increases performance). We avoid the tautology by deriving the dimensions of effective use from theory, i.e., these dimensions should increase performance according to representation theory, but whether they do is an empirical question.

Figure 4 A Closer Look at Link 1 in the General Framework (Figure 3)  
![](/api/attachments/GA87KK7B/fulltext/images/909d630e33d60d7740e5fea4e687ed4d28b76059b652ba6274846e73dd2ec362.jpg)

Each dimension in Figure 5 reflects a state that can vary from high to low. The performance dimensions, effectiveness and efficiency, are well known (Beal et al. 2003, Campbell 1990). Because we relate effective use to both of them, it might be more accurate to refer to it as “effective and efficient use,” but we use the phrase effective use for simplicity. When assessing performance, effectiveness is typically assessed in terms of goal attainment, and efficiency in terms of one’s level of goal attainment for a given level of input (e.g., effort or time) (Sonnentag 2002). The contribution in Figure 5 lies not in the dimensions of performance, but in the dimensions of effective use and their links with performance. We derived these dimensions from the premises of representation theory. Our rationale is as follows.

First, the basic premise of representation theory is that an IS provides representations. To obtain benefits from an IS, a user must be able to access these representations through the system’s surface and physical structure. Thus, we define our first dimension of effective use, transparent interaction, as the extent to which a user is accessing the system’s representations unimpeded by its surface and physical structures. A second premise of the theory is that people seek faithful representations. Thus, we propose our second dimension of effective use, representational fidelity, as the extent to which a user is obtaining representations from the system that faithfully reflect the domain being represented. Finally, a third premise of the theory is that faithful representations enable individuals to act. Thus, we define our final dimension of effective use, informed action, as the extent to which a user acts upon the faithful representations he or she obtains from the system to improve his or her state.<sup>7</sup>

As Figure 4 shows, the dimensions form a hierarchy because each lower-level dimension is necessary but not sufficient for the higher-level dimension. In other words, a user must be able to access representations to obtain faithful ones, and must be able to obtain faithful representations to take informed (rather than misinformed) actions on the basis of them, just as we showed earlier in our more general model in Figure 2. Including each of these dimensions in our conception of effective use overcomes a lack of completeness in past measures of system use, which have typically defined system use either purely in terms of individuals’ use of the system (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006), or in terms of their use of the information from the system (DeLone and McLean 1992), rather than considering both aspects.

To avoid confusion, we should clarify why all of these dimensions are assessments of use, not simply assessments of a system or user. This is emphasized by our use of verbs in each definition such as access, obtain, and act, rather than nouns like ability or quality that would instead reflect properties of a user or system. Although this might seem obvious for transparent interaction, it might be less so for representational fidelity and informed action. For instance, one might argue that representational fidelity is instead a property of a system, not a property of use. To clarify our view, note that system use involves a user, system, and task (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). Thus, to assess use, an observer will rate how well the user is using the system in that task. In the case of representation fidelity, an observer will rate how well the representations the user obtains from the system faithfully reflect the domain. This is an aspect of use because different users may use the same system, but obtain different levels of representation fidelity, because one uses it more effectively than the other (e.g., one may make errors when inputting or retrieving representations or one may not know how to detect and correct for infidelity when it exists). Of course, representational fidelity can be defined differently, as a property of the system alone. In this case, we could focus on the fidelity of the system’s underlying deep structure (e.g., whether the tables of an inventory system match the phenomena in a firm’s inventory context). However, we have defined representational fidelity in terms of what users obtain from the system when using it, which involves the deep structure and the tokens that populate it, in use.

The representational fidelity of the system before use simply provides a baseline for studying representational fidelity in use because whether the fidelity is, initially, good or bad, we are interested in the fidelity that is due to the user’s actions (such as entering, manipulating, retrieving, or viewing representations). For the same reason, when assessing informed action, an observer will rate how well the user is leveraging faithful representations obtained from the system in a task. This is not simply a product of the system, or the user, but rather a product of their interaction at a point in time (how the user is leveraging the system’s information in a task).

Although our dimensions are untested, prior case studies suggest their applicability. Zuboff’s (1988) studies are particularly relevant because she focused on the representational aspects of information systems. The importance of transparent interaction is evident in her observations that users who had difficulty navigating their systems felt “lost 0 0 0 just floating” (p. 209), “plunged into darkness” (p. 63), and unsure of where things on their screens “came from” and where they “went” (p. 130). Her case studies also highlighted the importance of representational fidelity. At one paper mill, operators were so concerned about fidelity that they “would run back and forth between the control room and the production area in order to verify the system’s readings” (Zuboff 1988, p. 85). Other studies have likewise shown that users will employ creative efforts to improve representational fidelity, e.g., entering incorrect data into a system to make a faulty program work properly (Gasser 1986) and entering one type of data into a field for a different type of data when they can find nowhere else to record it (Boudreau and Robey 2005, Gasser 1986). Finally, Zuboff’s most well-known finding was that individuals must exploit the informating power of information systems. She found many cases where workers got good information from their systems and acted upon the information to improve productivity. Not doing so was found to be a real loss:

0 0 0 the disappointing performance could be attributed not to what operators were doing, but to what they were not doing. The operators’ errors were sins of omission—an underutilization of the data interface resulting from their refusal to notice, to think, to explore, to experiment, or to improve (Zuboff 1988, p. 413).

Overall, we propose that representational fidelity, transparent interaction, and informed action define one’s level of effective use. Effective use, in other words, is an aggregate construct formed by its dimensions (Law et al. 1998). The dimensions are also hierarchically related.<sup>8</sup> Using the language of Zuboff (1988) and Leonardi (2007), transparent interaction activates the informating potential of an IS, representational fidelity ensures that this potential is positive, and informed action leverages it.

The links between each dimension and performance in Figure 5 can be explained as follows. For transparent interaction, we propose that it mainly improves performance by saving users time when working on the system, increasing efficiency. A secondary benefit is that when individuals interact with their system more seamlessly, they are likely to stay focused on what they are doing, which is often critical to performing effectively (Eysenck 1982, Zuboff 1988, pp. 188–192). In extreme cases, a lack of transparent interaction could also reduce effectiveness by impeding task execution, for example, if users are unable to find and use the features they need to perform their work. For representational fidelity, we propose that it primarily improves effectiveness by improving users’ understanding of the domain being represented and reducing their uncertainty about it, both of which should facilitate task closure (Straub and Karahanna 1998). A secondary benefit is that when representational fidelity is high, users can afford to spend less time verifying fidelity (unlike the workers in Zuboff’s cases who had to run back and forth between the system and the plant), increasing efficiency. For informed action, we propose that it primarily improves effectiveness by improving one’s state in the domain; conversely, ill-informed actions reduce effectiveness by damaging one’s state. Informed action can also increase efficiency by saving time that might have been spent on recovering from errors made if one’s actions had been ill informed. Table 3 provides examples of these different effects, illustrating both the direct effects on performance described here and the indirect ways in which each dimension improves performance by supporting its counterpart dimension (e.g., transparent interaction enables representational fidelity).

Before moving on to the drivers of effective use, we should point out one complexity associated with the dimensions of effective use that may not be obvious at first. Specifically, we would expect variation in the range of effective use exhibited for different users, systems, and tasks. For instance, assume that a firm employs analysts to manipulate and assemble data from a large system to make complex inventory decisions. Because the activity is complex, we might expect a sample of analysts to exhibit a fairly wide range of levels of transparent interaction (because of navigation problems), representational fidelity (because of manipulation or retrieval errors), and informed action (because of making poor decisions based on the wrong data). On the other hand, assume that a firm employs clerks to enter long lists of fairly simple transactions into an easy-to-use form. Because of the simplicity of the work, we would probably expect a sample of clerks to exhibit minimal variation in transparent interaction (because the form is simple), representational fidelity (because the transactions are simple), and informed action (because there would not seem to be much opportunity to take action in this case). This difference is not a weakness of our measure, but rather a natural result of how simple it is to master some instances of use versus others. Researchers would typically be interested in observing variations in effective use across users at a point in time (i.e., cross-sectionally) or within users over time (i.e., longitudinally). Our dimensions would work in both cases because even in the simplest possible case, there is still likely to be some variation on these dimensions, e.g., some clerks will inevitably navigate a data entry form more effortlessly than others (transparent interaction), some may make data entry mistakes or spot errors and avoid entering them (representational fidelity), and some may take actions, such as checking with a superior, if they see something interesting in the data (informed action). What this issue does highlight, however, is the importance of understanding the context of use being studied if one is to understand or make judgments about the levels or range of effective use observed in any given case. We return to this issue of context later.

The value of adapting surface structure and physical structure stems from the theory’s premise that the sole purpose of these structures is to support access to representations. Thus, any adaptations to them that facilitate improved access are desirable. Once again, given the inherent malleability of information systems, such adaptations are not only desirable, but often feasible. Finally, the value of learning also stems from the fallibility of representations because a user must learn how fallible a representation is (i.e., its fidelity), to know if he or she should go to the effort of obtaining it and acting on it. The value of learning also stems from representation theory’s notion that information systems consist of a complex set of structures because to interact well with complex structures invariably requires learning.

In addition to adaptation and learning both stemming from representation theory, another reason to propose both (rather than just one) is that they reinforce each other, because adaptation actions are more likely to be effective if an individual has learned what actions to take, i.e., can take educated actions.

Although past work (especially qualitative work) has shown the importance of adaptation and learning (Barki et al. 2007, Boudreau and Seligman 2005, Majchrzak et al. 2000), our analysis goes further to describe different types of adaptation and learning (shown in Tables 4 and 5) and their different effects on effective use. First, we propose two ways that users can improve transparent interaction. One is to take the system “as is” and learn how it offers representations through its surface and physical structures. Rather than take the system as is, another way to improve transparent interaction is to adapt the surface and physical structure in an educated manner, i.e., informed by learning these structures [2]. For example, the system may allow users to modify the default sequence of forms shown to a nurse when inputting a record. By using this feature (adapting the surface structure), nurses could improve their interaction with the system. Likewise, the system may only allow users to see part of a record at a time, but nurses may work around this by adapting the physical structure (e.g., by hooking up an additional monitor, and running two sessions, one on each screen, to see more of the record at a time) to improve their access. As noted above, the reason for proposing an interaction effect is that adaptation and learning are complementary. After all, uneducated adaptations may fail to offer benefits or may even cause problems.

We propose a similar logic for representational fidelity. One way to improve this dimension is to take the system as is and focus on learning what to access and how to access it. Users are more likely to know what to access if they have learned the fidelity of the representations in the system, because representation theory assumes that users want representations that faithfully reflect the domain. Users know how to access representations if they have high levels of transparent interaction. In our earlier model of effective use (Figure 5), we proposed that transparent interaction enabled but did not determine representational fidelity. This was because transparent interaction concerns access, not fidelity. When transparent interaction is coupled with knowledge of fidelity, the link becomes more certain, because users know both what to look for and how to access it [3]. For example, when searching a health record for issues with a client, a nurse who scores high on both of these dimensions is likely to focus his or her efforts on reading the data that he or she knows accurately reflect the client’s state. Of course, one’s ability to know how well a record accurately reflects a real-world state depends on one’s knowledge of the record/representation as well as the real-world domain it is intended to reflect [4].<sup>9</sup>

## 3.4. Summary and Boundary Conditions

The two models we proposed help to fill the gap in the literature that we noted at the outset, by suggesting what effective use involves (its dimensions) and what drives it (its antecedents). To develop the models, we followed Ostrom’s (2005) approach in proposing a high-level framework, applying it to our context, and then deriving the models. This enabled us to link all our major decisions, from the choice of our overall framework to the specific elements of our models, to representation theory. It thereby enabled us to propose an answer to our guiding research question by showing what this theory of information systems (representation theory) implies for what effective use involves and what drives it.

In Table 6, we summarize the way we developed the theory by making deductions from representation theory. Overall, we tried to draw deductions that seemed self-evident, not requiring further defense from theories from other disciplines. As noted at the outset, other researchers may have drawn different deductions to us. Thus, our work is simply a starting point and could be extended in many ways.

The final step in outlining our theory is to examine its boundary conditions, i.e., factors that moderate its applicability in different contexts (Whetten 1989). A full analysis of context is a major undertaking outside the scope of our work (Johns 2006). It also requires us to go beyond the confines of representation theory and examine factors that other theories highlight as being important. For both of these reasons, our analysis of boundary conditions is necessarily brief, but we provide an initial analysis by examining how several links in our high-level framework (specifically links 1, 2, and 4, in Figure 3) are moderated by four elements of context intimately connected to system use: user, system, task, and time (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, p. 671). Table 7 provides our analysis. The analysis suggests that users are more likely to take actions to improve effective use and performance, and these actions are more likely to be successful, when

• users are more knowledgeable, experienced, motivated, and supported;

• systems and tasks are simple, flexible, familiar, and independent of other systems/tasks;

• users can take actions, and see their consequences, quickly.

Overall, our analysis has two implications. First, it offers guidance for researchers who wish to test our models. For example, if researchers test our models in contexts in which users get immediate feedback on performance, they should expect users to achieve more effective use and higher performance more quickly than in contexts in which feedback is delayed. Second, it helps underscore the importance of actions that users take to improve effective use. This is because not only can users’ learning and adaptation actions have immediate benefits for effective use, as shown in our models of the drivers of effective use, but these actions can also change the user context (making users more knowledgeable) and the system context (making the system more or less complex), which can affect the entire process of improving effective use and performance, as Table 7 shows. This reinforces the overall theme of our high-level framework—that improving effective use is an ongoing, error-prone, and somewhat unpredictable process—because it suggests that in addition to their more immediate effects, adaptation and learning could have distal effects that occur through changing the context of use, and these effects may be difficult to predict or appreciate ex ante. Some of the most interesting work in testing our theory may well lie, therefore, in these moderating effects.

## 4. Discussion

In this section, we discuss how our work extends past research and ways in which the theory could be extended.

In addition to these specific ways in which our research extends prior work, a more general difference is that our study represents the first concerted effort to ask what effective use would involve from the perspective of a theory of information systems. Different theories of information systems may have different implications for what effective use involves. It would be valuable to examine what effective use might involve from the perspective of other views of information systems such as socio-technical systems (Bostrom and Heinen 1977), digital options (Sambamurthy et al. 2003), or ensembles (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001). Rather than considering these to be competing views, we see them as opportunities to further explore the nature of effective use and what drives it. Such research could lead to a deeper understanding of information systems themselves, and our theories of them.

Finally, it is important to restate that our theory is set at a general level. The only studies that we could find of effective use have examined specific systems, ranging from mobile phones, to digital libraries, to collaboration systems, among others (see Table 1). We recognize that people use specific systems, not systems in general (Ramiller and Pentland 2009), so there is substantial merit in taking this route. Ultimately, we view theories of specific systems and systems in general to be complementary, however, because it is difficult for any theory to be at once simple, accurate, and generalizable (Weick 1979).

## 4.2. Extending the Theory

Our work could be extended in several ways. One would be to extend the way that we considered representations. For example, although information systems offer one form of representation, individuals can use many other types as well, such as paper records, physical artifacts, and human memory (Chandler 2002, Hutchins 1995). It would be valuable to examine how users choose among different forms of representation, use them in concert, and improve their use of multiple forms over time. In addition, our theory focuses on how users leverage representations themselves, but users can also share representations with others (such as in a team or social context) so that others can take informed action. It would be valuable to pursue this idea to extend our research to multiple levels of analysis.

A second approach would be to reconsider our assumptions about the nature of representations. Consistent with representation theory, we argued that if a system fails to represent a domain faithfully, users will benefit from adapting the system so that it more faithfully reflects the domain. A different approach would be to change the domain so that it more faithfully reflects the system (e.g., by creating and implementing new work practices required by an ERP system) (Robey et al. 2001). Both types of adaptation could improve representational fidelity, but they would do so in different ways. Allowing for both possibilities would strengthen our theory and provide a link between this work and the literature on how organizations and systems coevolve (Lassila and Brancheau 1999). A related direction would be to assess the robustness of the theory in different contexts. For instance, one could compare contexts in which the faithfulness of a representation is more straightforward to contexts in which reality is equivocal or liable to misrepresentation (Daft and Weick 1984; Zuboff 1988, pp. 354–355).

A third approach would be to focus on other elements of our overall framework. For instance, although we focused on the link between “actions” and “consequences,” it would be valuable to consider the links to and from “perceptions.” Zuboff’s studies provide many examples of the perceptions and associated cognitions, attitudes, and emotions that users have when working with representational media and how these can drive behavior. Describing the perceptions of many workers, she remarked (Zuboff 1988, p. 63),

It is as if one’s job had vanished into a twodimensional space of abstractions, where digital symbols replaced concrete reality. Workers reiterated a spontaneous emotional response countless times— defined by feelings of loss of control, of vulnerability, and of frustration.

We believe that for many users—not just these workers—ineffective use will be associated with feelings of lost control, vulnerability, and frustration. More effective use should help mitigate each of these problems. For example, representational fidelity should engender feelings of trust. Trust refers to a willingness to be vulnerable to another entity (Rousseau et al. 1998). When representational fidelity increases, users are more likely to trust their systems because they will have more positive expectations of the consequences of relying on those representations, e.g., when using them to make decisions (Nicolaou and McKnight 2006). We also expect that transparent interaction and informed action will engender feelings of control and competence. These feelings arise when people feel they can take actions and those actions will yield intended results (Ryan and Deci 2000). Such feelings are precisely what help people avoid frustration (Bessiere et al. 2006). Transparent interaction should help users feel more competent and in control because users will feel less obstructed by a system’s material features and more able to interact with its representations. Informed action should have a similar benefit, but on an even greater scale because users are obtaining control not only over their interaction with the system, but how they leverage it in their work. It would be useful, therefore, to test these predictions and determine how these feelings sustain existing ways of working or trigger corrective actions. In such studies, researchers would have to remain aware that positive and negative effects could sometimes occur simultaneously. For example, when users trust a system, they are more likely to invest in actions to improve their use of it—a beneficial outcome, but they could also become less vigilant for limitations associated with their systems and rely on them when they should not do so—a costly outcome (Butler and Gray 2006, p. 220). More theory is needed to determine what level of trust the most effective users would exhibit.

Another direction for research is to develop suitable measurement instruments. Researchers need instruments to test the relationships in our model, and practitioners need instruments to assess how effectively systems are being used and assess the benefits of interventions to improve effective use (such as initiatives to support user learning or adaptation). Although the development of a full set of instruments awaits future research, we have developed an initial set, shown in our appendix. This initial set includes both self-report and independently rated measures because we defined effective use and performance objectively and therefore self-report measures alone may prove insufficient. Interestingly, the best way to measure behavior is still an open question (Furr 2009, p. 370). Because effective use, learning actions, and adaptation actions are all behaviors, the development of measures to capture them aligns with the recent movement in psychology to refocus efforts on measuring behaviors (Baumeister et al. 2007). Therefore, IS researchers who contribute to this topic could learn lessons that are valuable to other fields as well.

Finally, we noted earlier that we expanded representation theory beyond its traditional use. It has traditionally been used from a so-called “internal view” to study the design of conceptual modeling grammars (Weber 1997). We believe that understanding how to effectively use a system can inform that work too, i.e., in the language of representation theory, research taking an external view can inform research taking an internal view. Such research could be undertaken in many ways, but to give one example, consider the unified modeling language (UML). UML is arguably the most widely used conceptual modeling language, but it has long been criticized for omitting users’ realworld concerns (Arlow et al. 1999). Researchers have recently tried to improve it by allowing analyst to validate with a given user, what specific representations he or she requires (Masri et al. 2009). Such research could be extended by allowing users to verify how faithful the representations need to be for their tasks, how faithful they have been in the past, and what actions a user could/should take on the basis of any given representation. All of these would be useful items to validate during requirements elicitation because they would help designers develop systems that users can effectively use, which is the main goal of design (Winograd and Flores 1986, p. 137).

## 5. Conclusion

Information systems are designed to help people and organizations achieve goals, but to achieve these goals the systems must be used effectively. Although it is vitally important for individuals and their organizations to understand how effectively their systems are used—and how to raise this level of effectiveness— researchers have little theory to help them understand these issues. We demonstrated one way of addressing the problem, drawing on the representation theory of information systems to develop a theory that explains how effective use and performance evolve, as well as detailed models that explain the nature of effective use and its drivers. We believe that our theory offers a distinctly different perspective on effective use and a stimulating platform for research on how systems are, and need to be, used, to attain desired outcomes. Such research is required if we are to help individuals and organizations reap the true reward of investing in information systems.
