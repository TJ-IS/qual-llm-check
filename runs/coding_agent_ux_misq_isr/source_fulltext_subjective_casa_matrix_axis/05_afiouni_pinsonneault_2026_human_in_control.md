# A Human-in-Control Model: Taking the Helm in Navigating AI Delegation

Rania Afiouni,<sup>1</sup> Alain Pinsonneault<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Université du Québec à Montréal, Canada, afiouni.rania@uqam.ca <sup>2</sup>McGill University, Canada, alain.pinsonneault@mcgill.ca

## Abstract

The growing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into organizational workflows is fundamentally reshaping work and how individuals delegate tasks. This paper examines how workers strive to preserve their personal control through cognitive and behavioral processes shaped by contextual congruence, a concept we introduce to capture the alignment between workers’ preference for AI delegation and their work environment. We develop the human-in-control (HiC) theoretical model, which conceptualizes personal control as a dynamic process rooted in the experience of AI delegation. The model identifies four adaptation pathways through which workers negotiate how direct task control is distributed between them and the AI systems. Each pathway is characterized by a distinctive configuration of primary and secondary control processes that workers mobilize to maintain, restore, or enhance their personal control. This research contributes to IS scholarship by offering a comprehensive, integrated framework that explains how workers reconfigure their sense of personal control in contexts that encourage or limit AI delegation. It extends adaptation theory beyond scenarios where technology is integrated into work, addressing conditions where AI delegation is constrained against workers’ preferences, thereby creating distinct forms of disruption through deprivation. Through these contributions, the HiC model provides researchers with a novel perspective for studying delegation to AI, bridging personal control theory with existing adaptation frameworks by addressing the control tensions in human-AI work.

Keywords: Personal Control, Delegation, Human-In-Control, Artificial Intelligence, Contextual Congruence, Adaptation

## Introduction

Given the frequent leaps in artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities, the scope of what AI can do is increasingly dynamic and hard to define. Although predicting its exact trajectory is challenging (Berente et al., 2021), it is evident that each development of AI enables it to take on increasingly complex roles and broader cognitive capacities (Benbya et al., 2024; Schuetz & Venkatesh, 2020), such as multitasking, creating new content, and managing entire workflows with limited human input (Alavi et al., 2024; Benbya et al., 2024; Mukherjee & Chang, 2025). AI systems now represent work counterparts (Anthony et al., 2023) that exhibit a growing ability to decide and act independently (Agerfalk, 2020; Murray et al., 2021), introducing new dependencies between humans and machines (Lindebaum et al., 2020; Lyytinen et al., 2021).

In this evolving landscape, delegation emerges as a central mechanism reshaping work (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Stelmaszak et al., 2025), freeing workers for higher-value tasks, and improving performance (Demetis & Lee, 2018; Knote et al., 2021; Lyytinen et al., 2021). However, it also raises concerns about workers’ sense of control (Adam et al., 2024). While the role of technology in redistributing control is well established (Sewell & Taskin, 2015), delegating tasks to AI introduces distinct threats to workers’ sense of control (Grote et al., 2024). On the one hand, delegation follows the principle of absoluteness of responsibility, which holds that while task execution may be transferred, ultimate responsibility remains with the delegator (Bushardt et al., 1991; Pham et al., 2010). Interestingly, when delegation is to AI rather than a human, this principle is more strictly applied since AI cannot assume even a minimal share of responsibility, whether legal or moral (Coombs et al., 2020; Pham et al., 2018). On the other hand, AI contradicts the parity of authority and responsibility, another foundationa principle in management theory. According to this principle, individuals should possess adequate authority to effectively carry out tasks for which they are responsible (Koontz et al., 1976). Yet in AI delegation, the human delegator cedes authority rights over the execution of a task to AI and retains responsibility for it. This decoupling between the locus of authority and the locus of responsibility raises accountability concerns for workers (Grote et al., 2024), especially given the limited transparency into the delegatee’s reasoning processes. Unlike humans, who can communicate their reasoning and receive mid-task guidance or correction, AI often executes tasks without communication or contextua responsiveness, impeding the human’s ability to influence outcomes in real time (Nucci, 2020). Indeed, AI’s opacity (Zhang et al., 2021), its insensitivity to implicit cues such as frustration or anxiety (Tarafdar et al., 2023), and its limited grasp of evolving social and situational nuances (Zou et al., 2025) all weaken the possibility of reciprocal communication between delegator and delegatee. These constraints complicate the delegation experience and increase uncertainty and unpredictability (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 1998; Rowe et al., 2023), both known to indicate loss of control (Baronas & Louis, 1988). In brief, workers are responsible for tasks they have little authority over and outcomes they do not understand (Parker & Grote, 2022), which goes against their natural need to have control over what they are responsible for.

Through this study, we offer a theoretical perspective that recognizes the uniqueness of delegating to AI and provide a model that captures how workers maintain a perception of personal control in such delegation contexts. Specifically, we argue that the AI-induced transformation of work renders established modes of personal control inadequate and prompts workers to seek new ways of experiencing it. Our conception of personal control is one of an alignment process of the self and environmental forces that starts with efforts to change the environment and then, short of changing it, aligns the self with it (Rothbaum et al., 1982). We propose a personal control adaptation model (Human-in-Control (HiC) model), which suggests that a control disruption inevitably leads individual workers to strive to restore it (Leotti et al., 2010). Adaptation in that regard is a process of enhancing control, which places the emphasis on worker cognition and behavior at work. Bringing a control perspective to the fore of the delegation conversation places the human at the center and recognizes that AI’s profoundly disruptive nature (Benbya et al., 2021) extends adaptation beyond the boundaries of human-AI interaction and its immediate implications. Broadly speaking, individual workers adapt to significant change by constantly trying to maintain or strengthen their sense of control (Rothbaum et al., 1982).

Our main contribution is twofold. First, we contribute to the literature on delegation to AI (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Fügener et al., 2022) by introducing a perspective of personal control that builds on the alignment between the work environment and the worker’s preference of whether to delegate to AI or not. Second, we extend adaptation to respond not only to contexts where AI dominates work, but also to when workers expect or aspire to greater delegation. This opens new conceptual ground for studying adaptation to failed, delayed, or constrained AI implementations. To our knowledge, this is the first study that introduces personal control as a process of adaptation in human-AI work contexts. With this perspective, we answer multiple calls for a deeper scholarly exploration of work with AI and a microlevel explanation of delegation to it in light of control tensions (Coombs et al., 2020; Grote et al., 2024; Konsynski et al., 2024; Teodorescu et al., 2021).

In the following, the first section lays the conceptual foundation for our study and sets the model’s theoretical and conceptual boundaries. Second, we review the literature on the dynamics of control and delegation that support a negotiated and nonbinary understanding of delegation. Third, we present the two-process theory of personal control that guides the development of our model. Fourth, we present the HiC model, starting with the worker’s delegation preference and its congruence with the context and leading to the restoration, maintenance, enhancement, or possible reduction of the worker’s personal control. Finally, we discuss contributions and future opportunities for conceptual and empirical research that can build on our model.

## 2 Study Boundaries and Conceptualizations

We begin by clarifying our conceptualization of AI and defining the boundaries of the HiC model, specifically around the two parties in the delegation relationship: the AI system and the human worker. In conceiving AI, scholars studying the transformation of work dynamics have predominantly emphasized either its technological ability or its digital agency. From the first perspective, AI is an ability that allows machines to perform cognitive functions typically requiring human intelligence, such as perceiving, reasoning, learning, interacting with the environment, problem solving, decision-making, and even demonstrating creativity (Rai et al., 2019). This view captures dynamics of substitution and complementarity between workers and intelligent tools (Baer et al., 2025; Krakowski et al., 2023; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021) but fails to capture AI as an active collaborator (Anthony et al., 2023; Choudhary et al., 2023). Moreover, a focus on capabilities can render AI definitions obsolete as technology advances. Therefore, there is a need for a fluid definition of AI, recognizing that it increasingly represents not just what machines can currently do, but also the frontier of what they may soon be capable of (Berente et al., 2021). Such fluidity is essential for developing theoretical frameworks that can accommodate ongoing technological shifts (Konsynski et al., 2024). Consequently, a growing body of work conceptualizes AI as a form of digital agency capable of acting autonomously on behalf of workers and intentionally constraining or enabling them (Agerfalk, 2020; Murray et al., 2021). This perspective of digital agency and autonomy aligns with a delegation lens in which the artifact can decide and act, often without human intervention or knowledge (Baird & Maruping, 2021).

It is this fluid conceptualization of AI that we adopt, and this notion of autonomy that anchors our model’s boundaries. Building on this foundation, AI systems are situated along a continuum of autonomy, from lowagency assistants to highly autonomous systems capable of making real-time decisions and initiating actions (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Castelfranchi & Falcone, 1998). Accordingly, we define the delegatee boundaries of our model in terms of AI’s degree of autonomy along this continuum and, more specifically, by the threshold at which AI autonomy becomes sufficient to alter control. Below a certain threshold, which varies for reasons that lie outside the scope of this study, workers may not feel the need to engage in control efforts, as the system’s autonomy does not significantly challenge their agency.

Within our model, we also set boundaries around the human actors involved as delegators. The worker is conceived not as a passive party but as an actor with a margin of maneuver who can adapt, resist, or shape the process. A broad range of workers fulfill this condition of autonomy. For example, knowledge workers who are particularly susceptible to AI-driven disruption due to the complex cognitive tasks they perform (Benbya et al., 2021; Parker & Grote, 2022) typically possess high levels of autonomy (Mazmanian et al., 2013) and thus fall within this range. Other workers in routinized or procedurally constrained environments may also experience control disruption and retain some space to maneuver. Therefore, our model applies to workers across varying levels of discretion, from service administrative staff to researchers and professionals (Bader & Kaiser, 2019; Jarvenpaa & Klein, 2024). What matters from a control perspective is primarily their ability to influence the delegation process. Accordingly, we define the conceptual boundaries for the delegator to include all workers who possess a minimum threshold of autonomy enabling them to actively engage in control processes, adapting, resisting, or otherwise reshaping the delegation process. This typically excludes roles where autonomy is severely limited, such as factory workers and certain scripted occupations.

In brief, the HiC model attends to the interplay between the expanding autonomy of $\mathsf { A I } ^ { 1 }$ and the enduring, adaptable autonomy of workers. This interplay presumes that workers and AI do not operate in isolation, but in contexts where tasks are delegated to AI, with humans retaining the capacity to engage in control processes. This boundary setting excludes scenarios where workers neither interact with the AI nor depend on its output for their other work, and, instead, centers on contexts where interdependencies between human and AI enable meaningful control dynamics to unfold.

## 3 Dynamics of Delegation to AI and Control in the Literature

The prevalent conceptualization of control in IS and management research builds on agency theory and aims to align the controlled entity with a principal’s objectives (Ouchi, 1979). When applied organizationally, this form of control has been conceptualized as actions by organizations or managers to align employee behavior with organizational goals (Kirsch, 1997, 2004). More recent literature on algorithmic management has adopted the same instrumental understanding of control to study algorithmic control, where digital systems mediate or automate oversight and evaluation mechanisms (Kellogg et al., 2020; Mohlmann et al., 2023; Rahman, 2021). Across these streams of literature, control— whether managerial or algorithmic—is generally treated as an externally exercised mechanism of behavioral alignment. A task-level variation of this conceptualization of control is direct task control, defined as worker action on the task execution process itself to influence how, when, or whether a task is carried out (Nucci, 2020). Rather than aligning another actor’s behavior as in managerial or algorithmic control, direct task control concerns the individual’s own intervention in task performance, allowing them to retain discretion over specific task elements.

This form of control is particularly relevant in contexts of AI delegation. A foundational insight in studying delegation to AI is that it rarely involves a full transfer of all rights and responsibilities for task execution (Baird & Maruping, 2021). Workers often want to retain control over certain elements of the task, which can often be divided into components distributed between humans and machines. Only the necessary subset of rights and responsibilities for the delegated component is transferred (Aggarwal & Mazumdar, 2008; Baird & Maruping, 2021). Consequently, delegation varies in degree, depending on how modular the task is and how execution rights and responsibilities can be distributed across its components (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Stelmaszak et al., 2025). The more direct control the delegator retains over the task, the less the delegation, and vice versa (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 2000). Therefore, delegation is inherently a relinquishment of this form of control and can only happen when some of it is ceded to the delegatee. This inverse relationship reveals delegation as a variable, negotiated process in which direct task control is distributed and reconfigured over time.

The negotiation in delegation between retaining and ceding direct task control is evident in much of the literature on human-AI work. A consistent pattern across studies is the desire to remain meaningfully involved in AI tasks, such as through workarounds and manipulations (Bader & Kaiser, 2019). In particular, behaviors of adaptation to the opacity of AI in this literature often reflect direct control retention efforts. In clinical settings, practitioners interrogate AI outputs to make sense of system recommendations and preserve control over diagnostic decisions (Lebovitz et al., 2022). In highly analytical environments, collaborative interpretation of AI-generated outputs fosters deeper domain understanding (Anthony, 2021). Other forms of intervention include personalizing AI recommendations (Fügener et al., 2021), triangulating input data to validate AI insights (Mikalsen & Monteiro, 2021), and adjusting direct control levels through reinforcing or reducing tolerance for uncertainty (Constantinides et al., 2024). Direct control adjustment is also reflected in human-inthe-loop configurations, where human involvement can shape task performance by refining AI outputs and improving decision outcomes through feedback (Nunes et al., 2018). When these feedback loops function well, they enable algorithmic learning, resulting in improved accuracy and more reliable knowledge generation (uit het Broek et al., 2021; Saghafi et al., 2022; van den Broek et al., 2021). However, interventions are not always beneficial. They can undermine outcomes when humans override accurate AI advice (Ge et al., 2021) or when engaging in delegation leads to erosion of humanspecific expertise and knowledge diversity (Fügener et al., 2021). An alternative to human-in-the-loop configurations is machine-in-the-loop, which positions humans in a leading role and reflects a preference for retaining centrality and oversight (Lubars & Tan, 2019). These studies show that interventions are often desired by workers and aim at balancing direct task control and task delegation.

Another conceptualization of control is personal control, which is highly relevant to delegation and is at the core of our HiC model. Personal control is anchored in psychology and represents a subjective and experiential view of control understood as an internally developed perception or a sense of influence (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Rothbaum et al., 1982; Skinner, 1995). The literature in IS and management shows that this psychological form of control is essential for the success of delegation to AI (Adam et al., 2024; Grote et al., 2024). Yet it has been largely neglected, despite many studies addressing fragmented elements of it in the form of selfdirected responses to AI delegation. Indeed, delegation to AI not only shifts task responsibilities but also disrupts how individuals perceive their ability to influence outcomes, which reduces their perceived personal control (Skinner, 1995).

For many workers, especially those with deep expertise, the integration of AI into core tasks can undermine their sense of competence and provoke feelings of deskilling and identity threat (Adam et al., 2024; Strich et al., 2021). Structured but opaque AI systems further complicate this experience by introducing role ambiguity, particularly for those who value order and clarity in their work (Tang et al., 2022; Tarafdar et al., 2023). In response, workers often exhibit emotional reactions, particularly when AI delegation threatens autonomy. They may experience discomfort or resistance when they are unable to influence task execution or when surveillance and delegation anxieties arise in sensitive settings such as healthcare (Husairi & Rossi, 2024; Park et al., 2022). Responses to personal control disruption can also be made through cognitive strategies to manage uncertainty, engaging in algorithm sensemaking, and searching for explanations and reconstructing meaning in the absence of transparency (Lebovitz et al., 2022; Mohlmann et al., 2023). Yet even with these efforts, uncertainty persists, as cognitive biases like verification bias and belief oscillations interfere with the ability to judge AI reliability (de Vericourt & Gurkan, 2023). Where AI systems demand attention but remain inscrutable, workers must navigate the tension between improved performance and the increased cognitive effort required to assess and act on machine outcomes (Boyaci et al., 2023). This psychological form of control has been treated in extant IS research as a priori perceptions that influence how individuals cope with new IT events (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). When the event is an AI implementation, workers may apply these perceptions either to reduce the disruption of their work routines or to empower themselves to effectively leverage the technology (Chen et al., 2022). Yet under conditions of constrained autonomy or unpredictable AI behavior, such perceptions are challenged, giving rise to emotional discomfort, resistance, and even risks to selfperception that further threaten personal control (Adam et al., 2024; Husairi & Rossi, 2024; Park et al., 2022). Therefore, gaining personal control is not the starting point of adaptation in a context of delegation to AI; it is its sought-after outcome.

In conclusion, the literature demonstrates how delegation to AI can disrupt workers’ perceptions of personal control and create significant psychological and operational challenges. However, while current research effectively describes the disruptions in control caused by delegation, it remains fragmented in its explanation of isolated responses that partially explain control restoration. It has not yet explained how workers might effectively integrate direct task control with cognitive and emotional adjustments to regain and sustain a robust sense of personal control. Developing an integrated theoretical framework of personal control in delegation to AI is thus essential for a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between interventions that reduce delegation and self-directed responses to it.

## 4 Background Theory: Personal Control

Personal control<sup>2</sup> is an innate psychological necessity for every human being. It is inherently desirable, and individuals naturally strive to increase it (Leotti et al., 2010; Rothbaum et al., 1982). Conversely, a loss of personal control may result in withdrawal and sabotage (Allen & Greenberger, 1980/2013). When significant, the loss of personal control can be devastating (Skinner, 1995) and may lead to serious negative consequences for mental health and well-being (Rothbaum et al., 1982). Thus, beyond being desirable, personal control is essential. The motivation to gain control can naturally stem from a perceived loss of control. We argue that it can also stem from a proactive desire to enhance one’s control rather than a mere response to losing it. This proactive approach to gaining control reflects a fundamental human drive to achieve greater autonomy and mastery with new and evolving systems (Nevo et al., 2016).

In their seminal article, Rothbaum et al. (1982) conceived personal control as a two-process alignment of the self and the environment. The first process brings the environment into line with one’s wishes, and the second refocuses efforts on the self to bring it into line with environmental forces. Primary control has both functional and temporal primacy over secondary control. Functionally, it provides greater adaptive value for the individual. Temporally, it comes first, as individuals generally attempt to change their environment before resorting to changing themselves (Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995; Rothbaum et al., 1982). In practice, especially when engaging in primary control, changes to the self accompany changes to the environment. This emphasizes the importance of understanding primary and secondary control as designations of salience rather than mutually exclusive processes. Indeed, the two processes are intertwined and are believed to always coexist. However, differences in emphasis are noted: When the former is salient, the process is termed primary control, and when the latter is salient, it is termed secondary control.

Of critical importance to our model, this process approach to control implies that personal control evolves over time (Alvarez, 2008). Unlike other control concepts, such as Rotter’s (1966) locus of control, it is not a stable attribute of the individual. A further key distinction of this twoprocess control is that when individuals choose to fit with their environment and go with the flow, this is not interpreted as uncontrollability. Rather, it is a defensive controllability that contrasts with the offensive and less subtle primary control behavior. Secondary control, in contrast, acknowledges that individuals can achieve a sense of control through inward change, even when behavior appears passive, withdrawn, or submissive. The self-oriented process of secondary control recognizes that inward efforts compensate for failure to induce change or enhance the value of a chosen goal. Consequently, they are not equated with the loss of control (Rothbaum et al., 1982). A balance between primary and secondary control is essential for well-being, although primary control has both functional and temporal primacy (Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995; Rothbaum et al., 1982). In other words, individuals typically try to engage in primary control first. If they have a reason not to or if their efforts are unsuccessful, secondary control becomes more salient. While in the original theory, primary control is mostly action-based and secondary control mostly cognitive, it is difficult in practice to separate cognition from action, as they are intertwined in control efforts. Therefore, we differentiate the two based on their target, namely the external world for primary control and the internal self for secondary control (Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995). The perspective of secondary control is particularly important in organizational settings where employees feel they cannot control outcomes directly (Greenberger & Strasser, 1986). However, the emphasis on secondary control does not imply that individuals never experience uncontrollability. Rather, it stresses that uncontrollability is unsustainable, as the human need for control inevitably drives efforts to restore it (Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995; Rothbaum et al., 1982).

## 5 Human-in-Control, a Control Model for Adaptation

Understanding how workers experience and manage the loss of direct task control in delegation to AI, and how they restore a subjective sense of personal control, is the central focus of this paper. Workers’ adaptation is anchored in tasks they own that are or can be executed by AI. The specific circumstances that bring a task into consideration for delegation are outside the scope of our model and can be addressed by future research. Instead, we focus on the worker’s preference regarding their delegation. This preference, which indicates comfort with ceding task execution to AI, serves as the entry point to adaptation. Because delegation is not always enacted, the degree of (in)congruence between this preference and the surrounding context that enables or constrains delegation profoundly shapes the worker’s experience of personal control. When congruence exists, workers experience alignment between their preference and the context, thereby avoiding disruption and often gaining opportunities to enhance control. In contrast, incongruence arises when contextual pressures conflict with the worker’s preference, disrupting control and autonomy. The intersection of delegation preference and contextual congruence defines four distinct pathways to personal control, each characterized by primary and secondary control processes that workers mobilize to maintain, restore, or enhance control over their work. These pathways, while analytically distinct, unfold dynamically and lead to transient, continuously reconstructed states of personal control. Workers may shift between pathways as preferences or contextual conditions evolve, with feedback loops enabling iterative adjustments. The HiC model (Figure 1) depicts these pathways, their associated control processes, and the recursive structure of adaptation through which workers negotiate personal control in AI delegation. Different pathways lead to various levels of personal control, which workers may attempt to change over time. In cases of prolonged misalignment, workers can experience brief periods of uncontrollability, which naturally prompt iterative cycles through the model.

The remainder of this section follows the structure in Figure 1 to explain the HiC model, starting with the worker’s delegation preference and its congruence with the work environment. We then explain the pathways to personal control and the control processes they involve, keeping the perception valence constant for clarity. Changes to perception are discussed in Section 5.4, where we explain the iterative nature of the model. In this final section, we address the transient state of personal control and the role of context in changing it. Hereafter, whenever we mention control, we are referring to personal control unless otherwise indicated.

## 5.1 Delegation Preference

We begin the discussion of our model with the worker’s preference to delegate a task that AI can execute, building on AI delegation literature to explain it. Scholars often use willingness to delegate as a proxy for openness to work with an AI system. It is typically conceptualized as an anticipatory and attitudinal variable that captures the intention to cede task execution to AI under certain conditions (Liu et al., 2012). At the same time, Baird and Maruping’s (2021) delegation framework accounts for the influence of the delegating agent’s preference regarding delegation. In fact, willingness and preference are not independent since the willingness to delegate is partly contingent on preferences (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 1998, 2000; Freisinger & Schneider, 2024). However, we focus on the worker’s preference due to its control relevance. While willingness is a useful construct when studying drivers of delegation, it has limitations for understanding how control-building processes unfold in delegation. Specifically, it captures the workers’ intent but not their comfort in delegating to AI, which is critical for control. Also, it does not account for what happens when the delegation is non-volitional and arises from organizational initiatives rather than the worker’s own decision (Strich et al., 2021). In these cases, task execution may be transferred regardless of individual intent, rendering willingness inadequate for studying how workers respond.

Delegation preference instead offers a control-relevant alternative, reflecting how comfortable a worker is with AI performing a task. Its importance has been recognized from the perspectives of the worker delegating a task, the person affected by that task (Freisinger & Schneider, 2024), and the manager deciding on what AI to invest in (Queiroz et al., 2024). In the HiC model, we adopt the point of view of the delegating worker who, through cognitive and emotional processes, constructs preferences regarding what goals they want to achieve and how (Baird & Maruping, 2021).

As such, these preferences are sensitive to cognitive biases. For example, individuals may prefer delegation if they have a positive bias of algorithm appreciation, where they tend to favor machine advice over human advice for the same advice quality (Logg et al., 2019). In contrast, algorithm aversion represents a tendency to disfavor algorithmic systems, even if the system is objectively more accurate than human judgment (Dietvorst et al., 2018). In addition, many factors were empirically found to shape such preferences. For instance, workers might desire to delegate less of a task when they are skilled at it and thus enjoy it (Lubars & Tan, 2019) or when they have little visibility into the AI’s execution or lack the ability to intervene or reverse its actions (Fügener et al., 2022; Liu et al.,

2025). Other factors that shape delegation preference include a person’s underlying motivation for doing the task, the level of convenience they desire, how difficult they believe the task to be, the level of risk they associate with its completion, their tolerance for uncertainty, their trust in the AI, and the confidence they have in the delegation process (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Constantinides et al., 2024; Freisinger & Schneider, 2024; Lee & Moray, 1994). These factors are not all independent. For instance, motivation may be shaped by how difficult the task is perceived to be and how skilled the worker feels in performing it (Lubars & Tan, 2019). Similarly, trust can help mitigate perceived risks of delegation and strengthen confidence in the AI’s ability to execute the task accurately and responsibly (Jacovi et al., 2021).

Just as delegation itself is not binary, neither is delegation preference. For simplicity, we conceptualize a preference not to delegate as a preference for little or no delegation and for retaining high direct task control; all other cases are treated as a preference to delegate.

## 5.2 Contextual Congruence

A worker’s delegation preference is not necessarily implemented. In fact, context plays an important role in facilitating or hindering it. More specifically, a worker’s context includes, in its closest circle, other organizational members and technological tools, and is thus referred to as active context. At a higher level, the latent context within the organization consists of elements such as organizational structure, culture, and strategy. Lastly, the external organizational context includes, among other things, clients, regulators, competitors, and occupational norms and values (Argote & Miron-Spektor, 2011). Forces exerted at any level in the context either encourage delegation behaviors that are consistent with the worker’s preference or pull the worker in a direction opposite to that preference. We define contextual congruence as the alignment between a worker’s delegation preference and a context that enables its implementation. It captures the idea that the context supports and aligns with the worker’s inclination, making it possible to implement their preferred course of action. As such, it inherently fosters personal control through alignment and the reduction of friction between desire and environment, as well as by enabling the worker to exercise their choice.

First, a focus on congruence recognizes that while workers within the HiC boundaries enjoy a certain degree of autonomy at work, their agency is not unlimited. They might want to delegate or not, but their ability to satisfy their preference can be constrained by factors that are often governed by contextual forces at any level within or outside the organization. Examples of factors in a context unfavorable to delegation include the lack of necessary AI tools or infrastructure to support delegation, the lack of opportunities for delegation, and the interferences by others inside or outside the organization (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 2000). Another example that is especially relevant to industries such as healthcare and finance is a regulatory framework that prohibits delegation to AI for specific tasks. In contrast, when the context encourages a worker’s preference to delegate or counters their reluctance, a common contextual force is the top-down push by management for digital transformation or AI adoption, driven by goals of efficiency, cost reduction, or competitiveness (Vial, 2019). In brief, a worker’s preference is not necessarily aligned with the direction of these combined forces.

Second, in a situation of alignment between delegation preferences and context, the worker can exercise their choice. Control theorists consider choice a vehicle for achieving a perception of control (Averill, 1973; Leotti et al., 2010). Workers who have a choice to act consistently with their preference can exercise their agency, which studies suggest leads to feelings of confidence and success (Leotti et al., 2010). This aligns with findings that choice, even under stress, is inherently desirable and enhances personal control, regardless of whether a tangible benefit is anticipated (Bown et al., 2003). Therefore, workers who perceive a congruence between their delegation preference and the surrounding conditions that allow them to act on it are likely to experience the situation positively. Conversely, when they are stripped of choice and pressured to act against their preference, they are likely to perceive the experience negatively.

As evidenced in the literature on coping with disruptive IS events (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005), individuals adapting to various circumstances assess positive experiences differently from negative ones. Moreover, theories such as cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957), self-determination (Deci & Ryan, 1985), and control (Gregory, 1978) suggest that individuals strive for consistency among their beliefs, attitudes, and actions, and that such consistency promotes positive feelings and perceptions. Specifically, in the context of control, they do not deal with control over events separately from control over their feelings in response to those events. Indeed, when a salient situational event occurs, people associate it with a positive or negative perception, each leading to different behavioral and cognitive efforts to increase their sense of control (Greenberger & Strasser, 1986; Gregory, 1978) and adapt to the new situation. For instance, secondary control may involve learning and using strategies to enhance pleasure in positively perceived experiences, whereas, in negatively perceived experiences, it might focus on coping mechanisms to mitigate distress (Bryant, 1989).

In conclusion, we argue that when workers experience congruence between their delegation preference and the context, this alignment fosters positive perceptions of their experience, whether they delegate to AI or not. On the other hand, when there is a misalignment, such as when workers are pressured to delegate against their preference, it disrupts their sense of autonomy and control, leading to a negative perception of their experience. Consequently, they pursue personal control differently based on how they perceive their experience. In our model, we propose four control-building pathways involving primary and secondary control processes. The pathways are distributed along two dimensions: whether the worker prefers to delegate to AI and whether contextual congruence is present.

## 5.3 Pathways to Personal Control

The HiC model presents four pathways for workers to reconstruct their personal control through the salience of either primary or secondary control. Workers often oscillate between the two saliences. To illustrate the coexistence of the two types of control and the permeable movement between them, each pathway is represented in Figure 1 as a shape that combines both in a gradient of salience, though each begins with primary control. When a worker does not engage in a primary control process due to a lack of motivation or opportunity, or engages in it but fails, secondary control becomes focal (Rothbaum et al., 1982). In such situations, secondary control complements the missing primary control, either compensating for its failure or identifying an alternative goal that improves the chances of its success in the future (Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995). A shift from primary to secondary control reflects secondary control’s compensatory role vis-à-vis primary efforts. Conversely, a shift from secondary to primary control reflects secondary control’s preparatory role for renewed primary control. Adaptation requires iteratively balancing these control processes and integrating them effectively (Rothbaum et al., 1982), with higher levels of personal control achieved when primary control is salient than when adaptation is largely based on secondary control (Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995).

In describing the four pathways and their individual control processes, we assume a stable delegation preference. Influences that may alter preferences are addressed in the feedback section, which discusses iterations in the HiC process and the transient nature of personal control states. This structure reduces complexity and enhances clarity, which we further reinforce through examples of knowledge-intensive work with critical outcomes. Throughout, we use the case of a claims analyst in an insurance firm as a running example, summarized in Table 1. Although the HiC model is not confined to knowledge-intensive, highstakes contexts, these contexts provide strong and unambiguous illustrations of the control processes.

## 5.3.1 Pathway A: Contextual Congruence with a Preference for Delegation

Workers along Pathway A have a preference for delegation and the opportunity to act on it, thereby exercising choice. In this scenario, the congruence between their preference and contextual forces supporting delegation validates their choice, maintaining a positive perception throughout the delegation experience. Rather than adapting to a negative disruption of personal control, this pathway illustrates a process that reinforces it.

Adaptation in this case is a contextually supported and potentially transformational process aimed at elevating personal control. For example, consider a claims analyst at a large insurance firm who delegates routine claims assessments to Clara, an advanced AI system capable of evaluating, recommending, and autoapproving straightforward claims. The analyst’s preference for delegation is driven by the desire to offload this time-consuming task that the analyst does not particularly value, and is supported by Clara’s high accuracy rates, robust documentation capabilities, and compliance with regulatory standards. In parallel, management is keen to see a return on its investment in Clara and has implemented a change management plan that includes rethinking workflows and training employees on this new AI. More than aligning with the analyst’s preference, the push for Clara opens possibilities for leveraging the freed resources.

A successful pass along this pathway is characterized by low levels of direct control where workers seldom question the AI decision rationale (Jussupow et al., 2021; Lebovitz et al., 2022). As the AI clears their workload from the delegated task or part of it, the liberated time and cognitive resources can be applied to other areas (Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2020). How workers allocate these resources differs between primary expansive and secondary developmental control processes.

Expansive control: Some workers are more likely than others to positively enrich their jobs in response to complexity. For these workers, the resources that are freed by delegating to AI open opportunities for proactive, innovative behavior (Benbya et al., 2024; Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2020). We argue that workers motivated by the need to enhance control levels utilize these resources to focus on higher-level activities and engage in more valuable work. Such control behavior is an expression of role breadth selfefficacy, whereby employees can take on broader duties beyond their primary role (Tang et al., 2022). Since they are relieved of dull tasks, they exhibit extrarole behavior that enriches their role through higherskilled, more meaningful tasks (Parker & Grote, 2022).

Returning to the example of the claims analyst, successful delegation of routine claims to Clara allows the analyst to shift focus toward more complex, ambiguous claims or strategic activities such as developing improved risk models, providing strategic advice to underwriting teams, or engaging in crossfunctional collaboration. Through expansive control, the analyst proactively expands the scope and depth of their responsibilities, which has been established as an indicator of increased personal control (Baronas & Louis, 1988). In another example focused on relational enrichment, delegating routine forecasting tasks enabled planners to participate more actively with internal design teams and external strategic partners (Angelopoulos et al., 2023). In general, added tasks vary by domain, but common ones involve supervision and collaboration (Coombs et al., 2020). With more delegation and more resource reallocation, expansive control can be associated with broad job transformation beyond a few task changes, as illustrated by the case of lawyers, where delegation has enabled new ways of practicing, packaging, and delivering legal services (Kronblad, 2020). In sum, workers engaging in expansive control increase their responsibilities, elevate their tasks, and enrich their role and relationships, thereby enhancing their personal control.

Developmental control: As with any secondary control process, developmental control either compensates for low primary control—expansive in this pathway—or serves to prepare for increasing it. In its compensatory role, a possible scenario is one of choosing delegation not for the opportunities it opens, but rather out of aversion to feelings of regret triggered by missing out on its benefits (Feng & Gao, 2020). In another scenario, workers may wish to enjoy the unburdening of the delegated task and may not necessarily seek expansion. In these and similar situations, workers savor the positive outcomes of delegating to AI without actively trying to obtain further ones (Bryant, 1989), enhancing their personal control thanks to the reduction in their workload (Barley et al., 2011). This reduction allows them to reflect on and appreciate the successful outcomes of delegation (Abdel-Karim et al., 2023), reinforcing confidence in their choice to delegate and increasing satisfaction with the work process. In doing so, they exercise a form of cognition that is particularly effective in case of failure to achieve expansive control as it focuses on sensemaking and acceptance (Rothbaum et al., 1982).

Whether due to failure, lack of opportunity, or personal preference, workers engaging in developmental control processes allocate the resources released by delegation toward better performance in value-adding tasks that are already within their primary role. Clinicians, for instance, were found to enjoy increasing focus on patient care when unburdened by a generative AI from the daunting and non-rewarding yet necessary task of documenting patient records (Van Veen et al., 2024). In another example, delegation of loan-granting decisions empowered new loan consultants to strengthen their professional role identity and gain reassurance (Strich et al., 2021). This form of savoring is often unconscious (Bryant, 1989), but there are also conscious forms in which one achieves developmental outcomes beyond performance. For workers who believe they do not have the ability to succeed in expansive control processes, developmental control, with its focus on the self, presents an opportunity to develop this ability. This can take the form of building new skills, whether related to knowledge of AI or to other areas, like the domain or the context, that enable leveraging AI for expansive control or even for continued relevance in the current role (Krakowski et al., 2023). Learning new skills can occur vicariously from the AI itself. Examples of such learning include collaborative skills (Jarvenpaa & Valikangas, 2020) and data management and analytical skills (Mikalsen & Monteiro, 2021). Even with an AI that lacks explainability, learning can happen by inspiration from observing its outcomes. Research suggests that medium-skill workers might benefit the most from developmental control processes, where they gradually build their competencies in collaboration with AI. In contrast, high-skill workers may struggle with developmental control, as their performance may decrease (Choi & Schwarcz, 2024).

Engaging in developmental control, the claims analyst may use the freed time to reinforce their core competencies. They might review a subset of Clara’s outputs to deepen their understanding of edge cases, attend internal briefings on AI deployment to stay informed, enroll in training sessions, process more claims, or simply grow a sense of comfort with their choice of delegation and reframe it as wise prioritization. Along this developmental process, personal control is enhanced through reducing the workload, improving current tasks, and/or gradually building the skills and mindset for a potential future expansion.

Whether engaging in expansive or developmental control processes, workers enhance their personal control along Pathway A by aligning the environment and the self at higher levels of efficiency and accuracy. Specifically with expansive control, this alignment is also accompanied by more responsibilities, choice, and role breadth. Pathway

A, especially when expansive control is salient, thus leads to higher levels of personal control.

## 5.3.2 Pathway B: Contextual Incongruence with a Preference for Delegation

Similar to Pathway A, workers engaging in Pathway B exhibit a preference for delegation. However, unlike in Pathway A, they face contextual incongruence in their desire to delegate. In other words, these workers are unable to delegate to AI to the extent they would prefer. Therefore, this pathway begins with misalignment between their delegation preference and contextual forces that hinder it, stripping them of choice and disrupting autonomy and personal control. This misalignment is a source of frustration and leads to a negative perception of their experience of working without AI. Rather than adapting to changes caused by delegating tasks to AI, workers navigating this pathway adapt instead to the deprivation of delegation opportunities. This phenomenon, where workers experience constraint from their inability to delegate tasks to technology, has been largely overlooked in IS literature, though industry reports highlight its growing prominence (Microsoft, 2024).

In the case of the claims analyst, contextual incongruence arises when, for example, the analyst is required to manually review each case, even though Clara is technically capable of processing routine claims. Management could be mandating full human oversight to avoid perceived reputational or legal risks or to emphasize the importance of maintaining human involvement. Other contextual forces can include internal IT support deprioritizing Clara’s integration with the claims platform, leaving the analyst to work across fragmented systems that hinder smooth delegation. Some informal norms, such as being viewed as cutting corners or lacking diligence, might also discourage delegation to AI. For these and other reasons, workers might find themselves unable to delegate to AI despite wanting to. They deal with contextual incongruence through primary championing control and secondary recalibrating control processes.

Championing control: Since primary control processes change the external environment to align it with the self, workers engaging in championing control processes aim to reduce contextual incongruence by bringing the context closer to their preference. Therefore, they typically address obstacles to congruence by advocating for more delegation. In practice, this involves individual or collective efforts to secure necessary resources, obtain managerial approval, or influence organizational policy changes supportive of AI delegation (Bassellier et al., 2003; Howell & Boies, 2004). Alternatively, some workers seek unofficial ways to access AI. The growing phenomenon of “bring your own AI” illustrates a form of primary control where employees introduce AI tools into their workflows to compensate for organizational delays or restrictions (Microsoft, 2024). While this is only feasible for some individual use tools, it signals a broader bottom-up dynamic in which workers seek to shape organizational conditions so that their task environment aligns with their delegation preference. Within organizations, such championing can involve advocating for resources to increase delegation when AI is implemented but underutilized or poorly integrated. For a shorter-term reduction of contextual incongruence with desired AI delegation, workers can seek delegation benefits in alternative ways, such as delegating to colleagues or assistants, or implementing fixes through non-AI tools to manage the workload until more AI delegation becomes possible.

In the case of the analyst, championing control may involve highlighting how Clara, the firm’s AI system, handles routine claims with high accuracy but is restricted to a narrow subset of cases. The analyst might document instances where manual review of similar low-complexity claims introduces delays or inconsistencies, presenting these as evidence for expanding Clara’s scope. In strategic meetings, they could emphasize how broader delegation to Clara would reduce bottlenecks, accelerate claim resolution, and maintain compliance with regulatory thresholds. To reinforce this case, the analyst may take on a superuser role, demonstrating best practices and training colleagues to increase confidence in Clara’s use.

Through such primary control efforts, workers seek to shape the organizational context to support their preferred level of delegation and achieve the benefits they expect from it, thereby restoring their personal control that was disrupted by contextual incongruence.

Recalibrating control: This control process helps workers manage frustration and maintain personal control perceptions even when delegation is less than desired. Short of changing the context, workers can reduce contextual incongruence by directing the change toward themselves, either to reattempt championing or to cope with the deprivation of AI delegation. Preparing for succeeding at primary control, they can develop skills that will enhance future delegation, such as the claims analyst learning how claims automation must align with internal audit requirements or compliance mandates. The analyst may also look to colleagues in other departments or regions where Clara has been successfully integrated, observing how they’ve adapted their workflows and what kinds of cases they chose to delegate. These indirect insights serve not only to inform the analyst’s own expectations but also to vicariously build confidence and strengthen their network to increase the chances of collective championing for a future transition. Otherwise, the analyst might cognitively reframe the situation by attributing the failure to external factors beyond their control, drawing on attributional bias to reduce personal frustration and close the gap with their work context (Rothbaum et al., 1982; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).

Facing a lack of primary control can also be handled through adjusting aspiration levels by devaluing the unattainable goal in what is known as the sour grapes effect (Elster, 1983; Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995). This is a form of control where one convinces oneself that delegation to AI was never worth it anyway. Moreover, adjusting expectations helps in aligning with the reality of not being able to delegate to AI. This involves setting realistic goals and focusing on what can be achieved without AI within the existing constraints. With the right expectations, workers can avoid disappointment as predicted outcomes do not contradict actual ones (Rothbaum et al., 1982). Also referred to as defensive pessimism (Norem & Cantor, 1986), this strategy involves cognitive adaptation by anticipating that contextual support for AI will not happen, thereby increasing predictability, which is known to enhance personal control perceptions (Baronas & Louis, 1988).

At best, Pathway B can restore disrupted personal control to its initial level. Successful championing means that primary control has moved the context from incongruence to congruence, realigning it with the worker’s delegation preference. If control is restored, this alignment can later lead to enhancement only if the worker follows it with control-enhancing processes, as described in Pathway A. Conversely, especially when secondary recalibrating control is salient, the worker is likely to restore alignment at lower levels of expectations and goal achievement, thereby experiencing a reduction in personal control. In extreme cases, when contextual incongruence persists and recalibrating control efforts fail, workers might disengage by reducing their effort or investment in the task, feeling disempowered by the inability to delegate to AI as they believe they should. This strategy allows them to mentally or emotionally withdraw (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005) as a means of coping with the stress of incongruence, yet it still reduces their personal control.

## 5.3.3 Pathway C: Contextual Congruence with a Preference for No Delegation

Workers in Pathway C prefer minimal or no delegation to AI. Contextual congruence enables them to choose the retention of direct task control. As a result, they develop a positive perception of their experience and enhance their sense of personal control, even without benefiting from delegation’s efficiency gains, though sometimes at the cost of added demands on existing resources (Boyaci et al., 2023). For instance, the claims analyst in our running example may opt for manual review of all property damage claims rather than using Clara, the firm’s AI system, emphasizing the need for judgment in borderline cases and ongoing familiarity with evolving regulatory requirements.

Preference for no delegation typically arises due to concerns about the tension between authority and responsibility, task criticality, or personal reservations. These reservations may stem from distrust, discomfort with the technology, or high levels of task-specific expertise (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 2000; Lubars & Tan, 2019). In cases where tasks are core to the workers’ roles but not necessarily critical, concerns about skill erosion and regulatory implications can also drive the decision to avoid or limit delegation (Berente et al., 2021; Lindebaum et al., 2020; Parker & Grote, 2022). Even without these concerns, some workers avoid delegation due to fears of replacement, poor choices that may result in regret or blame (Fleming et al., 2010), or added workloads, prompting them to dismiss algorithmic advice (Kawaguchi, 2021). The latter can be particularly relevant in contexts of high workload where, paradoxically, delegation to AI is the most needed (Boyaci et al., 2023). Consequently, the desire to maintain direct control leads to either eliminating delegation altogether or reducing it to a minimal level. In conclusion, workers following this pathway are aligned with a context that does not pressure them to delegate. Consulting firms are typical examples of such contexts, where consultants have access to a range of systems they can use at their discretion, choosing to adopt one tool or another without having a direct obligation to do so. In such environments, contextual congruence contributes to workers’ personal control by allowing them to avoid or minimize delegation to AI through primary conservative and secondary provisional control processes.<sup>3</sup>

Conservative control: This control process focuses on retaining direct task control. This can be done through either ignoring the outcome of the AI or closely supervising and monitoring its performance. In the former, primary control involves efforts to maintain the existing contextual congruence by deeply engaging with the task they chose not to delegate and actively resisting organizational initiatives for the future introduction of

AI in areas where they prefer human control. In the latter, workers significantly reduce delegation without eliminating it, recognizing the value of leveraging AI’s capabilities without being overly reliant on its outcome (Dellermann et al., 2019). In doing so, they can implement two types of direct control: influencing, aimed at preventing mistakes, and adjustment, aimed at correcting them (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 2000).

Taking the insurance example again, both the insurance firm and the claims analysts often err on the side of caution and insist on having the human-in-theloop for major decisions (Allianz, 2025). In practice, the analyst may keep human control over highexposure or ambiguous claims. Case elements such as incomplete documentation or claimants with prior history may require expert judgment that Clara’s contextual intelligence cannot reliably handle. On the other hand, an emphasis on adjustment control brings to the fore the role of humans in compensating for machine errors (Jussupow et al., 2021). Conservative control might also manifest as efforts to influence organizational policies or culture. By cognitively shifting their focus from individual preferences to collective harm prevention, workers can advocate for more transparency in AI decision-making processes, push for the inclusion of non-AI alternatives in more workflows, or highlight the importance of maintaining human expertise and judgment. In sum, as contextual congruence does not disrupt the worker’s personal control in this pathway and as the aim in this control process is to shield the environment from potential delegation downsides, successful conservative control maintains initial levels of personal control.

Provisional control: Conservative control efforts may fail. Workers then risk spending more time and cognitive effort reconciling AI outcomes with their knowledge than on finding alternative actions (Boyaci et al., 2023; Coombs et al., 2020). In such cases, secondary provisional control gains prominence. If its goal is to eventually achieve conservative control, provisional control provides opportunities to build skills to better understand the technology and interpret its outcomes or to prepare for more effective engagement with management. For the claims analyst, this might include proactive steps to strengthen skills outside delegated tasks, such as enhancing AI literacy or analytical capabilities.

Otherwise, this control process focuses on preventing harm to oneself, especially through the retention of one’s skills and expertise. Indeed, one risk of technology is the unintended loss of unique human knowledge (Fügener et al., 2021). AI can foster complacency and reduce cognitive engagement, eventually leading to the loss of skills. This risk of skill erosion is especially problematic when workers lose understanding of tasks they delegated to the AI while still being accountable for them (Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023; Sward et al., 2022). To mitigate the risk of losing actuarial skills, the analyst in our example might build and test their own probabilistic models using historical claims data, maintaining fluency in statistical reasoning, industry assumptions, and compliance considerations.

Other secondary control efforts focus on mentally reaffirming the value of human judgment and expertise. The continuous use of skills, even alongside advanced technological tools, reinforces self-efficacy and helps workers maintain a sense of personal control (Bandura, 2012). As skills and expertise also emerge through social interactions (Lave & Wenger, 1991), workers might seek feedback through collaborative exchanges and maintain active engagement with others making similar delegation choices. In addition, positively framing their delegation experience reduces control loss (Rothbaum et al., 1982). Workers who frame their choice as an opportunity to engage deeply with the work might find satisfaction in emphasizing the mastery of complex tasks.

In sum, Pathway C does not disrupt workers’ existing levels of personal control, given the congruence between their preference for minimal delegation and the context’s stance. Conservative and provisional control processes jointly—though with primacy for conservative—help workers sustain their personal control through deliberate task engagement and cognitive reinforcement of their expertise and the importance of their role.

## 5.3.4 Pathway D: Contextual Incongruence with a Preference for No Delegation

Workers in Pathway D also prefer minimal or no delegation, yet encounter contextual incongruence because the context encourages or mandates delegation to AI. This misalignment significantly disrupts workers’ sense of control, creating a negative perception of the delegation experience. Contextual pressures driving this incongruence commonly include organizational mandates, management directives and expectations, competitive pressures, and industry standards that encourage or require the use of AI. For instance, an insurance firm may mandate delegation to Clara to streamline routine claims processing, directly contradicting a claims analyst’s preference for human oversight.

Among all four pathways in the HiC model, this pathway represents the most significant disruption to personal control. Like Pathway B, it involves contextual incongruence that creates a misalignment between the worker’s preference and the environment. However, while Pathway B’s incongruence prevents a preferred delegation, here incongruence constrains workers into delegation against their will, thereby exerting greater pressure on their autonomy. To restore disrupted control, workers adopt primary circumventive and secondary accommodating control processes.

Circumventive control: This form of primary control is a proactive strategy aimed at circumventing the perceived risks or downsides associated with delegating tasks to AI systems, whether delegation is explicitly mandated or expected. Workers engaging in this control process intentionally counter incongruent contexts to maintain their desired direct control over tasks. While reaching out to management and explaining concerns might be a first line of primary control action, it is not always effective, and workers may resort to exceptional actions like seeking external legal advisors to strengthen their argument (Asatiani et al., 2021). Circumventive change efforts may also be directed toward the AI system itself. For instance, experienced loan consultants were found to analyze the decision-making structures of the AI and manipulate the data, tweaking AI outcomes when they judged them unfair to customers (Strich et al., 2021).

In practice, a claims analyst facing mandated delegation to Clara might intentionally adjust the classification of certain cases, such as labeling them as “complex” or “exceptional” to route them outside Clara’s automated pipeline and into manual review processes using legacy systems. They may also exploit procedural timing. For example, the claims analyst might hold back routine claims until the end of the reporting cycle so they can first review borderline cases themselves, submit claims in batches that are likely to be flagged for human oversight, or wait until audit deadlines require manual checks. By using these process rhythms and deadlines, workers can create opportunities for human reasoning and reduce pressure to delegate fully to Clara. Even with traditional IS, workers often use workarounds to complete tasks more efficiently or to better meet personal or organizational objectives (Azad & King, 2008). Specifically with AI, workarounds through a traditional IS or a manual process can help circumvent the AI and ensure full oversight and direct control over the task (Bader & Kaiser, 2019). In line with this, scholars have emphasized the importance of human oversight in machine learning to ensure fairness and prevent harm to moral and ethical standards (Teodorescu et al., 2021).

Beyond the task, circumventive control may involve seeking support from colleagues who share similar reservations about AI, thereby creating a collective approach to managing tasks without AI. It may also entail engaging with various stakeholders to shape the broader context, such as collaborating with management to evaluate AI’s effectiveness (Teodorescu et al., 2021). For the claims analyst, this could include proposing a protocol mandating manual review for high-risk or ambiguous cases, ensuring alignment with external regulatory standards, and limiting worker responsibility for consequential AI outcomes. The analyst may also work with compliance teams to examine whether Clara’s decision logic adheres to evolving insurance regulations (e.g., fairness and explainability requirements) and help with adjustments. Through such initiatives, the analyst can contribute to establishing best practices that prioritize human oversight and align organizational AI policy with frameworks that emphasize human control and accountability. The goal of all these behaviors is to restore personal control by minimizing the impact of contextual pressures and maintaining as much direct control as possible over their tasks.

Accommodating control: Simply put, accommodating control processes involve giving in to an unpleasant situation or preparing to change it. Such processes may support circumventive efforts by leveraging passive involvement to develop strategies that favor their success (Rothbaum et al., 1982). In addition, workers may engage in cognitive strategies aimed at reducing tensions from unwanted delegation. They can mentally adjust their understanding of task responsibilities to better accommodate AI involvement (Konsynski et al., 2024) or reframe the task to emphasize potential gains, aligning preferences with context (Bockstedt & Buckman, 2025).

More broadly, the idea is to emphasize any beneficial aspects of AI’s role, thereby fostering a more positive outlook on delegation. Workers may also accept the incongruent context as an unavoidable reality, focusing on adjusting their attitudes or expectations to fit the situation. Such processes involve letting go of the desire for significant direct control and finding contentment in the aspects of the task still under human influence (e.g., communicating decisions to stakeholders). The creation of narratives that justify the situation, aligning it with values or beliefs, helps rationalize the use of AI or manual methods as ultimately beneficial, even when the immediate context suggests otherwise. For example, the claims analyst from our example might frame leveraging AI as forward-looking professionalism, portraying Clara’s involvement as a necessary adaptation to the evolving nature of insurance work, where data volume and regulatory scrutiny are rapidly increasing.

Additionally, workers might distance themselves by framing delegation outcomes as management’s responsibility or attributing the incongruence to external factors beyond their control, rather than viewing it as a personal failure. They may also seek out examples of colleagues or role models who have successfully navigated similar incongruence, using these examples to build confidence and a sense of control (Rothbaum et al., 1982). While these accommodating control processes do not change the external conditions, they can enable workers to reduce stress in less-than-ideal circumstances.

Overall, successful navigation of Pathway D through circumventive or accommodating control processes restores disrupted personal control, typically at reduced levels, especially if accommodating control is salient. Persistent failure of these control efforts may result in worker disengagement, reducing task involvement and emotional investment as a final protective measure against unwanted delegation pressures.

In conclusion, and as suggested by control scholars (Bryant, 1989; Heckhausen & Schulz, 1995; Rothbaum et al., 1982), our description of control processes in the HiC model emphasizes salience. The behaviors and cognitions we propose for each are typical of that process but not exclusive to it. For example, workers following an expansive control process might interrogate the AI—a behavior we include under conservative control—provided interrogations do not impede the enhancement of responsibilities, which is key to expansive control. Similarly, we present championing as a way to reduce contextual incongruence with workers’ preference to delegate. In reality, while championing prominently serves to restore control, it may also be part of primary control processes rooted in contextual congruence. This is particularly true when the focus is on encouraging colleagues to adopt AI while supporting the organizational goal of diffusing AI across units. The pathways therefore describe typical, albeit nonexclusive, scenarios.

## 5.4 A Continuous Process with a Transient Outcome

The HiC model is a continuous and dynamic process that can iterate at any point in its course. At the pathway level, failure to achieve alignment without changes in delegation preference or contextual congruence will likely result in continued attempts along the same pathway. Otherwise, a change of pathway may occur mid-process under the same preference if contextual congruence shifts. Such a change can stem from external factors, such as a regulatory shift, or from the worker’s influence through previous control efforts. Namely, achieving championing or circumventive control implies success in aligning the context with one’s delegation preference and thus movement to a congruence pathway. For instance, when championing efforts lead management to allocate more resources to supporting AI, the worker shifts into contextual congruence with a preference for delegation, engaging Pathway A. In the other direction, a worker might attribute failure in Pathway A to a lack of resources and subsequently move to Pathway B, championing for these resources or readjusting expectations through recalibrating control.

In addition, iterations mid-process that loop back to the starting point occur when the delegation preference changes. This preference is situational, since neither humans nor AI can be assumed to always be the better fit for a task (Baird & Maruping, 2021). As such, it is sensitive to feedback and contextual factors that evolve throughout the delegation process. From a psychology standpoint, preferences adapt to context, shifting with task demands and cognitive effort (Bettman et al., 1998). Therefore, interactions with the AI often trigger such changes. During interactions, critical incidents can distort perceptions of risks and vulnerabilities and alter the need for direct control. Conversely, with interactions comes familiarity, and people tend to trust systems they are familiar with (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). This familiarity provides workers with knowledge to better judge their own and the AI’s capabilities (Fügener et al., 2022). Indeed, a change from no delegation choice to delegation choice has been observed when learning more about AI replaces prejudiced algorithmic aversion with algorithmic appreciation (Turel & Kalhan, 2023). Broader contextual factors also contribute to such changes as workers are continuously exposed to cues that they use to construct and interpret events.

In general, the interface experience when interacting with AI is liminal, characterized by the simultaneous presence of various distinct forces and possibilities that shape human experience (Zhang et al., 2021). Such an understanding of feedback from interactions, whether with AI or with the context, highlights the continuously evolving nature of the HiC model and is represented in Figure 1 by a dashed line denoting a permeable membrane between the tab of personal control processes and the tab of delegation, including both delegation preference and its contextual congruence. However, while the experience is liminal, the delegation preference changes only when influencing factors cross a threshold. Indeed, not every experience with AI or the context shifts the balance. The change might not be sufficient to warrant altered behavior, and multiple occurrences are likely needed before the preference moves decisively in a new direction.

Another feedback loop (represented by arrows in Figure 1) is triggered neither by contextual changes nor by human-AI interactions, but rather by the worker’s dissatisfaction with their level of personal control. In one direction, they might desire higher levels, as Pathways B and D risk leading to a reduction in control even when successful, especially if secondary control is salient. Secondary control in situations of contextual incongruence is used as a last resort when other options are unavailable or ineffective. Therefore, if unsuccessful at reducing misalignment with the context, it can lead to uncontrollability. This state is not sustainable, as the deeply rooted need for control makes prolonged uncontrollability untenable (Greenberger & Strasser, 1986; Leotti et al., 2010; Rothbaum et al., 1982). The worker is likely to persist in restoration attempts until balance is regained. Similarly, because individuals differ in their need for personal control, someone who has experienced diminished control, for example through disengagement, may seek to increase it by iterating through the HiC model with a stronger emphasis on primary control. In the other direction, individuals do not always favor higher personal control, especially if greater control brings unwanted responsibilities or workloads (Spector, 1986). A desire to minimize their responsibility might thus explain why workers give up a pathway to enhanced personal control, such as Pathway A. In conclusion, the HiC model is characterized by a dynamic and iterative process that leads to transient states of personal control.

## 6 Discussion

In this study, we propose a theory of personal control reconstruction in the context of AI task delegation centered on how workers respond to perceived disruptions to control over work. We develop the HiC model, which identifies four distinct adaptation pathways through which workers restore, preserve, or enhance personal control in the face of delegation to AI. Rather than treating control as a binary of retention or loss, it demonstrates how control is cognitively and behaviorally reconfigured. Our model advances IS scholarship by offering a more nuanced understanding of control, adaptation, and human delegation to AI. Below, we articulate the study’s research contributions.

Our primary contribution lies in the personal control perspective we bring to the study of delegation. We develop a theoretical model that conceptualizes personal control as a dynamic process of adaptation through which workers negotiate their experience of delegation to AI. While personal control is often treated in IS as a background variable, psychological outcome, or predictor of adoption (Adam et al., 2024; Baronas & Louis, 1988; Beaudry & Pinsonneault,

2005), we reposition it as a core theoretical construct for understanding how humans adapt to delegating— or not—to AI. In contrast to existing studies that explore user resistance, detachment, workaround behavior, or role identity shifts in isolation (e.g., Bader & Kaiser, 2019; Strich et al., 2021), the HiC model unifies diverse responses under a single conceptual arc, namely the dynamic and recursive effort to restore, preserve, or enhance personal control. By integrating Rothbaum et al.’s (1982) process approach to personal control, we bridge cognitive, emotional, and behavioral processes and provide IS researchers with a model that explains delegation not merely as a transactional change in task allocation, but as an ongoing personal effort to stay in control.

In further support of this integrated control perspective, we introduce the novel construct of contextual congruence, which is the alignment between an individual’s delegation preference and the opportunities or constraints embedded in their work environment. While AI is commonly described as a disruptive technology (e.g., Benbya et al., 2021; von Krogh, 2018), we propose that it is not the presence of AI, nor the act of delegation to it, that disrupts a worker’s sense of personal control. Rather, it is the degree to which individuals are able to act in accordance with their preferred mode of engaging with AI. Accordingly, workers who can delegate in a delegation-friendly context, and those who can retain control in contexts that favor human oversight and intervention, are unlikely to experience control disruption. Such disruption is associated with situations where workers are compelled to delegate against their preference or are unable to delegate despite wishing to. These two asymmetries profoundly shape the worker’s relationship to the delegation process. In this sense, contextual congruence becomes the critical condition that governs how personal control is experienced and reconstructed over time. By foregrounding contextual congruence, our model brings into focus the central role of context in shaping how delegation to AI is experienced and enacted. It offers empirical researchers a new lens for analyzing the role of sociotechnical context in enabling or constraining human autonomy in AI work environments.

Our second contribution lies in expanding the theoretical scope of adaptation by bringing to the fore the phenomenon of delegation deprivation. We show that adaptation occurs not only when AI systems are introduced into work activities, but also when delegation to AI is constrained, especially when this conflicts with workers’ delegation preferences. Prior IS research has predominantly conceptualized adaptation as a response to the introduction of technology, where a new system disrupts tasks, roles, or routines, prompting users to accommodate or resist (e.g., Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Tyre & Orlikowski,

1994). Yet in contemporary workplaces, workers increasingly form anticipatory relationships with AI, envisioning it as a tool for offloading cognitive effort and enhancing performance. When organizational, technical, or regulatory barriers limit delegation to AI, these workers experience a distinct form of control disruption, not from AI intrusion but from its constrained availability.

Our reframing of adaptation challenges the assumption that it is contingent solely on the manifestation of technology (e.g., Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005), and instead offers a broader perspective in which both excessive and limited AI involvement can provoke adaptive efforts. It opens new lines of inquiry into how workers engage in bottom-up advocacy to increase automation or recalibrate expectations when greater technological involvement is desired. It also invites closer examination of the affective and cognitive labor involved in sustaining unrealized delegation preferences over time. This includes how workers interpret, endure, or contest the organizational decisions that limit AI delegation. By conceptualizing adaptation as emerging not only from what technology does but also from what it is prevented from doing, we expand adaptation theory to account for adjustment grounded in anticipation and organizational inertia.

We also contribute to the literature on control in IS and management by bridging two parallel streams that have evolved largely in isolation. We theorize the nuanced and nonlinear relationship between direct task control and personal control, responding to recent calls to revisit control theory in the AI era (Grote et al., 2024). We demonstrate that both retaining and relinquishing direct task control can enhance perceptions of personal control, depending on how these acts align with an individual’s delegation preference and the broader context. This framing allows us to connect two strands of control research that are typically treated separately: control as enacted behavior (e.g., exerting influence over task execution), and control as a psychological experience (e.g., autonomy, competence, or reduced stress). In prior IS work, these have often been examined through distinct lenses, such as algorithmic control (Kellogg et al., 2020; Mohlmann et al., 2023) versus perceived control (Adam et al., 2024; Baronas & Louis, 1988), without accounting for how they dynamically interact in workers’ experience of either delegating tasks to AI systems or retaining them under human control. We therefore encourage scholars to move beyond binary conceptions of personal control as “high” or “low” and instead conceptualize it as an emergent phenomenon shaped by the ability to exercise or withhold direct control in alignment with personal preferences and contextual conditions.

## 7 Future Research Opportunities

The HiC model offers a novel framework for understanding how workers preserve, enhance, or restore a sense of personal control when delegating tasks to AI systems. We aim to present IS and management researchers with a theoretical lens that positions personal control as a central perspective on delegation from humans to AI and to encourage both conceptual and empirical studies that build on this foundation. An important and natural next step for future research is to investigate how adaptation to AI delegation varies across workforce segments. Comparative analyses across different regulatory environments, occupational roles, skill levels, task criticality, and organizational sizes could shed light on the heterogeneous ways in which workers experience and manage personal control when delegating tasks to AI. Moreover, future research could extend beyond the individual level of HiC to study distributed delegation among multiple humans and AI (Stelmaszak et al., 2025), examining how teams negotiate delegation decisions, share control processes, and collectively experience contextual congruence or incongruence. Understanding these collective dynamics would complement individual-level adaptation mechanisms and broaden the model’s applicability to collaborative AI work settings. Additionally, delegation to AI is often shaped by power asymmetries and broader ethical considerations that extend beyond individual preferences, influencing whether workers experience contextual congruence or resistance to mandated delegation. Future research could examine how organizational power structures, including hierarchies, control over resources, and access to technical expertise, and ethical issues, such as autonomy, fairness, transparency, and accountability, shape circumventive and accommodating control processes. Such inquiry would illuminate the political and normative dimensions of adaptation, extend governance theories of AI, and guide organizations toward more ethically aware delegation practices.

While many broader research directions can be envisioned, we choose to give particular attention to three additional avenues of research, selected for their direct connection to the novel contributions introduced in this paper and their relevance to strengthening HiC’s explanatory power. First, we suggest deepening our understanding of the temporal evolution of delegation preferences and their interaction with contextual congruence. Specifically, this could take the form of exploring the conditions under which delegation preferences remain stable or shift significantly over time, and how contextual factors might accelerate, mitigate, or mediate these changes. Such research could reveal not only when and why preferences change, but also how the context, across active, latent, and organizational layers (Argote & Miron-Spektor, 2011), may exert competing pressures, either facilitating smooth adaptation to AI or generating persistent friction that disrupts personal control.

Second, the HiC model addresses Pathway B situations involving delegation deprivation where workers desire to delegate but are constrained by contextual barriers. Given its practical importance and theoretical novelty, significant opportunities exist for immediate empirical exploration. Researchers could identify contexts in which delegation deprivation commonly emerges, document workers’ specific cognitive and emotional responses, and empirically validate the mechanisms outlined by our model. Additionally, future research could examine the role of delegation deprivation in shaping broader organizational outcomes. For example, chronic delegation deprivation may prompt heightened organizational turnover or disengagement if workers feel persistently obstructed in their attempts to modernize or streamline tasks. Conversely, transient or short-term deprivation might stimulate constructive bottom-up championing, leading to eventual organizational innovation or process improvements once obstacles are addressed. By investigating delegation deprivation, future research could significantly advance theoretical understanding of adaptation processes in AI-driven work environments and clarify how unmet technological expectations affect workers, and how responses can either mitigate or exacerbate control disruptions.

Third, our HiC model does not explicitly explain the role of trust in shaping delegation preference or control processes. Yet a comprehensive understanding of control cannot be achieved without considering trust, as the two do not exist independently, are dialectically linked, and synthesize in specific delegation decisions and behaviors (Castelfranchi & Falcone, 2000; Jacovi et al., 2021). Exploring this dialectic would substantially enrich theoretical perspectives on human-AI interaction.

Each of the three directions offers opportunities to deepen theoretical understanding and provide rich insights into human-AI delegation. Because HiC offers a new perspective, our goal is not to constrain the development of these directions with narrowly defined research questions, but to open space for diverse lines of inquiry that can test, refine, and extend its relevance. This breadth is intended to stimulate theoretical pluralism and support the model’s evolution across a wide range of empirical and disciplinary contexts. ## 8 Conclusion

This study advances a human-centered understanding of AI delegation by framing personal control as an evolving process of adaptation. Through the HiC model, we offer a lens for exploring how individuals navigate the uncertainties of working with intelligent systems, not just through what they do but also through how they relate to their context and themselves. We hope this work sparks further inquiry into the complex, often subtle ways control is experienced, challenged, and reimagined in a world increasingly shaped by AI.

## About the Authors

Rania Afiouni is an assistant professor in the Department of Analytics, Operations, and Information Technology at the School of Management, Université du Québec à Montréal. This work was initiated during her doctoral studies at the Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University. Her research interests include the changing nature of work with AI and other emerging technologies, with a focus on delegation, worker adaptation and control, and job transformation. Her work has been presented at leading conferences, including the International Conference on Information Systems, the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, and the Academy of Management. Before joining academia, she was an IT consultant and an entrepreneur in IT services specializing in ERP solutions.

Alain Pinsonneault is a Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada, of the National Order of Québec, and of the Association for Information Systems. He is a Distinguished James McGill Professor and the Imasco Chair of Information Systems at the Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University. His current research interests include the organizational and individual impacts of information technology, user adaptation, and business models in the digital economy, e-health, and the business value of IT. His research has appeared in numerous journals, including Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, and Organization Science. He has served on the editorial boards of several journals, including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Organization Science, and Journal of Management Information Systems.
