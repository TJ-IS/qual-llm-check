# Contextualizing the twin concepts of systematicity and transparency in information systems literature reviews

Guy Pare´<sup>1</sup>, Mary Tate<sup>2</sup>, David Johnstone<sup>2</sup> and Spyros Kitsiou<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>HEC Montre´al, 3000 Chemin de la Cote-Ste-Catherine, Montreal, QC H3T 2A7, Canada; <sup>2</sup>Victoria University of Wellington, Wellington, New Zealand; <sup>3</sup>University of Illinois at Chicago, Chicago, IL, USA

Correspondence: Guy Pare´, HEC Montre´al, 3000 Chemin de la Cote-Ste-Catherine, Montreal, QC H3T 2A7, Canada. Tel: (514)340-6812; E-mail: guy.pare@hec.ca

## Abstract

Recently there has been a great deal of advice published for information systems researchers aiming to conduct standalone literature reviews, and this advice has been, at times, confusing, contradictory and contested. In this opinion paper, we harmonize and resolve some crucial elements of this debate. In our view, literature review articles need to adhere to the same high standards of quality and trustworthiness as other empirical studies. We argue that a systematic approach, accompanied by transparent reporting, is essential for positivist as well as interpretivist reviews, regardless of their specific type, scope and methods. In terms of structure, we first recap the main genres of review articles used by information systems scholars, and present a high-level framework of the steps required to develop a literature review article. For each step, we then explain how the twin concepts of systematicity and transparency should be understood and embedded in the process of developing review papers across a wide range of genres, including positivist aggregative reviews as well as interpretive syntheses using iterative, inductive and abductive approaches.

Keywords: literature review; systematicity; transparency; trustworthiness; rigour

## Introduction

The rapid growth of the information systems (IS) field in the past decades has created knowledge products (e.g. empirical findings, conceptual frameworks, taxonomies, theories, research models) at a rate far outpacing the capacity of individual scholars for recall, sense-making and use. In all likelihood, the IS knowledge base will continue to expand. The domain’s expansion translates into a body of knowledge that is increasingly diversified and fragmented, both in terms of its content and methods (e.g. Pentland, 1995; Ravichandran et al, 2005) as well as its research community (Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Klein, 2003; Hirschheim & Klein, 2006). The complicated state of IS research makes it difficult to know what we know as well as what we do not know, and makes reviewing previous research particularly challenging while simultaneously essential to further advances in knowledge.

This central issue has been recognized in a recent explosion of interest in editorials, essays and research articles investigating methods and approaches for conducting IS literature reviews. These have included conceptual contributions, which discuss the nature and characteristics of different types of review articles (e.g. Rowe, 2014; Pare´ et al, 2015; Okoli, 2015a), quality considerations (e.g. Bandara et al, 2015; Templier & Pare´, 2015) and methodological contributions, which range through groundedtheory approaches (e.g. Wolfswinkel et al, 2013), soft systems methods (e.g. Sylvester et al, 2013), stylized facts (e.g. Houy et al, 2015), critical discourse analysis (e.g. Wall et al, 2015), as well as detailed guidelines for specific steps associated with the review process (e.g. vom Brocke et al, 2015).

This explosion of interest in literature review methods has also engendered some debate. Recently, it was argued in an opinion paper that the increasing expectations for systematicity in literature reviews have been insufficiently challenged and posed a risk for the development of our discipline (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015a). A clear outcome of the debate generated by these authors is that there is currently widespread confusion with respect to the range of genres of literature synthesis articles and, most importantly, a lack of agreement about quality, terminology and characteristics, including the characteristics of systematicity and transparency that are the focus of this article.

In this line of thought, we would like to explain the particular contribution of this paper, and its position with regard to other recently published articles on related topics. Our main intent is to provide a simple, theoretically grounded, generalizable, yet flexible framework and set of properties that will assist researchers to improve the quality of their review articles. Our unifying framework will greatly assist the reader in sense-making and harmonizing other recently published advice. For example, many of the papers included in the recent CAIS special issue on the literature review ‘‘fit’’ into our framework at various points. The paper by vom Brocke et al (2015) elaborates on aspects of the initial steps in our framework, while the detailed tutorial by Schryen (2015) follows a broadly similar process to that described in our framework, but concentrates on qualitative literature reviews only, and offers detailed practical ‘‘how to’’ tutorial style guidance rather than a unifying theoretical and paradigmatic discussion. From the recent JIT debate papers, we pick up ‘‘calls to action’’ and resolve some critical areas of confusion.

In short, we posit there is a need to clarify what systematicity and transparency mean in the particular context of literature reviews, explain why systematicity (as a quality attribute) should be distinguished from the systematic literature review (SLR) type, illustrate how systematicity and transparency can be applied to different review genres and explain why producing trustworthy reviews is important for the future development of our discipline. In making this selection, we acknowledge that systematicity and transparency are not the only quality criteria which may be applied to literature synthesis articles. Quality is a rich and multi-faceted term which has been debated by scholars since the time of Plato. It can be conceptualized from many perspectives, including value, as a gestalt (I know it when I see it); meeting expectations, and conformance to specifications (Garvin, 1984, 1987; Reeves and Bednar, 1994). However, definitions and conceptualizations of quality are domain-specific: ‘‘The search for a universal definition of quality has yielded inconsistent results. Such a global definition does not exist; rather, different definitions of quality are appropriate under different circumstances’’ (Reeves and Bednar, 1994, p. 419). Therefore there is not a universal definition of quality we can apply. We clarify that it is not our intention to offer a complete conceptualization and definition of all the possible quality attributes and conceptualizations of literature review articles. We are not setting out to discuss ‘‘what does quality in literature synthesis really mean?’’ in its entirety. We have selected the twin attributes of systematicity and transparency, at this time, because they are both critically important and currently the subject of some confusion within our field.

Before we address the issues of systematicity and transparency in detail, the following section briefly describes the various genres of literature reviews that are most relevant to the IS community.

## Diversity of relevant literature review genres

Literature review approaches and methods, both within and outside IS, are undergoing a period of rapid devel opment (Gough et al, 2012; Tate et al, 2015). Indeed, there exist various families and types of reviews that differ considerably in terms of their primary motivations, scope, epistemological positions, means by which they are conducted and targeted audiences (Cooper, 1988; Rousseau et al, 2008; Pare´ et al, 2015). One way of classifying IS literature reviews was recently proposed by Rowe (2014). His classification distinguishes four broad genres of reviews according to their primary goal with respect to theory. First, some reviews aim at describing a phenomenon with little or no contribution to theory. These reviews summarize the extant literature on a particular topic of interest to provide the readers with a broad and comprehensive background for understanding the current state of knowledge in that area. In their typology, Pare´ et al (2015) distinguish three particular types of reviews under this genre, namely, narrative, descriptive and scoping reviews. While these types of review differ in terms of their coverage strategy and data synthesis approaches, they address broad research questions and the researchers’ voice is usually neutral.

Second, other reviews aim at understanding a particular phenomenon or problem through related concepts that have been investigated in prior research (Rowe, 2014). These reviews may provide a historical look at how particular research traditions (i.e. schools of thought) have unfolded over time and shaped the kind of questions being asked and the methods used to answer them. For instance, drawing on Kuhn’s (1962) notion of scientific paradigms, Greenhalgh et al (2005) proposed a review method, called meta-narrative, to synthesize, illuminate and clarify complex and heterogeneous empirical evidence. The approach relies on narrative-interpretive reasoning to produce ‘‘storied’’ accounts of key research traditions. Understanding may also refer to examining consistencies and inconsistencies (Booth et al, 2012) which may be accomplished with the help of various forms of literature reviews, including conceptual and critical reviews. For one thing, examining the structure and function of a concept is the purpose of concept analysis, which is a formal linguistic exercise to determine the defining attributes or basic elements of a given concept (Walker & Avant, 2010). It helps us to distinguish the likeliness and unlikeliness between concepts (e.g. Barki & Hartwick, 1989). For its part, a critical review primarily aims to analyse the extant literature on a particular phenomenon to reveal contradictions, controversies or inconsistencies (e.g. Sylvester et al, 2013; D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; Keutel et al, 2014). Reviews that seek to problematize a particular area of investigation by highlighting inconsistent theoretical assumptions (Schultze, 2015) can also be considered as a particular form of this type of review.

The third genre of reviews aims at testing theory by aggregating prior empirical findings. Aggregative synthesis, which is rooted in, and informed by, a positivist and quantitative tradition, can be defined as ‘‘a process for accumulating knowledge relevant to a given topic, question, or issue and for showing relationships among the pieces of knowledge and the question or issue’’ (Ward & Read, 1983, p. 11). The most prominent type of aggregative reviews, both within and outside IS, is the systematic review (King & He, 2005; Rowe, 2014). Systematic reviews can use narrative methods for the analysis and synthesis of study results (e.g. frameworks, groupings, clusters, tabulation and thematic analysis), vote counting techniques or meta-analytic methods. Vote counting tabulates the number of statistically significant studies supporting a theory or hypothesis and compares this with the number of studies that found non-significant results (Newbert et al, 2014). Meta-analysis involves the use of statistical methods and effect sizes to summarize and combine the results of independent studies. Other forms of aggregative reviews include systematic reviews of qualitative research and umbrella reviews (also known as overviews of systematic reviews).

The fourth genre of reviews proposed by Rowe (2014) is aimed at explaining. Explanatory reviews, also called theoretical reviews in Pare´ et al (2015) or configurative reviews in Gough et al (2012), bring together diverse streams of work and use various synthesis approaches and methods. Webster and Watson (2002) wrote an editorial in MIS Quarterly to encourage and guide IS scholars in conducting, structuring and writing theory development reviews. Their high-level coverage of the review process was recently supplemented by a detailed guide to developing IS theories with the systematic review methodology (Okoli, 2015b). Importantly, several interpretivist review approaches and methods aimed at theory building have also been proposed including meta-synthesis (Hoon, 2013), meta-ethnography (Noblit & Hare, 1988; Lee et al, 2015), grounded-theory (Wolfswinkel et al, 2013) and critical interpretive synthesis (Dixon-Woods et al, 2006). Here, synthesis ‘‘refers to the accumulation of primary evidence with the purpose to generate interpretive explanation rather than prediction’’ (Hoon, 2013, p. 526). These interpretive reviews usually employ the logic of abductive reasoning which ‘‘begins when reviewers intuit that a set of seemingly unrelated findings are in fact related and ends with models of these relationships that can themselves be formally tested’’ (Sandelowski et al, 2012, p. 326). This process captures the intuitive and sometimes inexpressible leaps of creativity and imagination that characterizes it.

Whereas we distinguish these four genres of reviews as having distinct motivations and goals, we concur with Okoli (2015a) that it is possible for a single review article to pursue multiple overarching objectives and, hence, to combine two or more review types or methods. Pare´ et al (2015) called these hybrid reviews and used, as an illustration, the review by Dube´ and Pare´ (2003) which critically appraised the level of methodological rigour in IS positivist case research (critical review) and identified trends and patterns in our collective use of this research method over a period of 10 years (descriptive review). In the following section, we explain how systematicity and transparency should be understood in the process of developing review articles.

## Systematicity and transparency in literature reviews

As explained above, literature reviews can play a significant role in advancing or disseminating knowledge, supporting evidence-based practice, developing new theories and shaping future research studies in our field. Nonetheless, just like any other research enterprise, when reviews are not designed and conducted rigourously using methodologically sound practices and techniques, they can contain biases, errors, misinterpretations or other major flaws that can impair their rigour and limit their usefulness for research and practice (Kitsiou et al, 2013; Templier & Pare´, 2015). For example, when the selection of studies in a review is not guided by consideration of clear eligibility criteria in relation to the conceptual definitions, theories and study designs to be included (e.g. experimental designs, case studies, grounded-theory studies, ethnographic studies), exclusion of relevant articles that otherwise should have been included in the sample can pose a serious problem and introduce selective bias in results and inferences (Petticrew & McCartney, 2011). Similarly, without the use of an organizing framework or a logical structure for the extraction of relevant concepts and characteristics from the included studies, there is a high risk of omitting, misclassifying or misrepresenting key information in the synthesis stage (Webster & Watson, 2002).

In recent years, the methodological quality of literature reviews has come under scrutiny, causing lively discussions regarding their trustworthiness. As mentioned earlier, the IS field has not escaped similar concerns and debates (e.g. Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015a, b; Chiasson, 2015; Schultze, 2015; Oates, 2015; Watson, 2015). We would like to highlight some key issues that motivate our present contribution. Among other things, the meaning of the term ‘‘systematic’’ and the extent to which it is captured fully by the SLR genre; the need for methodological flexibility in literature analysis; and the importance of improved quality overall in review papers were debated.

First, there remained throughout the JIT debate some conflation of the quality of systematicity, or being systematic, with the genre of the SLR, as a highly structured, inflexible and specific positivistic approach to literature review. Like Schultze (2015), we criticize Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic (2015a) for not sufficiently appreciating the fact that all literature review types, not just the SLR, should be systematic. In our opinion, systematicity must be conceived as a continuum, from low to high, rather than having a single, narrowly proscribed meaning. More precisely, we concur that (1) regardless of the type of review that is conducted, a systematic approach must be followed for the different steps of the review process (Schultze, 2015; Booth et al, 2012) and that (2) while not all reviews need to be equally systematic, they all require a minimum level of systematicity (Rowe, 2014; Booth et al, 2012).

Second, another aspect of the recent debate concentrated on the importance of methodological flexibility in the approach to conducting standalone literature reviews. Chiasson (2015) raised concerns about the danger of ‘‘a methodological checklist restricting the ability of the authors to pursue alternative means and ends’’ (p. 175), and Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic (2015b) argued that it was important to allow changes to ‘‘the prescribed protocol of SLR to become open to emerging understanding of the investigators, enabling them to further specify search terms’’ (p. 189). We entirely concur with the need for flexibility in one’s approach depending on the aims and genre of the literature synthesis being conducted. Our discussion explicates how this can be achieved in a way that is fully consistent with taking a systematic and transparent approach.

The third and final issue we would like to highlight from recent debate is a general call-to-action for ‘‘a broader discussion on literature review values and aims and the ways to achieve them’’ (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015b, p. 192), including raising both the efficiency and effectiveness of literature reviews (Watson, 2015). In essence, this call-to-action forms the motivation for our paper. We will explain systematicity and transparency as ‘‘review values’’ that can be flexibly applied by researchers to fit with their review goals and paradigmatic and methodological choices. We will show that they are (or should be) attributes shared by positivist and interpretivist literature reviews.

In an effort to further stimulate consideration of the abovementioned issues, we posit that trustworthiness for literature reviews is as crucial as in other forms of empirical studies. Therefore, it should be considered as a virtue that should govern many, if not all types of reviews, irrespective of their nature, scope and research objectives. Trustworthiness refers to the truth value (Lincoln & Guba, 1985) of a review article. Truth value asks whether the researchers have established confidence in the truth of their findings. As emphasized by Booth et al (2012), the fundamental question becomes How do we know that the authors’ conclusions are grounded in the data retrieved from the review process and not an argument fabricated to support a prior conclusion?

We believe the notion of trustworthiness deserves additional clarification in the context of the recent JIT debate. The nature of IS research is different to fields such as medical research, where the SLR originated. Medical researchers are typically asking relatively closed questions, and are interested in the trustworthiness of the results of a literature review. A trustworthy and transparent process must be assured in order to have confidence in the results, but the results are the major focus. By contrast, IS researchers often ask more open and contested questions. In this case, and especially with interpretivist reviews, it is not expected that an exact set of results will be reproducible. Nevertheless, the trustworthiness of the authors’ conclusions are founded in the description of the process used by the researchers to conduct their review.

Figure 1 proposes a framework which is based on the identification of two interrelated aspects of trustworthiness that we consider to be relevant to the abovementioned review genres. First, in quantitative reviews, as in meta-analyses, truth value is often assessed by how well threats to the internal validity of the review have been managed (Aytug et al, 2012). In qualitative reviews, as in meta-syntheses and meta-ethnographies, truth value can be defended on the grounds that the resulting analysis is demonstrably grounded in the available evidence, that is, that it is plausible or credible (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Dixon-Woods et al, 2006). To ensure internal validity or credibility, a review should therefore be well executed using fit-for-purpose methods. To achieve this, researchers must display a high level of systematicity in their approach and methods (Booth et al, 2012). The term ‘systematic’ is used here to qualify review articles, and not to classify them (Rowe, 2014; Schultze, 2015). When used as a qualifying adjective it refers to the fact that authors are being systematic in their approach to literature reviews, in a general sense as a perceived virtue, without following specific protocols as found in SLRs and metaanalyses. Precisely, systematicity is defined as a disposition towards organized, methodic, and orderly inquiry that uses various methods and processes to search, screen, assess, analyse and interpret relevant information with a view to achieving a set of specific research goals (Valanides & Charoula. 2008: Borko et al. 2007).

Second, the principal opportunity to evaluate the trustworthiness of a review lies predominantly in the adequacy of reporting (Oxman & Guyatt, 1991; Booth et al, 2012). Indeed, inadequate reporting of methods, procedures and techniques used in each step of a review represents a significant impediment to the quality of al review articles. For instance, a review may appear incomplete and unreliable because it fails to provide a clear and transparent account of the search strategy and information sources searched to identify relevant studies, the criteria used to include or exclude studies, or the procedures used to analyse studies (vom Brocke et al, 2015). Therefore, trustworthiness also requires reliability (positivist tradition) or consistency (interpretative tradition) to ensure reproducibility of the review process. Auditability of a review is only possible if the process is made explicit and transparent. In essence, transparency is defined as the completeness with which a review is presented and whether important methodological aspects about its design and execution are clearly or explicitly reported (Shea et al, 2009; Liberati et al, 2009). Hence, a review article should not appear as a ‘‘black box’ that leads the reader to make assumptions about ‘‘what was done’’ and ‘‘how’’ it was realized (Pare´ et al, 2015).

Many advocates of aggregative reviews emphasize reproducibility of findings as one of the ideals of a highquality literature review, and yet imply that such reviews are appropriate for interpretive research as well. We do not agree. Instead, we posit that, in standalone reviews, reproducibility must focus on the reproduction of the methods, and shall not guarantee an exact replica of results, given this is still a human-based activity, and the literature pool changes over time. Importantly, we argue that reproducibility of methods is relevant and important for positivist and interpretivist reviews because it contributes to the synthesis exercise in two important ways, namely, credibility and guidance. First, credibility is established through the use of explicit, complete statements about the methods used – making it clear that the methods not only sound reasonable when reported, but can, in fact, be replicated if required. Second, reproducibility of review methods also provides guidance with respect to further research. In the case of interpretive reviews, transparency can help teach other researchers how to go about similar research in other contexts with no thought of replication of results. For positivist reviews, transparency serves as a platform for an updated review on the same topic area some years later. Next, we explain and illustrate how the key notions of systematicity and transparency are embedded in various genres of review.

## Contextualizing systematicity and transparency in various review genres

While only a handful of review types possess prescribed and explicit methodologies, there is consensus among methodologists as to the generic steps involved in conducting a review project (e.g. Cooper, 2010; Booth et al, 2012; Aveyard, 2014). The six steps that comprise our framework (see Figure 2) reflect the main activities usually involved in developing and conducting a standalone literature review. These steps are (1) developing a review plan and protocol; (2) searching the extant literature; (3) selecting studies or screening; (4) appraising the quality of included studies, (5) extracting data and (6) analysing data and formulating conclusions. In practice, these stages are not as linear as implied and, given that much depends on the nature of the goals and research questions, the process may be highly iterative. While others argue that systematicity may be more important for particular stages of the review process and for particular review types (e.g. vom Brocke et al, 2009; Rowe, 2014; Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015a), we believe that both systematicity and transparency can be built into each step of the review process of all review genres. In this line of thought, Figure 2 presents a pluralistic approach that builds on the principles of systematicity and transparency to improve the trustworthiness of literature reviews in our field.

## Step 1: developing a review plan/protocol

It is a truism to say that scientific enquiry and many other human behaviours, are almost always preceded by some form of explicit or implicit plan or intention (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Therefore discussion of the first step in the process, ‘‘developing a review plan’’, - should not centre on whether such a step is necessary, but on the nature and characteristics of planning for different review families

The basic activities involved in this step are formulating a review question selecting the appropriate review type and method, and developing a review protocol. They are tightly linked and interdependent. Indeed, a good research question presupposes a sound understanding of the corpus of literature to be reviewed, and informs the choice of review genre and the selection of analysis methods, which in turn inform the characteristics of the plan.

Step 1 depends extensively on the authors’ knowledge and understanding of the body of knowledge in the area of interest. The characteristics of the previous studies determine what questions are relevant and what review methods can be considered. For example, the existence of a large number of previous quantitative studies might suggest an aggregative study using a meta-analytic review which, in turn, would suggest a more structured, linear approach to the review process. For its part, a large diversity in the extant literature, characterized by the use of theoretical different models and methodological approaches, might suggest a meta-narrative review to understand and describe the discourse across various research traditions. This would also imply that a more iterative approach to planning, to accommodate the emerging narratives, would be most appropriate. In all cases, however, we posit that the planning process needs to be conducted in a systematic manner, and clearly articulated and reported to provide transparency.

While all review plans require a systematic and transparent approach there are a number of different ways in which this planning can be conducted. We suggest the review planning approach falls somewhere on a continuum between highly linear and highly iterative, as shown in Figure 3. At one end of the continuum is the highly detailed, sequential and a priori plan (as found in metaanalyses), while at the other end is the semi-structured, emergent and highly iterative plan (as found in critical interpretive syntheses). Most review plans will fall somewhere in between. We note also that planning is not confined only to the beginning of the review. Even in sequential approaches, mini-plans for specific tasks and activities may be developed throughout the review process, while highly iterative approaches may involve a great deal of re-planning almost continuously throughout the life of the review project (Booth et al, 2012).

If we consider the specific activities involved in step 1, one of the main requirements is to formulate explicit research goals and to link these to the research questions. For one thing, we argue that systematicity and transparency require clearly articulated and justified review objectives, even if these goals have emerged or iterated during the course of the research. Importantly, since a literature synthesis is a type of research activity, the extensive advice aimed at empirical researchers about the importance of a good research question also applies to review efforts. The research questions are critical to any form of scientific investigation. They help provide focus and militate against undisciplined data collection and analysis (Booth et al, 2012). This is the case even if the research question is a ‘‘working question’’ that will require revision. Even inductive and grounded studies need to start somewhere. Wolfswinkel et al (2013), in their guidelines for conducting literature review using grounded-theory, note that ‘‘the specific research questions of a review are likely to only become genuinely clear and precise in successive stages of the review process. Starting a review with a well-defined research problem allows a researcher to progressively refine its scope into intriguing yet ‘answerable’ research questions for the review’’ (p. 47).

The initial phase mainly involves determining the ‘‘who what, and how’’ of a review (Booth et al, 2012). In some cases, such as in aggregative reviews, it may involve the development of a detailed review protocol, including detailed planning for the sequential conduct of subsequent steps such as search strategy, data extraction, coding and analysis. A detailed a priori protocol can be an important means of protection against researcher bias (Moher et al, 2015; Higgins & Green, 2008). In other cases, such as reviews aimed at explaining, it is generally considered unacceptable to plan the literature review activities closely. For instance, realist reviews, which provide an explanatory analysis aimed at discerning what works for whom, in what circumstances, in what respects and how, cannot use a protocol-driven approach. These reviews are ‘‘more about principles that guide than rules that regularize’’ (Pawson et al, 2005: 32). This is not to say that rigour is not an equally important consideration with such reviews. However, the processes of quality assurance are more dependent on explicitness and reflexivity on the part of the researchers undertaking the review.

We are aware that some review methodologists consider protocol development as a separate step from planning (e.g. Kitchenham & Charters, 2007). We have chosen not to separate the two. Let us briefly explain why. The notion of a review protocol is sometimes demonized as something that is developed a priori and not changed, that has the effect of ‘‘regulating’’ the researcher and requiring them to follow an inflexible ‘‘set of rules’’ (Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015b, p. 190). In this sense, it is an optional step which is only applicable to certain review types such as SLRs and meta-analyses. This is not the view adopted here. Instead, we conceive the review protocol as a ‘‘living plan’’. When viewed this way, all literature review projects shall have a protocol, but depending on the aim and genre of the review there will be differences in how early in the project the protocol is finalized, how flexible it is, and how frequently it is revised. Hence, we consider protocol development in a more iterative approach to literature review, as the first draft of the review plan and as a living document. As the review progresses, it becomes a work log that records how the research project had evolved. Researchers may deviate from their original plan, but the protocol enables them to do so explicitly and transparently; changes are consciously made and documented. In short, this concept of a ‘‘living protocol’’ and evolving plan is fully consistent with our recommendations for planning iterative review projects, and assures the transparency of the process as the review develops.

To conclude, we posit there is no conflict between inductive or emergent approaches and systematicity and transparency, and we find it telling that the majority of IS authors who have presented inductive synthesis methods (e.g. Wolfswinkel et al’s (2013) grounded-theory approach, Sylvester et al’s (2013) meta-narrative and Wall et al’s (2015) critical discourse analysis) have presented detailed and clearly articulated plans for using their respective method. They have often also included descriptions of how they managed iteration in the planning process to ensure full transparency. The decision about how early in the planning process to make detailed decisions about subsequent phases rests with the research team, depends on many factors, and can follow many processes from highly structured to highly inductive and iterative. In all of these processes, systematic planning and transparent documentation of the research team’s decisions (including iterations and revisions) are essential.

## Step 2: searching the literature

The methods and analytic processes that researchers use to search databases and other sources identify relevant articles, and make decisions about their suitability constitute elements of paramount importance that are unique to the scholarship of literature reviews and hold great implications for their internal validity (Cooper, 1988). Searches can be complicated and iterative, particularly when reviewers are investigating complex bodies of evidence, emerging phenomena and concepts, or when they are searching multiple databases with different structures and user interfaces (Greenhalgh et al, 2005; vom Brocke et al, 2015). Hence, they require careful consideration, planning and high degree of systematicity to ensure consistency, efficiency and elimination of potential errors (Aveyard, 2014). Systematicity in the context of literature searching refers to the use of a rigourous and clearly articulated ‘‘search strategy’’ (Hart, 2001) that describes the stages, activities and overall anatomy of the search process, and should not be confused with the exhaustiveness or comprehensiveness of the search that aggregative reviews must exercise for the identification of relevant sources.

As shown in Figure 2 search activities involve defining search terms, choosing appropriate databases and information sources to search for relevant articles (e.g. conference proceedings, journals and registries); developing advanced search strategies to translate the research question into a format that the search engine of each database can understand and developing adjunct search methods to identify articles that may not be captured by electronic searches (e.g. manual searches of journals, forward and backward searches of references and communications with experts). Systematicity also requires establishing tasks, specific roles and analytic processes that define who conducts the searches, at what point in time, and how references are retrieved and managed.

A transparent search strategy that aligns with the research questions of the review is a fundamenta requirement for all types of reviews regardless of their scope of inquiry, the period covered or the coverage strategy (Cooper, 1988; Hart, 2001; vom Brocke et al, 2015). When planning a review, researchers should consider the type of literature search and degree of coverage that is appropriate for the review in question (e.g. comprehensive, selective or representative), taking into account budgetary and time constraints. Assessments should be based on preliminary searches or scoping exercises with repeated iterations, existing reviews and advice from other researchers with expertise in the topic of interest (Greenhalgh et al, 2005; Arksey & O’Malley, 2005). Drawing on different search methods and databases to access a variety of sources ensures that most relevant articles are retrieved for a high-quality literature review (Conn et al, 2003; Greenhalgh & Peacock, 2005). In short, building an effective search strategy is essential for all types of reviews.

Depending on whether the review process is organized in a linear manner or is highly iterative, the aforementioned activities will be designed and executed differently. As vom Brocke et al (2015) maintain, sequential literature search approaches are predominantly used in aggregative reviews, and will use a step-by-step process. For their part, literature reviews that aim at understanding or explaining (Rowe, 2014) may require an ‘‘iterative search approach’’ during which researchers engage in exploratory literature searches to learn more about the themes of the topic of interest, refine and extend the initial search strategy to collect additional related works, and then repeat the process until saturation is reached (Greenhalgh et al, 2005). IS scholars have recently proposed structured approaches that actively advocate iterative, emergent approaches to searching without compromising systematicity and transparency. For instance, Sylvester et al (2013) utilized a soft systems approach and rich pictures to represent research literature, and advocate for a systematic and transparent (but iterative) approach to searching the literature.

In short, whether reviews encompass an a priori search strategy or a highly iterative search process, we posit that high-quality IS reviews should include sufficient details about how studies were searched. This can establish a transparent process and allow readers to judge if the methods used and decisions made were appropriate.

## Step 3: selecting studies

This step seeks to refine the pool of relevant studies by way of a screening process focused more on the content of the published studies. Both steps 2 and 3 can be seen as a collective attempt to achieve an optimal set of articles, ready for analysis – ‘optimal’ in the sense that the set includes only articles that will contribute to the review outcome, and excludes all articles that will not. This explains why some authors combine the two as a single step (e.g. Cooper & Hedges, 2009; Bandara et al, 2015; Schryen, 2015).

Essentially screening involves defining a set of inclusion and exclusion criteria in terms of specific aspects of the content. For example, Kitchenham and Charters (2007) argue these criteria should be applied to each study’s stated research questions to establish applicability to the review objectives. Okoli (2015a) provides a broad list of criteria, including methodology type, participant type and empirical data type. Intuitively, one might ask if inclusion criteria are sufficient for the screening process – either an article meets the criteria (and is in) or it does not (and is out). However, from a transparency perspective, this only provides half the ‘story’. We have not characterized the types of extant literature that fail to meet the inclusion criteria, nor is it clear why they have failed. Exclusion criteria addresses these issues explicitly. Ideally, the combination of inclusion and exclusion criteria should embrace the majority of the literature base identified in the preceding step, with only scattered outliers falling outside both sets of criteria.

Screening is necessary for practical reasons particularly where review objectives require a representative or comprehensive coverage strategy, as is the case with scoping, descriptive and aggregative reviews (Pare´ et al, 2015). If this step were to be omitted, the screening processes would be subsumed into the following steps, and the outcome could well be much the same. Cheung and Thadani (2012), for example, systematically excluded articles during the data extraction phase (step 5). However, the detailed analysis and synthesis involved in the data extraction and data synthesis steps can be extensive, painstaking and time-consuming. Therefore, limited time and resources provide a strong incentive to work towards that optimal set of articles before extraction and synthesis take place. Some screening processes may be determined prior to the start of step 3 (e.g. Turner et al, 2010), while others may emerge once it is under way (e.g. Daudt et al, 2013, Arksey & O’Malley, 2005). Some processes may be refined and then reapplied. Other review types, requiring less comprehensive approaches, will focus on a selective collection of studies (Pare´ et al, 2015).

Systematicity plays an important role in the screening process for three main reasons. First, each article in the pool from step 2 must be subject to the same screening process to ensure there is consistency in terms of what is included in, and excluded from, the final set of articles. Second, even if the review objective involves a less comprehensive, more selective approach, as can occur with narrative and critical reviews, there will be some kind of explicit criteria that help determine the final pool of studies to emerge from this step. Third, any variation risks selection bias by wrongly including or excluding a particular study. Systematic screening encourages consistency across candidate articles. For its part, transparency involves clear, comprehensive statements about the screening methods, with a focus on auditability and reproducibility, both internal and external. External reproducibility refers to the need to establish credibility through explicit, complete statements about the methods used while internal reproducibility refers to the need for consistency of method application across researchers within a team involved in the screening process. Transparency of methods and approaches is necessary to facilitate this consistency.

## Step 4: assessing quality of the studies

Once all eligible studies for inclusion have been identified, review authors may need to formally assess the internal and external validity of each study, as well as the overall quality of research evidence included in the review to guide the subsequent stages. This two-step process, known as quality assessment, represents a complex, yet important practice that has emerged over the years as a recommended guideline mainly for aggregative reviews (e.g. systematic reviews, meta-analyses and umbrella reviews). Aggregative reviews, which aim to support evidence-based decision making, must assess and take into consideration in the interpretation of results the internal validity of the included studies (Higgins & Altman, 2008). Otherwise, review findings may be distorted by studies at high risk of bias. For example, metaanalysis of results from studies that contain systematic errors can result in erroneous conclusions. However, for other types of reviews that aim to map out, describe or categorize the existing literature and identify gaps, patterns or trends (e.g. scoping reviews, narrative reviews, critical reviews), quality assessment in its strict sense is not essential or a key requirement.

The notion of quality assessment involves the use of validated and structured appraisal methods for determining whether any of the individual studies included in the sample has been affected by significant bias and confound and the extent to which these have an impact on the study results and overall body of evidence (Petticrew & Roberts, 2006; Oates, 2011; Kitchenham et al, 2009; Kitsiou et al, 2013). When the main goal of a review is theory testing, it is critical to identify all potential flaws and risks of bias in each study, in order to first determine their impact on the internal validity of the studies, and second their implications for the findings of the literature review as a whole (Booth et al, 2012). In the health sciences, these two steps represent critical and distinct components in the quality assessment process, which are generally known as risk of bias and quality of evidence assessments.

Risk of bias focuses on the internal validity of the primary studies (Petticrew & Roberts, 2006). It attempts to address the question of whether each study included in the review answers its research question in a reliable manner free from bias (i.e. systematic errors). As mentioned earlier, different studies employing different research designs (e.g. experimental or observational) and methods (e.g. quantitative or qualitative) may be susceptible to or contain different types of bias, depending on how they are designed and conducted. Risk of bias assessment involves the use of validated criteria or quality appraisal instruments for systematically evaluating key aspects about the design, execution and analysis of each study to determine the degree to which results may have been influenced by bias or methodological flaws (Booth et al. 2012: Petticrew & Roberts. 2006). For example, it might involve the use of criteria that bring into question the recruiting and sampling techniques of a study, the allocation of participants into groups, the balance of characteristics between compared groups or respondents and non-respondents, the methods used to measure constructs or outcomes, as well as the validity and reliability of constructs (Deeks et al, 2003). However, it is important to note that the variety of study designs and varying susceptibilities of each design to different biases, makes it difficult to find or even produce a generic and robust instrument that can be used to assess risk of bias across all types of studies (e.g. empirical, nonempirical and qualitative). Therefore, depending on the type of primary studies to be included in the review, the authors need to consider at the planning stage the criteria or instruments to be used to assess quality. For example, systematic reviews that plan to include experimental and observational or qualitative studies (e.g. cohort or case control studies) should use different instruments specific to each type of research design. There exist a plethora of instruments within the social and health care sciences which authors can review and choose from (e.g. Higgins & Altman, 2008; Deeks et al, 2003; Long & Godfrey, 2004). Also, there are several methodological papers (e.g. Jarvenpaa et al, 1985; Straub, 1989; Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 1993; Klein & Myers, 1999; Dube´ & Pare´, 2003; Straub et al, 2004; Sivo et al, 2006; Petter et al, 2007; Pare´ et al, 2013) that can be used as a starting point to develop quality appraisal instruments that would suit the needs and particularities of our field as well as those of the broader social sciences.

Methodologists suggest that the process of critical appraisal should be carried out independently by more than one reviewer using standardized methods to minimize subjectivity and eliminate the possibility for errors or omissions (e.g. Higgins & Altman, 2008; Petticrew & Roberts, 2006). This is rather different from simply reading a study closely to see if it is ‘‘good enough’’ or from using a ‘‘hierarchy of evidence’’ system to rank studies according to their study design, as Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic (2015a) maintain. ‘‘Hierarchy of evidence’’ systems, such as the one from the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine which ranks studies according to the relative strengths and precision of their methods, were established in the health sciences to aid researchers and practitioners determine study appropriateness when attempting to answer a research question, conducting an empirical research study or a systematic review, making a decision or looking for the best available evidence in a specific area (Petticrew & Roberts, 2006). These ranking systems should not be confused with the methods used for appraising the quality of empirical studies, nor should they be used as a substitute to assessing the internal validity of the studies. Although some study designs might be better suited than others to answer a research question, we concur with Booth et al (2012) that this should not give researchers the false impression that a poorly conducted experimental study may provide better evidence than a well-conducted observational study. Importantly, critical appraisal in literature reviews should not be confused with journal rankings, journal impact factors, number of article citations or other external criteria not directly associated with the internal validity of the included studies. In short, risk of bias assessment is a highly structured and formal process that requires researchers to first select (or develop when not available) an appropriate quality appraisal instrument or set of criteria; establish procedures on how critical appraisal of the primary studies will occur, how disagreements between assessors will be resolved (i.e. a consensus process should be in place) and how the results from this process will be reported in the review article. Systematicity in the selection and application of quality appraisal criteria and transparency with regard to judgements made about each study are cornerstones in this process.

The second type of assessment called quality of evidence, aims to take an overall look at the body of research evidence to determine the degree to which one can be confident that an estimate of effect or association is close to the quantity of specific interest i.e. near the ‘‘true’’ value (Guyatt et al, 2011). Such assessment occurs once the results from all primary studies have been synthesized. To assess the overall quality of the research evidence included in the review, by outcome of interest, researchers must explore and take into consideration various factors that may have an impact on results and inferences. These factors include (a) risks of bias in the research studies (i.e. internal validity issues), (b) inconsistencies between the included studies in results or inferences (i.e. statistical heterogeneity between estimates of effect or associations), (c) indirectness/external validity (i.e. generalizability or applicability of the results), (d) imprecision (i.e. impact of random error in the studies due to small sample sizes) and (e) publication bias (i.e. publication of research findings depending on the direction and statistical significance of results).

In sum, the methods used in performing both types of quality assessment should be carefully planned, executed and reported in aggregative reviews. For both types of assessment, it is recommended that more than one reviewer applies the criteria independently. The process followed along with how disagreements between assessors were resolved should clearly be stated. The instruments or criteria employed should also be described or referenced, with an explanation of how these assessments are incorporated into the synthesis and interpretation of the results. Although other genres of literature reviews might also benefit from assessing the quality of the included studies and the body of research evidence as a whole, quality appraisal for these reviews is not as essential as it is for aggregative reviews. For instance, realist reviews, which seek to explore complex areas of reality, reject a hierarchical approach because multiple research methods are needed to illuminate the richer picture (Greenhalgh et al, 2005). A reason why the use of quality checklists is not the answer to the complex challenge of realist review is because the primary study is rarely the appropriate unit of analysis. Indeed, realist reviews often chose only one element of a primary study in order to test a very specific hypothesis about the link between context, mechanism and outcome. The solution to quality appraisal is therefore ‘‘to cut directly to the judgement’’ (Greenhalgh et al, 2005:30) and consider both relevance (whether the study addresses the topic of interest) and rigour (whether a particular inference has sufficient weight to make a methodologically credible contribution to the test of the theory) as two dimensions of fitness for purpose for a particular realist review. In a similar fashion, proponents of critical interpretive syntheses (Dixon-Woods et al, 2006) reject the quality appraisal methods used in conventional aggregative reviews and prioritize primary studies that appear relevant rather than particular study types that meet particular methodological standards. In doing so, interpretative syntheses therefore prioritize ‘‘signal’’ (relevance) over ‘‘noise’’ (the inverse of methodological quality) (Edwards et al, 2000).

## Step 5: extracting data

While steps 2 and 3 above focused on identifying primary studies that are likely to be relevant to the review objective and step 4 established their value in terms of quality, influencing their relative contribution towards this objective, step 5 comprises the identification and extraction of relevant data that will be used for analysis and synthesis. It should be noted that some published guidelines may combine steps 3 through 5 in different ways (e.g. Kitchenham & Charters, 2007; Bandara et al, 2015; Schryen, 2015). For instance, Booth et al (2012) suggest data extraction could include eligibility data (based on inclusion/exclusion criteria) and quality assessment data, thus integrating steps 3 and 4 with step 5. Because the steps can be quite iterative in nature (see Figure 3), these variations in method guidelines should mostly be seen as a labelling issue, rather than a method issue.

There are three main aspects to data extraction in literature reviews: planning the extraction process identifying and extracting the data, and organizing the data in preparation for analysis. First, the planning process comprises a data identification plan that helps systematize how the researchers will recognize relevant data in the text, and a data extraction plan will help them target which parts of the text will provide relevant data. It is helpful to place the level of planning detail on a continuum, based on the review type (Gough et al, 2012). Narrative reviews, for example, often involve very little planning, based on the idea that the researchers will recognize good data when they see it. At the other end of the continuum are those review types with narrowly defined research questions, where the constructs, and the relationships between them, are well-defined and can be readily identified and extracted, as they normally occur with descriptive and aggregative reviews. These types of reviews allow for data identification plans to be established at the start of this process, possibly represented as a coding structure (for qualitative analysis) or as a frequency table (for quantitative analysis). For those reviews that fall in between these extremes, such as metanarrative and scoping reviews, data identification and extraction plans may evolve as the relationship between the review objective and the literature base is clarified through iterative examination. Prior engagement with the literature, in combination with concepts drawn from the research questions and knowledge gleaned over steps 1 through 4, will ensure there is sufficient understanding to provide at least an initial, albeit simplified and/or high-level, plan for iterative approaches.

Second the process of identifying and extracting data operationalizes the corresponding plans. As a general rule, the more explicit and comprehensive the plans are, the more efficient and less iterative the identification and extraction processes need to be. This is particularly important where grounded-theory approaches, requiring open coding, are concerned, as redefining coding structures, and consequently recoding the data, can be timeconsuming and labour-intensive (Wolfswinkel et al, 2013). Data extraction forms can be particularly useful where a range of data items need to be collected from each study and where there are many studies in the selection pool. For example, data extraction forms may be used to collect descriptive data (information about the characteristics of the study) and results (information about the outcomes of the study).

Third, the extracted data need to be organized in preparation for analysis. Where quantitative analysis is employed, as is the case with meta-analysis and descriptive reviews, the data are typically organized into frequency tables and forest plots within spreadsheets or statistical packages. For its part, qualitative analysis requires data to be stored in a structured format such as tables, networks or repositories based on coding structures such as those provided by computerized tools like NVivo. Bandara et al (2015) provide an extensive set of guidelines on the design of coding structures and the use of tools to analyse qualitative data.

In sum where plans are developed for data identification and extraction, whether a priori or emergent, they will be used systematically in practice, and as has been noted in previous steps, these plans need to be transparent for internal and external reproducibility. Internal reproducibility becomes a critical issue where grounded approaches are used, as interpretive exercises like coding can be notoriously inconsistent, even when employing the same data extraction forms. Extra safeguards, such as having at least two members of the research team code the same subset of studies for discrepancy checking and follow-up discussions, may be necessary to achieve reliability or consistency.

## Step 6: synthesizing data

The final step sees the organized data transformed through a synthesis stage. Again there are issues with terminology. Some authors of review guidelines refer to this step simply as ‘synthesis’ (e.g. Rousseau et al, 2008; Schryen, 2015), while others use the term ‘analysis’ (e.g. Bandara et al, 2015). Cooper & Hedges (2009) prefer to use the word synthesis more broadly, encompassing the entire research review process, with the goal ‘‘to integrate empirical research for the purpose of creating generalizations’’ (p. 6). However, there is a sense that the term synthesis involves bringing a larger set of raw, organized data together into a compact, integrated, representative and simpler set of data that form the basis for an analytical evaluation that will address the review objective. Thus, ‘‘the synthesis stage aims to tell you what the literature says. The subsequent analysis stage focuses on telling you what the literature means’’ (Booth et al, 2012, p. 170).

Booth et al (2012) state data synthesis is characterized in three main forms: quantitative, qualitative and integrative (mixed). They warn that the type of extracted data does not necessarily imply what types of synthesis might work best. For example, quantitative data, typically organized in frequency tables, can be synthesized statistically if the primary studies’ outcomes are homogeneous in format (as in meta-analyses and umbrella reviews). However, if the primary studies are heterogeneous, then a qualitative approach to synthesis, incorporating content analysis or thematic analysis, may be all that is possible (qualitative systematic reviews).

When the extracted data are qualitative in nature there are a range of approaches to synthesis available, unsurprisingly most of them qualitative as well. For example, content analysis and thematic analysis approaches may be sufficient (scoping reviews), though more interpretive methods may also be employed in order to build theory (theoretical reviews), evaluate the literature (critical reviews) or simply discuss the nature of the literature (narrative reviews). However, qualitative data can also be subjected to quantitative synthesis through the use of techniques such as comparative case analysis proposed by Ragin (1987). A good illustration of this method in our field can be found in Rivard and Lapointe (2012). Another method of data transformation is called metasummary of qualitative findings (Sandelowski & Barroso, 2003).

The degree to which plans for synthesis whether a priori or emergent, are clearly delineated will depend on the review type, and the continuum developed in Step 5 can be applied here as well. Once again, narrative synthesis, condensing the extracted data into a ‘story’, occupies one end of the spectrum, while statistical synthesis occupies the other. Again, the more clearly delineated the plan, the more straightforwardly transparent the reporting.

In short, analysing data ‘‘provides [the reader] with an opportunity to assess the strength of evidence for drawing conclusions about the results of synthesis and their generalisability in your extended context’’ (Booth et al, 2012, p. 170). Quantitative synthesized data lend itself to statistical evaluation and hypothesis testing. Planning for, and reporting on, such a systematic process should be relatively straightforward. However, qualitative synthesized data may require a range of techniques to both evaluate the evidence and establish its implications in terms of addressing the research questions. Dixon-Woods et al (2006) argue that this is the time to critically appraise the body of literature under scrutiny, involving a creative process of reflective and interpretive analysis. They also mention that this process is more difficult to express in a written form; yet the more one writes about this process, the more likely the results will be better understood.

## A note on writing the review article

Although writing the review is sometimes seen as the distinct step, we consider it to be a process that is performed in parallel to the review process per se. Figure 2, which was developed with this notion in mind, demonstrates how transparency in terms of reporting is explicitly executed at each step of the review process. We believe that introducing another step would simply replicate the instantiations of transparency already presented in steps 1 through 6. Having said that, it is beyond the scope of this paper to provide a set of detailed guidelines about how to write a review article. Prospective IS authors are invited to consult various relevant sources that specifically address writing issues (e.g. Baker, 2000; Torraco, 2005; Short, 2009; Baumeister, 2013). Below, we briefly discuss some of the main writing challenges facing review authors.

One challenge is to decide upon an appropriate structure and method for objectively and transparently presenting the main review findings, while also taking into consideration the intended audience and space restrictions of the targeted publication outlet (Booth et al, 2012). The intended audience and publication medium will determine the format of the review, as well as the organization of the material and tone of the accompanying narrative. The length of literature reviews often varies depending on the scope of inquiry and number of included studies. Word limits or other restrictions imposed by journals or other publication venues can have an impact on the reporting quality of a review and therefore, must be considered in advance. Review authors must also identify the primary audience before commencing with the writing, taking into account the audience’s prior states of beliefs and particular needs. Potentially interested audiences might include researchers, practitioners, funding agencies, policy makers or general public.

The structure and methods for organizing and writing the review will also depend on the type of review and its main purpose (Rowe, 2014). Increasing recognition of the importance of transparency and replicability in scientific research has led, in recent years, to the publication of guidelines and templates for several review types, which can be found at the EQUATOR Network Resources Website (http://www.equator-network.org/). For example, qualitative systematic reviews and meta-analyses must adhere to explicit reporting standards described in the PRISMA statement (Liberati et al, 2009; Moher et al, 2015), which is now endorsed by many journals (e.g. Journal of the American Medical Informatics

Association; Journal of Medical Internet Research) and organizations (e.g. Centre for Reviews and Dissemination, Cochrane Collaboration). Growing interest in realist synthesis led to the publication of the REMESES standard with the intent to increase the systematicity and transparency of this particular type of review (Wong et al, 2013). As another example, Webster and Watson (2002) provided general guidance about the writing of IS reviews aiming at theory development. It is worth adding that prospective review authors can also refer to the TAIMRAD (Title-Abstract-Introduction-Methods-Results-Discussion) elements included in the PRISMA and REMESES statements. Key writing guidelines associated with each section of a review article (TAIMRAD) are presented as supplementary material in an appendix.

## Concluding remarks

It has long been suggested that ‘‘the goal of any empirica science is to pursue the construction of a cumulative base of knowledge upon which the future of the science may be built’’ (Curran, 2009:77). From this point of view, knowledge not only stems from a revolutionary process (Kuhn, 1962) but can also emerge from a backward oriented integration of previous evidence (Campbell, 1974). For this reason, we argue that the four genres of literature reviews discussed herein represent an extraordinary enablement to facilitate that journey in our discipline. The recent, yet growing interest in reviews as a legitimate approach to investigate relevant IS research questions must therefore be interpreted as a positive sign. In this paper, we argue that trustworthiness be considered as a virtue that should govern review articles, irrespective of their particular goals, scope and methods. More precisely, there is increasing recognition that most, if not all review forms should be planned and conducted with the twin aspects of quality of conduct (i.e. systematicity) and quality of reporting (i.e. transparency) in mind. A case in point is meta-ethnography which, as originally conceived by Noblit and Hare (1988), resisted making the process of data analysis explicit (Campbell et al, 2011). However, the growth in the application of this interpretative method has shown that ‘‘potentially valuable lessons can be learnt from better understanding the practical actions and reasoning researchers employ in order to realize the methodology’’ (Lee et al, 2015, p. 337). Another revealing illustration is provided by Wolfswinkel et al (2013) who argue in favour of transparency about the choices made during the entire grounded-theory reviewing process. Hence, we concur with Tate et al (2015) that ‘‘the days of the ‘naı¨ve narrative synthesis review- with no explicit attention to purpose, world-view, quality, context, hegemonic assumptions, characteristics of inclusion and exclusion, characteristics of the source literature, and methods of analysis- should be over’’ (p. 5). Like many others, we posit that more systematic and transparent reviews hold the potential to possess more validity/credibility and to manifest improved reproducibility of methods.

Our recommendations apply to primarily positivist and interpretivist reviews, as these are the dominant paradigms in our field. However, although not fully explored, we argue there are no inherent contradictions between the principles we advocate, and those applying to literature analysis articles using other paradigms, such as critical (Chua, 1986) and critical realist (Mingers et al, 2013) perspectives. For example, in a critical realist literature analysis that presented excerpts from peerreviewed articles as evidence, and then made an abductive argument to propose the ‘‘best explanation’’, it would still be incumbent on the authors to establish that the body of evidence they presented was significant, representative, accurately represented and not ‘‘cherry picked’’. Similarly, a critical review would need to establish the extent and seriousness of the issue posited by the authors. By way of example, consider the critical paper by Vickers (1999) (it is not presented as a literature analysis although it draws extensively on quotes from peer-reviewed articles as evidence) which critiques ‘‘the terminological and methodological capture of IT by specialists [which] continues through patriarchal and coercive structures that have remained despite the continuing failure of current and past methodologies to deliver’’. We suggest that critical arguments of this nature would carry maximum weight if the authors could demonstrate a systematic approach in their search for evidence.

In a similar line, we have focussed primarily on ‘‘conventional’’ empirical research articles as the ‘‘data’’ which should be included in a literature review. Once again, depending on the nature and purpose of the review, there is no reason why different bodies of literature should not be selected and synthesized. For example, papers by design scientists may represent situations as problem and solution statements (March & Smith, 1995). It could be argued that the descriptions of the problematic state and the desired state are models of tentative theories that can be evaluated for inclusion or exclusion in a literature review. Our framework is agnostic as to the corpus of literature that may be included. This is one of a number of areas that would deserve attention in future work. Developing a comprehensive conceptualization of quality in review articles is another.

To conclude, we hope that our efforts to harmonize and clarify what the notions of systematicity and transparency mean along with some illustrations will contribute to the publication of more trustworthy reviews, both within and outside our field
