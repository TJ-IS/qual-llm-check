## Abstract

There is a plethora of theories to explore the disciplines of business, management and sociology, with institutional theory being widely used to explore a range of research challenges. In the area of Information Systems (IS), the use of institutional theory remains in its infancy, with much potential for adoption. Much of the rationale underpinning the proposed research is that a systematic review and synthesis of the normative literature may support the direction of further research and the use of institutional theory in exploring pertinent research challenges facing the IS community. This study also serves to signpost cross-disciplinary research, and thus opens up a whole new research paradigm. Therefore, this article seeks to provide a bibliometric analysis and a comprehensive and systematic review of the literature pertaining to institutional theory to ascertain the current ‘state of play’ of the theory. Information on a series of variables was extracted after conducting a review of 511 articles across various disciplines that have utilised institutional theory, published in 210 peer-reviewed journals between 1978 and 2008. The findings suggest that the positivist paradigm, empirical and quantitative research, the survey method and organisation/firm as a unit of analysis was used predominantly in combination with institutional theory. The results of this study may have implications for researchers, journal editors, reviewers and universities.

## Introduction

n today’s dynamic and consumer-driven market, organisations need to respond continuously to changing customer needs. However, the manner in which organisations respond to these changes is often dependent on the socio-political, economic and technological influences exerted by the environment in which they operate. Institutional theory has been used by many researchers for well over three decades to study the impact of these external forces on organisational behaviour. An institution is typically bounded by a set of social, political, economic and legal contexts that ascertain the basis for production, exchange and distribution of goods and services (Davis and North, 1971). These institutional environments are characterised by the elaboration of rules and requirements to which individual organisations must conform if they are to receive acceptance and legitimacy (Scott, 1995; Lawrence et al., 2002). As King et al. (1994) posit that an institutional environment is a social system that exerts influence and regulation over other social entities as a persistent feature of social life. Therefore, for organisations to endure, they must conform to the rules and belief systems prevailing in the environment in which they exist (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Scott, 1995). Organisations in different socio-economic and political contexts may often react differently to similar internal and external challenges due to constraints imposed by the environment they exist in. Conversely, the socio-economic and political contexts shape the institutional structure of a particular organisation, thus presenting it with an advantage or disadvantage for engaging in particular business activities.

The ‘institutional perspective’ has been used for some time by various researchers (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Zucker, 1987) to explain why certain organisational structures and ideals endure. In particular, the last two decades have seen an augmented use of institutional theory in social sciences (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; Zucker, 1977, 1987), political sciences (March and Olsen, 1989; North, 1990; Miranda and Kim, 2006) and, to a lesser degree, information systems and technology (IS/IT) research (Teo et al., 2003; Currie, 2004; Currie and Guah, 2007; Lian et al., 2007).

The relevance of institutional theory is particularly notable in the context of understanding the impact of internal and external influences on organisations that are engaged in various change programmes, particularly those that involve IT-induced change. Yet, while many studies have applied an Institutional Perspective to study social and political phenomena in an organisational context, few have focused on using the theory to understand the adoption and diffusion of IS/IT or the impact of IT-enabled change in organisations. The most noteworthy studies that deal with the latter are those undertaken by Teo et al. (2003) (electronic data/ information interchange – EDI – adoption), Liang et al. (2007) (Enterprise Resource Planning – ERP – adoption), Currie and Guah (2007) and Mark (2007) (on IT-enabled process transformation). These studies are identified and briefly discussed further in the next section.

The aforementioned studies clearly express the importance of institutional theory in understanding organisational behaviour, and also show its diversity in terms of diffusion and use in a number of disciplines. Scattered research on institutional theory in multi-disciplines may pose difficulties for new researchers who wish to understand and utilise it for different purposes. Therefore, reviewing and profiling the existing research literature that has utilised institutional theory is likely to be of benefit to researchers in assisting them to identify existing strengths and weaknesses of the pertinent research streams and methodologies, in promoting discussion regarding critical issues in the area, and in assisting in the identification of alternative theoretical and methodological perspectives.

Given the significance of institutional theory, it is surprising that there have been no reviews or meta-analytic articles published that profile its use. The only study that the authors have come across is that by Mignerat and Rivard (2005), which focused on positioning articles that have used an Institutional Perspective in IT research to identify the impact of institutional pressure (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Scott, 2005) in changing organisational norms. However, to our knowledge no studies exist that focus primarily on reviewing the literature relating to the use of institutional theory from a multi-disciplinary perspective. Meta analysis can help to obtain a more precise estimate of relationships between research themes, and can assist in finding patterns across studies and determining future research directions (Palvia et al., 2007; Avison et al., 2008). The primary aim of this article is therefore to provide a comprehensive and systematic cross-discipline review of the literature pertaining to the use of institutional theory in order to ascertain the current state of the theory’s use along a number of dimensions.

This overall aim is realised by means of the following four objectives:

1. to identify and determine various demographic variables (such as subject category, journals and year of publication) associated with publications that have utilised institutional theory as a theoretical lens;

2. to classify publications according to the units of analysis commonly investigated utilising institutional theory;

3. to classify publications with use of institutional theory according to the theoretical and methodological approaches; and

4. to identify and classify IS publications that have utilised institutional theory as a theoretical lens. In order to realise these objectives, a systematic and comprehensive review of 511 articles appearing in 210 different peerreviewed journals (see Table 1) during the period 1978– 2008 was conducted.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows. In the next section, we present a brief discussion of the existing literature and review the use of institutional theory in different contexts. In the subsequent section we provide a discussion of the method we employed in our analysis of the trends of diffusion and use of institutional theory. Our findings are presented in the fourth section and then a discussion on the use of institutional theory in IS discipline is provided. Finally, the last section presents our conclusions from this work and the limitation to the approach.

## An overview of institutional theory literature

An institution is a social structure that is made up of a collection of individuals or organisations within which collectives exercise action or orientations in a constrained environment that will continuously be altered over time (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Barley and Tolbert, 1997; Scott, 2001). The ‘institutional perspective’ has been used by various researchers to explain why certain organisational structures and ideals endure, and to study the internal and external influences on organisational patterns. According to Robey and Boudrea (1999), ‘organizations acquire institutional properties by drawing from abstract ideals in a society, such as competition, progress and efficiency’ (p. 176). Institutional theory posits that structural and behavioural changes in organisations are determined less by competition and the desire for efficiency, and more by the need for organisational legitimacy (Liang et al., 2007). Institutional theory is most prominent in sociology (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; Zucker, 1977, 1987; Jepperson, 1991), organisational studies (Zucker, 1977, 1985, 1987; DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Powell and DiMaggio, 1991; Scott, 1995; Tolbert and Zucker, 1996; Mizruchi and Fein, 1999), political science (March and Olsen, 1989; North, 1990; Keohane and Martin, 1995; Miranda and Kim, 2006) and economics (Williamson, 1981, 1985; Hodgson, 1998) literature. Furthermore, a number of researchers including DiMaggio and Powell (1983), Zucker (1985), Avgerou (2000), Orlikowski and Barley (2001), Scott (2001), Teo et al. (2003) and Scott (2005) have looked at the institutional context for studying the human and technology influence on organisations.

From a socio-organisational perspective, Kondra and Hinings (1999) attempt to develop a taxonomy of organisations within an institutional environment in order to conceptualise how organisations respond to violations of institutional norms and the resultant implications these actions have on the potential for change. Conversely, from a political angle Keohane and Martin (1995) use institutional theory to explore how international relations work between global organisations and between countries and continents, while North (1990) highlights rules that structure economic processes at the national level. Equally, from an economic viewpoint, Institutional Economists such as Hodgson (1998) tend to highlight the formal and regulative rules that constrain organisational behaviour.

As stated before, a number of studies have also adopted an institutionalist perspective to examine IT-related phenomena such as IT innovation, IT development and implementation, and IT adoption and use (Teo et al.,

2003; Mignerat and Rivard, 2005; Liang et al., 2007). Geels (2004) uses institutional theory to analyse long-term dynamics of socio-technical systems and the co-evolution of technology and society. From an IT perspective, it is argued that institutional analysis can help IS researchers understand ‘how technologies are embedded in complex interdependent social, economic, and political networks, and how they are consequently shaped by broader institutional influences’ (Orlikowski and Barley, 2001: 154).

More recently, institutional theory has been used to study the effects of internal and external influences on large-scale IT implementations. For instance, a study by Liang et al. (2007) has focused on the influence of top management (Chatterjee et al., 2002) in mediating the effect of institutional pressures on IT assimilation in large-scale enterprise systems. Similarly, Mark (2007) and Currie and Guah (2007) have used institutional theory to evaluate the National Programme of Information Technology (NPfIT) in the UK, the world’s largest public sector IT programme. In this context, Currie and Guah used institutional theory to evaluate the wider organisational factors that influence the NPfIT programme, while Mark (2007) responds to this by examining the appropriateness and applicability of the method chosen (institutional theory) by Currie and Guah in the light of the complex and dynamic process that is involved in the transformation of the National Health System (NHS) in the UK. In this context, Robey and Boudreau’s (1999) study is also relevant in that the authors combine institutional theory with three other methods (organisational politics, organisational culture and organisational learning) to study the organisational consequences of information technology (i.e. the transformational effects of IT).

The aforementioned contexts show that institutional theory has been used widely in organisational studies for some time, and has recently been applied in other areas, including IS/IT research. However, in our opinion only one study has attempted to review the use of institutional theory in IS/IT and/or other (i.e. political, social, economic) research. This study was undertaken by Mignerat and Rivard (2005), whose research aimed to position articles that have used an Institutional Perspective in IT research. These authors selected IT studies that used the institutional perspective from management and IT journals with a Journal Citation Report impact factor above 0.8. A search of 16 publication outlets was used in this study, resulting in 19 articles in which institutional effects played a central role. However, the primary focus of this study was to identify the impact of institutional pressure (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Scott, 2001) in changing organisational norms. In addition, this study also reviewed the IT phenomenon under study and found three general themes: IT innovation, IT development and implementation, and IT adoption and use. Given this context, it is clear that no studies exist to date that offer a systematic and crossdisciplinary account of the theory, thus further corroborating the purpose and aim of this article.

## Classifying and analysing the institutional theory literature

The research presented in this article employed a combination of bibliometric analysis (Chao et al., 2007; Williams et al., 2009) and meta-analysis (Palvia et al., 2007; Avison et al., 2008; Dwivedi and Kuljis, 2008; Dwivedi et al., 2008, 2009) as a means of categorising accumulated knowledge on the diffusion and use of institutional theory across disciplines. The overall analysis was undertaken in the following three steps, which are described in detail below: Step 1 – Bibliometric Analysis; Step 2 – Longitudinal Literature Review; and Step 3 – Analysis of IS Publications utilizing institutional theory.

Rather than limiting the focus of this study to analysing only the IS literature that has utilised the theory, we have adopted a cross-disciplinary analysis of the use of institutional theory. There are two main reasons for doing this: (1) very few studies on IS have utilised institutional theory, and therefore any exclusive analysis of this literature would have been limited in its approach; and (2) a broader understanding of the theory can only be comprehended by analysing the studies published in other relevant disciplines and the main developments reported there. Moreover, the IS studies that have applied the theory have only adapted some component or element of the theory that fits a particular research purpose. Therefore, the authors suggest that by widening the analysis, future research directions can be drawn for IS research from other disciplines.

## Step 1: bibliometric analysis

In conducting this study, we made use of the academic journals database provided by Thomson Scientific (previously known as the Institute for Scientific Information (ISI)). Thomson Scientific publishes the Science Citation Index (SCI) and the Social Science Citation Index (SSCI) as two of three elements of its Web of Science<sup>s</sup> product. The reason for selecting this database is that the majority of journals across disciplines are included within either the SCI or the SSCI. Therefore, it is possible to search for and locate a significant proportion of the published material (Chao et al., 2007) that has utilised institutional theory across various disciplines using the Web of Science<sup>s</sup> search facility. Moreover, restricting the search activities to a single publication database removed many of the potential problems of duplication inherent in the use of multiple data sources. The Web of Science product provides two main search-techniques, that is, ‘General Search’ and ‘Advanced Search’. The search technique used within this research exercise was the ‘General Search’. The main reason for employing a ‘General Search’ approach was simply that its easy-to-use characteristics facilitate the repetition of searches without any confusion, making it straightforward to obtain consistent results in repetitive searches, provided the same search criteria are applied. This method of data collection was adapted from a previous study on Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) (Chao et al., 2007) and adoption and diffusion (Dwivedi et al., 2008).

In order to identify publications specific to the ‘institutional theory’ area, a single search term or keyword was employed in this study. The search criteria included the keyword: ‘institutional theory’. In order to find as many articles as possible that utilised the keyword, the search was restricted to occurrences of the ‘institutional theory’ keyword appearing in the articles’ abstracts and keyword lists. Search utilising the single search term resulted in the extraction of 552 (on 15/04/2008) records providing details on publications relating to ‘institutional theory’, which included research articles, reviews, news items, book reviews and many other categories of document. As we were only interested in the research articles and reviews, in order to exclude the non-relevant materials a further search was undertaken to identify non-relevant document types, which resulted in a final list of 527 articles. We further refined our search output by restricting it to articles published in English only, which resulted in a final list of 511 articles published in 211 journals (see Table 1) from different subject categories (see Table 2). All 511 items were then examined manually in order to cross-check and confirm the relevance of the search results.

A number of analyses were then conducted on the search output employing the various analysis tools available in the Web of Science<sup>s</sup>. Count and percentage data were generated for the assorted variables utilised to categorise the search output. Variables analysed included subject category (see Table 2), journal in which the article appeared (see Table 1), and year of publication (see Table 3).

## Step 2 - longitudinal literature review

A further detailed manual analysis was then conducted in order to extract various items of information that could not be obtained directly from the Web of Science<sup>s</sup> database. In order to do so, we examined each of the abstracts of the articles contained in the search results. These abstracts were then individually scrutinised in order to obtain and record information such as the unit or level of analysis, the research paradigm, issues pertaining to primary data, the form of technology examined, and so on. It is important to note that due to time and resource constraints the results generated in this phase of the article arise from the analysis of a total of 102 articles. These articles were selected using a systematic sampling technique whereby we selected every fifth abstract from the list of 511 articles, which resulted in sample of 102 articles.

The data obtained from this analysis relating to the variables under examination were first recoded in SPSS v.14, and then count and percentage values were generated, the results of which are illustrated in Tables 4–9. With the exception of the variables referring to the methodological approaches, data on all the other variables were recorded without considering the pre-specified categories. For the methodological variables, we adopted categories from the previous studies of Avison et al. (2008), Dwivedi and Kuljis (2008) and Dwivedi et al. (2008, 2009).

Step 3 - analysis of IS publications utilising institutional theory The analysis undertaken in steps 1 and 2 collected and analysed data from various subject categories/disciplines. In order to provide implications to IS researchers, it is important to know the current state of use of institutional theory and its associated information in IS/IT-related publications. Publications relating to institutional theory were identified from the following sources: As described in step 1, utilising WoS database, a total of 28 publications were identified. For this purpose a combination of following two sets of keywords were searched in keyword list and abstract of the article – Topic ¼ (‘institutional theory’ OR ‘institutionalization’ OR ‘institutionalism’) AND Topic ¼ (‘IT’ OR ‘INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY’ OR ‘IS’ OR ‘INFORMA TION SYSTEM’ OR ‘INFORMATION SYSTEMS’ OR ‘ICT’ OR ‘INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATION TECHNOLOGY OR ‘INFORMATION AND COMMUNICATION TECHNOL-OGY’). Similar coding and analysis to that described in step 2 was then undertaken to identify theoretical and methodological underpinnings and practices of IS researchers when utilising institutional theory (see Table 10).

A review of the research in the literature listed in Table 10 reveals that institutional theory has been applied by different authors to study the implementation and adoption of IS/IT across many different contexts. In Butler’s (2003) article, institutional theory is used to explain how the commitments of social actors influence and shape the development of web-based IS. In this study, the author found that a high degree of ‘institutional tension’ was in evidence between the various actors involved in the development of web-based systems, mainly because of established commitments to their ‘communitiesof-practice’ and the need to maintain existing power relationships. In Cavalluzzo and Ittner’s (2004) study, they discuss how government organisations implement management control systems to meet legislative requirements but do not use these systems for internal improvements. The authors use institutional theory to conjecture how the limited perceived benefits realised from mandated organisational changes in government organisations tend to be symbolic, but have little effect on internal operations. Chatterjee et al. (2002) draw from institutional theory as a conceptual lens to examine the impacts of specific institutional factors (such as top management advocacy, strategic investment rationale and coordination) on the extent of assimilation of Web technologies into e-commerce strategies and activities in firms. In particular, the authors test the relationships among these factors using quantitative measures and the extent of the impact they have on organisational assimilation of Web technologies.

One of the most prominent recent studies on the application of institutional theory to examine the adoption of IS in organisations was conducted by Liang et al. (2007). They develop and test a theoretical model to investigate the assimilation of enterprise systems in the post-implementation stage within organisations. The authors use their research model to explain how top management mediates the impact of external institutional pressures on the degree of usage of ERP systems. Similarly, the study by Salmeron and Bueno (2006) aims to determine whether institutional theory can explain the assimilation of IS/IT in SMEs; in particular, they explore whether organisations in the same industry eventually reach the same level of IS/IT assimilation as a consequence of sharing a similar environment. On a slightly different theme, Hu and Quan (2006) use institutional theory to investigate the external institutional influences on corporate IT budgeting processes. Interestingly, Chiasson and Davidson’s (2005) use of institutional theory is much broader than the aforementioned studies. They draw from the theory to assess how industry influences IS activities, and, conversely, how industry should be addressed in IS research. The authors conclude that industry provides an important contextual ‘space’ to build new IS theory and to evaluate the boundaries of existing IS theory.

The use of the theory in public sector contexts is also quite popular. For instance, in their article, Dill and Anderson (2003) explore the diffusion of ethics-related technology policies in US schools using neo-institutional theory as a means to explain why some schools are establishing such policies while others are not. Haughton (2006) uses institutional theory to analyse what institutional isomorphic pressures might be influencing the Canadian customs brokers’ ICT projects. In doing so, the author reasons that the motives for an organisation’s decision to undertake an ICT project transcend belief in the project’s inherent utility. The article by Sawyer et al. (2005) draws on institutional theory to study computerisation in the US residential real estate industry, and Soares-Aguiar and Palma-dos-Reis (2008) use the theory to study the adoption of e-procurement systems in a Portuguese context.

Continuing the public sector theme, another popular use of institutional theory is in the context of healthcare. Various prominent IS researchers have used the theory to understand the recent ICT-enabled transformations that are taking place in primary and secondary healthcare settings. Among these studies, Currie and Guah’s (2007) application of institutional theory as a conceptual lens to examine the NPfIT project and the modernisation of the NHS in the UK is one of the most instructive cases of the use of the theory. The prominence of the NPfIT project and subsequent impact of this particular study were such that they prompted Mark (2007) to critically analyse and evaluate the applicability and appropriateness of the method chosen (i.e. institutional theory) by Currie and Guah in their study. Staying with the secondary care setting, Davidson and Chismar (2007) use institutional theory to examine the interaction of institutional and technology change triggers in alignment processes during the implementation of a computerised physician order entry system in an acute care hospital.

The use of institutional theory has also been quite popular with researchers in examining the concept of inter-organisational (EDI). For instance, Damsgaard and Lyytinen (1998) use institutional theory to study the different diffusion patterns of EDI in Finland. To examine the dynamics of EDI diffusion, the authors consider alternative adoption patterns that operate beneath specific institutional incentives and programmes. They probe individual organisational cases within specific industrial contexts to dissect interactions between multiple factors that are likely to affect the diffusion of EDI. In a separate study, the same authors examine the impact of intermediating organisations (such as industry associations), and how they have contributed to advancing the EDI diffusion process in the grocery sectors of Hong Kong, Denmark and Finland (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 2001). Similarly, Teo et al. (2003) use institutional theory as a conceptual lens to understand the factors that enable the adoption of inter-organisational systems; the authors empirically examine the institutional pressures faced by CEOs CIOs and CFOs to adopt financial electronic data interchange systems (FEDI).

It is also interesting to note that many of the above researchers (cited in Table 10) have combined institutional theory with other theories to study the effect of IS/IT on organisations. For instance, Bala and Venkatesh (2007) combine institutional theory with two other theoretical perspectives, the relational view of the firm and organisational inertia, to explain the assimilation of inter-organisational business process standards in organisations. Similarly, institutional theory is combined with organisational ecology by Cannon and Woszczynski (2002) to explain the difficulties faced by organisations as a result of the YK2 problem, the associated pressures placed by them by constituents who were aware of the YK2 issue, and how this impacted the decision-making process of IS managers during this period. Robey and Boudreau (1999) propose Institutional Theory together with organisational politics, organisational culture, and organisational learning to explain information technology’s role in organisational change. Tingling and Parent (2004) combine institutional theory with decision theory to study the organisational evaluation-and-selection decision-making process of enterprise systems. Further, Hayes (2008) draws on the social constructivist literature on institutional theory to explore the ways in which ICTs are inextricably interlinked with institutionalisation processes. Using institutional theory and an in-depth case study of an ICT-led change project, Hayes examines how and why the values of one institutional group became dominant across an organisation.

Some of the studies listed in Table 10 also use institutional theory to examine how external pressures influence human behaviour. Ituma and Simpson (2007) draw from institutional theory to describe how human behaviour is shaped and regulated by social structures by examining the trajectories of careers of Nigerian workers. In Miranda and Kim’s (2006) study, the authors empirically examine the premise that organisations’ structures constitute disparate institutional contexts, which differentially constrain the application of decision-making rules in the context of IS outsourcing. Nevo et al. (2007) use institutional theory to explore the tension between internal and external IT capabilities in the realisation of enhanced IT productivity in firms. In this context, the authors examine how external consultants may not always share the same norms and beliefs held by the internal staff in an organisation, which may consequently lead to their efforts in achieving organisational goals being compromised.

Comparison of data from the literature review with general publications raises a number of issues. Most notably, in the referenced disciplines, much attention has been given to theoretical advancement and adaptation for a particular purpose; a number of disciplines have developed/ adapted the theory in their own context, as shown in the various examples of it (such as institutional theory of capitalism, institutional theory of downsizing, institutional theory of law and institutional theory of police). Although Mark (2007) noted the limitations of the theory in examining IS-related issues, no effort is seen to modify and adapt institutional theory for the IS context. Only a few studies (Robey and Boudreau, 1999; Chismar and Davidson, 2005) have looked at it from a theoretical advancement perspective, and clearly there is a need to undertake further conceptual/theoretical or non-empirical research in relation to the theory. Another notable issue is that very few research methods have been utilised by IS researchers when applying institutional theory. The findings obtained from analysis of literature from referenced disciplines suggest that even an experimental approach can be used to investigate micro/individual-level issues in combination with institutional theory. However, no IS study has yet utilised ‘experiment’ as a method; this clearly indicates potential avenues to undertake such research. Other such examples can be drawn by examining the data presented in the tables above.

## Conclusions

This article intended to provide an overview of the current state of diffusion and use of institutional theory by presenting the results of a systematic, comprehensive and longitudinal review of 511 articles appearing across 210 different peer-reviewed journals during the period 1978–2008. We have presented the results of our investigation along a series of dimensions, including the journals most often publishing articles on institutional theory, the most commonly used units of analysis, methodological practice and use of primary data, and sector/industry/ context examined utilising the institutional theory approach. The following are the key conclusions that emerge from this literature review article:

\- Most researchers who have used institutional theory have drawn from the theory to study the adoption of IS/IT in organisations and the impacts of adoption on organisational change. In this respect, various studies of adoption, ranging from internal ERP systems to interorganisational systems such as web-based applications to EDI systems, are discussed. Nevertheless, the use of institutional theory is comparatively narrow and limited in the IS domain when considering its wider use in organisational studies.

\- The theory offers a good conceptual basis for studying transformational change in new environments such as the public sector (e.g. as shown in the studies by Haughton, 2006; Currie and Guah, 2007; and Davidson and Chismar, 2007). These studies offer insights into relatively new and large-scale change initiatives that are complex and difficult to study. The lessons that emerge from these studies offer many practical guidelines for decision makers and implementers of change, as well as theoretical implications that merit further study.

\- In some cases, in order to develop a better understanding of the empirical context under consideration, institutional theory may need to be combined with other theories and research models. As shown in the literature, this will enable researchers to use it more effectively to cover a wider breadth of organisational issues and behaviours.

Our intention in conducting this investigation is to provide a useful and usable resource for future researchers. In keeping with previous ‘state of play’ studies of this nature, we posit that this literature review article has discussed the use of the institutional theory in current IS research, and, wherever relevant, its possible implications for future research. Furthermore, we argue that the findings of this study may help new researchers in this area to identify relevant journals to refer to and in which to publish their work on the theory. The review presented in this article can also help new researchers to strengthen the area of research by facilitating consideration of less used but useful alternative theoretical and methodological perspectives. When considering research in terms of the research paradigm, the positivist approach is currently employed to a greater extent than the interpretive and descriptive/ theoretical approaches. This clearly suggests that researchers are neglecting richer paradigms (i.e. interpretive) that have implications for editors, reviewers and authors. Similarly, the utilisation of empirical and quantitative techniques and survey research methods appears to have been much preferred over other available alternatives. We anticipate that this article will prove to be a useful source of information for those readers who wish to learn more about the various facets pertaining to the existing body of published research that has utilised institutional theory. Moreover, readers also may benefit from becoming aware of how the various research approaches/methods fit with the different theories/models and units of analysis.

## Areas for further research

The results of this study revealed that institutional theory has been used to study various different facets of IS/IT adoption in organisations and related influencing factors. When examining the articles analysed in this study and the trends in ICT-enabled change, future use of the theory can focus on the following research directions:

\- How firms can facilitate more effective transformation of their traditional bricks and mortar businesses to a Web-enabled business environment, and understanding underlying institutional pressures that influence such change.

\- The influence of external pressures in shaping and institutionalising ICT-enabled change, in particular in the public sector in the context of implementing transformational government and/or electronic government initiatives. In this respect, institutional theory can offer a good conceptual lens to study new ideas such as open systems and interoperability; networked innovations; knowledge sharing; and shared services as applied in the public sector context.

\- Examining the institutional pressures that alter human behaviour and attitude towards organisational change.

\- The IS community would also benefit from studies that focus on synergy between internal IS/IT capabilities and external skills that consultants bring to organisations in change projects and/or outsourcing-type arrangements. In this context, the outcomes of the relationship between the two parties, their interactions and the institutional pressures that facilitate or hinder working relationships can be studied.

\- Another overlooked area in current studies is the institutional influences that demand that organisations maintain existing IS/IT systems in a particular manner (i.e. by performing modifications either incrementally or radically). It is interesting to note that most studies on institutional theory focus on new IS/IT adoptions, while the longer-term perspective of how the system in maintained and improved after institutionalisation is largely ignored.

## Limitations

It is important to acknowledge that our study has a number of limitations, and readers should be aware of these and indeed interpret the material presented in this article within the context of these limitations. Our search activities were restricted to occurrences of the three keywords in the article titles only, and we acknowledge that there may be numerous studies that lack all three keywords in the title but still may have utilised institutional theory in the main text. A further limitation is the extraction of theoretical and methodological data from limited search outputs. The search was limited to the journals indexed only in the Web of Science<sup>s</sup>, but there are many well-known journals in various disciplines that are not indexed in this product, and this clearly will have limited our ability to identify all relevant articles. Although further research is required to determine the extent of the influence of such factors, we believe that this article has analysed the largest number of articles in comparison to other existing review articles on this theme. Nevertheless, we recognise that more comprehensive research is required to reduce the impact of the limitations we have identified in order to provide a greater understanding of the domain of research with institutional theory as a lens.
