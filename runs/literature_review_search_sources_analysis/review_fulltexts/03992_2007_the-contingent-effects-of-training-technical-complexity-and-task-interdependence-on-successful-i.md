## Abstract

Research has investigated the main effect of training on information systems implementation success. However, empirical support for this model is inconsistent. We propose a contingent model in which the effect of training on IS implementation success is a function of technical complexity and task interdependence. A meta-analysis of the literature finds strong support for the model, explaining the inconsistent findings reported in the literature. Implications for theory and practice are discussed.

Keywords: Training, IS implementation, task interdependence, technical complexity, meta-analysis, IS success, transactive memory

## Introduction

End-user training is a critical intervention to support the successful implementation of information systems innovations (Bostrom et al. 1990; Bronsema and Keen 1985; Compeau et al. 1995). For this reason, the implementation of IS innovations is typically accompanied by substantial investment in formal training programs (Olfman and Pitsatorn 2000; Swan et al. 1999). However, despite large investments made by organizations in end-user training, implementation expectations are frequently not realized (Nelson et al. 1995; Sauer 1993). Jasperson et al. (2005) highlight the need to develop richer theoretical conceptualizations of the effect of training on implementation success.

Research on implementing IS innovations typically models a direct effect of training on implementation success (see, for example, Olfman and Pitsatorn 2000; Yetton et al. 1999). However, this model is incomplete as it does not include the effect of situational contingencies that influence the effect of training (Gallivan et al. 2005). Extending Gallivan et al.'s argument, this paper identifies two situational contingencies that influence the effect of training on end-user adoption and successful implementation: the complexity of technology (Attewell 1992; Rogers 1983) and task interdependence (Fichman 1992; Kang and Santhanam 2003-04).

The paper begins by developing two hypotheses specifying how the relationship between training and IS implementation varies with technical complexity and task interdependence. The sample selection procedures, sample characteristics, measurement instruments, and meta-analysis are then described. The results provide strong support for the two hypotheses, namely, that the effect of training on IS implementation success is a positive function of both technical complexity and task interdependence. Finally, validity threats and implications for theory and managerial practice are discussed.

## Training and Information Systems Implementation Success

The literature identifies a number of processes through which training contributes to implementation success. Here, we review that literature and synthesize a model, identifying the effects of training on both individual and interindividual cognitions, and how changes in those cognitions influence implementation success. We then extend that model, formally developing two hypotheses specifying the moderating effects of technical complexity and task interdependence on the relationship between training and implementation success. The meta-analysis testing these two hypotheses is reported in the “Results” section.

## Training, Individual Cognitions, and Implementation Success

An extensive research literature drawing on theories of individual learning has examined the effectiveness of various training methods to impart technical knowledge to end users (Olfman and Pitsatorn 2000; Santhanam and Sein 1994; Yi and Davis 2003). Typically, experts explain the application to novice users in classroom settings, demonstrate how to use its technical features, observe the learners practicing and provide feedback (Yi and Davis 2003). Training engages end users in cognitive activities through which they acquire knowledge imparted by trainers (Gallivan et al. 2005). Although end users also acquire knowledge from other sources, including situated learning, learning-by-doing, and learning-by-using (Attewell 1992), training is an important source of knowledge for them and an important organizational intervention contributing to implementation success (Nelson and Cheney 1987).

End users need to acquire new knowledge to be able to use new IS applications effectively (Attewell 1992; Nelson et al. 1995; Rogers 1983; Sein et al. 1999). Synthesizing the literature, Kang and Santhanam (2003-04) identify three knowledge domains that training programs should deliver: application knowledge covering commands and tools embedded in IS applications; business context knowledge covering the use of IS applications to effectively perform business tasks; and collaborative task knowledge covering how others use the application in their tasks.

Training enables end users to acquire knowledge in each of the three domains and, therefore, to overcome knowledge barriers to successful implementation (Attewell 1992). While end-user training programs generally focus narrowly on imparting specific application knowledge, such training programs can also help create other forms of knowledge (Kang and Santhanam 2003-04). Explicit application knowledge acquired in training programs can activate other knowledge-creating processes that combine with existing knowledge of task and context to create new knowledge (Alavi and Leidner 2001). Alavi and Leidner identify two modes of knowledge creation that can contribute to this process. The combination mode of knowledge creation involves individuals creating “new explicit knowledge by merging, categorizing, reclassifying, and synthesizing existing explicit knowledge” while the internalization mode of knowledge creation “creates new tacit knowledge from explicit knowledge” (Alavi and Leidner 2001, p. 116). Both processes can contribute to the creation of business context knowledge and collaborative task knowledge for end users, even when the focus of training programs is to impart application knowledge to end users.

## Training, Interindividual Cognitions, and Implementation Success

In addition to the effects on individual cognitions, training influences implementation success through its effect on inter-individual $^{2}$ cognitions, specifically, through the creation of a transactive memory system (Argote 2005; Liang et al. 1995) and the development of collaborative task knowledge (Kang and Santhanam 2003-04).

Transactive memory has its origins in the phenomenon of employing external storage, such as written records, as an extension of individual memory. In particular, transactive memory develops when individuals use the memories of other individuals as external storage (Wegner et al. 1985). It consists of two components: knowledge contained in the memories of individual group members, and knowledge-relevant communication processes among group members (Wegner et al. 1985). A key element of transactive memory systems is knowledge encoded in group members' memories about the knowledge held by other group members—the shared conceptualization of “who knows what” (Hollingshead and Brandon 2003; Wegner et al. 1985).

Transactive memory contributes to successful implementation by enabling end users to access a greater pool of relevant knowledge (Wegner 1987). The shared division of cognitive labor involved in the encoding, storage, and retrieval of information leads to greater efficiency, effectiveness, and coordination in the utilization of knowledge (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004). Groups with a well developed transactive memory system have been found to perform more effectively than groups with impaired transactive memory systems (Argote 2005).

Training is an important enabler in the development of transactive memory (Argote 2005). Encoding of information in transactive memory is more effective when group members are able to communicate and exchange rich information about their tasks and their expertise (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004; Hollingshead and Brandon 2003). Typically, training provides a venue and opportunity for group members to gather information about members' expertise through watching group members perform tasks and sharing information during conversations (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Brandon and Hollingshead 2004). Consistent with these processes, groups that train together are found to outperform groups whose members are trained separately (Argote 2005).

Training also contributes to implementation success by enabling the development of convergent models of collaborative task knowledge. Discussions of the effect of transactive memory have focused mainly on a shared conceptualization of “who knows what.” Brandon and Hollingshead (2004) extend this discussion and argue that shared conceptualizations of both task and expertise are important, as group members “must attend not only to who knows what but also to who does what” (p. 635). They consider knowledge of task-expertise-person relationships as the basic building blocks of group shared mental models. Training provides individuals with opportunities to obtain reliable information about other group members’ tasks and expertise, leading to the convergence of shared mental models among group members. Convergent models of collaborative task knowledge enable a form of tacit coordination between group members, contributing to group and individual performance.

The above conceptualization of interindividual cognitions treats them as interdependent patterns in individual cognitions of group members that enable coordination and contribute to group performance (Wegner 1987). For instance, Brandon and Hollingshead (2004) propose that shared patterns of task–person–expertise associations resident in individual cognitions of group members contribute to performance of group tasks. This conceptualization enables researchers to study transactive effects arising out of interdependent patterns in individual cognitions without defining a “group” mind outside its members’ individual cognitions (Wegner 1987).

Adopting this conceptualization, we treat collaborative task knowledge as an interindividual cognition. Consistent with this, Kang and Santhanam (2003-04, p. 276) define collaborative task knowledge as an understanding of “the interdependent relationships among all users’ work procedures,” enabling “users to assess the collective consequences of their individual ways of using a collaborative application in the context of a business process,” coordinating across individuals and contributing to group performance.

## The Effect of Training on Implementation Success

The brief overview above of the literature identifies four pathways, presented in Figure 1, through which training contributes to implementation success. Two are individual-based, developing application knowledge and business context knowledge. The other two are interindividual-based, developing transactive memory and collaborative task knowledge.

Figure 1 is not an exhaustive model of paths through which training influences implementation success. Other pathways are discussed in the literature. For instance, training may influence implementation success through its effect on the beliefs of end users, their attitudes, and their perceptions of usefulness and ease of use $^{3}$ (Agarwal and Prasad 1999).

There is an extensive research stream on the direct effect of training on implementation success embedded in Figure 1 (see, for example, DeLone 1988; Fuerst and Cheney 1982; Leonard-Barton and Deschamps 1988; Yetton et al. 1999). Cumulatively, that research reports inconsistent findings. In a meta-analysis of the implementation of decision support systems, Alavi and Joachimsthaler (1992) estimate the 95 percent confidence interval for the correlation between training and implementation success to be between 0.02 and 0.75. Since their meta-analysis, similar variances in the correlation between training and implementation success have been reported in the IS implementation literature, varying from -0.04 (Russo 1993) to 0.51 (Cho and Kim 2001-02).

Table 1 reports the descriptive statistics for these studies. Only 31.4 percent of the observed variance in the correlation between training and implementation success is explained by sampling error. The unexplained variance in correlation values across studies could be due to the presence of moderator variables (Hunter and Schmidt 2004), providing empirical support for Gallivan et al.'s (2005) speculation that training exerts a contingent effect on implementation success.

Two hypotheses are developed below to explain the unexplained variance reported in Table 1. One is that the strengths of the paths linking training to implementation success through individual cognitions vary systematically with technical complexity. The other is that the strengths of the paths through interindividual cognitions vary with task interdependence. The analysis extends the simple main effects model of training on implementation success to include the moderating effects of technical complexity and task interdependence on that relationship.

## Role of Technical Complexity

Rogers' (1962, 1983) theory of diffusion of innovations is used by Fichman (1992) and others to model the implementation of IS innovations. Rogers identifies complexity as one of five factors influencing the adoption of innovations by end users. Specifically, end users are more likely to reject innovations that are technically complex, require advanced skills for users, generate inconsistent outcomes or need expert support (Attewell 1992; Eveland and Tornatzky 1990; Rogers 1983). Attewell (1992) explains this effect by arguing that complex technologies create knowledge barriers that inhibit end-user adoption. Within this research stream, researchers have hypothesized a direct negative effect of complexity on implementation success (see, for example, Premkumar and Roberts 1999; Thompson et al. 1991; Tornatzky and Klein 1982).

However, empirical support for the direct effect of technical complexity on implementation success is inconsistent, similar to the inconsistent support for the effect of training on implementation success. For instance, Tornatzky and Klein (1982) conducted a meta-analysis of the research on Rogers's (1962) model and found that empirical studies report a mixed effect of complexity on end-user adoption. Similar findings are also reported by Agarwal and Prasad (1997) and Nedovic-Budic and Godschalk (1996). For example, Nedovic-Budic and Godschalk investigated the implementation of geographic information systems technologies at four sites over a period of 6 years. They found that high levels of complexity adversely affected successful implementation in only one of the four cases.

The inconsistent findings for the effects of both training and technical complexity on implementation success support the following conclusion: Independent main effects of training and technical complexity do not adequately model the phenomenon. Here, drawing on the model in Figure 1, we propose that the effect of training on IS implementation success is a contingent function of technical complexity.

Under conditions of high technical complexity, training has a strong effect on implementation success. Knowledge embedded in individual cognitions has an important effect on task performance (Alavi and Leidner 2001). Technically complex IS innovations require end users to work with unfamiliar technologies and often require them to perform their tasks in different ways (Attewell 1992; Robey et al. 2002). This requires enhancement to the content of individual cognitions to overcome increased knowledge barriers to end-user adoption and implementation success (Attewell 1992; Fichman and Kemerer 1997). Technical complexity affects both the application and business context knowledge that end users need to acquire to effectively use those IS innovations (Kang and Santhanam 2003-04). Expert trainers are often the primary source for such knowledge and, therefore, formal training is a critical intervention through which end users acquire such knowledge.

In contrast, for innovations characterized by low technical complexity, end users face limited knowledge barriers. They can acquire the required knowledge through a number of informal mechanisms, including self-learning, learning by doing, and informally from peers (Leonard-Barton and Deschamps 1988; Robey et al. 2002). Indeed, when knowledge barriers are low, end users may be able to use IS innovations in the absence of formal training. Therefore, when technical complexity is low, formal training has a weak effect on application and business context knowledge, and on implementation success.

The above analysis shows that, when technical complexity is high, training has a strong effect on individual cognitions and, hence, on implementation success. In contrast, when technical complexity is low, training has a weak effect on implementation success. Formally,

Hypothesis 1: The effect of training on implementation success is a positive function of technical complexity.

## Role of Task Interdependence

An extensive stream of research has hypothesized a negative effect of task interdependence on implementation success. For example, Ginzberg (1980) argues that IS innovations supporting interdependent tasks face greater barriers to implementation success as compared to those supporting independent tasks. Hackathorn and Keen (1981) show that the level of difficulty in successfully implementing systems increases as the level of task interdependence increases. Specifically, IS applications supporting interdependent tasks face implementation difficulties because of the misfit between the routines embedded in existing interindividual cognitions and those required for effective performance with new technologies (Edmondson et al. 2001; Ginzberg 1980).

However, empirical support for the direct effect of task interdependence on implementation success is inconsistent. Contributing to that research stream, Sanders and Courtney (1985) and Guimaraes et al. (1992) hypothesized direct effects of task interdependence on implementation success. Both studies found weak or nonsignificant effects.

The inconsistent findings for the effect of task interdependence on implementation success, combined with the above reported inconsistent findings for the effect of training on implementation success, support the following conclusion: The independent main effects of training and task interdependence on success do not adequately model the phenomenon. This conclusion is similar in form to the conclusion above concerning the effects of technical complexity and training on implementation success. Here, we again draw on Figure 1 to model the joint effects of training and task interdependence and to develop the argument that the effect of training on IS implementation success is contingent on task interdependence.

The argument has three components. First, the implementation of interdependent-use IS innovations is frequently accompanied by the introduction of new business processes that disrupt existing task routines (Robey et al. 2002). Such changes require end users to enhance the content of individual cognitions and to develop new routines to cope with new task interdependencies (Edmondson et al. 2001; Sharma and Yetton 2003). Successful implementation depends on “knowledge about the interdependent relationships among all users’ work procedures… [that] enable users to assess the collective consequences of their individual ways of using a collaborative application in the context of a business process” (Kang and Santhanam 2003-04, p. 276). In contrast, with independent-use IS innovations, collaborative task knowledge is not critical to successful implementation as end users can independently adopt such innovations and capture performance gains from them (Kang and Santhanam 2003-04; Sharma and Yetton 2003). The effect of collaborative task knowledge on implementation success is contingent on task interdependence.

Second, the effect of training on developing collaborative task knowledge is also contingent on task interdependence. Training enables communication and interaction that can sensitize users to the needs of others and to assessing the consequences of their use patterns on others. $^{4}$ End users are more likely to recognize the importance of collaborative task knowledge when others' performance is dependent on their own behavior (Kang and Santhanam 2003-04).

Third, the effect of training on implementation success through transactive memory is contingent on task interdependence for two reasons. One is that training has a strong effect on developing effective transactive memory when task interdependence is high. Task interdependence is an important prerequisite for stimulating the development of transactive memory (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004; Wegner et al. 1985). End users are more likely to attend to others' expertise, tasks, and needs when they recognize the cognitive interdependencies (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004). Under this condition, group members rely on each other to take responsibility for storing information, stimulating the development of transactive memory (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004). Training facilitates communication and improves the accuracy and effectiveness of transactive memory (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004).

The other reason is that transactive memory has a strong effect on implementation success when task interdependence is high. For interdependent tasks, successful implementation depends on effective group performance. Transactive memory provides shared mental models and enables a form of tacit coordination that contributes to improving group performance (Argote 2005; Brandon and Hollingshead 2004; Lewis 2004). As noted above, groups have been found to perform better on interdependent tasks when group members have shared knowledge about the skills and knowledge of each member (Hollingshead and Brandon 2003). In contrast, such coordination and knowledge of others' skills is not critical to the performance of independent individual-based tasks.

The above analysis shows that, when task interdependence is high, training has a strong effect on implementation success. In contrast, when task interdependence is low, training has a weak effect on implementation success. Formally,

Hypothesis 2: The effect of training on implementation success is a positive function of task interdependence.

## Method

Hypotheses 1 and 2 are tested using meta-analysis (Glass et al. 1981; Hunter and Schmidt 1990, 2004). This technique enables researchers to cumulate findings from multiple studies to draw valid conclusions and explain variability in findings across multiple studies. Meta-analysis techniques are particularly suited for testing moderating effects, such as the effects of technical complexity and task interdependence presented in Figure 2. The meta-analysis techniques employed here are similar to those employed in recent IS research (Alavi and Joachimsthaler 1992; Dennis et al. 2001; Kohli and Devaraj 2003; Sharma and Yetton 2003).

## Sample

The sample for this meta-analysis consists of empirical studies reported in journals, conference proceedings, and unpublished dissertations. Following Hunter and Schmidt (1990, 2004) and Sharma and Yetton (2003), studies were located through multiple literature searches. These include bibliographic databases such as ABI/INFORM, Science Direct, Sociological Abstracts, and Dissertation Abstracts.

These were searched using multiple keywords including information systems, training, implementation, success, and correlation. Manual searches of key journals including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, and Decision Science were conducted. The search period covered studies published to 2004.

Bibliographies of studies identified above were also examined to locate additional studies. Dissertation Abstracts were included in the search specifically in order to overcome the potential bias of higher effect sizes associated with published journal articles. Abstracts of over 1,000 articles and dissertations were examined to identify studies for inclusion in the meta-analysis. This comprehensive search strategy both increases the power of the meta-analysis, by maximizing the number of studies, and reduces source bias.

Appendix A lists the studies included in the meta-analysis and reports the data obtained from the primary studies. A study was selected for inclusion in the meta-analysis if it satisfied two criteria. First, the study reports the correlation between training and implementation success. Second, it provides an adequate description of the technology and/or task to code the measures of technical complexity and/or task interdependence. The most common reasons for excluding a study from the meta-analysis are that it is qualitative or theoretical, or, if quantitative, does not report the relevant correlation.

The search identified 11 journal articles, 8 dissertations, and 1 conference paper. Of these, five journal articles and one dissertation contributed multiple data sets. Multiple data sets within a publication are considered as separate studies for the purpose of meta-analysis. The search process identified a sample of 27 studies.

## Measurement of Variables

## Implementation Success

Following Alavi and Joachimsthaler (1992) and Sharma and Yetton (2003), use and user satisfaction are employed as measures of implementation success. $^{5}$ Use and user satisfaction are the most commonly employed measures for implementation success (Agarwal and Prasad 1997; DeLone and McLean 1992; Saga and Zmud 1994). The key managerial challenges faced in the implementation stage are overcoming various forms of end-user resistance, and motivating end users to develop and adopt new behaviors (Kwon and Zmud 1987). Use and user satisfaction represent the success of various managerial interventions designed to meet those challenges and promote end-user adoption. Of the 27 studies included in this meta-analysis, 18 employed use as the measure for implementation success. The other nine studies employed user satisfaction.

## Training

Training captures the extent to which formal training was provided for end users. This includes formal training provided by vendors as well as in-house training provided by organizations. In all, 21 studies employed training as the construct label. The other six studies employed slightly different labels for the construct, including participation in training programs, satisfaction with training, and availability of training. Even though the construct labels varied across studies, the items employed to operationalize the construct are very similar and tap a common underlying construct.

## Technical Complexity and Task Interdependence

Technical complexity and task interdependence are estimated for each study included in this meta-analysis using the information available in the primary studies. Based on that information, a brief description of the IS innovation investigated in each study was generated. The descriptions include all information provided in each primary study regarding the IS innovation, the technology employed, the task supported by the IS innovation, the organizations participating in the study, and the end users sampled. All portions of text were taken verbatim from the primary studies to generate the descriptions. No changes were made to the text except to link extracts that came from different parts of each primary study. $^{6}$

In the literature, there is no generally accepted measure of technical complexity. Here, three questions were selected from Attewell (1992), Premkumar and Roberts (1999), and Cho and Kim (2001-02) to measure technical complexity (Table 2). Three expert raters, each with a minimum of 5 years' experience as a senior manager in the IS industry or as a project manager, and one of the authors, rated each study description on this three-item scale of technical complexity (Table 2). The scale exhibits acceptable internal reliability (standardized item alphas for the four raters are 0.87, 0.81, 0.78 and 0.75) and inter-rater reliability (intra-class correlation coefficient for the four raters is 0.81, $p \leq 0.01$ ).

Following Sharma and Yetton (2003), task interdependence is measured on Pearce at al.'s (1992) six-item scale of task interdependence (Table 3). Three different expert raters, each with a minimum of 8 years' experience in the IS industry as a senior manager or consultant, and one of the authors rated each study description on task interdependence. The scale exhibits acceptable internal reliability (standardized item alphas for the four raters are 0.96, 0.89, 0.87 and 0.82) and inter-rater reliability (intra-class correlation coefficient for the four raters is 0.86, $p \leq 0.01$ ).

The findings support a contingent model in which training is a necessary and critical component of a successful implementation strategy when technical complexity and task interdependence are high, but a weak and noncritical component when they are both low. In the extreme case when technical complexity and task interdependence are both very low, training has a trivial effect on implementation success. So, there is no main effect of training on IS implementation success.

## Implications for Research

This paper develops a model of the effect of training on implementation success (Figure 1). Extending earlier research, the model emphasizes the roles of both individual and interindividual cognitions in implementation success. The role of interindividual cognitions has not been adequately addressed in earlier research on the effects of training on IS implementation success. The model in Figure 1 can serve as a template to guide future research.

A key finding of this study is that the effect of training on implementation success is contingent on both technical complexity and task interdependence. This finding makes important contributions to the literature on the effect of knowledge barriers on successful implementation (Attewell 1992; Fichman and Kemerer 1997; Ravichandran 2005). That stream of research argues that the technical complexity of innovations poses knowledge barriers to end-user adoption and that those barriers can be overcome by access to technology-related knowledge (Attewell 1992). This study contributes to that stream of literature by providing empirical support for the joint effects of training and technical complexity on implementation success.

The findings reported in Table 5 also contribute to that stream of research by identifying the role of task interdependence in posing knowledge barriers to successful implementation. The joint effects of training and task interdependence on implementation success show that task interdependence poses knowledge barriers to end-users' adoption. Future research should extend the search for knowledge barriers beyond technology to include the broader task context.

This study also contributes to the research on knowledge barriers by examining the role of an organizational intervention, end-user training, in overcoming knowledge barriers. Prior literature in that stream has examined the roles of external organizations, pre-existing organizational characteristics, and end users in overcoming knowledge barriers. Attewell (1992) identifies the role of supply-side institutions that possess the required technology-related knowledge through interventions including consulting and outsourcing services. Fichman and Kemerer (1997) and Ravichandran (2005) identify the roles of existing stocks of technical knowledge, the ability to acquire that knowledge easily and economically, and the influence of knowledge sharing. Attewell also discusses the role of end users in acquiring task-related knowledge through processes of situated learning, and discovering knowledge through learning-by-doing and learning-by-using. Future research should investigate the effects of other mechanisms in overcoming knowledge barriers.

The findings of this study also have broad implications for IS implementation research. In a recent study, Dennis and Reinicke (2004) comment on the limitations of models of technology acceptance that presume individual users' perceptions of usefulness to be the dominant factor influencing use. Further, they argue that those limitations are particularly salient in cases where “technology [is] designed to support a process that exists in a surrounding ecosystem of users with potentially different goals and expectations, competing technologies, and pre-existing organizational cultures, norms and expectations” (p. 14). It is in those cases that interindividual cognitions play an important role in successful implementation. The findings above on the moderating effect of task interdependence support Dennis and Reinicke’s claims.

In addition, we extend Dennis and Reinicke's criticism of the technology acceptance model and argue that such models have limited utility in prescribing managerial interventions to support successful implementation. Such models typically explain implementation success as an outcome of end-users' perceptions of usefulness and ease of use. However, technology implementation is an outcome of a number of actions taken by both managers and end users. Those actions, including training and changes to organizational processes, result in the creation of new knowledge and structures that are critical to successful implementation (Jasperson et al. 2005; Sharma and Yetton 2003). Individual perceptions of usefulness and ease of use are an outcome of those actions, and vary at different stages of the implementation process (Dennis and Reinicke 2004). To offer guidelines to managers to support successful implementation, technology acceptance models need to include a role for human agency in explaining social outcomes

Recent reviews propose that the task context is central to conceptualization of the IT artifact and that focusing on the task context is a fruitful approach for implementation research (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Orlikowski and Iacono 2001). The findings of this research support future research in that stream by identifying the role of two variables characterizing the task context, technical complexity and task interdependence. We encourage future researchers to model implementation success as a function of human intervention and situational contingencies grounded in the task context.

This study extends the literature on transactive memory systems by applying it in a new context and suggesting avenues for future research. The effect of training on transactive memory has typically been demonstrated among coworkers and workgroups in laboratory settings (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004; Wegner 1987; Wegner et al. 1985). These conditions enable rich interaction and information sharing, both of which are considered important for the development of transactive memory (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004).

In contrast, the context of IS implementation frequently includes dispersed groups, separated by organizational boundaries, while performing interdependent tasks. In the absence of direct communication, interindividual cognitions play an important role in effective task performance. It is unlikely that the end users, who were involved in the training programs investigated in the primary studies included in this meta-analysis, experienced the close relationship, rich interaction, and information sharing typically associated with the development of transactive memory. Despite this, the joint effects of training and task interdependence suggest the operation of transactive processes. We acknowledge that there are unresolved debates in the literature about the nature of interindividual cognitions and their relationships with individual cognitions (Wegner 1987). However, those issues do not affect our conclusions regarding the joint effects of training and task interdependence. Extending Brandon and Hollingshead's (2004) analysis, we speculate that task interdependence supports the development of transactive memory and shared mental models, even in the absence of rich interaction and explicit coordination. Further research is needed to investigate the development of interindividual cognitions.

Interdependence is considered essential for the development of transactive memory. The empirical literature typically demonstrates this effect by examining the effect of training on performance of independent versus interdependent tasks (Argote 2005). The joint effects of training and task interdependence reported in this study suggest that future research should investigate the moderating role of task interdependence on transactive memory effects.

Earlier research has focused on explaining the effects of transactive memory on repetitive task performance parameters, including time, cost, and quality (Argote 2005). Extending the context to which this theory has been applied, we speculate that transactive memory effects extend to performance on complex one-off tasks, such as those involved in IS implementation. We suggest that future research should investigate transactive memory effects in this context.

The above conclusions are subject to a number of limitations. Most importantly, the conceptual model in Figure 1 has not been fully tested. Specifically, the moderating effects of technical complexity and task interdependence on the four paths mediating the effect of training on implementation success have not been tested.

This study validates the restricted model presented in Figure 2. Future research should test the full model, integrating the models presented in Figures 1 and 2. This is a major challenge. That research would also contribute to the debate in the literature on the relationship between individual and interindividual cognitions.

Future research should also address the other major validity threat that this study employs only secondary data to test the hypotheses. Further studies are needed to test each specific effect in the full model.

## Implications for Practice

The findings also have important implications for the design of efficient and effective training programs. First, the effectiveness of training is substantially higher in high knowledge barrier contexts than in low knowledge barrier contexts. This suggests a heuristic for avoiding both under-investment and over-investment in training.

Second, the contingent effects of both technical complexity and task interdependence reported here are consistent with anecdotal evidence and speculation in qualitative studies that organizations that invest in both technology- and task-related training achieve higher levels of implementation success than those investing only in technology-related training (Robey et al. 2002). Similarly, Kang and Santhanam (2003-04) find that users who undergo conventional training focusing on technical skills master technical skills but do not develop collaborative task knowledge, resulting in difficulties in performing tasks using the collaborative functions of the system. Taken together, the findings suggest a need to go beyond technology training, such as user certification commonly employed in ERP implementation, and to address also task-related issues in training. In terms of the model presented in Figure 1, current training practices focus primarily on developing individual cognitions. Organizations need to develop training practices that contribute to developing interindividual cognitions.

Finally, although this study does not directly investigate the content and design of training programs, $^{8}$ the findings enable us to speculate on the benefits of training programs addressing multiple knowledge domains. We speculate that a two-stage training program may be effective for high technical complexity and high task interdependence contexts. Stage one would focus on individual cognitions, providing technology-related training and developing end-user technical competencies. The second stage would focus on interindividual cognitions, providing task-related training, increasing collaborative task knowledge, and developing transactive memory. Further, following from the findings of the literature on transactive memory, we suggest that users who share task interdependencies should train together, at least in stage two.

## Conclusion

Extending previous research, this study develops a model in which training influences implementation success through its effects on both individual and interindividual cognitions. Following from the model, the effect of training on implementation success is contingent on both technical complexity and task interdependence. The model developed and tested here extends theory, explains the inconsistent findings reported in previous studies, and provides the basis for developing efficient and effective training strategies to fit specific contexts. Training is a necessary and critical component of a successful implementation strategy when technical complexity and task interdependence are high, but a weak and noncritical component when they are low.

## Meta-Analysis Techniques and Analysis of Validity Threats

An important application of meta-analysis techniques is to explain the variability in correlation values across studies by testing for the effect of moderator variables (Hunter and Schmidt 2004). Consider the case where the correlation between two variables, X and Y, is investigated in a number of studies and the correlation values vary across studies. It could be hypothesized that a variable Z moderates the effect of X on Y and that could explain the variability in correlation values across studies. Hypothesizing that Z moderates the effect of X on Y, is equivalent to hypothesizing that the correlation between X and Y ( $R_{X,Y}$ ) is a function of Z. The moderating effect of Z is estimated by the magnitude of $b_{1}$ in the following univariate regression equation:

$$
\mathrm{R} _ {\mathrm{X.Y}} = \mathrm{b} _ {0} + \mathrm{b} _ {1} \mathrm{Z} + \text { error }
$$

In estimating the above equation, the correlations between X and Y ( $R_{X,Y}$ ) are obtained from primary studies reported in the literature, and Z is typically estimated for each study based on the information available in the primary studies. A weighted least squares (WLS) regression is conducted to account for the different sample sizes between studies (Hunter and Schmidt 2004).

When two variables $Z_{1}$ and $Z_{2}$ are both hypothesized to moderate the effect of X on Y, the moderating effects of $Z_{1}$ and $Z_{2}$ are estimated by the magnitudes of $b_{1}$ and $b_{2}$ , respectively, in the following multivariate equation:

$$
\mathrm{R} _ {\mathrm{X.Y}} = \mathrm{b} _ {0} + \mathrm{b} _ {1} \mathrm{Z} _ {1} + \mathrm{b} _ {2} \mathrm{Z} _ {2} + \text { error }
$$

This study hypothesizes that the effect of training on implementation success is moderated by both technical complexity and task interdependence. These hypotheses can be tested by estimating the following regression equation:

$$
\mathrm{R} _ {\text { Training.Implementation   Success }} = \mathrm{b} _ {0} + \mathrm{b} _ {1} (\mathrm{TC}) + \mathrm{b} _ {2} (\mathrm{TI}) + \text { error }
$$

where TC = Technical Complexity and TI = Task Interdependence

H1: The effect of training on implementation success is a positive function of technical complexity, is investigated by testing the significance of $b_{1} > 0$ .

H2: The effect of training on implementation success is a positive function of task interdependence, is investigated by testing the significance of $b_{2} > 0$ .

## Correcting for Measurement Artifacts

Correlations reported in primary studies are attenuated by a number of measurement artifacts, such as reliabilities of measures employed in the primary studies (Hunter and Schmidt 2004). Hunter and Schmidt recommend that the correlations reported in primary studies be individually corrected for measurement artifacts before testing for the effect of moderator variables. Following this, we obtained the reliabilities of training ( $Rel_{Training}$ ) and implementation success ( $Rel_{Success}$ ) reported in the primary studies (see Appendix A). Following Hunter and Schmidt (2004, pp. 34-36), the reported correlations were individually corrected as follows:

$$
\text { Corrected   correlation } = \frac {\text { Reported   Correlation }}{(\mathrm{Rel} _ {\text { Training }}) ^ {1 / 2} \times (\mathrm{Rel} _ {\text { Success }}) ^ {1 / 2}}
$$

Table B1 reports summary data of the reliabilities of the two measures reported in the primary studies. Following Hunter and Schmidt, reliabilities for studies employing multi-item measures but not reporting reliabilities were set at the mean reliability value for the measure, while the reliabilities of studies employing one-item measures were set at a low value of 0.60. The WLS regression was carried out on the corrected correlations (Hunter and Schmidt 2004, pp. 122-125).

## Evaluating Validity Threats in Meta-Analysis

The findings of this study are subject to a potential validity threat arising from source bias. Source bias (also referred to as the “file drawer problem”) can be a potential validity threat when the primary studies included in a meta-analysis are drawn mainly from journals (Hunter and Schmidt 2004). There is an expectation that journal editors have a preference for accepting manuscripts with significant findings only. This could lead to a situation where correlations reported in published studies are significantly higher than those reported in unpublished studies. Consequently, estimated effect sizes reported in published studies are biased and tend to be significantly larger than the “true” value of the effect size across the population. $^{9}$

This potential validity threat can be addressed in meta-analysis in two ways. First, source bias can be minimized by including unpublished studies (e.g., dissertations) in the sample. Second, the effect of this potential validity threat can be empirically controlled for in meta-analysis by treating the potential validity threat as a control variable. Specifically, if the sample includes journal studies and dissertations, a dichotomous dummy variable, Source (0 for dissertations; 1 for published studies), can be included in the regression equation that tests for the hypothesized moderating effect of $Z_{1}$ :

$$
\mathrm{R} _ {\mathrm{X.Y}} = \mathrm{b} _ {0} + \mathrm{b} _ {1} \mathrm{Z} _ {1} + \mathrm{b} _ {2} (\text { Source }) + \text { error }
$$

The above equation estimates $b_{1}$ while controlling for the effect of source bias. Consequently, test of the hypothesis based on the estimate of $b_{1}$ obtained in the above equation is not subject to the validity threat arising from source bias.

A similar technique can be employed to test for the potential validity threat arising from multiple operationalizations of measures. In most fields, multiple variants of measures are employed across studies. This could lead to a potential validity threat if the correlations with one set of measures are significantly different from another set. For instance, it could be speculated that correlations of training with perceptual measures of use are higher than those with behavioral measures of use. $^{10}$ That speculation could be the basis of a potential validity threat. This can be addressed by including a dummy variable, Operationalization (0 for behavioral measures of use; 1 for perceptual measures of use), in the following equation:

$$
\mathrm{R} _ {\mathrm{X.Y}} = \mathrm{b} _ {0} + \mathrm{b} _ {1} \mathrm{Z} _ {1} + \mathrm{b} _ {2} (\text { Operationalization }) + \text { error }
$$

The above equation estimates $b_{1}$ while controlling for the effect of different operationalizations of measures. Consequently, test of the hypothesis based on the estimate of $b_{1}$ obtained in the above equation is not subject to the validity threat arising from different operationalizations of measures.

In this study, we tested for source bias by including a dummy variable for journal/dissertation in the equation, and for the effect of multiple operationalizations by including dummy variables for behavioral versus perceptual measures of use, and for behavioral versus perceptual measures of training.
