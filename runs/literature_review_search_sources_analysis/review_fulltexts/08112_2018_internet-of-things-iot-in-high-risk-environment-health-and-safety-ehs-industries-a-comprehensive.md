# Internet of Things (IoT) in high-risk Environment, Health and Safety (EHS) industries: a comprehensive review

## Abstract

The rise of ubiquitous systems is sustained by the development and progressive adoption of the Internet of Things (IoT) devices and their enabling technologies. IoT has been shown to have significant potential in high-risk Environment, Health, and Safety (EHS) industries. In these industries, human lives are at stake and IoT-based applications are primed to offer safe, reliable, and efficient solutions due to their ability to operate at a fine granular level and provide rich low-level information. We review existing published research on IoT-based applications in high-risk EHS industries with specific emphasis on healthcare industry, food supply chain (FSC), mining and energy industries (oil & gas and nuclear), intelligent transportation (e.g., connected vehicles), and building & infrastructure management for emergency response operations until 2016. We also highlight IoT-related challenges and proposed solutions in high risk EHS industries. We then conclude by presenting research challenges and expected trends for IoT in these industries.

keywords: Internet of Things (IoT); Environment, Health and Safety (EHS)

## 1 Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to the network of physical objects such as home appliances, medical devices, vehicles, and RFID (Radio-Frequency Identification) tags. Through this network, communication among these objects ('things') takes place with no human intervention requirement in the loop. Each thing can also be uniquely identified at the item-level[118]. Technological advances and progressive adoption of IoT technologies have enabled the development of new information and communication systems - the ubiquitous information systems. A goal of ubiquitous computing is to interconnect the physical world through networks such as the Internet. IoT and Cloud Computing are key enabling technologies to build such information and communication systems $[34]$ . In general, IoT forms network of objects that (1) have unique identifiers, (2) may have the capability to collect data from the environment primarily through sensors, (3) operate and interact with the physical world by processing collected data, and (4) use the Internet standards to communicate and analyze the data for further services. Because the working environment in a majority of the EHS industry is highly dynamic with respect to process, labor, and equipment management, IoT-enabled real-time Decision Support Systems (DSS) are critically valuable often through process mining and optimization techniques.

Moreover, progress in open wireless communication technologies such as Radio Frequency Identification (RFID), Bluetooth, and Wi-Fi provide improved ways to connect objects together and enable communication not only among themselves but also with the Internet. Thus the interconnection among intelligent sensors creates Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN) that have the capability to collect, process, and communicate analyzed results in consort with the environment. According to Olgerta et al., Mobile Business Intelligence (m-BI) enhances the capabilities of organizations to make decisions ‘on-the-move’ and m-BI reflects an organizing version that is prominent during the innovation comprehension process $[72]$ . Gendron et al. explain how mobile computing, cloud computing and Big Data support the BI Sweet Spot ecosystem $[31]$ . In order to discuss the impact of mobile clinical decision support system (CDSS) on decision making process and outcomes, multi-criteria evaluation of a mobile field triage decision support system in terms of effectiveness, efficiency, satisfaction and use has been illustrated by Haghighi et al. $[38]$ . Picoto et al. use a conceptual model for assessing the post-adoption stages of mobile business usage and value from an organizational perspective $[78]$ .

IoT use has been growing rapidly thanks to key applications in industries. With the ability to collect, communicate/share and process data, IoT provides huge opportunities to industries. For example, IoT-enabled tracking and monitoring can improve the efficiency of production and finished product distribution. In addition to improved operations, IoT also offers many innovative solutions such as the creation of new business models. For example, sales of goods could be augmented with related services with the help of IoT-generated data and real-time connectivity $[12]$ . In order to evaluate the business value of RFID $[10][11]$ technology, Tzeng proposed a framework that emphasizes delivering business value through refining business processes and expanding the business model with the hope that it encourages more organizations to adopt RFID $[96]$ . However, IoT adoption in large industrial applications faces many challenges. The main challenges include (1) Energy efficiency, (2) Communication and data-related challenges (connectivity, latency, throughput, standardization), (3) Scalability (network size, interoperability) and (4) Security and safety (reliability, privacy protection).

Some of these challenges are shared by different IoT technology components: from sensor devices to back-end system and Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA) design $[56]$ . IoT applications have already experienced reasonable success in some industries such as healthcare services, food supply chain (FSC) and infrastructure monitoring (e.g., energy and environmental management and related application domains) $[34]$ $[21]$ $[50]$ . Overall, the similarity among these industries is that lives are at stake at the workplace or in the final product/service. Therefore, we can classify these as high-risk Environment, Health and Safety (EHS) industries. EHS is a discipline and specialty that involves practical aspects of environmental protection and safety at work. In simple terms, it is what organizations must do to make sure that their activities do not cause harm to anyone. Our focus is on high-risk EHS industries with specific emphasis on healthcare service industry, food supply chain, mining and energy industries (oil & gas, nuclear), intelligent transportation (connected vehicles), and building and infrastructure management related to the smart cities concept and emergency response management. IoT-based applications are rather widespread in these industries. For example, $[58]$ identifies and classifies current research themes related to data completeness in healthcare, $[105]$ proposes ways for the IS community to engage in the development of environmentally sustainable business practices, and $[36]$ consider solutions that ensure uncongested and smooth pilgrim flows with extensive real-time reporting. A goal of this paper is therefore to present the characteristics of IoT-based applications in high-risk EHS industries through a comprehensive review of published research. We also discuss IoT-related challenges and current trends in these industries.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe the research methodology. In Section 3, we present the specifics of IoT-based applications in high-risk EHS industries. We emphasize IoT-related challenges and discuss key solutions that are highlighted in existing published research on high-risk EHS industries in Section 4. We discuss research challenges and future trends for IoT in high-risk EHS industries in Section 5. In Section 6, we provide a summary of this paper and conclude.

## 2 Research methodology

We present a comprehensive review of published research on IoT technology and its applications in high-risk EHS industries. To operationalize this, we searched for published papers in international peer-reviewed journals or books in electronic bibliographical sources that include Scopus and Web of Science mainly by keywords or combination of keywords such as Internet of Things (IoT), Environment, Health and Safety (EHS), Radio Frequency Identification (RFID), Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS), healthcare, Wireless Body

Area Network (WBAN), Food Supply Chain (FSC), mining, oil and gas, nuclear, connected vehicles, Vehicle to Vehicle (V2V), AUtonomous Vehicles (AUV), intelligent transportation, smart city, and emergency response. We then expanded our search through Google Scholar since ISI journal listing is generally considered short in the social sciences and humanities disciplines $[27]$ . The initial search based on these keywords resulted in 50 papers. We read the abstracts of all 50 papers and selected 30 papers that are highly relevant to our study, and disregarded those with a narrow focus only on the EHS industry. In the next stage, we used additional keywords obtained from the results of our initial search and classified EHS industries into five categories: healthcare, food supply chain, mining and energy, intelligent transportation, and building and infrastructure. This resulted in 115 papers after filtering by categories, topic relevance, time of publication, and contributions. The selected papers included computational and analytical methods, conceptual models, design science, and empirical studies.

The distribution of papers across journals shows that the papers were mainly published in journals that cover interdisciplinary topics such as Decision Support Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and International Journal of Production Economics. The distribution of published papers across years 2004 - 2016 is shown in Fig. 1, indicating the increasing attention paid to this general area. The distribution of papers across different industries is shown in Fig. 2 and only the high-frequency cases across journals is summarized in Fig. 3. To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first of its kind to review IoT applications in the EHS industry.

## 3 IoT characteristics and applications in high-risk EHS industries

## 3.1 Motivations for research and implementation of IoT systems in high-risk EHS industries 3.1.1 Healthcare industry

IoT use in healthcare is enabled by several factors and needs. The factors that are mentioned in existing research publications include: A) an ageing population [34] with increasing number of chronic diseases [3] and fast-growing population with increasing access to healthcare services, especially in Asian countries [117]; B) a lack of availability when it comes to important patient-related medical information [95] and eventually the lack of systematic management of diseases throughout the patient pathway [20]; C) the emergence and deployment of key enabling technologies [95] [75] such as multi-agent technology [95], RFID technology [95] [3] as well as wearable technologies [28]; D) the growing adoption and implementation of enterprise information system (EIS) [75]; E) the healthcare industry's turn from a hospital-centered model to a hospital-home-balanced model that is slowly moving toward a home-centered model [53]; F) the lack of adequate training and support to users [19] who are particularly conservatives (both patients and physicians) [65] [20]; G) the lack of consideration of data security and privacy (e.g., eavesdropping operations or data tampering) [85].

The different requirements observed in existing literature are derived from the aforementioned factors, and include: A) A greater and easier access to health records and related information [28]; B) The improvement in healthcare services quality and cost-efficiency [95] [41] [86] [65] [20], particularly in terms of reduced medical errors and increased patient safety with optimized processes [95]; C) Interoperable and ubiquitous healthcare services [75] [28]. In particular, there is a need for higher integration of devices and services that could cover all healthcare segments [75], scalable IoT platforms [28], and IoT-based applications that integrate the entire disease pathway [20]; D) A customer-centric healthcare environment that engages patients in their own healthcare [28] [3] [53] since patients prefer treatment personalization [41]; E) The highly-required compliance of IoT platforms with security and privacy regulatory and socially-expected standards [28] [85].

IoT-based healthcare includes clinical care, home care, healthcare assistance $[85]$ and healthcare logistics. The clinical and home care gap is reduced through pervasive IoT solutions $[28]$ . Labor, medical device management, IoT-enabled real-time Decision Support Systems (DSS) with the possible incorporation of process mining and optimization techniques are critically $[119]$ $[65]$ $[94]$ in the dynamic healthcare working environment, .

## 3.1.2 Food supply chain [FSC]

IoT-based FSC applications cover the supply chain from farm to fork $[74]$ and include farm production, food processing, packaging, sales & marketing, logistics & distribution, retail, consumers' interactions, and waste management $[83]$ . These comprise five stages: produce, store, transport, sell, and consume $[74]$ .

Several factors facilitate research and deployment of IoT-based applications in FSC. We identify the following factors from related published literature: A) Variability and perishability of products [101][60][100][83][66]; B) Several recent food safety accidents [101][60][5][40][26], especially in Europe (e.g., the 2013 horse meat scandal) and in China (e.g., the 2008 Chinese milk scandal); C) The increasing foodborne illness cases [71][90][5] where foodborne and waterborne diseases kill about 2.2 million people annually (World Health Organization, 2002); D) The distance that food travels increases significantly with globalization [5]; E) End-consumers demand quality [5] [100] and do not sufficiently trust existing food labels [5] while the FSC is becoming increasingly customer-oriented [5]; F) Lack of feedback mechanism, unpredictable supply variation and consumer behavior changes [74][101][83][66]. According to the European Commission Information Society (2008), periods of overproduction and shortage alternate; G) Sustainable requirements are increasing [101][100][83] especially when it comes to the management of waste [5]: For example, 300 million tons of food products are wasted annually worldwide primarily due to deficient refrigeration (International Institute of Refrigeration); H) A large amount of data has already been collected but with poor integration and intelligent support[100].

Based on the above, we identify different needs and requirements that can explain the development of IoTrelated applications throughout the food supply chain: A) The need for an information system that encompasses the entire food supply chain [74][6][7]. Therefore the need for interoperability between each stakeholder's information system is strong and consequently the need for related global standards [5]; B) The need to obtain rich and real-time information on products throughout the FSC: location, origin, characteristics, product batch, history throughout the FSC processes [74] and with the appropriate level of granularity [101]; C) The need to maintain efficiently the integrity and quality of food products [71], especially to meet legal requirements and standards[5]; D) The need to provide transparency as well as qualitative and trustworthy information to end-consumers [71][5][83]; E) The entire industry needs to improve response time (e.g., for product recalls) [5]; F) The need to optimize and re-plan the processes efficiently and quickly while demand and supply are still hard to predict [101][5]; G) The need for intelligent solutions to analyze large amounts of heterogeneous data while seamlessly integrating data and devices for interoperability and flexibility purposes [100].

## 3.1.3 Mining and energy industries

In the mining and energy (oil & gas and nuclear) industries, several factors explain the popularity of IoT-related applications in research and in business applications: A) A significant number of professionals are directly or indirectly involved in the production process [111]; B) The production and operation facilities are numerous and widely distributed [113][67][43] (e.g., multiple site structure with both on-shore and off-shore oil facilities with long distances of separation among sites [67]); C) A large variety of processes and operations that could lead to a wide range of situations [70][2] (e.g., pipelines could be under water, underground or above the ground [2] or in harsh environmental conditions that include salt water, mountain or desert [99]); D) Development and increasing interest in cloud computing for oil exploration [62]; E) Several safety concerns (e.g., damage to the environment, loss of life) [70][67][59] such as the leaking oil pipeline in Michigan (USA) in 2010 that remained undetected for 17 hours [70] or risk of radioactive materials release [59]; F) Inaccurate collected data [51][60] that results from manual control processes and approximate estimation (e.g., in oil wells, daily production is estimated based on manually collected liquid samples) [113]; G) A lack of unified information network platforms that lead to large amounts of data that are isolated from one another [113].

We distinguish requirements that explain the increasing interest for IoT-based applications in the mining and energy industries: A) The need for a high degree of cooperation among different professions/departments/sites that make up the industry or a company [111][113][67]; B) The need for a real-time evaluation system [70][43]; C) The need for reliable and efficient intelligent tools to accurately monitor defects and error occurrence [67].

The main IoT-based applications in mining and energy industries (oil & gas, nuclear) include the digital mine [111], oil and gas exploration [62], oil and gas management operations [62][46] such as pipeline operations [70][67][2][108] and oil well operations [113][99], and digital nuclear power plant (NPP) [33][59][43][80].

## 3.1.4 Connected vehicles

Connected vehicles refer to the applications and the enabling technologies that connect the vehicle to its surroundings [97], both inside and outside the vehicle. In this industry, several factors are direct or indirect causes of the appeal for IoT-based applications: A) Urban traffic environment is complex and variable [120]: growing urbanization [61][93](especially in China [120]) leads to an increasing number of vehicles in large cities and therefore to more traffic congestion and economic challenges and environmental issues [61]; B) Aging population is also a factor in improving road safety [93]; C) Car accidents are numerous and are often lethal. There is room for improvement in order to reduce the number of deaths due to car accidents [110]. According to WHO (in 2002), $25\%$ of the deaths due to car accidents could be decreased by increasing safety warning systems at highway intersections [69]; D) Existing collected information are inaccurate and not sufficiently shared [[120]; E) The users demand increasing levels of data access while on road [61]; F) Governments facilitate the implementation of connected vehicle concepts and technologies by promoting regulation in favor of connected vehicles (e.g., in Europe, automatic emergency call systems will be mandatory in all new European cars by April 2018) [61][97]; G) The new transportation service delivery ecosystem (e.g., Zipcar, Uber) has changed consumers' vision on transportation services through more flexible multi-modal use [93].

From published research, we identify different needs/requirements that highlight the move toward IoT-related applications: A) The increasing need for situation awareness [97][48] among not only drivers but also all stakeholders such as pedestrians; B) The urgent need to mitigate traffic accidents and hazardous road traffic situations [97][61][48]; C) The need to improve traffic congestion [97] and the overall efficiency of road transportation systems[61]; D) The need for low latency and high reliability in real-time applications [61][93].

IoT-based applications in connected vehicles include traffic safety and efficiency, infotainment, parking assistance, roadside assistance, remote diagnostics, and telematics in autonomous self-driving vehicles $[97]$ , and classified as $[97]$ : (i) road traffic safety applications, (ii) road traffic efficiency and (iii) value-added services.

## 3.1.5 Building and infrastructure management

Building and infrastructure management is an industry that covers different applications from the ones that are underlined by the broad and general concept of Smart City $[92][112][77][88][49]$ to more detailed applications in emergency response operations (e.g., firefighting operations) $[25][109][115]$ . On one hand, the concept of Smart City includes several heterogeneous IoT-based applications: structural health of buildings, waste management, air quality monitoring, noise monitoring, traffic congestion, city energy consumption, smart parking, smart lighting, etc. $[112]$ . On the other hand, the IoT-based applications in emergency response management provide faster, more efficient, and effective emergency response operations $[25]$ [109]. They achieve an efficient coordination of the different participating organizations. They provide better situation awareness and enable the “visibility of response forces and their remaining capabilities” $[109]$ . They also handle production monitoring and scheduling in a distributed manufacturing environment $[35]$ .

In this industry, as mentioned in published research literature, IoT-based applications find attractiveness in different existing factors: A) Wide and heterogeneous range of resources that are also geographically highly distributed [92]; B) Ageing and growing population increases the pressure on various aspects of urban life [77]; C) The increase in the number and intensity of disasters (e.g., bird flu in 2003, earthquakes, fires, floods, terrorist attacks or incidents such as Nanjing propylene explosion accident in 2010) have increased interest in emergency technology and management in order to reduce the possibility of serious human casualties and property damage [25][109]; D) Situational awareness and human decision-making failures during catastrophic incidents such as Bhopal, the firefighters' deaths during 9/11 and the Three Mile Island nuclear crisis [109]; E) Development of emergency response information systems (ERISs) [109].

From these factors, we identify requirements mentioned in publications that match with potential value-added solutions provided by IoT-based applications: A) The need for visualizing various types and granularities for effective and efficient applications [92]; B) The need to transparently and seamlessly incorporate a large number of different and heterogeneous systems [112]; C) Behind the smart city concept, there are existing needs for efficient use of public resources with better quality and cost-effective services to citizens [112]; D) The need for better situation awareness and decision-making support in emergency response operations [25][109].

## 3.1.6 Conclusion: high-risk EHS industries

In Table 1, we summarize the main motivations in the industries that we considered. We identify some common attributes that are shared by high-risk EHS industries. Firstly, existing publications attempt to address issues on the lack of data availability or reliability (i.e., inaccurate data or data not sufficiently exploited). Secondly, as expected in high-risk EHS industries, increased safety (by reducing human errors or human and material casualties in disasters) is a motivation that is highlighted in publications. Thirdly, publications in high-risk EHS industries emphasize complexity and heterogeneity of the observed processes or things that require IoT-based applications to tackle them. The need for higher quality of services (especially for real-time services) seems to drive existing research on high-risk EHS industries. However, not all high-risk EHS industries share the same motivations. Ageing and growing population or customer-centric industry are changing factors that explain published research on IoT-based applications in several high-risk EHS industries but not in the entire sector. As a result, we also identify motivations that are shared among some industries but may be considered as points of difference inside the broader high-risk EHS sector (e.g., the growing sustainable requirements).

## 3.2 Specific characteristics of IoT applications in high-risk EHS industries

## 3.2.1 System architecture

A set of decision support features is provided by analyzing online information based on Simon's bounded rationality in [18]. The traditional architecture for remote health monitoring [41] comprises: A) A physical layer that consists of a Wireless Body Area Network (WBAN) for data acquisition and some services executions; B) communication and networking layer for data transmission; C) service layer for cloud processing.

In FSC, for similar layers (identification, sensing, actuation/data exchange/information integration/application services [101]), research papers propose specific architectures in response to FSC complexity and requirements. Therefore, supply chain-wide IoT systems can be [101]: A) Centralized: data are collected in shared databases; B) Linear: (especially traceability) data are transferred from one FSC stakeholder to the next; C) Distributed: FSC stakeholders interconnect their own information systems to exchange (especially traceability) data.

The distributed approach is the choice option in existing published research, especially with the development of the Electronic Product Code Information Services (EPCIS) [101]. But existing information and traceability systems in FSC are based specifically on the partial linear approach (“one up, one down”) [6]

In mining and energy industries, publications propose different architectures. One proposition is to integrate situation awareness in information systems to improve reliability of a real-time evaluation system $[70]$ , acting and tracking. The sensing mechanism enables the system to identify exceptions as well as collect abnormal data. The acting mechanism enables the operational decision maker to act accordingly through filtering, automatic business rule and workflow monitoring. Eventually the tracking mechanism enables him/her to follow up on the progress of the operations. Otherwise the structural components of the existing proposed architectures look very similar to that in other industries: an acquisition layer, a transport layer and a service layer [113].

IoT solutions architecture for intelligent transportation is shared between centralized and decentralized topology [97]. From the sensors layer, data are exchanged wirelessly via different communication technologies (especially short-range communication technology such as IEEE 802.11p and cellular networks such as 3G or LTE [97]). This communication system forms a vehicular ad hoc network (VANET) [97][61][110] that could be autonomous with processing capabilities. For example, fog computing could bring intelligence at the edge of the network[9]). Nevertheless, a centralized network topology such as cellular networks could be selected [97]. As a result, all data are transferred to the centralized network through base stations (BS) [97]. However, the dominant architecture in existing research uses a different networking level and not only that of a VANET or a centralized network [110][30]. To create a pervasive environment, the cloud resources could be organized in three layers [110]: a vehicular cloud that is an inter-vehicle network (VANET), the roadside cloud (a local cloud that is established with the adjacent roadside units), and a central cloud (that could be accessed through cellular communication or short-range communication with road-side infrastructure). In [30], those different levels of cloud computing form the paradigm that lead to the development of autonomous vehicles (AUV).

In building and infrastructure management, most of the services are based on a centralized architecture $[112]$ . Various heterogeneous data streams from peripheral devices deployed over the urban area converge toward a central back-end system, the control center, that provides storage and processing capabilities. Interoperability being a crucial issue, the IoT service architecture needs to link unconstrained protocols (commonly used as the Internet communication standard) and constrained protocols (required by constrained devices) $[112]$ .

For emergency response, information systems have stronger requirements, such as the need for dynamic and real-time information, to work in extreme and stress-filled environments [109].

## 3.2.2 Sensor level

In healthcare, the first specification for IoT-based applications arises from the sensor level. The sensor types themselves span a wide and heterogeneous range $[28]$ . For example, environmental passive sensors or body-centric RFID tags that are wearable or implantable. These can further be divided into more specific categories according to their different technical properties (e.g., the use of chemically interactive materials to measure temperature, humidity or the presence of specific gases) $[3]$ . These sensors need to have the capabilities to measure health-related information (e.g., detect chemical signatures) $[28]$ . A number of heterogeneous data inputs can be integrated with the IoT platform $[41]$ . Lastly, sensors used in healthcare have a focus on specific properties such as power consumption $[28]$ . Moreover sensor networks are expected to be non-invasive in order to not constrain patient's mobility and to be more easily accepted in a personal environment [86].

In FSC, IoT-based applications are focused on traceability. Therefore, the sensor layer in FSC is mainly made up of RFID tags that are not specific to the FSC domain $[74]$ . The essential characteristics of traceability sensors include breadth (i.e., the amount of collected data), depth (i.e., the continuity of the traceability information throughout the FSC), and precision (i.e., data reliability with reference to the movement of food products) $[5]$ . Since food product spoilage could have many causes (microbial infestation, bio-chemical changes, mechanical damages and physical changes $[74]$ ), the sensors used in these applications must be more heterogeneous to achieve richer information collection: future IoT applications for FSC should consider developing a comprehensive sensor portfolio in a systematic way $[74]$ . For instance, to detect pathogens in food products, the development of mobile safety laboratories are enabled by the combination of biosensors and IoT $[90]$ . $[24]$ proposes a sensor network architecture for FSC based on RFID tags and autonomous embedded modules.

In mining and energy industries, the sensor layer needs to be sophisticated to collect data from a wide range of sources. In the coal industry, sensors measure a few different data types: geo-stress data, seismic data, ventilation data, hydrology data, gas information, miners position, among others $[111]$ . The acquisition layer in oil well monitoring includes flow sensors, water temperature sensors and oil-water ratio sensors $[113]$ .

The sensor layer in these industries include a large and wide distribution of sensor nodes to collect essential data across various processes and operations $[2]$ . These nodes may be at different locations (under water, underground or above the ground). So, sensor calibration and positioning could be affected by many environmental effects (e.g., wind, humidity) $[2]$ . Therefore, the use of ground-penetrating radar techniques or acoustic transducers enable pipeline monitoring to be reliable and in real-time with the deployment of fewer nodes $[2]$ .

Eventually, energy efficiency and sensor lifetime are crucial parameters for sensor networks in these industries. The leverages to increase both these parameters include decreasing the wake-up time of the nodes or reducing data transmission rate. To this end, aggregated sensing, fog computing techniques via intelligent nodes or vibration energy harvester are possible solutions that could facilitate such leverage instantiations $[2]$ .

IoT-based applications use many heterogeneous sensors to generate and communicate accurate and relevant data. Advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS) are based on different sensor technologies in addition to vision/camera systems $[97]$ . The collected data could be classified as $[120]$ : traffic flow (e.g., flow rate, speed, density), traffic emergency information, real-time information about in-transit vehicles and drivers (e.g., location, driver tiredness status), and dynamic traffic control and management information.

As a consequence, the number of sensors that are employed in a vehicle is booming. By 2020, it is estimated that a vehicle could have as many as 200 intra-vehicle sensors [79]. These intra-vehicle sensors have specific characteristics: A) They are stationary, and enable the network to have a stable topology over time [61]; B) They are usually directly (one hop) connected to an electrical control unit (ECU) and form a simple star-topology [61]; C) They do not have energy constraints thanks to wired power connection in the vehicle [61].

In addition to intra-vehicle sensors in connected automobiles, road-side sensors also provide data that enable IoT-based applications in connected vehicles $[30]$ . There is a need to tackle sensor lifetime issue to avoid the need for mass replacements in shorter time periods since automobiles typically last longer than 10 years $[37]$ .

In building and infrastructure management, sensing devices are geographically highly distributed and heterogeneous [92][25]. They usually have constrained computational resources [92].

## 3.2.3 Communication

In healthcare, communication links are restricted by regulatory and industry standards especially when it comes to implantable devices. The sensors have to be compliant with local emission regulations $[3]$ .

In FSC, publications do not discuss specific communication standards. Information routing in distributed models occur through hybrid routing approach rather than a centralized one. By combining centralized and peer-to-peer routing to connect all users to one another, the IoT-based information system facilitates more confidentiality through direct communication while reducing data traffic with the central server $[100]$ .

In mining and energy industries, communication is a key feature of the IoT system because the sensors are widely scattered and distributed due in part to the specific structures and processes in those industries (e.g., sensors could be widely distributed underground as well as under water or over the ground). However traditional communication modes such as wireless digital radio, wireless bridge, satellite communication, and Zigbee result in a high error rate in these industries [113] (e.g., wireless signals are attenuated underground and under water [2]). Therefore, extensive use of the world's currently available mobile communication system, the GPRS or GSM, is necessary for comprehensive coverage [113][2]. In addition, since sensor nodes could be under water, underground or above the ground, the use of wireless communication is also combined with wired communication or electro-magnetic induction techniques to transmit the signal [2].

For nuclear power plant management, wireless communication has to deal with the challenges that are associated with electromagnetic interferences $[33]$ . Moreover, in these industries, designing, developing, and maintaining communication systems is a matter of high security. $[2]$ .

In connected vehicles, multiple categories of communication are required for seamless connected links between different components of the connected vehicle environment: A) Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication [97][9][61]; B) Vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) communication [97][9]. This category can further be divided into vehicle-to-sensor (V2S) communication, vehicle-to-Internet (V2I) communication, and vehicle-to-road infrastructure (V2R) communication [61]); C) Infrastructure-to-infrastructure (I2I) communication [9].

Here, infrastructure refers to access points such as Roadside units (RSUs), Wi-Fi, 3G, LTE, smart traffic lights, etc. [9]. Vehicle-to-Internet communication is commonly based on off-the-shelf technologies that are available for mass-market [61]. So connected vehicle manufacturers have brought in the connectivity from drivers' smartphones (3G/4G) or they have built in the connectivity by including cellular services in the car dashboard [61]. The other solution to have vehicle-to-Internet communication is a drive-thru Internet that allows the user to connect to the Internet when the vehicle is within Wi-Fi coverage [61][73]. This solution results in a lower cost for data acquisition but requires a huge infrastructure deployment to have good coverage.

In the connected vehicle industry, a communication challenge is due to the high mobility of the vehicles. High vehicle mobility generates high frequency changes in network topology while communication is limited and surrounding obstacles are numerous $[61]$ . Other challenges also exist in connected vehicles. The harsh environment in intra-vehicle communication is primarily a result of severe scattering within very limited space and often with no line-of-sight $[61]$ . If interferences are a problem, with an increasing number of connected vehicles and high urban density, spectrum scarcity is an issue that affects both V2V and V2R communication $[61]$ . Security is important to protect communication in intelligent transportation systems from malicious attacks $[65]$ . Multi-hop techniques are used to maintain connectivity between vehicles and the Internet $[61]$ .

In building and infrastructure management, the communication layer requires links across a wide geographical area to support transfer of potentially large amount of data among a large number of sensing devices $[112]$ . Based on their goal in regard to the realization of IoT-based applications, communication technologies are either unconstrained (traditional LAN, MAN, and WAN communication technologies such as Ethernet, WiFi) or constrained (IEEE 802.15.4, Bluetooth and Bluetooth Low Energy, IEEE 802.11 Low Power, PLC, NFC and RFID) $[76]$ . When unconstrained technologies offer higher reliability and throughput and lower latency, they require high energy consumption. This is one of the reasons why constrained technology is sometimes preferred in order to save energy while ensuring data transfer despite their lower quality.

## 3.2.4 Back-end system

Data analytics is crucial in healthcare for quick and accurate decision-making $[28]$ through data storing, reporting, visualization, monitoring or prediction. It enables IoT-based solutions to enhance traditional healthcare from a post facto diagnose-and-treat reactive paradigm to a proactive framework for disease prognosis [41], from a descriptive approach to a predictive one [16] especially when paired with machine-learning [16].

In FSC, the back-end system is crucial for IoT systems. By enabling information exchange among all FSC stakeholders, the back-end system allows IoT solutions to encompass the entire FSC $[74]$ . Moreover, data analytics is necessary to process the huge amount of collected data. Therefore, a back-end system with the right capabilities (storage and processing capacities, user-friendly user interfaces, etc.) facilitates better and more specific management of the FSC. For example, the First Expired First Out priority could be implemented to manage the inventory rather than the classical First In First Out logic $[83]$ .

In mining and energy industry, the key successful feature is a well-designed combination of cloud computing technology and an information management system $[62]$ . The back-end system usually includes a technical database, a real-time database, an enterprise resource planning database, and large data warehouse $[62]$ .

In intelligent transportation, the back-end system could play different roles. It could be the crucial component of a centralized network system such as in a cellular network $[97]$ . However, in more decentralized networks such as VANET $[97]$ , its role is more to provide a higher level of intelligence with more processing capabilities (analytics and big data) as well as storage capabilities $[9]$ .

In building and infrastructure management, the back-end system plays a central role because it gathers the data from various heterogeneous devices $[112]$ . The back-end system is the core of the control center that provides storage and processing capabilities. In emergency response management, the control center needs to gather continuous and real-time information to be effective and efficient during each emergency response moment (mobilization, preliminary situation assessment, and intervention) $[109]$ .

## 3.2.5 Business/market aspects

IoT-based healthcare applications could cover a wide consumer market demand despite its reliability and regulation issues $[50]$ . These applications have better intrinsic value indeed because they tend to trigger more systemic effects $[20]$ . Healthcare industry is different from traditional industries because human lives are at stake. IoT solutions consider safety as the top priority with the consequence that this industry tends to be more resistant to change. The expected payback period on investment is also long $[65]$ .

In FSC, IoT-based applications are still at an early stage $[74]$ . The state of current FSC could explain this fact. FSC is very complex indeed: A) it includes a large diversity of stakeholders and industries from farm-to-fork $[71]$ $[74][100]$ with the dominance of SMEs $[83]$ ; B) it is spread across the world and, with globalization, food products increasingly travel longer distances $[74][5]$ ; C) the processes are very complex: converging and diverging flows (e.g., two raw agricultural products are processed to form one food product; conversely, several products such as meat and milk are from one animal) and high process variety (produce, store, transport, sell, consume [74]) [74][101][100]; D) the quality of food products changes throughout the entire FSC [5].

In mining and energy industry, IoT-based applications could result in significant competitive gains. In mining, self-driving vehicles promise to raise productivity by 25% and output by 5% or more and cut health and safety costs as much as 20% by reducing the number of workplace accidents $[12]$ . However, IoT-based applications not only involve the collection of data but also the provision of real-time decision-making support with the help of collected data. In the oil-drilling industry, only 1% of the data from the 30,000 sensors on a typical oil rig are used $[12]$ , signifying the huge potential when more such data are used to make decisions.

## 3.2.6 Conclusion: High-risk EHS industries

To summarize, we gather the main general characteristics of IoT applications by industry in Table 2. Overall, in high-risk EHS industries, system architecture design is divided between centralized architecture for more encompassing IoT-based applications and decentralized architecture for more sophisticated and specific IoT-based applications. In the sensor layer, devices are usually numerous and widely distributed across physical locations. They are also heterogeneous in order to collect data from complex and heterogeneous environment (processes or things). In terms of communication, standardization is highly required in order to reach better interoperability among heterogeneous data sources. However, resource-constrained devices require novel solutions to overcome the constraints. Solutions such as multi-hop routing have become prevalent or widely adopted in existing research. For back-end system, the same division as in system architecture design is seen. Centralized back-end systems gather all the data from all sources to provide services (especially big data and storage). Decentralized architecture leads to the back-end system as a support for a more autonomous system. Additionally, it brings intelligence and storage capabilities. Since business aspects are more various and heterogeneous, it is difficult to identify a trend among the high-risk EHS industries.

## 4 IoT-related challenges in high-risk EHS industries

## 4.1 Challenges as mentioned in published research papers

Current research in high-risk EHS industries attempts to understand and address the gap between small implementations of successful research projects $[91]$ and large systematic adoption in the entire industry: in healthcare industry $[75][50][19][20]$ , in FSC $[74][101]$ , in mining and energy industries $[62][113]$ , in intelligent transportation industry $[98][61][37]$ , and in building and infrastructure management industry $[112][103]$ .

To successfully become mainstream and to deploy large IoT systems throughout the specific high-risk EHS industry, the IoT-based applications need to deal with the following challenges. We can classify them as traditional technical IoT-related challenges - that are commonly listed as mentioned in the introduction - and social and economic challenges that deal more with the environment rather than the IoT system itself.

The following are the IoT-related technical challenges: A) Energy efficiency; B) Communication and data-related challenges (connectivity, latency, throughput, standardization); C) Scalability (network size, interoperability); D) Security and safety (reliability, privacy protection).

For social and economic challenges, extant literature identifies the following: A) Business model B) Standardization: For instance, in healthcare, researchers have developed several different protocols especially for wearable devices without taking into account the interoperability with other systems [86]. In China, for example, hospitals have developed their own information systems based on their own standards that makes future higher integration (regional or national) difficult [117]; C) Compliance with regulatory and industry standards; D) Community support and staff training; E) Affordability vs. high cost of implementation; F) Absence of global or national standards and regulation; G) Time-to-market for IoT-based applications.

## 4.2 Proposed solutions offered by published research papers

## 4.2.1 Healthcare industry

Research suggests strategic placement of gateways in IoT systems to enhance their properties in order to deal with energy efficiency issues, and data availability and reliability issues while meeting patient's needs (mobility and convenience requirements, non-invasive devices) [82][75][64]. The gateway or bridging point is the unit that connects the sensor network to the back-end system, which is usually a remote health center. In the healthcare industry, those gateways are stationary and reduce resource constraints, in contrast with low-power sensor nodes that require high energy-efficiency to last longer and reliably record critical health information. Thus the gateways can be enhanced with more properties: local data storage, local data processing power (data compression, fusion, filtering), data mining (the gateways could form a so-called “fog computing layer”), security-related tasks (thanks to the processing and memory properties) [82]. The gateways also make interoperability (in terms of device, protocol or data) and reconfigurability easier [82]. As a result, the “smart e-Health gateway” could become a local database that ensures data availability and that performs intelligent processes to deliver quicker preliminary information without compromising the energy-efficiency of sensor networks [82].

Some research publications mention multi-hop networks in IoT-based healthcare applications $[64]$ $[39]$ . Existing research defines Body-to-Body Network (BBN) as a mesh network that uses people to transmit data within a limited geographic area $[64]$ . This concept can readily deal with user mobility requirement as well as issues like energy-efficiency and reliability (quality of service[QoS]). Such multi-hop communication offers the possibility to connect larger sensor networks by enabling more wireless links between each node $[39]$ . The WBANs are connected to each other and data routing is created to transfer the data up until the connector device (i.e., the gateway). Thus BBN design should consider an energy-efficient routing, QoS-aware traffic management and mobility management $[64]$ . However, new and traditional challenges appear with the BBN concept such as wireless channel propagation issues (energy-efficiency, interference and coexistence of different communication technologies that share the same radio spectrum, interoperability issues, ethical challenges) $[64]$ .

Another solution that has been recommended to improve IoT solutions in healthcare is parallel processing $[22]$ . Task level parallelism (TLP) consists of processing different health data streams in parallel. The use of multicore technology enables optimization of available resources in terms of processing performance and power consumption. As a result the sensor network has the potential to have an increased lifetime.

Low adoption rate could be managed by providing an IoT solution with a value-centric approach rather than a technology-centric one $[75]$ . Therefore the business plan is as important as the integrative architecture of the IoT solution. A win-win situation could be created by a carefully designed surplus-sharing contract under which both the supplier and the health care organization will improve with change in the scanning system [15]. In order to integrate in-home healthcare in a larger IoT solution, [75] proposed a business plan that consists of a cooperative ecosystem between traditional healthcare providers and Internet content providers. Moreover, different types of IT-enabled capabilities could shape, and be shaped by, the home health care provider's responses to environmental changes [89]. A value-centric approach could result in higher user acceptance especially if medical staff (e.g., physicians, nurses) are aware of the overall value generated as well as that specifically for them [19]. [54] finds that technology push, need pull, and presence of champions are determinants of RFID adoption in organizations, and the relationship between those three categories and the likelihood of adopting RFID are strengthened or weakened by organizational readiness. As a result, training and awareness campaigns have an important role in wider adoption [19]. [50] also recommends taking a piecemeal approach to bring IoT innovation in healthcare in order to avoid the resilience from this particular conservative industry and why [107] thinks perceived service availability (PSA) and personal innovativeness in IT (PIIT) may be the important drivers in TAM and TPB. In general, RFID barriers are considered to be lower and benefits to be higher by both future implementers and current implementers compared to non-implementers [84].

Several publications propose theoretical or pilot projects to tackle different issues such as better integration of pervasive healthcare services with general healthcare services $[75]$ $[86]$ , an adaptive knowledge-based system for healthcare logistics $[65]$ , a leaner and more parsimonious model than the DeLone and McLean model which identifies the particular factors that are important to achieve success $[17]$ , higher privacy protection and more security in IoT-based healthcare applications $[85]$ $[45]$ , the creation of an IoT solution through the integration of eHealth smart devices, telemedicine instruments and hospital information systems $[29]$ , a 2Bin inventory system for controlling medical supplies $[87]$ or the development of real-time IoT-based solutions for mobile health with a focus on interoperability (by using standard IoT hardware and an existing operating system) $[39]$ .

## 4.2.2 Food supply chain

Proposals for novel architectures or IoT platform implementation that encompasses the entire FSC exist. But those solutions struggle to be adopted by FSC stakeholders. The lack of a comprehensive information system that encompasses the entire FSC could be due to a lack of comprehensive picture among FSC stakeholders $[74]$ . The prevailing use of RFID solutions and a technology-centric approach (focus on what the technology provides) in existing research leads to a low adoption rate or a poor collaboration toward larger IoT solutions. The dominant framework should be value-centric (focus on what the technology should provide). IoT-based applications with income-centric (e.g., shelf-life prediction, sales premium, precision food production, insurance cost reduction) focus are more likely to be adopted because of an expected shorter payback period $[74]$ .

Other barriers have been identified to explain or avoid unsuccessful realization of large IoT platforms $[101]$ : A) The potential unwillingness to share information among stakeholders; B) The lack of confidence regarding data security and reliability; C) Limitations of existing contractual relationships and pricing strategies; D) The need for critical mass; E) The uncertainty about impact on business practices and business model.

To overcome these barriers, existing research papers recommend the use of generic technologies and SaaS-approaches (Software as a service) as proposed in the FIspace platform [101][7].

The trustworthiness of the IoT system is crucial for its successful adoption. It is used in pilot projects such as the Internet of Agricultural Things (AIoT) with the use of GS1 standards and electronic pedigree $[60]$ . With IoT, physical objects can leave their traces in the cyber space: electronic pedigree is an example of this kind of digital signature. Thus electronic pedigree is a crucial technology that has been enhanced for the specific use of FSC in existing research: features have been added from the pedigree standard (EPCglobal, GS1 now) to reach more trustworthy tracking service to monitor and supervise the production and supply of food $[40]$ .

Several research papers propose novel architectures (such as [101][100][116][7]) or even propose the creation of pilot projects (such as [60] in China or [121][66][47]). Both focus on finding IoT-based solutions that could encompass the entire FSC or make each stakeholder's integration within the FSC easier. Thus the Smart Agri-Food Logistics systems [100][47] succeed to gather three key features: real-time virtualization, logistics connectivity (good throughput and reliable data exchange) and logistics intelligence (i.e., extracted intelligent analysis and reports from the collected data). The information system benefits also from high flexibility and scalability through the use of Generic Enablers and Domain-Specific Enablers. They are software modules that can be easily re-used and shared to enable further customization (pick, plug and play) [100][47]. Eventually the objective of the Smart Agri-food Project is to create a marketplace for IoT-based applications in FSC similar to Google Play or Apple Store [47]. This market place, the FIspace platform, enables searching for and the use of specific services and applications such as Greenhouse management and control scenarios [7].

## 4.2.3 Mining and energy industries

Existing research highlights the key role of cloud computing technology and big data analytics in the development of IoT-based applications in mining and energy industries (oil & gas, nuclear) $[62][70][67][2]$ . Cloud computing technology has the potential to overcome some IoT-related challenges: throughput (for example with the batch processing job approach $[62]$ ) or affordability (renting infrastructure equipment and using service providers of cloud computing applications $[62]$ ). Overall cloud computing technology enables more innovation in the industry through the use of open source software that could benefit the entire industry $[62]$ . Moreover the use of machine learning techniques increases the functionalities and reliability of big data analytics. For instance, the use of magnetic flux leakage (MFL) sensors and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) allows for a better and more reliable estimation of the defect depth in oil and gas pipelines [67].

The main mentioned solution in existing research to tackle the communication issue in these industries is the use of GPRS or GSM, the world's current mobile communication system, for comprehensive coverage [113][2]. To optimize the communication and data transmission process and in the end reduce crucial energy consumption, the gateways and sensor nodes could have processing capabilities in order to pre-process the data through compression or filtering techniques [2]. In [99], the network structure for oil well monitoring and control system is designed with the adoption of a clustering algorithm in order to lower energy consumption and so increase communication reliability. Moreover using multi-hop routing enables the systems to reduce the communication range of each node. It results in a more energy efficient solution [2]. The combination of multi-hop routing and computational capabilities results in better secure communication because in case of failure or malicious disruptions, the wireless networks can reconfigure the topology in order to ensure data transmission [43]. Even in nuclear power plant management, multi-hop routing is a proposed solution to electro-magnetic interferences and other constraints that reduce communication reliability [33][43]. [33] implements multi-hop routing via Peer-to-Peer communication in a Zigbee wireless sensor network. However this last solution has a low adoption rate in practice because sensor deployment does not have a predefined topology [2].

For IoT-based applications in mining and energy industries, published research includes theoretical frameworks or implemented projects. To create a digital mines system, $[111]$ proposes a framework including the main expected applications in the industry: mining digital platforms, production technology management system, safety management system, and production information integrated query system. Others deal with similar frameworks that enable the mining and energy industries to have guidelines to integrate and implement IoT-related applications in mining $[80]$ , oil production industry $[46][99]$ , and nuclear power plant management $[59]$ .

Pilot IoT-based system projects or applications in oil pipeline monitoring $[70]$ $[108]$ and in radiation monitoring at nuclear facilities $[33]$ $[80]$ have been implemented. $[70]$ proposes a small/medium-scale project for reliable predictive maintenance system (PMS) in oil and gas pipeline operations. Situation awareness design (sensing, acting, tracking) supports better and standardized management and monitoring of oil and gas pipeline operations. $[108]$ implements an oil and gas pipeline nondestructive testing system through the combination of ultrasonic testing and magnetic flux leakage (MFL) sensors. $[80]$ has implemented and tested a passive sensor that is based on hydrogen-pressure dosimeters for wireless high radiation monitoring in nuclear power plants.

## 4.2.4 Connected vehicles

Enabling reliable and efficient communication while taking into account the high mobility of the things in intelligent transportation is crucial. To this end, several institutions (especially the European Telecommunications Standards Institute, ETSI) have proposed standards for all parts of the protocol stack $[97]$ . The aim of standardization is the interoperability between different components, devices or systems. For instance, in Europe, 30MHz has been set apart only for the use of vehicular communication at 5.875-5.905GHz in road traffic safety applications; for non-safety-related applications, it is 20 MHz at 5.855-5.875 GHz $[97]$ .

Existing published research also highlights some solutions to address the mobility challenge. GeoNetworking enables a station to adapt data routing by focusing more on the list of neighbors it can hear rather than maintain routing tables $[97]$ . $[110]$ proposes a three-layered architecture for the vehicular network (vehicular cloud, roadside cloud and central cloud) in order to achieve efficiency, continuity and reliability of cloud services for mobile vehicles. Another solution creates a communication path (e.g., with several trucks) that could lead to less mobility-related interferences and therefore to a more reliable signal $[61]$ . However this technique is feasible only on a highway and not in urban areas where line-of-sight is blocked by the surrounding environment. Eventually, these solutions rely on higher level cooperation among vehicles by using multi-hop techniques to relay data through V2V and V2R communications with more reliability $[61]$ .

Dedicated short-range communications (DSRC) use for V2V and V2R communications leads to spectrum scarcity risk $[61]$ . A solution is the use of complementary technology such as dynamic spectrum access (DSA). It allows vehicles to communicate opportunistically on spatially and/or temporally vacant licensed spectrum of other communication systems. Another solution is the use of TV white space for V2V communication in urban areas due to its propagation and building penetration properties vs. the DSRC frequency band $[104]$ .

To reconcile mobility challenges and the need for real-time information and services, the use of fog computing is an option $[9]$ . Fog computing brings intelligence to network edge by providing constrained processing capabilities at network nodes. It results in better latency and location awareness. It also allows for widespread node location in networks while ensuring mobility and interoperability among the large number of nodes.

If data privacy and security concerns are solved, [93] identifies five connected vehicle killer applications that rely on the integration of multiple data streams: fatigue detection, real-time assistance for parking and accidents, anger management and stress reduction, keyless authentication, and Do-it-yourself (DIY) diagnostics.

The regulatory aspect of privacy is significant. For example, the European Parliament has strengthened the draft law's data-protection clause to align with mandatory implementation of the automatic emergency call (eCall) system in all new European cars by April 2018 [71]. On the other hand, [98] defines the requirements to visualize the design quality of the system with a focus on safety and privacy. With the same focus, $[52]$ reviews architectural requirements (confidentiality, integrity, authentication, communication and timing) and existing solutions (such as honeypots attached to wireless gateway to learn about new attacks and then to improve the next system version) to deal with safety and privacy concerns and so avoid disastrous safety implications. $[114]$ provides another solution framework to protect connected vehicles from malware attacks.

There are also proposed designs or implementations of IoT-based applications (such as the five killer applications for connected car $[61]$ , the pedestrian safety application through automatic braking or evasive steering $[48]$ ) or supporting components (such as a platform for prototyping telematics services $[37]$ ) in order to increase the adoption rate of IoT-based applications in the automotive industry. Through a prototyping platform for connected-vehicle software and application templates, $[37]$ presents a pattern-based approach to decrease time-to-market of IoT-based applications (thanks to reusable functionality and guidelines) and increase flexibility (each of the platform services are designed to deal with unpredictable workloads). $[32]$ implements a road condition monitoring and alerting application. This application relies on the use of an in-vehicle Smartphone and the concepts of crowd sourcing and participatory sensing. It offers an affordable and energy-efficient solution while using rich processing capabilities (of smartphones) and protecting data privacy (via anonymization).

## 4.2.5 Building and infrastructure management

Some studies present solutions to tackle interoperability issues. [92] introduces a framework for a decentralized service-oriented cloud platform for the deployment and execution of context [57] and user-centric IoT applications. This framework provides interoperability through a decentralized cloud platform that results in scalability and flexibility for real-time smart city applications. Some research studies go further by proposing frameworks to realize a unified urban-scale Information and Communications Technology (ICT) platform [42][68]. On a smaller scale, an experimental study at the University of Padova (Italy) implemented a smart city to promote early-adoption of open data IoT-based applications in public administration [14]. A smart lighting application was developed through the deployment of a wireless sensor network with more than 300 nodes.

Interoperability issues could sometimes be related to political issues with the need for cooperation among all stakeholders. In smart city applications, $[102]$ suggests the creation of a dedicated department in the city to avoid arguments about the attribution of decision-making power among different stakeholders.

Other solutions tackle general IoT challenges and issues that undermine the applications' credibility and attractiveness. [77] proposes a business model for smart cities: sensing as a service. It consists of creating a marketplace for sensor-generated data that benefits from the creation of a win-win situation: data publishers and service providers as intermediaries between sensor owners and data consumers. This cloud computing environment, through participatory sensing, reduces data acquisition cost, incentivizes people to get sensors and to share data, and so makes more data available for potential service providers. However, even if anonymization could tackle the data privacy issue, many challenges still remain and make this model less attractive. For $[115]$ , the government should be the leading entity to build a safety management model because it could tackle issues related to social acceptance (e.g., acceptance of the pervasive sensors) by ensuring public welfare.

## 4.2.6 Conclusion: high-risk EHS industries

We classify existing proposed solutions by previously identified challenges (Table 4). Existing published research in high-risk EHS industries seems to be focused on offering solutions to tackle technical challenges more than social and economic issues. As safety concerns are very high in high-risk EHS industries, existing research attempts to focus first on reliable and scalable IoT-based applications rather than on adoption rate (even if some research papers tackle such issues especially for more advanced IoT-based applications).

## 5 Research challenges and future trends

Based on our review of existing published literature, we identify several future trends in healthcare: A) The Internet of Bio-Nanothings [1]: Synthetic biology and nanotechnology tools that allow the engineering of biological embedded computing devices through biological cells' functionalities (execution of DNA-based instructions, the transformation of chemical energy, molecular communication), new IoT solutions offer huge opportunities such as intra-body sensing and actuation. However it comes with challenges for research such as the interconnection between heterogeneous networks that support both electrical and biochemical domains (biocyber interface issues) [1] B) The convergence of pervasive healthcare systems and smart environment systems [82]. This is the result of a home-centered paradigm in healthcare services. The increasing integration of smartphones and IoT healthcare solutions [29][39] could lead to more convergence between both environments as smartphones play an important role in the everyday lives of a large proportion of the population.

Based on our review of existing literature in FSC, we identify future research trends. The challenges and trends include: A) The use of machine learning algorithms to develop valuable prediction models $[71]$ and to better overcome specific FSC constraints (such as decentralized and heterogeneous supply vs. unpredictable demand changes resulting in 8.3% of worldwide out of stock rate $[71]$ ) and thus increase reliability of IoT-based applications such as shelf-life prediction or precision agriculture $[74]$ or real-time pricing mechanism $[71]$ B) The use of sophisticated sensors that could reliably record relevant information (e.g., for liquid products or bulk products). Such sensors could benefit from technological advancements in isotope analysis, DNA barcoding, chemometrics, infrared spectroscopy (Near Infrared Spectroscopy and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy)

and Raman spectroscopy [6][26]. However those technologies are still in testing phases or are too expansive to be easily adopted by FSC stakeholders [6]. According to [26], with the current state of research, spatially offset Raman spectroscopy (SORS) seems to be the most convenient solution (rapid, user-friendly, already portable devices) to analyze food (thanks to its ability to collect chemically rich information through barriers or packaging) and so to expand the portfolio of sensors in IoT-based FSC applications.

In the mining and energy industries: The development of simple routing protocols could result in both decreased computational complexity and power consumption. Hence the end goal is to increase the network lifetime while implementing a more reliable and energy-efficient communication model $[2]$ .

In the intelligent transportation industry: A) The implementation of expensive multiple radio interfaces may slow down the deployment of IoT-based applications in connected cars. Future research could focus on low cost unified solution and better adoption rate [61]; B) Research highlights vehicular cloud benefits[110][30]. Future research could address the gap between theoretical frameworks and large-scale implementations.

In building and infrastructure management: Reliable and efficient routing protocols enable implementation of IoT-based applications for emergency response. [81] proposes a routing protocol for IoT-based Emergency Response with Global Information Decision (ERGID). It enhances real-time response capability of the network by improving data transmission (less end-to-end delay and packet loss) while reducing power consumption.

These trends for IoT-based applications in high-risk EHS industry incur evolving technical challenges in the information system, specifically those in communication (e.g., routing issues) and processing capabilities (especially for more predictive applications). Nevertheless, some future trends allow the possibility for novel solutions, especially in healthcare where technology-centric innovations are put forward.

Future IoT- and DSS- enabled research can be guided to tackle the main challenges in the EHS industry such as A) healthcare: security and privacy, standardization, and scalability; B) FSC: standardization, interoperability, flexibility, and scalability to develop IoT-based applications that encompass the entire supply chain; C) mining and energy industries: communication and energy consumption challenges and reliability issues; D) connected vehicles: reliable and efficient connectivity and communication are crucial as well as security and privacy issues; E) building and infrastructure management: interoperability and standardization challenges and privacy issues seem to be among the most important issues.

## 6 Conclusion

We provided a comprehensive overview of publications on high-risk EHS industries from an IoT-based perspective. Given the explosion of IoT-based applications and the significance of high-risk EHS industries, it is necessary to take stock of existing studies and results, and to identify gaps that can be addressed. The specific focus of our study is the high-risk EHS sector: healthcare services, FSC, mining and energy industries (oil & gas and nuclear), intelligent transportation, and building and infrastructure management with emphasis on emergency response operations. We discussed the motivations for existing research on IoT-based applications in high-risk EHS industries. We then highlighted specific characteristics of IoT-based applications in these industries: architecture design, sensor layer, communication, back-end system and business aspects. Concerning IoT-related challenges in high-risk EHS industries, we observe that existing research places more emphasis on technical challenges rather than social and economical issues. This focus leads to solutions that first tackle technical issues. Future research challenges and trends are similar: the main focus seems to be dedicated to technical challenges and trends, especially in communication and processing capabilities.

Both IoT technologies and the EHS industry have been developing very rapidly in recent years. We consider only published scientific articles. We did not consider trade publications such as magazines&newspapers, and patents. Another limitation is the absence of a benchmark for reference due to the lack of prior review in this general subject area. We believe that this comprehensive review, based on a large number of selected papers, provides an informative guidance for future advancement of IoT technologies in the EHS industry.
