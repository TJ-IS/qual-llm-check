# A review of methods used on IT maturity models development: A systematic literature review and a critical analysis

Rúben Pereira and João Serrano

Journal of Information Technology 1–18 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2020 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621988687DOI: 10.1177/0268396219886874 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf SAGE

## Abstract

Maturity models can be seen as support tools for an organization. Their importance is increasing in the scientific community and IT (Information Technology) organizations are starting to implement them. The main objective of maturity models is to evaluate and improve the organization’s practices by creating an improvement roadmap. However, the utilization of the methodologies and methods by this community for the development of this kind of tools is not consensual. Several investigators have created guidelines for the development of maturity models, but the authors are not adopting them; they prefer to adopt their own methodologies. In this research, with the objective of reviewing the methodologies, methods, and guidelines used by the scientific community to develop IT maturity models, a Systematic Literature Review and a Critical analysis were made in order to realize a comparison between IT maturity models and Non-IT maturity models. In total, 109 articles of maturity models’ development were analyzed. A discussion of the articles’ results was realized.

## Keywords

IT maturity models, methodologies, methods, development, systematic literature review

## Introduction

One of the main objectives of the organizations is to become self-sustainable by improving their capabilities in an economical manner. Hence, enterprises need to evaluate their current position to plan their proper investments (SchÃ, 2018) in a strategic way, as the knowledge of the maturity level of an organization is important to its improvement and evolution. The value of maturity concept is increasing in organizations being important to their development, coming to the point of being identified as a contingency factor for the adoption and improvement of governance structures in organizations (Pereira and Mira da Silva, 2012). This shows that more knowledge in this area is important and more research in this domain is needed. However, to “measure” the level of maturity in organizations, traditional measures were inadequate and consequently a new “assessing methodology” known as Maturity Models (Karni and Kaner, 2013) emerged. Maturity models are becoming gradually more important to organizations in different domains (Hammerschmid et al., 2017; Patas et al., 2013). Maturity models can be seen as a tool, used to evaluate the as-is state of an organization and to enhance organization’s capabilities by defining an improvement path, displaying the best procedures to increase process’s effectiveness and efficiency (Proença, 2016). Therefore, organizations are using maturity models to evaluate their methods and processes in accordance with the good management practices (Aguiar et al., 2018) This evaluation is “constructed” by a comparison between a set of criteria and characteristics, provided by a maturity model, and the organization activities behavior, shown in a gradual scale, assigning a state or a maturity level to an organization capability or a capability combination (Lã, 2011). One of the maturity models’ main roles is to identify organization’s weaknesses and strengths (Lahrmann et al., 2010).

This concept begun to be recognized 40 years ago (Carvalho et al., 2017), since then, numerous maturity models have been developed (Mettler and Rohner, 2009), provoking a substantial adherence by organizations of all areas and the attention of the research community (Achi et al., 2016). Despite that, the methodologies and methods used to develop this kind of “tools” are not consensual. Few authors of the research community have created guidelines for the “construction” of this type of models specific for IT domain, but many authors still do not adopt these methods, using “Ad Hoc” methodologies. This research used the Systematic Literature Review methodology to review the methodologies and methods used by the “scientific environment” on the development of maturity models in several domains. In total, 109 articles published in top journals and conferences were analyzed.

This research is structured as follows. The next section introduces the methodology used to gather information and obtain the final articles. The “Discussion” section contains a discussion about the results achieved. Finally, in the last section some conclusions of this research and its limitations are presented.

## Methodology

Systematic Literature Review is an approach to conduct a rigorous literature review. The authors Okoli and Schabram (2010) define this methodology as “a systematic, explicit and reproducible method for identifying, evaluating, and synthesizing the existing body of completed and recorded work produced by researchers, scholars, and practitioner.” Systematic Literature Review can improve literature reviews by bringing transparency and rigor in numerous ways (Mallett et al., 2012).

With the purpose of addressing the objective of this research, as aforementioned a systematic literature review was conducted. This systematic literature review was based on the guidelines of the author Kitchenham (2004) and the authors Jane Webster and Watson (2002). The steps taken to conduct this review are visible in Figure 1.

## Outlining systematic literature review

In an initial brief review with no keywords yet defined, it was possible to state that there is a considerable discrepancy of the methodologies and methods used on the maturity model’s development. The main objective of this research was the review of the methodologies and methods used by community research for the development of this tool, separating the IT articles from those who are not from the IT domain.

Furthermore, to obtain more information about the creation of maturity models, five electronic repositories were selected:

IEEE Xplore Digital Library (https://ieeexplore. ieee.org/Xplore/home.jsp);

• ResearchGate (https://www.researchgate.net);

• SpringerLink (https://link.springer.com);

• Elsevier (https://www.sciencedirect.com);

• ACM (https://dl.acm.org).

This review included only English and Portuguese articles and articles published on journals or scientific magazines and conferences proceedings were exclusively accepted. In addition, no date filter was used.

Initially, a search with the selected keywords in each repository was done without any filter. After that, four filters were created. However, all the electronic libraries use different “search approaches,” so a keyword adaptation for each repository was realized. It should be mentioned that in ResearchGate, each article was added to the support tool manually since it does not have a filtrate option and it is not possible to download all the results at once.

The first filter applies the keywords to the article title, or to the abstract or author keywords; on the second one, duplicated articles are removed; on the third filter, articles that are published in lower rank publications/journals are removed. For that reason, two websites were used, Scimago<sup>1</sup> and Conference Ranks,<sup>2</sup> which provide journals and conferences ranks, respectively. For conferences, only A, B, A1, A2, B1, and B2 ranks of ERA (Excellence in Research in Australia) and Qualis rank ings were accepted. When an article was assessed by both rankings, Qualis prevailed. For journals only Q1 and Q2 ranks were accepted. Finally, the last stage of filtration was realized by assessing articles introduction. The inclusion criteria of this filter were only the selection of articles that had the development of maturity models present. The articles that did not meet this requirement were rejected.

Specific keywords (listed below) for this search were used in all repositories with operators AND and OR, with the “Maturity Model” concept being the main keyword.

Keywords: “Maturity Model” AND (“Frameworks” OR “Best practices” OR “Management” OR “Research Method”)

Since many of the developed maturity models are based on frameworks, like Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies (COBIT), Information Technology Infrastructure Library (ITIL), or even on SPICE or on Total Data Quality Management (TDQM) frameworks, the authors decided to add the “Frameworks” keywords, not with the intention to shortening the final results, but in order to obtain the maturity models that had a bigger “impact” in the research community, by following established Frameworks by the scientific community. With the same view, this research used the “Best practices” and “Management” keywords, for the purpose of centralizing the maturity models that are constituted of a union of best practices and are a support tool for the management of organization “activities” in any domain.

## Conducting a systematic literature review

As aforementioned, result articles needed to “proceed” through four filters in each repository. Filtration process, by each online repository and each filter, is visible in Table 1.

As mentioned before, the articles of ResearchGate repository were added manually. Since the ResearchGate introduces the articles in a list form, the stopping criteria for this case was, when the articles below started to be about other “themes”. For this reason, the first filter was not applied in this repository. Since ResearchGate is a social network for professionals where it is possible to publish their own articles, accessible for all the community, there may be articles from other online libraries. In fact, in the ResearchGate search, six articles from IEEE repository and four from SpringerLink were found that were not found in their own repository with the same keywords, which shows the differences between each repository search approach.

The first filter had the purpose to separate the articles that are exclusively related with both domains of those who just made a reference of these concepts in the article body, by just selecting the ones that had the keywords in the title, abstract, and author keywords. These three article sections were chosen for being the main parts that summarize the article’s matter. With this filter, it was possible to discard a substantial number of articles.

The second filter had the intention to eliminate the duplicated articles. It is observable that most of the eliminated articles are from ResearchGate repository, due to the facts mentioned earlier.

On the third filter, 940 articles were ranked by their publication rank, which was in total nearly 625 publications since various articles had the same publication (conference/journal). Consequently, 408 article introductions were read and “evaluated” by inclusion criteria, which resulted in 109 final articles. The flow of the filtration process is visible in Figure 2.

## Contextualizing and definitions

Before proceeding to the discussion, it is important to “contextualize” the research. As mentioned before, several important concepts were extracted, which we called “vectors.” Three of those vectors are the research methodology, the methods, and the guidelines for the maturity model development. However, in some articles, a lack of clarification about the methodologies and methods adopted still exists.

To extract this type of information, it was essential to establish the definitions of the methodology and method concepts that are subjacent to this research since some of the literature continue to “confuse” the definition of these two concepts. Nevertheless, the definition of these concepts continues to be an open discussion in the research community. It is important to, in an equivalent manner, define the guidelines concept. This act of defining these concepts that underlie this research was done with the purpose of making an attempt of “normalization” of the structure in this kind of research, even knowing that the definition and structure can be different, depending on the context in which the research is inserted.

The authors Mackenzie and Knipe (2006) defined methodology as “the overall approach to research linked to the paradigm or theoretical framework.” In accordance was the author Crotty (2011) by defining the methodology as “the strategy, plan of action, process or design lying behind the choice and use of particular methods and linking the choice and use of methods to the desired outcomes,” that is, the principles or steps adopted to conduct the research.

In a similar way, the authors Mackenzie and Knipe (2006) defined the method as “systematic modes, procedures or tools used for collection and analysis of data.” The author Crotty (2011) has described the method as “the techniques or procedures used to gather and analyze data related to some research question or hypothesis.” Following these definitions, the authors of this research defined the methods concept as the “tools” used to extract information to accomplish the “methodology steps.” To address this principle, the authors of this research assumed that some of the methodologies extracted in this type of case, that is the development of a maturity model, and following the established definitions, can be considered methods as well. In this way, this kind of methodologies found was established as methods since they are used as an “auxiliary tool” to extract and gather information for the methodology pursuance until the final objective. Some of these examples that were found are Systematic Literature Review, Interviews, or even Case Studies.

This research assumed that the guidelines for the creation of a maturity model are the practical procedure, that is, the guidelines can be seen as a methodology inside of a research methodology. These guidelines are the steps needed to take in order to create an object as the maturity model.

Several articles were interpreted, and some of them did not “follow” any established methodology. When the steps taken to the development point are described and do not follow any established methodology, this research entitled that approach as an “Ad Hoc” research methodology. If any of these two options were not described, this research assumed that the research methodology was not specified (column “Not Specified”). The same occurred for the methods and the development guidelines.

Nevertheless, the core point of many of the selected articles was not the development of the maturity models. In these articles, the creation of maturity models was just an auxiliary tool for the problem described in the whole article, so the description of the entire process was, sometimes, not established. But still, the authors opted to add these articles to the research too.

## Methods

In the same way as the Methodologies subsection, articles in which the methods were not described were added to “Not Specified” column. There were not found any “Ad Hoc” methods; consequently, the column was not included.

The methods information of the articles was the hardest one to extract. This type of information was occasionally not clearly implicit in the text; therefore, in those particular cases, it became a task of interpretation on the authors’ behalf. By observing Table 7, it can be stated that in this research domain, researchers have used a broad number of methods. In total, 14 different methods were found in this research.

The development of some maturity models was done by improvement iterations, which consequently made many of the articles adopt more than one method. The total methods used by each article and each context are visible in Table 6. Around 54% of the articles only used one method, and only 30% utilized more than one method. Those who have used only one method were used in the same numbers by Non-IT and IT articles. The articles that used more than one method were in a substantial number from Non-IT researches.

These approaches by using multiple methods, like Delphi method or Case Studies, can be beneficial for this kind of development since many of the weak points in the initial phase of the maturity model are detected. However, in our sample, these kinds of methods are not usually used for this type of research. In fact, only 2% of the total article’s sample utilized the Delphi method (one of each context) and only 8% used Case Studies, being more adopted in Non-IT articles. Despite the fact that the researchers have used a big variety of methods, the utilization of these methods are “centralized,” that is, many of the methods were only adopted once, contrary to a few that were used in more than half of our sample. Those methods that were only adopted once, four are from Non-IT articles and two from IT articles. This can be justified since the Non-IT articles have a broad number of domains, which can be more practical use specific methods in those particular areas.

The main method utilized by the authors in both contexts was the Literature Review, which is the most used method, being applied in 68% of the total articles. According to Table 7, both “types” of articles adopted this method in same numbers, and observing by year, it is visible that the utilization of this method has been increasing over the years, mainly in Non-IT articles. This is happening possibly due to the growth of maturity model’ recognition, which “brought” the development of new maturity models in the community in any domain, which are being used as a comparison when researchers are creating new ones. This act of comparison helps researchers not just finding a “need” to create some of these tools in a certain domain, but also to help researchers learn from other maturity models already created.

## Guidelines for the maturity model development

Guidelines for the development of a maturity model, as mentioned before, can be seen as a methodology composed by the description of the steps that are necessary to develop a maturity model. On the extraction process, a variety of guidelines adopted by the researchers were found. Since the Design Science Research methodology has tremendously evolved, many of the researchers have created “guidelines” based on this methodology, in order to develop an “artifact.” In fact, the authors of this research decided to add some of these “guidelines” that are intrinsically connected with Design Science Research, as a guideline for the maturity model development even if not being specifically for this type of development. This type of guideline was created in a generic manner, having no specific area. One of the examples found was of Hevner et al. (2004). Still, several of these guidelines were created for the development of maturity models in specific areas, like IT. All the guidelines found are visible in Table 8.

By observing this table, it is seen that until the year 2009, in our sample, researches did not frequently use any guideline established by the scientific community. In the same interval of years, most of the IT and Non-IT articles adopted Ad Hoc methodologies. Once again, perhaps this occurred due to the fact of the maturity model importance being diminutive in that time, and none or few guidelines had been created. The “researchers own steps” were the guidelines indeed dominant, being the 47% of the total articles (both contexts had nearly the same numbers of utilization), having 35% of the articles not specified the steps taken for the development of the maturity model. The majority of the guidelines used by the authors in our sample were utilized sporadically, but more incidents in Non-IT articles, which can be justified with the same reasons that were explicit in the methods section, when the utilization of methods was utilized sporadically in the same way that guidelines were. However, this occasional utilization makes it hard to draw some conclusions in these cases.

Despite that, Becker guidelines have been adopted in last years, being 9% of the total articles. Becker (2009) guidelines have the Design Science Research methodology subjacent, being these guidelines designed for the IT Management domain. However, these guidelines in our sample were adopted for other domains like design automation or maintenance, which shows that these “steps” can be flexible for a variety of domains and not exclusively for the IT area.

This table shows that the authors are not using guidelines established by the scientific community on the process of maturity model development. One of the possible hypotheses that justifies this fact is the poor existence or inexistence of guidelines for specific areas. However, apparently the utilization of Becker guidelines is increasing, perhaps not just for his well-established steps, but also for the flexibility that these guidelines may have. The utilization of the Hevner et al. (2004) guidelines, even though they are not specific for the creation of a maturity model, had 5% of adoption. Nevertheless, since the utilization of this guideline was irregular, no conclusion can be draw in any context.

Becker guidelines have the Hevner guidelines subjacent, being these last one connected with Design Science Research methodology, which after all Becker guidelines find itself being connected in the same way with this methodology. This amplifies the Design Science Research as a feasible and practicable research methodology in this type of development. Becker guidelines are specific for maturity models, and for this reason, it will make more sense to adopt these guidelines instead of Hevner’s in the maturity model’s construction, as the numbers indicate that this has been occurring in recent years for both article’s type.

The Ad Hoc methodology was the dominant methodology; consequently, it had a big variety of methods adopted by researchers. Once again, Literature Review was the most used, being utilized in 67% of the articles that adopted this methodology. Interviews were utilized in 32% of the articles, being this method the second most used in Ad Hoc methodologies.

Even though a big number of articles did not adopt any methodology, Literature Review continues to be the preferred method. However, 15 of the articles that did not specify any methodology did not specify the methods used either.

The utilization of specific methodologies in certain contexts can be essential for the selection of methods to be utilized. Despite the fact that for conducting a research in development maturity model domain a broad number of methods is being used, it can be stated that in the Design Science Research methodology researchers prefer using both types of methods identified earlier. However, in Non-IT articles, it is visible that the authors did not use Interviews. Methods that use the experience of experts and are appropriate for this kind of development by improvement iterations seem to be important in this methodology, considering that Design Science Research is a methodology suitable to create artifacts in an iteration manner.

The varied utilization of methods in Ad Hoc methodologies is apparently due to the fact that with every researcher “taking their own steps” there is no restriction about adopting a certain method or a specific approach. Despite that, similar to Design Science Research methodology the most adopted were the Literature Reviews and Interviews, which can lead to the conclusion that no matter what methodology is being used in this domain, it is possible to use these kinds of methods for this type of development.

## Conclusion

The methodology is a generic step to conduct any research, where methods are the tools that will help researchers to achieve and complete that step. Methods are the essential instruments that will define the success or failure of any research. Depending on the objectives of any research, the researchers need to select the more suitable methods to their problem. However, in this research, the information extraction process was a harder task. Many of the articles did not specify the methodology and methods adopted, which is not a good practice in the scientific research area. It is imperative that researchers in this kind of domain, where the purpose is to create an innovation, define and specify this sort of information, not just to help other researchers improve their capabilities by observing errors that other researchers committed, but also to help new investigations in this area by assisting the scientific community in replicating their results or even use their results to improve or create new solutions.

This research aimed to investigate which methodologies, methods, and guidelines are being used in the development of maturity models with the comparison of IT and Non-IT maturity model’s development articles. To conduct this research, a Systematic Literature Review was adopted and a discussion of the results was made. This research extracted methodologies, methods, and guidelines information used in all the 109 articles found, with established definitions of the three concepts. The main conclusions are:

Most researchers are using their own methodologies, regardless of the methodologies established by the scientific community;

Design Science Research methodology utilization is growing in both contexts, being IT articles starting to follow the Non-IT articles in the adoption of this methodology, which in future for IT researches, can be a feasible option;

Researchers have utilized two types of methods: those who preferred more pragmatic methods and those who preferred comparing what has already been done. Many of the articles used one of each type, which can be a benefit since they can compare with others researches and use experts experience to construct their maturity model;

Even though the methodology and methods used can depend on which context the research is inserted in, the utilization of methodologies and methods was not consensual. However, over the last years in IT articles, it seems the utilization of Design Science Research as a feasible methodology and the use of methods like Literature Review and Interviews is starting to be consensual since they are becoming dominant in this kind of innovation;

The authors are preferring adopting their own guidelines instead of those established by the scientific and academic community. Nevertheless, the utilization of Becker guidelines is increasing, which can be a practical solution for this type of development, being these guidelines flexible for any kind of domain;

Finally, it is possible to conclude that as the importance and recognition of the maturity model concept increases, more solutions are being developed by the scientific community. However, this domain still has a long way of progression ahead.

This research has the limitation of the result articles being of a specific area, which means the conclusions can be different in researches of other particular areas. Besides the fact that the dimension of the sample is considerable, the articles are dispersed over the years which can induce conclusion errors about the methodologies and methods utilization evolution. Yet, in the process of information extraction, in some articles, it became a task of interpretation on the authors’ behalf. Nevertheless, this research can be used as a reference point for new researchers who intend to develop new Maturity Models, helping them understand which practices have been adopted in the development of maturity model’s domain.

## Author biographies

Rúben Pereira is an assistant professor at ISCTE. He has a PhD in information systems at Instituto Superior Técnico where he also graduated as a master in computer engineering and computer science. He has been a consultant in several industries, such as services, banking, telecommunications, and e-commerce, among others. He is the author of several scientific papers in the area of Information Technology Services Management and Information Technology Governance, covering the most adopted IT Frameworks like ITIL and COBIT. His areas of scientific interest extend to information technology risk management, business process management, continuous improvement and innovation, process optimization, digital transformation, among others.

João Serrano has a degree in computer science engineering at Instituto Politécnico de Leiria (ESTG). He is currently attending a master’s degree in computer science and management at ISCTE. In his academic career has explored areas such management, software development, and systems management.
