# The Role of User Participation in Information Systems Development: Implications from a Meta-Analysis

Jun He and William R. Kin g

Jun He is an Assistant Professor of MIS at the University of Michigan–Dearborn. He has an MBA from Tsinghua University and a Ph.D. from the University of Pittsburgh. His research interests include systems design and development, knowledge management, and methodological issues. He has presented a number of papers at meetings of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) and the Americas’ Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS) and has published in Communications of the AIS, Information & Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, and in the book Current Topics in Management.

William R. King holds the title University Professor in the Katz Graduate School of Business at the University of Pittsburgh. He has published more than 300 papers and 15 books in the areas of information systems, management science, and strategic planning. He has served as founding president of the Association for Information Systems, president of TIMS (now INFORMS), and editor-in-chief of MIS Quarterly.

Abstr act: This study synthesizes the research findings of 82 empirical studies on user participation in information systems development (ISD). Various ISD outcomes are addressed using a classification scheme involving two broad categories—attitudinal/ behavioral outcomes and productivity outcomes. The results demonstrate that user participation is minimally-to-moderately beneficial to ISD; its effects are comparatively stronger on attitudinal/behavioral outcomes than on productivity outcomes. This attitudinal/behavioral impact may largely be the result of the emphasis that has been placed on user participation by academics and consultants. The results of this analysis are compared to those of a meta-analysis in the broader management context of participation. The results are similar in terms of attitudinal outcomes, but different, and lesser, in terms of productivity outcomes. Since the current status of research in the broad area of participation is that the effects of participation are considered to be problematic, that status and the results of this study suggest that user participation alone may not be sufficient to achieve significantly improved ISD outcomes, and that different strategies should be employed based on the specific goals of ISD projects. If system acceptance is the ultimate goal, user participation should be designed to induce more psychological involvement among potential users. If productivity benefits are the focus, user participation should be designed to provide developers the needed domain knowledge. In sum, user participation should be treated as one of a number of means for ISD projects to be more successful.

Key w or ds and phr ases: information systems development, meta-analysis, user participation.

Since computer s came to be w idely utilized in business, the participation of users in information systems development (ISD) has received much research attention in the field of information systems (IS) [5, 46, 55, 79, 80, 129]. Users’ participative efforts are typically captured by the construct “user participation,” which is defined as the behaviors and activities that users or their representatives perform in the systems development process [5]. Many academics and consultants recommend user participation in ISD as an effective practice to achieve various favorable outcomes, including enhanced user satisfaction and increased system quality [70, 99].

Empirical studies have produced equivocal results regarding the relationship between user participation and ISD outcomes, even subsequent to the development of more rigorous research methodologies and better measurements [18]. Indeed, some studies have argued that user participation might impair, rather than improve, the performance of ISD project teams [14, 58]. A growing body of qualitative research has identified possible obstacles, drawbacks, or even negative effects arising from the practice of user participation, such as user-developer conflict [61], communication gaps [143], communication lapses [45], and increased ISD workload [14, 56]. These findings challenge the simplicity of assuming only positive effects of user participation on various ISD outcomes.

The user participation–ISD outcome relationship has fundamental importance to both IS research and systems development practices. This relationship serves as a primary basis for the study and advocacy of user participation in both the research and prescriptive literature [85]. Methodologically, the validity of this relationship is a prerequisite for explorations of any potential mediation that may help to explain exactly how user participation affects ISD project teams in the pursuit of successful systems development [8, 72]. For instance, acceptance of the user participation–ISD outcome relationship has stimulated the development of innovative systems development methods that rely on heavy user participation, such as prototyping [54], rapid application development [11], joint application design [17], participative design [17, 59, 60, 84], user-centered system design [86, 107, 137], and end-user computing [3]. The adoption of these development techniques has reshaped ISD practices in terms of team configuration [55, 144], management styles [95], conflict management [7, 118], and project team leadership [42].

User participation has had profound organizational effects. As noted by King et al. and others, “social action was powerfully shaped by social context, and in turn shaped that context” [78, p. 145]; user participation has altered patterns of institutional behavior by becoming a common practice in ISD [15, 94]. User participation has changed organizational culture and climates [117], empowered employees in day-to-day operations [101] and in the control of systems feature selection [83], affected the management of human resources [135], reshaped the process of product development [35], and revised the “rules of the game” that mold human interaction [15]. Key organizational processes, including organizational change, organizational learning, and the seeking of competitive advantage, have inevitably been affected [111].

Given its importance to organizations and to the IS field, the user participation–ISD outcome relationship must be validated and clarified to enable users to play their most effective role in ISD. To this end, we meta-analyzed previous empirical studies to ascertain a convergent pattern of the general effects of user participation on various ISD outcomes.

## Previous Reviews on User Participation Effects

Th e pr emise th at user par ticipation is cr itical to ISD has stimulated numerous studies to assess the effects of user participation on ISD outcomes. These studies may be classified as either of two types: those that focus on attitudinal/behavioral outcomes or those that focus on productivity outcomes. In order to draw definitive conclusions concerning the effects of user participation on these ISD outcomes, several researchers have attempted to summarize the results of the empirical studies of this topic.

For example, Ives and Olson [70] and Cavaye [18] are two frequently cited narrative reviews in this field. The two reviews covered empirical studies published during the time periods of 1959–81 and 1982–92, respectively. Both observed that only one-third of the reviewed studies had demonstrated statistically significant benefits of user participation on the investigated ISD outcomes. Most empirical studies of user participation produced either inconclusive or negative results. The inconsistency of these empirical findings raises serious questions concerning the hypothesized efficacy of user participation in ISD.

In an attempt to statistically summarize the research on this topic, Hwang and Thorn [68] synthesized research findings from 25 empirical studies (published during 1976–96) and concluded that user participation was beneficial to ISD with effect sizes that were strong in significance and moderate in magnitude (most of the mean effect sizes fell in the range of r = 0.3). Although this was an encouraging conclusion, close examination of this meta-analysis raises questions on article selection and results coding. One concern is that studies with negative results were underrepresented. Although the authors acknowledged that the user participation literature has yielded “varying results,” their sampled effect sizes were dominated by positive results: only 1 of the 35 collected correlation coefficients between user participation and various ISD outcomes was negative. Because this is at odds with the proportional makeup of the results of prior studies, the results of this meta-analysis could be inaccurate and misleading.

## Using Meta-Analysis to Assess the Relationship Between Participation and ISD Outcomes

Having a conclusive assessment of th e effects of user participation on various ISD outcomes is fundamental to the justification and development of user participation theory and practice. Meta-analysis has been advocated by numerous methodologists as a superior review method to the traditional narrative review [49, 57, 62, 119, 120, 145]. However, some concerns with this research methodology have also been discussed in the literature (e.g., [81]). In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis on user participation with special efforts to deal with these concerns (which are discussed later).

## Selection of Relevant Studies

To systematically sample as many as possible relevant studies, we searched through the following databases with the key words “user participation,” “user involvement,” or “user engagement.”

•	 Business Source Premier (EBSCO Host database), a primary business research database, from which about 173 studies are identified, with a wide publication coverage of 1974–February 2006.

•	 Social Science Citation Index (SSCI), another business research database collecting business research since 1992. This database is used as a complementary source for relevant studies.

•	 The Association for Information System Electronic Library (AISeL), an online repository for proceedings of AIS-sponsored conferences, including the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS), Americas’ Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS), European Conference on Information Systems (ECIS), Pacific Asia Conference on Information Systems (PACIS), Bled Electronic Commerce Conference (BLED), Australasian Conference on Information Systems (ACIS), Mediterranean Conference on Information Systems (MCIS), and Midwest Association for Information Systems (MWAIS).

•	 Digital Dissertations, a database containing citations, abstracts, or full texts of doctoral dissertations for all subject areas from over 1,000 accredited colleges and universities in North America and Europe.

In addition, bibliographies of previous review studies (i.e., [18, 68, 70]) were scrutinized for additional relevant studies. This rather comprehensive search resulted in 464 articles in total. Of these, 382 articles were eliminated because they (1) were not empirical studies, (2) did not study ISD, (3) did not measure user participation, (4) did not test the effects of user participation on ISD outcomes, or (5) presented redundant results from samples that had been reported elsewhere. The resulting 82 studies (including 15 Ph.D. dissertations and seven conference papers) provided the data for this meta-analysis.

## Selection and Treatment of Independent and Dependent Variables

## Independent Variable

Until the late 1980s, the terms “user participation” and “user involvement” had been used interchangeably in the IS literature. Barki and Hartwick’s [5] theoretical study made the first conceptual distinction between the two terms. After reviewing the study of involvement in the fields of psychology, marketing, and organizational behavior, they suggested that the term “user involvement” be used when referring to a subjective psychological state and defined it as “the importance and personal relevance that users attach either to a given system or to IS in general” [5, pp. 59–60]; they adopted a behavioral approach for “user participation” and suggested the term be used when “referring to the behaviors and activities that target users or their representatives perform during the system development process” [5, p. 59]. Because it provides conceptual clarity with sound foundations that have been demonstrated in other areas, this distinction has been widely accepted among IS researchers [85].

In this study, we adopted Barki and Hartwick’s [5] approach to distinguish the two constructs. Each of the sampled studies was first categorized as either a user participation study or a user involvement study by judging its approach to measuring user participative efforts in ISD as being either behavioral or psychological. Using the label of user involvement for a behavior measure was found to be not an uncommon practice even in the recent literature (e.g., [50, 109, 110, 111, 113, 132]); many studies with the key phrase of “user involvement” were judged to be user participation studies according to these criteria. Because of the dearth of user involvement studies that met the measurement criteria (we identified only eight such studies), these studies were dropped from our sample because such a small group of studies lack credibility for meta-analysis [37, 81]. Thus, only true user participation studies were collected as the sample data for the meta-analysis.

## Dependent Variables

Various ISD outcomes have been investigated as dependent variables in the user participation–ISD outcomes relationship. Following the commonly accepted scheme of participation review studies in the field of organizational behavior (e.g., [23, 24, 93, 138, 139]) and the field of IS (e.g., [68, 70, 99]), we classified dependent variables into two types—attitudinal/behavioral outcomes and productivity outcomes—giving careful consideration to measures that might not obviously fall into either of the two categories.

Attitudinal/Behavioral Outcomes. Attitudinal/behavioral outcomes refer to the attitudinal and behavioral consequences derived from users’ participative activities during the development of target IS. It has long been recognized that the attitudes of users are closely related to their actual use of the system [96, 116], and usage behavior is the means by which the full potential of the system may be realized [27] since system acceptance has been discussed as an ultimate goal of ISD projects [9, 55]. User participation is viewed as a practice that is conducive to favorable attitudes and behaviors, with motivational factors as the main intervening mechanism that leads to improved acceptance [70].

Various terms have been used in the literature to label this category, including “attitudinal and behavioral measures” [47], “system acceptance” [70], and “psychological buy-in” [99]. Three subcategories are employed here to classify the outcomes used in the literature—user satisfaction, use intention, and system use.

User Satisfaction. User satisfaction reflects the individual’s psychological state of readiness to use a system [131]; it is the most widely used measure for ISD success [29, 70]. User satisfaction refers to the extent to which users feel target systems or their deliverables (e.g., data and reports derived from using the system) meet their needs, requirements, and expectations [108]. Attitude measures that parallel satisfaction with a system, such as management information systems (MIS) appreciation [129], job satisfaction [16], process satisfaction [147], and end-user computing satisfaction [32, 33], are also classified into this subcategory. In studies where usage behavior is well monitored, some objective indices have been used for convenience and accuracy. For example, in an experimental study of forecasting decision support systems, Lawrence et al. [89] measured the fraction of forecasts adjusted by the participating subjects as the surrogate measure for user satisfaction. The justification for this measure was that a satisfied user would see little need to modify the forecast provided by the system while an unsatisfied user would reject the system results and use his or her own estimations instead.

Use Intention. Use intention measures the strength of one’s intention to use a target system [27]. Derived from the study of technology acceptance, the construct of use intention has been suggested in the literature as another important measure for ISD success [30]. In this study, the coverage of use intention is extended to include other closely related motivational factors that reflect or determine people’s usage behavior, such as perceived usefulness [40, 43, 89], perceived benefits [122], user friendliness [69], perceived system use [40], and end-user resistance [150]. Some derivative measures of a motivational nature are also placed in this category regardless of their labels. Examples include Lawrence et al.’s [89] system acceptance and Choe’s [19, 20] system usage: the former was a combination of perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, and the latter was a composite index of perceived frequency of use and willingness of use (square root of their product). Use intention reflects the tendency and willingness to use a system, and has been concluded as the single most important factor in predicting users’ actual usage behavior [27, 82].

System Use. System use refers to the extent to which users operate a system in their work settings. The premise is that an ISD project is successful only when the resulting system is accepted by the users and integrated into their work processes [144], and that “an unused system provides no value” [109, p. 69]. System use is often measured by the amount, or frequency, of system operation within a certain period of time [47]. The behavioral nature of this construct encourages the use of objective measures. However, measurement difficulties, including the lack of effective methods to monitor actual usage behavior and the lack of motivation from sampled organizations for sharing such information [29], constrain most field studies to rely on self-report measures to assess users’ behavior.

Most studies investigate system use at the individual level; only rarely has attention been placed on collective usage behavior. The few exceptions include Jarvenpaa and Ives’s [72] “progressive use of IT within a firm,” and Wixom and Watson’s [144] “organizational implementation success.”

Some researchers have argued that system use measures should be employed only in voluntary environments. When usage of a system is mandatory, the limited variance in usage will restrict the construct’s usefulness as an indicant of system success. This concern led DeLone and McLean to conclude that “usage, either actual or perceived, is only pertinent when such use is voluntary” [29, p. 68]. But Hartwick and Barki [55] found that in a mandatory environment, users still have control over the level or extent of use based on their personal judgments (e.g., attitude and intention) and that the resultant variance in their usage behavior qualifies the construct of system use as a valid dependent variable for ISD research. DeLone and McLean [30] later supported the validity of system use as a proxy for ISD success in both environments. They further contended that no system use is totally mandatory, and discontinuing use of a system that is not providing the desired results and benefits is always an option.

Productivity Outcomes. If user participation is a constructive practice that helps improve the performance of ISD projects, the improved performance should be observable with the resulting systems, users and their organizations, or in the development process. Productivity outcomes refer to the improved performance consequences derived from users’ participative activities during the development of target IS. User participants are able to help ISD project teams develop quality systems because of their knowledge and expertise of application domains that target systems are to support [87]. Cognitive factors that help developers elicit accurate user requirements and domain knowledge, which are prerequisite for the successful development of ISD projects [12, 25, 70], are suggested as the main intervening mechanism that leads to improved ISD productivity [70].

This category has been labeled as “system quality” in previous review studies (e.g., [70, 99]). We selected the term “productivity outcomes” not only for its consistency with participation research in other fields (e.g., organizational behavior [93], human resources [104, 115], education [23], and marketing [21, 44]) but also for its broad scope beyond the usual concept of quality that we have observed in the various measures used in the literature. After a careful review of the ISD literature, we classified these outcomes into five subcategories—individual impact, team performance, organizational impact, project quality, and project success.

Individual Impact. Individual impact refers to the extent to which target systems help users enhance their job performance or increase their task productivity. Objective measures are often used in experimental studies, where subjects’ task productivity can be readily measured in terms of quality (e.g., decision accuracy [89], recall accuracy [128], percentage of error [64]), quantity (e.g., data entry amount [65, 67]), or efficiency (e.g., ratio of delayed-work-process [66]). Some studies employed perception-based measures (e.g., perceived system benefits [69], perceived application impact [31]) to assess one’s perception of the extent to which the investigated system improves his or her job performance.

Team Performance. Team performance measures the extent to which ISD project teams are effective in delivering quality systems. Team performance is often measured in terms of meeting budget, schedule, or user requirements. Some conceptually narrow constructs, such as Brodbeck’s [14] “team viability,” which focuses on within-team cooperation, are also considered in this subcategory.

Organizational Impact. Organizational impact refers to the extent to which target systems help organizations to enhance overall performance. An exemplar variable in this subcategory is Palanisamy and Sushil’s [111] “IS enablement for competitive advantage.”

Project Quality. Project quality is often measured by the extent to which target systems are efficient, reliable, accurate, easy to use, and able to produce the information that users need. Besides composite instruments to assess a broad range of system features (e.g., Barki et al.’s [7] 18-item instrument), variables that focus on system functionality or system outputs are included in this subcategory. One example is Wixom and Watson’s [144] study of the implementation of data warehouses. In this study, the effects of user participation were tested on the flexibility and integration of data warehouse (or system quality, r = 0.005) and the quality of output reports (or data quality, $r = 0 . 0 5 2 )$ , respectively. The two variables were judged to be assessments of project quality and the average of their effect sizes $( r = 0 . 0 2 8 5 )$ was used in this meta-analysis.

Project Success. Project success refers to the overall performance of ISD projects with a focus on efficiency and productivity (e.g., completing the project on time, within budget, and with the proper functionality). Variables that assess ISD project performance by measuring the acceptance of, or resistance to, target systems among users (e.g., Wixom and Watson’s [144] “organizational implementation success”) are best thought of as belonging to the system use category. Besides comprehensive assessments of various success criteria (e.g., Guimaraes et al.’s [51] “overall success” and Brodbeck’s [14] “composite performance index”), some variables focus on the implementation stage of system development, such as Wixom and Watson’s [144] “project implementation success” and Gottschalk’s [50] “plan implementation success.”

## The Treatment of Effect Sizes

Effect sizes used in this meta-analysis were Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Most studies reported correlation coefficients between user participation and outcome variables. If such correlation coefficients were not available, other statistics (e.g., descriptive statistics, p-values, t-tests, F-tests, and d-tests [120]) were examined for possible conversion to correlation coefficients. Some studies reported raw data [132] or the correlation matrix at the instrument item level [91]. These data were extracted and LISREL was used to calculate the relationship of interest.

If a study had more than one effect size regarding a particular relationship in terms of the previously discussed classification of dependent variables, the effects were scrutinized for their similarities in both magnitude and research context. The following decision rules were used to ensure the accuracy and consistency of coding these results.

1. If multiple effect sizes were calculated from the same sample population, and the magnitudes of the effect sizes were close (judged by the chi-square test of homogeneity), these effect sizes were combined by calculating their average. This approach is suggested by Rosenthal and DiMatteo [120] as a conservative way to reduce bias toward such a study. (If multiple effect sizes from one sampled study were treated separately, the study would be overweighted in that each effect size would be treated with the same weight as other single studies.) One example is Palanisamy and Sushil’s [111] study of user participation in IS planning. In this study, users’ participative activities during IS planning were separated into two substages: strategic planning and information requirements analysis; and their organizational effects were assessed respectively on three dimensions: organizational change, competitive advantage, and organizational learning. This approach resulted in six effect sizes ranging from 0.1386 to 0.3911 [111, p. 153]. A chi-square test did not reject homogeneity among these effect sizes $( Q = 5 , p = 0 . 4 1 6 )$ . The average effect size (r = 0.26) was used in this meta-analysis.

2. Multiple effect sizes would be treated separately if (a) they were significantly different in magnitude, or (b) they were calculated from different sample populations. For example, to investigate the moderating role of user expertise on the effects of user participation, Saleem [121] conducted an experimental study and a field study. The sample populations were undergraduate students and company system users, respectively. Thus, the resulting effects were coded separately in this meta-analysis.

## Dealing with Concerns with Meta-Analysis

Although employing a rigorous and quantitative procedure, a particular meta-analysis can be questioned on issues such as small sample size, publication bias, and “apples and oranges” [81]. These concerns should be dealt with before a robust and reliable result can be concluded from a meta-analysis.

## Small Sample Size

The number of sampled studies has great impact on the statistical power of a metaanalysis. Analysts should base analyses on the largest number of studies available [100]. If the collection of studies is small or incomplete, conclusions drawn from analysis will be limited in scope.

This meta-analysis conducted an extensive search and sampled 82 relevant empirical studies. In contrast, another meta-analysis on this topic, Hwang and Thorn [68], sampled only 25 studies. The broad coverage of relevant studies adds credibility to the results of the meta-analysis as representing the investigated research domain [81].

However, one should note that several subcategories of dependent variables, such as team performance and organizational impact, are represented by few empirical studies. This reflects the fact that ISD outcomes in those subcategories have not been widely investigated. Because the statistical power of meta-analysis depends on both the number of studies and the total cumulated sample sizes [81], results in those subcategories must be interpreted with caution.

The concern of sample size led us to drop our intention of summarizing the effects of user involvement. Because the universe of studies was found to be limited in that only eight empirical studies were identified from the literature as investigating the effects of user involvement, we have chosen not to report the (possibly misleading) statistical meta-analysis results of those studies.

## Publication Bias (the “File Drawer” Issue)

Publication bias, or the “file drawer” issue, refers to the observation that significant results are more likely to be published while nonsignificant results tend to be relegated to file drawers. If only published studies are surveyed in a meta-analysis, the results may not represent a total research population, thus creating publication bias.

The best method of avoiding publication bias is to search multiple databases in a systematic way and sample studies from various sources [120]. In this study, we searched through four research databases as well as the bibliographies of previous review studies for relevant articles. Besides published journal articles, the final sample also includes 15 Ph.D. dissertations and seven recent conference proceedings. Thus, the concern of publication bias is alleviated to the degree practicable for this meta-analysis.

In addition, we divided the sample into groups of published and unpublished studies, and compared their meta-analytic results. Examination on the integrated effect sizes and the associated confidence intervals suggests that the effects of user participation from the two subsamples were consistent on both attitudinal/behavioral outcomes and productivity outcomes.

## “Apples and Oranges”

Another criticism of meta-analysis is that it may compare “apples and oranges,” aggregating results derived from studies with incommensurable research goals, measures, and procedures. This problem is not of great significance here, because we wish to obtain results that are generalizable to a fairly broad domain [81].

Studies sampled for this meta-analysis used different methods to capture user participation, including manipulations, single-item instruments, and multiple-item instruments that range from as few as two items to as many as 44 items. Because the purpose of this meta-analysis is to study user participation as a general phenomenon in ISD contexts, these various measures are treated equally in this meta-analysis.

As to the dependent variables, special attention was given to avoiding “the problem of attempting aggregation of too diverse a sampling of studies” [81, p. 672]. Each effect size was categorized by the detailed measure, rather than the label, of its dependent variable. For example, Wixom and Watson [144] used “organizational implementation success” and “project implementation success” as two dependent variables in their study of data warehouse implementation. The former was placed in the category of use intention because it measured perceived support and resistance to the implemented system; the latter was placed in the category of project success in that it measured “implementation-level success in completing the project on time, on budget, with the proper functionality” [144, p. 30]. Thus, the concern of comparing “apples and oranges” should be largely alleviated in this meta-analysis.

Hedges and Olkin’s [57] random effects method was used as the calculation procedure for the meta-analysis. Hedges–Olkin is one of three well-developed meta-analytic procedures that have been widely applied in the social and behavioral sciences [37, 38]. Basically, effect sizes are first transformed into Fisher’s z; weights of individual studies are calculated basing on sample sizes (using a chi-square analysis of sampling errors); a homogeneity test (chi-square test) is performed to justify the use of an effect-size-integration model (in this case, the random model); then, a weighted average is calculated from transformed effect sizes, the associating standard deviation, Z-statistic, and 95 percent confidence intervals. Finally, these statistics are transformed back to their standard forms for interpretation. Key results of the meta-analysis are presented in Table 2.

In Table 3, we compare meta-analytic results calculated from two subsamples of published versus unpublished studies. The results provide evidence that no significant publication bias exists among the sampled studies.

## Comparing User Participation Effects in ISD with Participation Effects in Organizations

Many r esear ch er s cr edit par ticipation r esear ch in organizational behavior and human relations as a theoretical ground for the study of user participation in ISD. Participation in organizations, sometimes characterized as participative decision making (PDM), refers to a management practice that encourages employees who are to execute the decisions, or who are affected by the decisions, to participate in the decision-making process [93]. PDM takes general organizational settings as its investigative context. In contrast, user participation is restricted to ISD processes, and can be considered as “a special case of PDM in which system developers and users substituted for superiors and subordinates” [70, p. 587]. Therefore, the comparison of the participation effects in the two fields might provide some insight on the efficacy of participation in different situations.

Numerous narrative reviews and meta-analyses have been conducted in the broader participation literature to synthesize the effects of participation on satisfaction and productivity. Among them, Wagner et al. [140] reviewed 86 published studies and conducted a meta-analysis on 124 correlation coefficients obtained from them. The number of sampled effect sizes is close to that of the meta-analysis conducted here. Wagner and colleagues selected Hunter and Schmidt’s [62] calculation procedure, and we used Hedges and Olkin’s [57] random effects model. The two procedures have been compared in several studies (e.g., [37, 73, 125]) with the conclusion that the two will produce similar results from the same data set. In fact, meta-analysis is usually advocated as a “formalized systematic review” procedure [62]. “Given the same set of data or sampled effect sizes from a literature body, different researchers should arrive at the same conclusion via meta-analysis” [81, p. 673].

Therefore, we used the results from Wagner et al. [140] as a reference to compare participation effects in ISD with that in PDM. The comparison focused on the overall effects of participation because the two meta-analyses employed different schemes to classify sampled studies into detailed outcome categories. The results are summarized in Table 5.

The two meta-analyses conclude that participation has positive statistical effects on both types of outcomes. Close examination of the integrated effect sizes reveals that the participation effects are almost identical on attitudinal/behavioral outcomes in the two meta-analyses, but are somewhat different on productivity outcomes, as suggested by their 95 percent confidence intervals. A Z-test comparing the two integrated effect sizes on productivity reveals a marginally significant difference (Z = 1.75, p-value (two-sided) = 0.08).

The difference of participation effects on productivity between the two meta-analyses may reflect different participation practices in the context of the two research domains. PDM takes general organizational settings as its research context, and there is no common rule to guide participation practice. In contrast, the user participation literature studies participation in restricted ISD contexts, where semistructured procedures do exist to guide user participation practices. The prescriptive literature (e.g., textbooks, handbooks, and others that provide guidelines and recommendations concerning ISD practices) is quite consistent on how user participation should be practiced. ISD project teams have commonly accepted these guidelines. As observed by Kirsch and Beath [85], the practice of user participation helps ISD project teams to develop systems in a smooth and formalized manner. Thus, user participation may be overrepresented if most ISD project teams follow these prescribed procedures, resulting in diluted impacts on productivity.

Another explanation could be that users tend to overrate their participation and underrate the quality of the resulting systems, leading to an underassessed user participation–productivity relationship. This is evident in Foster and Franz’s [39, 40] work where users and developers were found to have different perceptions about the effect of user participation on system quality $( r = - 0 . 0 5 $ versus r = 0.53). Most studies examine user participation and its effects from users’ perspective. The systematic bias of users, if it existed, could undermine the real effect of user participation on productivity outcomes. More research needs to be done to clarify this issue.

Albeit there is only slight difference in their synthesized effect sizes, the two meta-analyses are consistent in that participation has a smaller effect on productivity than the theories suggest. In the field of participative decision making, “research on participation’s outcome efficacy has yielded results that have proven to be largely disappointing” [140, p. 50]. The productivity benefit of user participation in ISD is shown here to be even smaller, raising concerns that some important factors or mechanisms may have been ignored or underinvestigated.

## Summary, Discussion, and Limitations

## Summary

By synth esizing th e findings fr om 82 empir ical studies on user participation, this meta-analysis shows a convergent pattern on the effects of user participation on various ISD outcomes. The results confirm that user participation is somewhat beneficial in ISD. Its effects on ISD attitudinal/behavioral outcomes were found to be significant and moderately strong in magnitude; its effects on productivity outcomes, albeit significant, were found to be limited with small effect sizes (r < 0.2).

## Discussion

These results are similar to those resulting from a comparable meta-analysis in the field of PDM.

Based on their assessment of the limited beneficial effects, Wagner and colleagues [138, 139, 140] raised questions to the practical significance of participation in PDM, particularly on productivity outcomes. The concern has led many researchers to investigate situational factors that either restrict or improve the effects of participation.

Some widely examined factors in PDM research include task interdependency and task complexity [139], group size [139], participation forms [24], types of participants [105], participation climate [105], and leadership styles [90]. However, Wagner [138] surveyed 10 representative meta-analyses of participation and found evidence of null findings in every instance.

Latham et al. [88] attributed the inconsistent findings of participation effects to participation often having been “framed” primarily as a motivational device while its cognitive aspects are ignored. Based on Locke and Schweiger’s [93] participation framework, it is mainly a cognitive mechanism that allows participation to affect productivity, and the knowledge and skills of participants are a key requirement for such a mechanism to take effect. In case of unknowledgeable participants, participation “would be wasteful of time and effort at best, and harmful to decision quality (if those with less knowledge outvoted the most knowledgeable member) or to efficiency (caused by delays) at worst” [93, p. 318]. Thus, the small effect of participation in PDM may be a result of ignoring participants’ knowledge and expertise as the significant determinant of productivity.

Wagner et al. [140] tested the use of a cognitive framework on the study of participation, but failed to detect any improvement in the results. They attributed this to the lack of reports in their sampled studies concerning the extent to which the knowledge and expertise of involved workers are necessary.

Similar research patterns can be found in the study of user participation in ISD. To enhance the predictive power of user participation on ISD success, many researchers have adopted a contingency framework, arguing that the effects of user participation on ISD success are constrained by various situational factors, including task factors such as task complexity [77], task–system fit [34], and system impact [131] technology factors such as system complexity [131], system attributes [31], team and organization factors (e.g., organization size [19], the evolution level of IS [19], overall user attitudes [77, 131], resource constraints [131], and top management support [131]), and development process factors (e.g., user–developer communication [103], development stage [40, 95], control over system features [85]). In addition, types of participation (such as the level of instrumental voice of users [64, 65, 66, 67, 89], the presence of user representatives [12, 14, 151], and discrete participative activities [102]) have also been investigated in various settings. The small number of studies in each case makes it impractical to conduct meta-analysis for conclusive results concerning these contingency factors. However, the overall small effect sizes and their associated confidence intervals revealed in Table 2 imply that none of these above factors is likely to guarantee a strong effect of user participation on ISD productivity outcomes.

ISD researchers have long recognized the cognitive nature of involving users in the development process. DeBrabander and Edstrom discussed patterns of communication between users and developers as a means for ISD success based on the assumption that “users and specialist bring different conceptual frameworks to the situation” [28, p. 198]. ISD success is guaranteed by an efficient flow of necessary information from users to developers [34]. Ives and Olson [70] first proposed a cognitive framework of user participation to study productivity benefits. Other researchers presented arguments that further strengthened the importance of users’ knowledge and expertise. For example, Curtis et al. [25] found that many ISD project teams lack the business knowledge needed to develop quality systems, Kirsch and Beath [85] observed that unknowledgeable user participants did not contribute meaningfully during system development processes, and Damodaran discussed the selection of appropriate user participants who were able to bring ISD project teams with “high levels of user competence and experience” [26, p. 366] as a strategy to achieve development success.

Several cognitive factors of user participation have been studied in the literature, including the discrepancy between desired and perceived user participation [32, 33], the realness of user expectations [47], user training and education [19], cognitive fit [83], and the source of system initiation [77]. However, direct assessments of the knowledge and expertise of user participants are rare in the literature. One exception is Saleem [121], where the domain knowledge of user participants was manipulated in an experimental study and assessed in a field study. The results demonstrated that user participation had significantly stronger impact on those who perceived themselves as functional experts than those who perceived themselves as functional nonexperts. The dependent variables used in that study included “system use,” “system resistance,” and “user satisfaction,” all of which were attitudinal/behavioral outcomes.

Because the influence of participation in the broader-than-ISD context is neither proven nor well accepted by researchers, there does not appear to be a basis for believing that participation in ISD has great efficacy, particularly in terms of productivity outcomes. Nonetheless, given its intuitive appeal, it may be thought of as being the conventional wisdom in ISD. This wisdom receives marginal support from our metaanalysis. However, the results of this study suggest that user participation alone may not be sufficient to achieve significantly improved ISD outcomes. Other important factors or mechanisms, for instance, the promise of bringing the knowledge and experience of participants into play, need to be considered to assess the productivity benefits of having users participate in ISD processes.

## Limitations of the Study

Despite the special effort expended to deal with some common weaknesses in metaanalyses, this study nonetheless suffers from some limitations. One limitation is about the coverage of sampled papers. Although the sampling procedure was exhaustive, we did not cover all possible databases and may have failed to find some articles online or through our university’s research library system. However, given the overall large number of sampled studies, adding a few additional studies would be unlikely to alter the conclusions.

Another limitation is about the treatment of effect sizes. In this study, we used reported effect sizes without adjustments for their measurement errors, such as perception-perception bias, single-method bias, and “attenuation effect” caused by imperfect construct reliabilities. Techniques of estimating and reducing these measurement errors are available, but require additional examination of each sampled study of its detailed research setting and methods, and probably overmodified statistical procedures, which are beyond the scope of this study. Both the number of the sampled studies and the total cumulated sample size are satisfactorily large, granting credibility to these results [37, 81].

## Implications for Researchers and Practitioners

Th e findings of th is study h ave str ong implications for r esear ch . The overall modest effect of user participation suggests that user participation alone may not be sufficient to predict the success of ISD projects, especially when productivity outcomes are of research interest. The process of ISD is inherently complex, involving the management of factors that are both technological and organizational and are dynamic in nature due to the constantly changing business environment [149]. Many of these factors are not controllable by the users. Thus, user participation should be used in conjunction with other important factors to achieve satisfactory ISD outcomes.

The use of contingency models, although effective in clarifying the effects of user participation under uncertain conditions, has been found to be fragmented in the literature. Future research that refines and replicates the testing of these contingency factors should be encouraged. The accumulation of empirical evidence is a necessary process for reaching conclusive results and an essential condition for the field to progress [63].

Of the 82 sampled studies, we have identified few mediators that have been empirically tested in the user participation–ISD success relationship, such as factors including user involvement [66, 76, 123], user attitude [66], procedural justice [64, 65], and information waste [110]. Given the development of the theoretical framework of user participation (e.g., [70, 99]), it is surprising that little research attention has been placed on these intervening mechanisms, that is, the motivational factors and cognitive factors that mediate the relationship between user participation and ISD success. Studying these intervening mechanisms would help to enrich our understanding on how user participation affects ISD processes. This should be a promising area for future research.

In addition, the comparatively weak relationship between user participation and ISD productivity outcomes calls for more research on the cognitive nature of user participation, with a focus on the mechanisms through which the knowledge and expertise of user participants can affect the development of ISD projects. This is consistent with the suggestion from participation research in PDM [88, 93, 140]. As little research has assessed the competence of user participants on their domain knowledge and expertise, this will be another promising area for the study of user participation.

This study also has strong implications for ISD practitioners. Results from this meta-analysis clearly demonstrate that user participation is not a panacea to guarantee improved ISD outcomes. Practitioners need to develop different strategies based on the goals of specific ISD projects. For example, if system acceptance is the ultimate goal, user participation should be designed to induce more psychological involvement (i.e., feelings that the target system is personally relevant and important) among potential users; thus, encouraging more users to participate in the ISD process will be a good approach. If productivity benefits are the focus, user participation should be designed as a special practice to provide developers the needed domain knowledge; thus, selecting knowledgeable users and helping them communicate more effectively with developers should be of great concern to project managers. In sum, user participation should be treated as one of a number of means for ISD projects to be more successful.
