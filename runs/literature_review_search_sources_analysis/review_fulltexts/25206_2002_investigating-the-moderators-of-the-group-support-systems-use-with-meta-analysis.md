# Investigating the Moderators of the Group Support Systems Use with Meta-Analysis

ALAN R. DENNIS AND BARBARA H. WIXOM

ALAN R. DENNIS holds the John T. Chambers Chair of Internet Systems in the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University. His research focuses on the use of computer technologies for brainstorming, decision-making, and collaborative work for teams who work in the same room at the same time, and those that work over the Internet. He is an associate editor for MIS Quarterly and serves as the executive editor for MIS Quarterly Executive.

BARBARA H. WIXOM is an Assistant Professor of Commerce at the University of Virginia’s McIntire School of Commerce. She has published in journals that include MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Communications of the ACM. Dr. Wixom is an associate editor for the Journal of Data Warehousing, and she has presented her work at national and international conferences.

ABSTRACT: This paper presents a meta-analysis that investigates five moderators (task, tool, the type of group, the size of the group, and facilitation) and their influences on the overall effects of group support systems (GSS). Results show that process satisfaction is higher for idea-generation tasks than for decision-making tasks. The GSS tool (that is, the use of level 1 or level 2 GSS) influences decision quality. Level 1 tools support the exchange of information, whereas, level 2 tools are designed to aid in decision-making. Decision quality is higher when using level 2 tools, however, there is no difference in the number of ideas generated when using level 1 or level 2 tools. Decision quality is lower for virtual teams, but there is no difference in the number of ideas generated between virtual teams and face-to-face teams using GSS. Group size is an important moderator when measuring decision time and satisfaction with process. The former is shorter for larger groups, and the latter is higher for larger groups. Process facilitation leads to higher decision quality and higher satisfaction with the process. These results illustrate the importance of examining the moderators of GSS use and the viability of conducting a meta-analysis to investigate a large body of research with seemingly conflicting or equivocal results.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group support systems, groupware, meta-analysis.

FOR ALMOST 20 YEARS, RESEARCHERS HAVE BEEN STUDYING the effectiveness and efficiency of systems that support synchronous and asynchronous teams working in both field and laboratory settings. Unfortunately, drawing some overall conclusions from this collective body of research about the general effect of group support systems has not been easy because GSS findings have been relatively inconsistent across stud ies [3, 5, 9, 18].

To address this concern, McLeod [34] and Benbasat and Lim [2] conducted metaanalyses of the cumulative research findings up to 1990 and 1992, respectively. Metaanalysis is a statistical procedure for combining studies, which provides a single index that takes into account all findings (consistent or inconsistent with expectations). The conclusions from both analyses were simultaneously encouraging and discouraging. On average, GSS use was found to improve decision quality and the number of alternatives or ideas generated across studies, but, counter to theoretical expectations, it appeared to increase the time taken to complete the task and reduce participant satisfaction. More recently, Fjermestad and Hiltz [18] undertook a comprehensive nonstatistical analysis of more than 230 articles and again reported mixed findings. They found that in most cases, use of GSS led to no improvements, even in the applications believed to be most suited to GSS use (idea generation), only 29 percent of studies found performance improvements. Shaw [41] performed a focused meta-analysis on GSS studies that measured satisfaction and found that GSS groups were no more satisfied than groups working manually.

The mixed interpretations from these analyses of prior research suggest to us that the next step for GSS researchers trying to extend the common body of knowledge and for practitioners seeking to apply research appropriately is to look deeper than the overall effects of GSS use. Rather than asking whether GSS use improves perfor mance, a better question is under what circumstances does GSS use improve perfor mance because there are moderators that influence the specific effects of GSS [2]. Tasks, tools, teams, and the process by which teams use GSS may have significant effects on performance [13]. Thus, overall effects may be less important than considering effects in the specific situations in which teams work.

This paper extends the previous analyses by investigating aspects of fundamenta contextual elements and their influences on the overall effects of GSS. We begin by examining five moderators (task, GSS tools, the type of group, the size of the group, and facilitation) and their potential effects on GSS use. The next section of the paper summarizes the analysis technique: statistical meta-analysis, which is particularly useful in cases where there are many mixed and potentially conflicting results [26]. The following section presents the results, and the final sections discuss the implications for future research and practice.

## GSS Moderators

THERE ARE MANY POTENTIAL FACTORS THAT MIGHT INFLUENCE the effect of GSS use. Dennis et al. [12] presents several dozen possible moderators organized in four key categories: the composition of the group itself, the task the group is performing, the tools the group uses, and the organization within which the group works. Fjermestad and Hiltz [18] use the same four categories, and also add possible moderators that examine the process by which the groups used the GSS.

In selecting potential moderators, we wanted moderators that were both theoretically important and practically relevant. Moderators required a theoretical basis that would suggest they might change the effects of GSS use and also had to have been widely studied in prior research to enable their analysis. We choose to focus on five moderators, four drawn from the four categories of Dennis et al. [12] and one from the new process category added by Fjermestad and Hiltz [18]. These are: (1) the group task, (2) the GSS tool, (3) the composition of the group, (4) the size of the group, and (5) the effect of facilitation in the group process.

To develop hypotheses and conduct analyses, we also must define the outcome variables of interest. We choose to focus on performance, which we believe is a critically important outcome of GSS research. Performance has been defined in different ways by different researchers [e.g., 45, 51]. We follow the approach of Drazin and Van de Ven [17], Benbasat and Lim [2], and Dennis and Kinney [10], who defined performance in terms of three major factors: (1) effectiveness as defined by decision quality or number of ideas generated, (2) efficiency as defined by the time to complete the task, and (3) participants’satisfaction with the process or outcomes. Whereas no one conception of performance is perfect, we believe this is a reasonable set of factors to triangulate on the performance construct.

## Tasks

MCGRATH [33] DIVIDED TASKS INTO FOUR CATEGORIES, two of which have been commonly used in GSS research: (a) generation tasks, and (b) decision-making tasks. For generation tasks, participants work together to produce a list of ideas. Generation tasks are additive tasks, in that the outputs of individual participants are combined to form the team output. Members are encouraged to diverge, or contribute a wide variety of ideas, and they need not select among ideas nor come to a consensus on a shared understanding. Decision-making tasks, in contrast, require participants to develop a shared understanding of criteria and alternatives and reach a consensus on which alternative(s) is best.

Idea generation and decision-making tasks have different primary measures of performance. Idea generation primarily focuses on the number of ideas produced per some time period, whereas decision-making primarily focuses on decision quality and time. Both tasks do share satisfaction with process and satisfaction with outcome. Because the number of ideas, decision quality, and time performance measures are not comparable, we will address these effects of task within each of the individual sections below by reporting the results for these performance measures separately. The only direct hypotheses we have for the effect of task relate to satisfaction.

Idea generation is a simpler task than decision-making because it is additive—the participants need not come to agreement on one single course of action. GSS are designed to enable the rapid sharing of ideas, thus offer clear benefits to groups engaged in idea generation. Groups engaged in decision-making also require the abilit to resolve disagreements and focus on one discussion at a time. Although GSS may offer some capabilities to support these activities, the relative advantages over unsupported interaction are not as clear as those for idea generation.

Fjermestad and Hiltz [18] compared idea generation and decision-making tasks and found that there were a greater proportion of positive effects to negative effects for idea generation across a wide array of outcome variables, some of which included satisfaction. Their analysis combined the entire set of outcomes so it is difficult to draw conclusions for specific factors such as satisfaction alone. Shaw [41] also compared GSS groups performing idea generation and decision-making tasks and found that the groups performing idea generation tasks were more satisfied. Therefore, we hypothesize that GSS will have greater positive effects on participant satisfaction for idea generation tasks:

H1a: Satisfaction with the outcomes will be higher for idea generation tasks than for decision-making tasks.

H1b: Satisfaction with the process will be higher for idea generation tasks than for decision-making tasks.

## GSS Tools

THERE ARE A VARIETY OF GSS TOOLS that differ in many ways. Rather than focusing on the multitude of individual features available, we chose instead to examine a fundamental issue that transcends the current version of different tools. Some tools, such as Lotus Notes, provide discussion spaces, and ways to store and organize documents. These tools, called level 1, primarily support the exchange of information [14]. Level 1 support includes two important components provided by electronic communication: parallelism and anonymity. Parallelism is the ability of group members to simultaneously enter information. No participant needs to wait for others to finish before contributing information. The need to wait to speak (termed production blocking) has been identified as a major cause of poor performance in verbally interacting groups [16, 30]. Parallelism mitigates production blocking resulting in process gains [20, 46]. Anonymity, on the other hand, enables group members to contribute comments without being identified, which may increase the motivation to participate. Without anonymity, individuals, particularly low status participants, may withhold ideas due to negative evaluation apprehension [16, 30] or may feel pressured to conform to the group majority or senior participants’views [24]. Anonymity reduces the reluctance to contribute information, because it shields the contributor from group reaction and deindividualizes the interaction [7].

Other tools, such as GroupSystems.Com, provide these same level 1 capabilities, but also include level 2 tools, information analysis tools designed to aid in decision making [14]. These tools quickly and easily organize, model, change, and rank information—that is, they transform data from individual contributions to shared team information. These level 2 tools include ways to organize and analyze information, such as by the use of voting and modeling. They affect the process and outcomes by helping the group to better understand the issues under discussion. They may show the level of agreement within the group about the facts and criteria, and act to integrate differing opinions [14, 40].

Level 1 tools are most useful for helping groups generate ideas, whereas level 2 tools are useful in a variety of situations, including both idea generation and decision making. Therefore, we would expect the use of level 2 tools to have a greater effect on decision quality and time, and a similar effect on the number of ideas generated. Fjermestad and Hiltz [18] compared the use of level 1 and level 2 tools and found that use of level 2 tools led to a greater proportion of positive effects across a wide array of outcome variables. Shaw [41] had similar findings when groups using level 1 and level 2 tools were compared. Therefore:

H2a: Decision quality will be higher when using level 2 tools.

H2b: There will be no difference in the number of ideas generated when using level 1 or level 2 tools.

H2c: Decision times will be shorter when using level 2 tools.

H2d: Satisfaction with the outcome will be higher when using level 2 tools.

H2e: Satisfaction with the process will be higher when using level 2 tools

## Virtual Teams

TEAMS CAN WORK TOGETHER VIRTUALLY, using GSS to work distributed in time and space over the Internet, or they can use GSS in specially designed meeting rooms that enable members to work face-to-face. Distributed virtual work (either in space or time) eliminates—or at least significantly reduces—traditional verbal and visual communication, forcing groups to rely more on the electronic communication provided by the GSS. Electronic communication is less capable of providing concurrent feed back from the receiver to the sender (e.g., nonverbal gestures such as head nods or quizzical expressions, very brief verbal messages such as “uh huh”). Concurrent feedback plays an important role in communicating that the receiver has understood the message [29]. Some research suggests that most shared understanding comes not from the verbal (or typed) components of communication, but rather from the nonverbal messages such as facial expressions and voice [32, 39]. Reduction in concurrent feedback reduces the accuracy of communication [29, 31] and increases the time taken to complete a task [27, 28].

Thus, we would expect the use of GSS by virtual teams to be less effective and take longer in situations where verbal communication is important. Verbal communication is important for decision-making because group members must come to a shared understanding [8]. Therefore, virtual teams should experience lower decision quality and take longer to reach a decision than teams that use the GSS in face-to-face settings. Idea generation performance should be less affected because it does not require the development of shared agreement. In contrast, Fjermestad and Hiltz [18] concluded that virtual teams were more likely to experience positive outcomes than face-to-face teams. We hypothesized:

H3a: Decision quality will be lower for virtual teams.

H3b: There will be no difference in the number of ideas generated between vir

tual teams and face-to-face teams using GSS.

H3c: Decision times will be longer for virtual teams

Similarly, the reduction in verbal and visual communication reduces social presence [42, 43]. It is generally accepted that media that lack concurrent verbal or visual feedback are less friendly, more impersonal, and even depersonalizing [19, 44, 48], making them less satisfying to use [2]. Additionally, Shaw [41] found that there was a difference in satisfaction between synchronous and asynchronous GSS-supported groups. Synchronous groups were more satisfied than groups working asynchronously. Therefore, we hypothesized that GSS for virtual teams would be less satisfying to use:

H3d: Satisfaction with the outcome will be lower for virtual teams

H3e: Satisfaction with the process will be lower for virtual teams.

## Group Size

ONE IMPORTANT PRACTICAL ISSUE for both organizational use of GSS, as well as researchers designing and interpreting studies, is the size of the group. The maximum effective group size for groups without GSS is believed to be five participants [42]. This limit was based on the fact that large-sized groups experience dramatically increasing process losses due to production blocking. Because the parallelism in GSS groups mitigates production blocking, the production-blocking process losses that would normally occur in large groups should be attenuated through GSS use.

Anonymity may also help attenuate process losses normally occurring in large groups. Anonymity is impossible to maintain in two-person groups and is highly improbable, even in three-person groups. In large groups, however, it becomes increasingly difficult to identify the author of a comment in an anonymous GSS environment [47]. Thus, the gains resulting from anonymity should be maximized in large groups—or at least in groups larger than some minimum needed to enable a participant’s identity to “get lost in the crowd.”

In summary, all else being equal, in non-GSS situations, a large group size typically creates an imbalance in which losses due to production blocking inhibit performance. Placing the same large group in a GSS environment shifts the balance, whereby the gains (from parallelism and anonymity) outweigh the losses. Fjermestad and Hiltz [18] conclude that larger groups outperformed smaller groups across a wide variety of factors. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H4a: Decision quality will be higher for larger groups.

H4b: The number of ideas generated will be higher for larger groups.

H4d: Satisfaction with the outcome will be higher for larger groups.

## Facilitation

IT IS COMMONLY BELIEVED THAT FACILITATION can improve outcomes [37]. Facilitation has two key dimensions: process facilitation and content facilitation [4, 35]. Pro cess facilitation attempts to help the group in structuring the process by which it uses the available GSS tools. Content facilitation attempts to improve task performance directly by offering insights, interpretations, or opinions about the task and various decision alternatives available to the group. Process and content facilitation act through different theoretical paths. Content facilitation has the most direct effect in that the facilitator acts directly to offer new interpretations or meaning to the data available to the group in much the same way as a regular group member, although likely with more influence [23, 35]. In providing content facilitation, the facilitator is acting as expertparticipant. In general, if the facilitator is skilled in the task, his opinions should add value. Thus, content facilitation should improve performance in the same way that the participation of skilled and valuable group members should improve performance.

Process facilitation acts by helping the group to appropriate the GSS into its meeting process in the most effective manner to achieve its goals. Process facilitation increases the chance that the group will adopt the GSS structures as intended by the designer [50] and helps the group respond to problems [1]. In providing content fa cilitation, the facilitator is acting primarily as an appropriation agent. In general, if the facilitator is skilled in the use of GSS structures and the group can be expected to have appropriation problems, then process facilitation should improve performance.

Thus, although content facilitation can be expected to have a general influence, process facilitation should have the most effect in situations in which the appropriation of the GSS is challenging for the group. Most GSS facilitators advocate only the provision of process facilitation, so that the facilitator remains impartial, although a few advocate the provision of both process and content facilitation (see [35]). Most prior studies have examined only process facilitation, so our hypotheses below will focus on process facilitation.

Over time, groups develop work structures or habitual routines that constrain group processes [22]. When faced with new work structures possible in the GSS, groups must appropriate them by incorporating the GSS structures into their existing structures—this means selecting which GSS structures to use and which traditional structures to discard, often through a process of trial and error, until new GSS-based structures emerge and become stable in work processes [15]. It is our contention that process facilitation is most important when there is a high appropriation need within the group. Once the group has appropriated the GSS structures, and new work processes have become stable, process facilitation becomes less important because the group is familiar with the structures and is more capable of engaging in effective process structuring without facilitation.

Groups have the highest appropriation need for facilitation when they are unfamiliar with how to apply the GSS structures to the task they face. Appropriation need can be reduced in at least two ways: experience in using the GSS for the task, and process restrictiveness [50]. Although training can help groups understand how to use the GSS, appropriation need can be significantly reduced by enabling the group to work together and experience the structures; successful appropriation requires more than just training. Process restrictiveness constrains the way in which participants can use the GSS, so that they are more likely to adapt the GSS structures in the way intended by the process designer. It is our contention that process facilitation provides more value to groups with a high appropriation need than groups with a low appropriation need (that is, groups with experience using a GSS for similar tasks, or groups using a highly restrictive process). Most groups in prior GSS research had little prior experi ence with the tools before being studied, therefore,

H5a: Decision quality will be higher when process facilitation is provided

H5b: The number of ideas generated will be higher when process facilitation is provided.

H5c: Decision times will be shorter when process facilitation is provided.

H5d: Satisfaction with the outcome will be higher when process facilitation is provided.

H5e: Satisfaction with the process will be higher when process facilitation is provided.

## Research Methodology

## Selection of Studies

META-ANALYSIS IS A SECONDARY DATA ANALYSIS TECHNIQUE that examines a set of prior published research. The first step is selecting a set of studies that investigates the performance of both a control group and a treatment group. We selected all re search that reported the results of a test of GSS use compared to interaction withou GSS, and provided statistics for any of five performance variables (that is, decision quality, number of ideas/alternatives, time, satisfaction with the process, or satisfaction with the outcome).

To locate studies, we performed computer searches on 12 databases, did manual searches through leading management information systems, psychology and communication journals, read previous literature reviews, and posted messages soliciting studies from the subscribers of the GSS-L listserv. We focused only on published refereed papers, including journal articles and conference papers, but omitted dissertations and working papers. We also omitted studies of e-mail and “chat” software.

This resulted in over 250 studies published between 1980 and June 1999, inclusive. We selected all papers that reported the results of a test of a treatment group (GSS use) to a control group (interaction without a GSS) for any of the five dependen variables (decision quality, number of ideas/alternatives, time, satisfaction with the process, or satisfaction with the outcome). This resulted in 61 articles and 119 usable data points (see Appendix).

Some useful and often-cited articles are not included in our analyses because they did not report any of the five dependent variables [e.g., 49, 51] or because they offered no direct comparison of GSS to no-GSS groups [e.g., 7, 36]. Almost one-half of the studies crossed the GSS and non-GSS condition with levels of some other independent variable. Studies containing several such comparisons were disaggregated and treated as separate data points. For example, Gallupe et al. [21] reported the comparison between GSS and non-GSS groups for five levels of group size. Since individuals were randomly assigned to conditions, we treated the five levels of group size as independent studies for the analyses. This procedure resulted in 116 usable data points.

## Dependent Measures

The final set of studies included one or more of the five dependent variables described above. The first one, decision quality, was defined by most researchers as the correctness or “goodness” of the group’s choice for choice tasks. The number of ideas/alternatives was operationalized as the number of ideas generated for idea generation tasks, or the number of decision alternatives generated for decision-making tasks. The third dependent variable, efficiency, was defined in most cases, as the time to reach decisions (none of the idea generation tasks in this meta-analysis permitted time to vary). The final two variables, satisfaction with the process and satisfaction with the outcome, were measured via post-session questionnaires.

## Analytic Procedure

It is beyond the scope of this manuscript to describe in detail the statistical algorithm underlying a meta-analysis (see [26]). In short, the procedure computes a weighted average effect size for a given dependent variable across an entire set of studies. The first step is to calculate an effect size (d) for each dependent variable in each study. This was done using the equation presented by Cohen [6]. In brief, the mean value for the dependent variable for the non-GSS subjects is subtracted from the mean value for the GSS subjects, and this difference is divided by the pooled standard deviation from both conditions (see also [25, 26]). The net effect of this transformation is that the differences are now standardized to a common metric across all studies, and this in turn means that effect sizes may be statistically combined and evaluated. The meta-analysis procedure produces the mean effect size and a standard deviation (see [26]). Positive effect sizes indicate that the mean effect of GSS use across all included studies was to increase the outcome measure (such as, increase decision quality, increase time taken).

To test the hypotheses, we used the three-step partitioning comparison procedure developed by Hunter and Schmidt [26].<sup>2</sup> With this procedure, the studies are first partitioned into two sets. Second, a meta-analysis is performed separately on the studies within each set to produce a set of statistics for each set. Third, t-tests are used to compare the mean effect sizes between the two sets to see if there are significan differences among them.

For H1 (task), the data set was partitioned into one set of studies in which the task was idea generation, and a second set for decision-making. For H2 (tool), the data set was partitioned into one set of studies in which a level 1 tool was used, and a second set for which a level 2 tool was used. For H3 (virtual teams), the data set was partitioned into two subsets—one in which the team worked face-to-face at the same time, and one in which the team worked in different places or at different times. For H4 (group size), the data set was partitioned into two sets, one for “small” groups (five or fewer members), and one for “large” groups (six or more members)—we chose a size of five because it is arguably the maximum optimal group size for non-GSS groups [42]. For H5 (facilitation), the data were partitioned into two subsets, one in which process facilitation was present, and one in which it was not. Process facilitation was classified as present if a facilitator assisted the participants in appropriating the GSS into their process by providing either technical facilitation or group process facilitation as defined by Griffith et al. [23, pp. 23–24]. Process facilitation was classified as absent if neither technical nor group process facilitation was provided.

## Discussion

THE OBJECTIVE OF THIS STUDY was to better understand the moderators that affect group performance when using GSS. We investigated five potential moderators drawn from four sets of factors argued to affect group performance, and Table 6 presents the results of the hypothesis tests. All five of the moderators had significant effects on one or more elements of performance. We will begin by looking at the results based on the five moderators and drawing conclusions for them. Then we draw implication for future research and practice.

## Effects of Moderators

The nature of the task had a significant effect on group member satisfaction. Using a GSS to make decisions was significantly less satisfying (in terms of work processes) than using one to generate ideas, and significantly less satisfying than working without the GSS (with a mean effect size for process satisfaction of –0.53). We believe that the most visible capabilities provided by the GSS (parallelism and anonymity) are most suited for idea generation. Idea generation can easily be done using the electronic communication provided by the GSS. Using a GSS to make decisions requires the group to either use electronic communication, which does not easily fit the demands of the task [11], or to mix the GSS’s electronic communication with traditional verbal communication, which can be challenging to understand for new users of GSS. Either alternative may prove less satisfying.

The type of GSS tools used also played a key role. Use of level 2 GSS, which provided more advanced support for decision-making, improved the quality of the group’s decision, compared to groups using a level 1 GSS or using no GSS (mean effect size = 0.26). We speculate that the ability of these tools to help groups organize and rank information by voting and modeling improved the group’s understanding of the issues under discussion. This improved understanding and more in-depth analysis meant that the groups were better able to make better decisions. Use of level 2 had no other significant effects compared to the use of level 1 GSS.

The structure of the groups, whether a virtual team working in different places or time or working together face-to-face was also important for decision-making tasks. Virtual teams make significantly lower quality decisions compared to groups working face-to-face, with or without a GSS. There were no significant differences in idea-generation performance. These findings suggest that distributed virtual teams can generate ideas and discuss issues well, but virtual teams are poor at making decisions, compared to teams that work face-to-face. Verbal communication is likely critical when converging on a decision, and GSS, in general, do not have a rich verbal component. For effective performance, organizations need to ensure that distributed teams working on decision-making tasks have some access to face-to-face meetings, a video conference, an audio conference, or a series of telephone calls to augment GSS.

We also see significant differences due to group size. Larger GSS groups took significantly less time and were more satisfied with the process than were smaller groups. Although smaller GSS groups took significantly longer than smaller groups without GSS (mean effect size 0.85), larger groups using GSS did not take longer than larger groups without GSS. Likewise, smaller GSS groups were significantly less satisfied with the process than groups without GSS, whereas larger GSS groups were significantly more satisfied than larger groups without GSS (mean effect size 0.49). It has often been argued that process losses (such as, production blocking) increase with size, so that larger groups working without GSS suffer more problems than smaller groups without GSS, and thus, larger groups gain more benefits from GSS use than do smaller groups who can function well without a GSS. Our results tend to confirm this. We will note, however, that there were no differences in ideageneration performance, suggesting that GSS can help even small groups to generate more ideas.

Facilitation is another important factor in GSS performance. GSS groups with a facilitator made better decisions and were more satisfied with the process than did GSS groups without a facilitator. Interesting, GSS groups without a facilitator made no better decisions, took longer, and were less satisfied than groups without a GSS. In contrast, GSS groups with a facilitator made better decisions and were as satisfied as groups without a GSS. We argued that the primary role of the facilitator was to help groups successfully appropriate the GSS into their work processes. The results tend to support this. Groups made better decisions and were more satisfied, reflecting a smoother appropriation with less confusion over the best way to employ the GSS to perform the task. Interestingly, there was no difference in idea generation performance, suggesting that GSS tools for idea generation may be simple enough or re strictive enough to make appropriation straightforward.

## Implications for Practice

We began this paper by pointing out the mixed results from prior research and asking what implications could we draw for the application of GSS by practitioners. Under what circumstances can GSS improve performance? We believe that our analyses enable us to draw several important implications for practice.

The simplest answer to this question can be drawn by simply looking through each analysis to find the circumstances that lead to the best performance. Thus, the ideal situation in which to apply GSS is for a larger group meeting face-to-face to perform idea generation under the guidance of a facilitator. One might also conclude that a large group meeting face-to-face to perform a decision-making task could benefit from the use of a level 2 GSS under the guidance of a facilitator.

However, sometimes managers face given situations. For example, suppose the manager has a virtual team assigned to a project. Our advice would be to have the team use the GSS to generate ideas and discuss issues, but to bring team members together face-to-face or in an audio- or video-conference when the team needed to make important decisions. If a manager has a small team (five members or less) that is capable of using the GSS, we would encourage them to use the GSS to generate ideas, but not for decision-making, as the GSS offers little value at the decision making stage.

Perhaps most importantly, GSS needs to be viewed as a tool. Appropriate use can improve performance and satisfaction for groups that are likely to experience problems without the GSS. However, like any tool, GSS use results in lower performance when not used properly.

This underscores the need for a facilitator to help the group successfully appropriate the tools in the manner most likely to lead to success. Using a GSS without the facilitator offers few benefits (except for idea generation) in cases where the group has little GSS experience. Although valuable, it is expensive—perhaps even costprohibitive—to provide a facilitator to every team using a GSS. However, even modest support from a technical facilitator can improve performance, particularly when teams are new to the GSS and must learn how to operate it and adapt its structures into their work processes. Once a team has some experience with GSS, it may be possible to have the team leader serve as the facilitator.

## Implications for Research

We believe that there also are important implications for future research. First and foremost, this study shows a lack of published research in many areas (such as, see Table 3). One of the most important areas is virtual teams because of their increasing presence in today’s business environment. Although virtual teams have long been the focus of GSS research, we have insufficient research on time and satisfaction measures of performance. We encourage researchers to investigate these holes in existing empirical research.

One limitation of this study is that it focused on only five moderators, although the GSS literature suggests that others, such as task complexity, may have significan effects [2]. We need additional basic research and subsequent meta-analyses to identify other potential moderators.

We also need to devote more research to the moderating factors identified here. We need to refine them to provide a greater theoretical richness and conduct more empirical research to more closely investigate their effects. This study has shown that moderators can significantly influence the outcomes of GSS, and more research has to be done to investigate the theoretical mechanisms that explain this behavior. Prac titioners often have little choice over some circumstances (such as working in a team, meeting in large groups), so from an applied research perspective, we need to understand how GSS can be redesigned for more effective use.

Facilitation is one moderator in particular that needs to be better understood. Facilitation has received very little attention in laboratory research, but is an important part of GSS use in the field [e.g., 38]. Researchers need to identify what aspects of facilitation are particularly important to groups. Once these are identified, it may be possible to provide the major contributions of facilitation in ways other than using a high-cost facilitator. For example, if the primary contribution lies in tool selection or fitting processes to tasks, then the provision of training or expert consultation (such as a help line) may provide many of the same effects without the need for a facilitator to be present as the group works. Conversely, if the primary contribution of facilitation lies in helping the group to adapt GSS structures into existing social structures or in addressing group conflict during the meeting, then training or consultation on this aspect may prove beneficial.

## Conclusion

ONE PURPOSE OF A META-ANALYSIS is to assemble the findings from a set of studies to draw overall conclusions about a phenomenon of interest [26]. The findings from a meta-analysis are meaningful and powerful because they represent the cumulative findings across all research conducted to date on the subject of interest. This comprehensive understanding allows a field to move ahead in the appropriate direction, building on knowledge that already has been developed and moving in ways that address questions that remain to be answered.

A mature field of study can overwhelm researchers and practitioners with extensive coverage and detailed examinations of a particular subject matter. A meta-analysis principal contribution comes from identifying potential boundary conditions to the main topics under investigation. At times, however, overall effects that are identified using meta-analysis do not communicate the underlying complexity of the subject at hand—the nuances of the topic area are lost when results are combined and generalized. In this way, boundary conditions are difficult to identify. It is only through the investigation of moderators that boundary conditions truly emerge.

Our analyses support this by illustrating that, although it may be difficult to draw overall conclusions about the use of GSS, the understanding of moderators helps reduce the confusion over the effects of GSS use. Boundary conditions, such as the task and type of group, create limits, which can be used to develop guidelines that direct research and pragmatic efforts. The use of GSS in different situations can have very different performance outcomes, and researchers and practitioners need to understand this if they are to employ GSS effectively.
