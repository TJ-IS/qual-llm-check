# The Impact of Trust and Recommendation Quality on Adopting Interactive and Non-Interactive Recommendation Agents: A Meta-Analysis

Sepideh Ebrahimi<sup>a</sup>, Maryam Ghasemaghaei<sup>b</sup>, and Izak Benbasat<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of administrative Studies, York University, Atkinson, Toronto, Ontario, Canada; <sup>b</sup>DeGroote School of Business, McMaster University, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada; <sup>c</sup>Sauder School of Business, The University of British Columbia, British Columbia, Canada

## ABSTRACT

Research on recommendation agents (RAs) originally focused on interactive RAs, which rely on explicit methods, i.e., eliciting user-provided inputs to learn about consumers’ needs and preferences. Recently, due to the availability of large amounts of data about individuals, the focus shifted toward non-interactive RAs that use implicit methods rather than explicit ones to understand users’ needs. This paper examined the diferences between interactive and non-interactive RA types in terms of how they influence the impacts of two important antecedents of RA adoption, namely recommendation quality and trust on users’ cognitive and afective attitudes and behavioral intention. To that end, we developed a set of hypotheses and tested them empirically using a metaanalytic structural equation modeling approach. Our findings provide strong support for the influence of interactivity on RA users’ attitudes and cognitions. While we found that recommendation quality exerts a strong influence on consumers’ cognitive attitudes toward interactive RAs, this influence is statistically non-significant in the context of noninteractive RAs, in which recommendation quality mainly drives consumers’ afective attitudes toward the agent. Furthermore, while we found that cognitive attitudes exert a stronger influence than afective ones on consumers’ adoption of non-interactive RAs, our results indicate that the reverse is true with interactive RAs. Given the recent rise in the popularity of non-interactive RA tools, our results carry important implications for researchers and practitioners. Specifically, this study contributes to the extensive literature on consumers’ use of RAs by providing a better understanding of the diferences between interactive and noninteractive RAs. For practitioners, the findings provide guidance for designers and providers of RAs on developing and improving RAs that are more likely to be adopted by consumers.

## KEYWORDS

Recommendation agents; electronic commerce; system adoption; online trust; recommendation quality; meta-analysis; TSSEM; heuristic-systematic model; afective response model; interactivity; recommenders

## Introduction

Recommendation agents (RAs) first elicit and/or infer consumers’ needs and interests, and subsequently identify products that best fit them [114, 146]. RAs are important components of e-commerce sites, which have experienced exponential growth during the COVID-19 pandemic. Empirical studies indicate that positive associations exist between providing consumers with recommendations generated by RAs and consumers’ decision-making eficiency and efectiveness [50, 87, 124], and, as a result, their willingness to reuse RAs for similar decisions in the future [9]. According to a McKinsey report [88], 35 percent of Amazon’s sales and 75 percent of streams on Netflix can be attributed to online recommendation systems.

To generate recommendations based on consumers’ preferences and needs, RAs first need to understand what consumers like and/or need. This process of understanding consumers is accomplished by eliciting consumers’ preferences explicitly or implicitly [80]. In the former case (i.e., interactive RAs), consumers are asked to indicate their preferences or needs. Interactive RAs (e.g., www.myproductadvisor.com) ask consumers several questions about their requirements (i.e., need-based RAs) and/or their preferences for various product attributes (i.e., feature-based RAs). The RA then generates recommendations for products that best meet these requirements/preferences. However, in the latter case (i.e., non-interactive RAs), consumers’ preferences are identified implicitly. Non-interactive RAs (such as the system embedded in www.Amazon.com) infer consumers’ preferences based on their profiles, which are built using various measures (e.g., age, location, browsing behavior, purchase history, etc.) [146].

Each of these two types of RAs have advantages and disadvantages. For instance, while using interactive RAs, consumers must spend time and efort to provide their preferences to the RA and then wait for the RA to show the best recommendations based on their needs/ preferences. This is not the case for non-interactive RAs, as users can see their recommendations quickly without having to spend much time in conveying their preferences to the system. While using interactive RAs is more time consuming, many users may decide to use them, since they are more confident that the recommendations provided are based on the RAs’ clear and explicit understanding of the users’ preferences [81, 82]. However, some users may prefer to use the non-interactive RAs because they do not need to spend much time and efort in evaluating the RA and they could still rely on some heuristics (e.g., their trust in the RA) to justify the use of the RA. While in recent years, the generation and collection of massive amounts of data on consumers, as well as the rapid development of sophisticated data analysis tools and techniques, have caused increasing popularity and use of non-interactive RAs in e-commerce websites, there is still a lack of understanding of how the key distinctions between interactive and non-interactive RAs would influence consumers’ attitudes and adoption intentions. This is especially important as a significant portion of the extant RA literature is based on more traditional interactive RAs. We will address this as yet unknown issue in this study.

It should be noted that while earlier information systems (IS) studies mainly have focused on users’ cognitive attitudes to explain the adoption of information technology [33], more recent studies have acknowledged afective attitudes’ important role as well [e.g., 78, 148]. Cognitive attitudes represent the utilitarian aspect of attitudes and are formed as a result of users’ evaluations of a system’s instrumental values, such as its usefulness and ease of use, while afective attitudes, such as enjoyment and satisfaction, are based on users’ feelings and emotions that they experience when using information systems [78]. Therefore, following the popular and well-supported “multi-component view of attitude,” which argues that “evaluations are influenced by cognition as well as afect” [2, p. 34], in this study we examine RA users’ cognitive and afective attitudes, their impact on users’ adoption of these systems, and how the two main RA types with diferent levels of interactivity impact them.

A high interactivity level in an information system provides users with greater autonomy over the use of the system, as well as flexibility and control of their interaction with it [5, 6, 64]. By creating “cognitively involving experiences through active control and two-way communication” [84, p. 60], interactivity increases users’ cognitive involvement in the process of using an RA to select products or services that best fit their requirements. Similarly, interactivity has been shown to increase consumers’ afective involvement when using e-Commerce websites [65]. Compared with non-interactive RAs, when using interactive RAs, users are likely to have heightened attention, engagement, and situational involvement in the task at hand. Furthermore, interactivity in an information system could enhance users’ competence and self-eficacy in collecting task-related information [64, 69]. We suggest that these diferences carry implications for users’ cognitive and afective attitudes toward interactive and non-interactive RAs.

Additionally, there are some inconsistent findings in the literature between studies that employed interactive versus non-interactive RAs. For instance, whilst Gudigantala et al. [49] identify recommendation quality as an important antecedent of perceived efectiveness of interactive RAs, Zhu and colleagues [158] report this relationship as nonsignificant for non-interactive RAs. The relationship between consumers’ trust in an RA with their satisfaction of an RA is another example for which contradictory results have been reported in the literature. For example, Roudposhti et al. [117, 118] in a study using non-interactive RAs, suggest the relationship to be weak; whereas other scholars like Lee and Choi [77] and Dabholkar and Sheng [32] find it as strong and significant among consumers using interactive RAs. As another example, the strength of relationship between consumers satisfaction and their intention to adopt an RA is reported as strong (i.e., correlations above 0.80) in some studies using interactive RAs [e.g., 77]; while some other studies [e.g., 6] that used non-interactive RAs find the relationship to be weak (i.e., correlation close to 0.40).

To study the impacts of the RAs’ interactivity on consumers’ attitudes and behavioral intentions, we employ the afective response model (ARM) for afective attitudes and the heuristic-systematic model (HSM) for cognitive attitudes. ARM [153] is a theoretically bound conceptual framework that provides a holistic and systematic reference map for studies that consider afect in relation to the use of any information technology. In line with the focus of this research, ARM is applied in situations where direct experience with technology is the stimulus [1]. In fact, ARM provides a framework for identifying the afective concepts in an episode of interacting with information technology and understanding how these afective concepts influence or relate to other factors. Therefore, ARM has been used in the extant literature to study users’ attitudes and behaviors such as users’ privacy-related decisions [36], the impacts of personalization on online shoppers [104, 105] and the role of emotions in the emergence of IT use patterns such as task and tool adaptation [121, 122]. Similarly, in this study, we employ ARM as the theoretical foundation for examining the role of afect in studying the impacts of RAs’ interactivity on users behavioral outcomes.

HSM is a dual-process approach to understanding attitudinal change as a result of receiving new information [15, 18]. According to HSM, when individuals receive a new piece of information, they engage in two distinct mental processes, one of which entails diligent processing of the information, while the other involves using heuristic cues to assess the new piece of information. HSM calls these two processes systematic and heuristic processing, respectively [38, 39]. However, considering that engaging in systematic processing requires expending cognitive resources, individuals engage in such processing only if they feel capable and are motivated.

Although various heuristic cues have been suggested as playing a role in shaping users’ attitudes, dual-process researchers mostly have focused on source likability, source credibility, source trustworthiness, and source salience [44]. Therefore, given the important role of trust in the RA literature [e.g., 9, 28, 135, 139], we adopt users’ perception of recommendation quality and their trust in an RA, respectively, as the systematic and heuristic cues that influence users’ attitudes toward the system and their future behavior. Likewise, these two variables can impact RA users’ afective response to these systems as well. According to ARM, users’ afective response to information systems can be based on the process and/or outcome of their interaction with systems [1]. Recommendation quality can impact consumers’ outcome-based afective responses whereas trust in an RA can impact process-based afective responses. To sum up, in this study, we investigate the following research question: How do the efects of recommendation quality and trust on consumers’ cognitive and afective attitudes of RAs difer for interactive versus non-interactive RA types?

Studying the diferences between interactive and non-interactive RAs in terms of the influences of the two important antecedents of RA adoption is the first step toward understanding the similarities and diferences between two related, yet diferent, sub-streams of RA research. Furthermore, learning about the diferences in impacts from trust and recommendation quality on users’ attitudes and their adoption of interactive and noninteractive RAs can help researchers and practitioners focus on developing and improving RAs that are more likely to be adopted by consumers.

In addition, it is important to understand the relative importance of trust and recommendation quality on consumers’ cognitive and afective evaluations of diferent types of RA. In the past decade, tremendous amounts of money have been spent by large companies such as Amazon on improving the quality of recommendations generated by their RAs. If this study finds the influence of trust on consumers’ cognitive evaluations of non-interactive RAs to be higher than that of recommendation quality, then these companies might want to simultaneously invest in developing and providing means that have been found to improve consumers’ trust. As the market size of RAs is predicted to reach \$12.03 billion by 2025 from only \$1.14 billion in 2018, with compound annual growth rate of 32.39% during 2020-2025 [62], it is critical for practitioners to understand the key distinctions between interactive and non-interactive RAs (the two main types of RAs) and how they influence consumers attitudes and adoption intentions.

To answer our research question, we employed a meta-analysis approach, which is a well-known quantitative methodology for reviewing a domain of literature and investigating whether and to what extent certain findings have been replicated successfully [40, 41]. Meta-analysis has been recognized as a method to extend knowledge about a topic, as it is a quantitative approach to synthesize and integrate empirical research findings [40, 111]. As a result, meta-analysis has been used widely in information systems research [e.g., 27, 55, 118, 120]. This paper reports the results from a meta-analysis conducted on 90 studies, collectively comprising a sample of 20,509 RA users. To this end, we employed two-stage structural equation modeling (TSSEM), a technique that combines meta-analysis and covariance-based structural equation modeling (SEM). TSSEM enables us to test theoretical models using meta-analytic data from extant primary empirical studies [21, 134, 135].

## Theoretical Background

## Interactivity

Interactivity is an integral concept in digital information communication technologies. While a holistic definition of interactivity remains elusive [8], some consensus has been reached in the literature on its main characteristics: (i) active control that allows users to exert their autonomy over the communication process; (ii) two-way communication that enables reciprocality between users and the system; and (iii) synchronous communication between users and the system [e.g., 43, 84]. Active control enables users to manage their communication with a system, particularly in terms of the contents to be examined and the pace at which to proceed, and in short, to act according to their goals. Therefore, interactivity through active control provides users with the capability to guide their interaction with a system [86]. Two-way communication refers to the ability for reciprocal communication between users and a system according to users’ communication needs. Synchronous communication (synchronicity) is “the degree to which users’ input into a communication and the response they receive from the communication are simultaneous” [84, p. 55]. In light of these three important characteristics, in this study, we adopt Liu’s [83, 84 p. 208] definition of interactivity as “communication that ofers individuals active control and allows them to communicate both reciprocally and synchronously.”

Most extant literature treats interactivity as a beneficial feature of modern technology. With a few exceptions [e.g., 67], IS scholars have argued for and empirically validated various beneficial impacts from interactivity. For instance, interactivity has been demonstrated to elicit eficiency [31, 127], efectiveness [31, 127], satisfaction [85, 127], flow [98], enjoyment [31, 64], trust [31, 102], telepresence [26], engagement [150], enthusiasm [69], cognitive and afective involvement [65], and confidence [5]. It should be noted that various types of interactivity have been suggested and studied in the literature, including interactivity concerning the interaction between users and systems (i.e., machine interactivity) [e.g., 31], between users and objects/products (i.e., object interactivity) [e.g., 64], between various users (i.e., computer-mediated interactivity) [e.g., 102], and one-way broadcasting and media interactions (i.e., mass media interactivity) [e.g., 122]. This study focuses on the first type: interaction between users and recommendation agents. While interactive RAs require consumers to input their preferences for various product or service features, as well as indicate their requirements and goals, non-interactive RAs use implicit methods and rely on individuals’ previous attribute preferences and behaviors to infer future preferences and requirements.

## Multicomponent Model of Attitude

Multicomponent view of attitude distinguishes the cognitive and afective components of attitude [e.g., 38, 101]<sup>1</sup> It suggests that an individual’s evaluations of an object are influenced by cognition as well as afect [2]. These two components although related, are distinct aspects of attitudes [29]. While the cognitive component refers to an individual’s beliefs, thoughts, or perceptual responses about an attitude object, the afective component refers to an individual’s feelings or gut reactions engendered by the object [58, 110]. Since the introduction of the multicomponent model of attitude, it has enjoyed strong popularity. Several empirical studies have found support for this distinction between cognitive and attitude evaluations [e.g., 29, 129]. Likewise, the information systems literature, has made similar observations in terms of diferential impacts of users’ cognitive and afective evaluations of information technology tools on their intention to use and actual use of these tools [e.g., 41, 79, 105, 148]. In this study, we investigate the impact of recommendation quality of and trust in an RA, on consumers’ afective and cognitive responses to RAs which consequently may impact consumers’ intention to use the RA; such relationships could be diferent between interactive and non-interactive RAs.

## Cognitive Component of Attitudes: The Heuristic Systematic Model

The heuristic systematic model (HSM), as one of the two main variants of dual-process theories, seeks to explain how individuals’ cognitive attitudes change as a result of receiving new information. HSM has been used in studies in which information asymmetry exists between the information provider and recipient [e.g., 82, 130]. This dynamic is particularly true in the context of consumers’ interaction with RAs, which represents an agency relationship whereby RAs are the agents of consumers, who are the principals [146].

According to HSM, after receiving a new piece of information, individuals engage in two distinct mental processes, one of which involves diligent processing of the information by attending to its details carefully to assess its credibility and quality [39]. However, doing this for every piece of information one receives is virtually impossible. Therefore, HSM suggests that, simultaneously with this diligent process, individuals engage in a less-efortful process of using heuristic cues to assess a new piece of information. HSM labels these as more- and less-efortful processes of analyzing new information systematic and heuristic processing, respectively [38, 39].<sup>2</sup>

As cognitive social psychology suggests, humans tend to be economy-minded cognitively, i.e., they tend to exert the least efort possible when processing information [46]. Therefore, heuristic processing often dominates systematic processing, as the former is less cognitively demanding than the latter. Simultaneously, HSM is open to the possibility that individuals could have motives other than preserving cognitive efort. More specifically, the model suggests that one of the key factors in individuals’ engagement in efortful processing is their perceived need for additional information [17, 89]. As such, systematic processing is activated when the gap between desired and actual confidence levels is wide. Considering that systematic processing involves in-depth scrutiny of information received, even individuals with high motivation levels need the cognitive ability and capacity to engage in such processing [18, 19].

Given that during systematic processing, individuals engage in the more-efortful process of scrutinizing the content of a new piece of information, the quality of the information has been consistently used as the systematic cue in the social psychology literature [e.g., 15, 72], information systems literature in general [e.g., 92, 123], and the RA literature in particular [37, 57, 59, 125, 149]. Various heuristic cues have been studied in the extant literature such as source credibility [12], trust in the source of information [e.g., 108, 109, 133], recommendation set size [125], perceived ease of use [14], number of reviews for a product [152], and source social connectedness [111]. While there exist several potential heuristic cues, we suggest source credibility, a well-studied heuristic cue in the dual-process literature [e.g., 15, 107], is relevant to users’ employment of RAs. Source credibility has been found as an important heuristic cue in both users’ adoption of information [123] and adoption of information systems for future use [12, 13]. Source credibility captures the competence and trustworthiness of the source of information [142]. In the context of recommendation agents, we suggest, trust in an RA can be used in lieu of source credibility because trust in the RA literature (and information systems literature in general) has been conceptualized as the beliefs in competence, benevolence, and integrity of these tools. Since “competencebased source credibility refers to expertise and expertness, while trustworthiness-based source credibility is a function of the perceived character and integrity of the source” [123, p. 56], a tight relationship exists between credibility and trust. Several previous information systems studies have similarly employed trust as a heuristic cue [96]. Thus, in this study, we explore the impact of recommendation quality of RAs (as systematic cue) and trust of RAs (as heuristic cue) on consumers’ afective and cognitive attitudes towards RAs. We also investigate whether such relationships difer for interactive versus noninteractive RAs.

## Afective Component of Attitudes: The Afective Response Model

While the majority of early information systems literature, such as the technology acceptance model [33] and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology [132, 133], primarily focused on users’ cognitions as antecedents of IT adoption, their afective responses enjoyed greater attention during more recent years. To provide a solid theoretical framework for understanding afective responses to technology, about a decade ago, Zhang [153] developed the afective response model (ARM), which strives to provide meaning and structure for users’ afect-related responses. Zhang suggests that afective concepts can be classified along five dimensions: i) the residing (i.e., where the afect resides: within a person, within a stimulus, or between a person and a stimulus), ii) the temporal (i.e., whether the afective response is more permanent or fleeting), iii) the particular/general stimulus (i.e., whether the afective response is applicable to a specific technology or a general class of technologies), iv) the object/behavior stimulus (i.e., whether the response is directed towards the technology or towards a specific behavior with the technology), and v) the process/outcome (whether the response occurs during use of a technology or represents an overall outcome and extended exposure to a technology) dimensions [1].

The last dimension (i.e., the process/outcome) is deemed specifically relevant to this study as its main focus is on interactivity and examining the diferences between interactive and non-interactive RAs. This is because the level of users’ interactivity with an RA can impact whether their afective response is mainly driven by the process of interacting with the tool or is mostly dependent on the outcome of deploying it. Specifically, according to the ARM, afective evaluations can take place at two levels: the level of the interaction process, which itself can have diferent levels (e.g., exposure, in-depth exploration) and the level of interaction outcome, with an emphasis on relevance, goals, consequences, or overall takeaway experience [153]. When a user is involved in an active interaction with an RA, they can evaluate the tool based on their direct experience. This evaluation is based on the specific interaction between the user and the RA and therefore, is process-based. Outcome-based afective evaluations are those made as a conclusion of one’s experience with directly interacting with an information systems [153].

## Research Model and Hypotheses

To respond to our research question, we propose a research model, outlined in Figure 1. Specifically, drawing on HSM and ARM, we suggest that users’ perception of recommendation quality and their trust in an RA influence users’ cognitive (perceived usefulness of the RA) and afective (satisfaction with the RA) attitudes. These attitudes, in turn, impact users’ intention to adopt the RA for future use. Additionally, we discuss the diferences between interactive and non-interactive RAs in terms of the hypothesized relationships. The following sections describe in detail the hypotheses outlined in Figure 1.

## Cognitive Dimension: Perceived Usefulness

The cognitive dimension of attitude consists of an individual’s beliefs or thoughts concerning the target object. Following the dominant theme in social psychology, early information systems research mainly focused on the cognitive dimension of attitude. The key cognitive variables in the adoption and post-adoption of information systems are often perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, social influence, and facilitating conditions [58]. Among these variables, this research focuses on perceived usefulness (PU) as our comprehensive search of the RA literature (the details of which will be discussed in the methodology section) shows that PU is the dominant cognitive evaluation of RAs in the extant literature.

Drawing on the HSM, we suggest that trust in an RA can act as a heuristic cue when consumers evaluate RAs in terms of their usefulness. Considering that the relationship between users and RAs is essentially of an agency nature, it is rife with uncertainty [97]. More specifically, users cannot be certain about RAs’ competence regarding their ability to perform the task efectively or RAs’ benevolence and integrity, as they might not be working solely in the best interests of the user and could be biased in favor of the merchant [136, 146]. If consumers believes that an RA lacks the required competence, benevolence, or integrity, they are less likely to believe that benefits would be derived from using the RA. These three aspects (i.e., competence, benevolence, and integrity beliefs) have been the three pillars in conceptualizing and operationalizing individuals’ trust in the IS literature [47]. Therefore, trust in an RA is essential in influencing users’ PU of the system, i.e., the degree to which they believe that using an RA would enhance their performance in the acquisition or purchasing of a product or service [132].

Figure 1. Proposed research model

According to HSM, when consumers are willing and capable of exerting more efort, they invoke the resource-intensive method of systematic processing [16, 17]. Consequently, the literature on HSM consistently identifies the major variable that afects individuals’ attitudes and cognitions in such situations as the quality/merits of the information [e.g., 15, 72]. Similarly, information systems researchers have considered the quality of information generated by information systems as the systematic cue in forming users’ cognitive attitudes [e.g., 92, 123]. In the current context, recommendation quality is selected as the variable afecting consumers’ cognitive attitudes as a systematic cue. Considering that RAs’ primary task is to provide consumers with product recommendations, the quality of recommendations that an RA provides plays a pivotal role in consumers’ perceptions of the benefits they could reap from using the system. Highquality recommendations make consumers’ decision-making process more eficient and efective, thereby playing an important role in increasing consumers’ perceptions of the RA’s usefulness.

H1: Consumers’ (a) trust in, and (b) perceived recommendation quality of, an RA are positively associated with their perception of the perceived usefulness of the agent.

Consumers do not always have a clear and stable set of preferences in mind in advance, but in many cases, they construct their preferences when asked about them [10, 73]. The fact that non-interactive RAs have implicit preference elicitation methods (i.e., noninteractive RAs do not explicitly ask consumers about their requirements and preferences) makes it dificult for users to gain insight into their own preferences [146]. Therefore, consumers are less likely to have a clear understanding of their preferences to assess the quality of recommendations and evaluate to what extent recommendations have met their preferences/requirements [146]. In addition, interactive RAs provide more clarity and transparency regarding what features/requirements were considered when generating recommendations [73]. For instance, when purchasing a pair of running shoes, the interactive RA on www.asics.com asks consumers about their running goals and habits, stability, etc. However, when using a non-interactive RA (e.g., the RA embedded in www.amazon.com), runners are not directly asked about their requirements; and recommendations for purchasing a pair of shoes are generated based on their profile as well as profiles of other runners. The interaction with an interactive RA and the questions asked by it, we suggest, facilitate consumers’ understanding of their own product requirements and increase their available cognitive resources to assess the quality of recommendations the RA has generated. As such, according to the HSM, consumers using interactive RAs are more likely to engage in the systematic process of assessing the quality of recommendations generated by these systems to evaluate the benefits they reap from using them (i.e., their usefulness). However, in the absence of or with limited cognitive resources available to non-interactive RA users to assess the quality of recommendations generated, as the HSM suggests [19], they are more likely to rely on the heuristic cue (i.e., trust in the RA) when forming their cognitive evaluation of these systems. Therefore, we propose:

H2a: The contribution of trust to users’ perceived usefulness of the agent is greater for noninteractive RAs than for interactive RAs.

H2b: The contribution of recommendation quality to users’ perceived usefulness of the agent is greater for interactive RAs than for non-interactive RAs.

## Afective Dimension: Satisfaction

A significant body of the psychology literature argues that to better understand individuals behavior, afective processes should be considered along with salient beliefs [e.g., 29, 119]. Similarly, information systems literature has underlined the role of users’ afective responses. Our comprehensive search of the RA literature (we will discuss the details in the methodology section) shows that consumers’ satisfaction with an RA is the dominant afective attitude in the extant literature. Satisfaction, defined as users’ overall experience of and feeling about the IS usage [11], has been typically considered as an afective state or response to using information systems.

As was previously discussed, trust in an RA has been conceptualized as the beliefs in competence, benevolence, and integrity of these decision aids. When using an RA, various factors can influence consumers’ trust in the system. For instance, higher perceived RA transparency enhances consumers’ understanding of the inner workings of an RA [138]. Consequently, if a consumer perceives that the RA has a high level of competence, this trust could help ameliorate the consumer’s perceived threat from the RA that could result from information asymmetry and ambiguity in the RA’s performance [136]. Furthermore, an RA that is judged to be benevolent and have integrity is expected to consider the consumer’s needs. For example, when an RA communicates to users regarding how the information provided by them is employed to generate recommendations, users are likely to find the RA as caring and develop high levels of trust [4]. This trust is likely to guide users’ processbased afective evaluations of the RA and their satisfaction with it [4, 77, 159].

High-quality recommendations – specifically, those with high levels of accuracy, familiarity, and novelty – have been demonstrated to increase consumer satisfaction [e.g., 23, 49, 81, 151]. High-quality recommendations are likely to satisfy consumers, as shopping online can be an overwhelming task due to the huge amount of information available online. Information overload, which occurs when consumers are given more information than they can handle within a specific period of time, can lead to feelings of frustration [76]. Therefore, an RA that can sift through available information and present consumers with accurate, high-quality recommendations is likely to increase their outcome-based satisfaction [81]. According to ARM, this satisfaction is mainly outcome-based as it is related to relevance, goals, and consequences of consumers’ use of RAs [99]. In light of the above discussion, we propose:

H3: Consumers’ (a) trust in, and (b) perceived recommendation quality of, an RA are positively associated with their satisfaction with the agent.

Similar to cognitive evaluations (i.e., PU), we expect RA interactivity to impact consumers’ afective responses towards RAs. Drawing on the afective response model, RA users’ satisfaction can be rooted in the process and/or the outcome of using the agent. While interactive RA users’ satisfaction can stem from both process and outcome, non-interactive RA users’ satisfaction stems mainly from the outcome of the process. In other words, consumers’ satisfaction with an interactive RA is rooted both in their interaction with the tool, as well as their judgment of the quality of recommendations that the tool generates after the interaction is complete. In addition, when interacting with a system, users’ outcome-based afective evaluations of it are influenced by their process-based afective evaluations of the system [126, 155]. However, when using a non-interactive RA, a consumer has little interaction with the system. As such, it is natural to expect the noninteractive RA users’ satisfaction with the tool to be outcome-based rather than processbased. Thus, we expect non-interactive RA users’ satisfaction with the RA to be based mainly on their evaluation of its output (i.e., recommendation quality). Similarly, in a recent study of users’ transactional interactions with conversational agents, Ghosh and Faik [48] observed users’ outcome-based afective evaluations to be based on their evaluations of the agent’s responses. In addition to these outcome-based responses, the authors have also observed process-based afect due to users’ perceptions of feeling understood [48].

When using an interactive RA, due to the high level of interactivity, consumers are more cognitively and emotionally involved [65]. As such the process becomes important in driving consumers’ satisfaction. It is during this process that consumers are asked about their preferences and needs. Consequently, if the tool asks a consumer about requirements and/or product features that are important to the consumer, it is more likely that the consumer’s feeling about competence, benevolence, and integrity of the tool come to the surface and drive their satisfaction with the tool, increasing their feeling of satisfaction with the process of interacting with the RA. On the other hand, if questions askd by an interactive RA do not cover requirements and/or features important to the consumer, then they are likely to derive low trust in and satisfaction from the tool. In studying trust in virtual teams, Jarvenpaa et al. [63] argue that if trust among team members is low, then errors are more likely to be interpreted as non-cooperative behavior. However, if the level of this is high, similar errors are likely to be attributed to external factors. In the context of recommendation agents, when consumers’ use of RAs involve interaction, we can follow a similar line of argument: if trust is built during consumers’ interaction with an RA, users are likely to be tolerant toward less than perfect outcomes (i.e., recommendations); however, if trust is not built, consumers are likely to be less satisfied when received similar results. Therefore, we suggest the following two hypotheses:

H4a: The contribution of trust to users’ satisfaction with an RA will be greater for interactive RAs than for non-interactive RAs.

H4b: The contribution of recommendation quality to users’ satisfaction with an RA will be greater for non-interactive RAs than for interactive RAs.

## The Impacts of Consumers’ Cognitive and Afective Attitudes

The positive impact of PU of an information technology tool on the intention to adopt it has received tremendous attention from information systems researchers and has been validated empirically in numerous studies (for a review, see [70, 90]). Similarly, in the context of RA adoption, consumers’ PU of an RA has been extensively demonstrated to impact their intention to adopt the agent for future use [e.g., 135, 158].

While the PU of an RA measures the benefits that consumers expect to reap from using the system, satisfaction with it measures consumers’ afective evaluation of the extent to which their expectations are confirmed when using the system. In the past three decades, considerable attention has been paid to consumers’ satisfaction as a determinant of their loyalty [34, 100], and RA literature is no exception, including several empirical studies that underline the impact of satisfaction on users’ intention to adopt RAs [e.g., 6, 77, 151]

H5: Consumers’ (a) perceived usefulness of, and (b) satisfaction with, an RA are positively associated with their intention to adopt it.

A significant portion of the literature on individuals’ adoption of information technology focuses on the influence of users’ attitudes toward the technology on whether they decide to adopt and use it. In this article, we follow the dominant approach in the psychology literature that attitudes comprise both cognitive and afective components [e.g., 2]. Both of these components are pivotal in driving individuals’ behaviors, and it has even been argued that a strong correlation exists between them such that the uniqueness of each component contributes little additional variance [101]. However, several studies adopted a diferent approach and contend that cognitive attitudes are the main drivers for cognitively driven (i.e., instrumental) behaviors, while afective attitudes spur afectively driven (i.e., consummatory) behaviors [e.g., 93, 129].

A similar trend can also be observed in recent IS literature. For instance, only cognitive attitudes have been found to significantly predict the adoption of spreadsheets [148] and employees’ intention to use enterprise resource planning systems [79]. On the other hand, afective attitudes were found to be the only predictor of adoption of mobile TV services [103] and the use of mobile health and banking services in urgent and unexpected situations [156]. And yet, in a third category of studies, both cognitive and afective attitudes significantly predicted technology adoption [66, 67, 78].

RAs are mostly utilitarian systems built for a particular purpose: recommending products and services that best fit consumers’ requirements and preferences. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect cognitive attitudes to be the main driver of consumers’ intentions to adopt RAs. In other words, considering that both interactive and non-interactive RAs are instrumental tools, we expect that (a) they will evoke more cognitive responses than afective ones, and that (b) the cognitive responses will be the main predictor of users adoption of them.

While interactive RAs are designed to be utilitarian tools, they are more likely to induce afective responses in consumers than their non-interactive counterparts. Interacting with information systems generally is associated with higher afective responses in users, which bring about stronger user loyalty toward highly interactive systems [31, 127]. Similarly, when interacting with RAs, users are likely to develop stronger afective attitudes toward the system, which then increase the likelihood that they will adopt the system for future use:

H6a: For both interactive and non-interactive RAs, the contribution of users’ perceived usefulness to their intention to adopt the agent will be greater than the contribution of users’ satisfaction.

H6b: The contribution of RA users’ satisfaction with RAs to their intention to adopt them will be greater for interactive RAs than for non-interactive RAs.

## Research Methodology

To test the proposed hypotheses, we adopted the random-efects TSSEM technique [21], implemented in “metaSEM” package version 1.2.5 [22, 23] in R version 3.5.0 [113]. The random-efects TSSEM synthesizes correlation matrices from several primary studies to evaluate a set of hypotheses proposed in a research model. TSSEM, an amalgamation of structural equation modeling and meta-analysis approaches, allows us to obtain a more complete picture of the hypothesized relationships and a more precise test of the entire structural model than either of those approaches alone [111]. This is due to the fact that, unlike bivariate meta-analysis, TSSEM does not neglect interdependencies among variables that could lead to overestimation or underestimation of efect sizes. Furthermore, compared with a single empirical study, TSSEM enhances the sample size, improves statistical power, and increases the accuracy of parameter estimates by correcting for sampling error [95].

TSSEM, as a more recent approach to integrating meta-analysis and SEM, overcomes three important limitations of the more conventional approach of meta-analytics structural equation modeling (MASEM): (i) choosing the appropriate sample size; (ii) analyzing the correlation matrix; and (iii) ignoring sampling variations across studies. Researchers have employed a variety of methods – such as arithmetic mean, harmonic mean, median, or the total of the primary studies’ sample sizes – to determine an efective sample size when conducting MASEM. However, these approaches rely on ad hoc solutions, as opposed to a strong statistical theory [21]. Considering that various statistics and tests (e.g., Type I error of the chi-square test statistics, the standard error of parameter estimates) depend on sample size, using diferent sample sizes in the analysis can result in diferent results and conclusions, thereby compromising the results’ reliability [75]. The second MASEM limitation is that it uses a correlation matrix as the observed covariance matrix when fitting structural models for covariance-based SEM. This approach could cause incorrect chi-square statistics and/or incorrect standard errors of parameter estimates [30]. Ignoring sampling variations across studies is the third MASEM limitation, as it treats the pooled correlation matrix as the observed covariance matrix without considering the sampling variances and covariances of the correlation coeficients. By ignoring the pooled correlations’ standard errors, MASEM does not reflect the sampling variation accurately [21].

To address these issues, TSSEM, a two-stage approach to meta-analytic structural equation modeling, has been proposed [21]. In the first stage, TSSEM receives as inputs the matrix of correlations for each primary study, as well as its sample size, and generates a pooled correlation matrix, as well as an asymptotic covariance matrix (ACM), which captures variances and covariances arising from pooling correlations across diferent primary studies by including the pooled correlations’ variance on its diagonal and the covariances among the pooled correlations on its of-diagonal elements [94]. These two matrices are the inputs for the second TSSEM stage, which also receives as input the hypothesized relationships between constructs in a research model. Analyzing these inputs, the metaSEM package estimates the relationships between the constructs in the research model.

It should be noted that TSSEM could be conducted based on two diferent possible models – fixed-efects and random-efects models. These two types of statistical analyses make diferent assumptions regarding the heterogeneity of efect sizes across primary studies. For instance, while random-efects TSSEM acknowledges that variations exist in efect sizes across studies, fixed-efects TSSEM assumes that efect sizes across studies are homogeneous; therefore, the variations observed between studies are the result of sampling error [21]. Due to heterogeneity among efect sizes in the primary studies, in this study, we employed a random-efects model. We will discuss more details about this heterogeneity in Section Heterogeneity of Efect Sizes. Before conducting TSSEM, we performed two main tasks, the details of which are detailed below: (i) identifying the primary studies and coding the efect sizes; and (ii) performing primary statistical analyses to ensure the absence of methodological artifacts in our data that could contaminate our results.

## Identifying Primary Studies and Coding Efect Sizes

To identify pertinent primary studies, we conducted a detailed search of recommendation agents’ adoption literature. To that end, we searched a comprehensive set of databases – JSTOR, ABI/INFORM, Association for Information Systems eLibrary, Scholars Portal, EBSCO, Science Direct, ProQuest, Emerald, and Google Scholar – using search terms such as “recommendation agent,” “recommender system,” and “online decision aid.” Our search initially yielded more than 800 articles, but they were not all relevant for inclusion in our meta-analysis. More specifically, we only included studies that (1) studied consumers’ attitudes and/or their intention to adopt RAs, (2) were conducted in the context of e-commerce, (3) were empirical, (4) examined constructs of interest in this study, and (5) included the statistics required for a meta-analysis (i.e., bivariate correlations and sample sizes) or suficient data to calculate these statistics. To ensure the reliability of this analysis, two of the authors independently assessed the papers and excluded those that did not meet the above criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. This systematic process resulted in 81 papers that contained 90 studies3F <sup>3</sup>(comprising 20,509 observations).

Next, for each study, based on the bivariate correlations provided by each primary study, we coded a 5×5 matrix of bivariate correlations among the five constructs in our research model (i.e., recommendation quality, trust, perceived usefulness, satisfaction, and intention to adopt RAs). To do this, we did not merely rely on the names that the original studies’ authors used for constructs, but instead reviewed the measurement scales to ensure that they were in line with the operationalizations we adopted for the constructs in our proposed model, detailed in Appendix A. We also coded each primary study’s reported sample size. To ensure the reliability of our coding procedure, two of the authors independently coded the data from every study. Subsequently, the inter-rater reliability was assessed and found to be 98% on average, exceeding the threshold value of 0.77 [74]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Primary Statistical Analyses to Ensure Data Integrity and Study Precision (Stage 1 of TSSEM)

Prior to testing the proposed hypotheses and research model, we ensured that statistical and methodological artifacts did not contaminate our analyses and results. Particularly, we checked for issues related to (i) non-independence of primary studies, (ii) outliers, (iii) missing studies, (iv) missing and incomplete data, (v) measurement errors, (vi) Type II errors, and (vii) heterogeneity of efect sizes [61].

## Non-Independence of Primary Studies

Non-independence of data sets extracted from primary studies could be an important issue, as meta-analysis methodologies assume that the primary studies are independent of each other [61]. To address this potential concern, we undertook two approaches. First, if two of the primary studies used one data set, we included only one of those studies in our analysis. Second, we followed a method proposed by Wood [144] to further check for the primary studies’ independence. Wood suggests that the primary studies be broken down into two groups, with the first group including studies that share at least one author, and the second group including all other studies. Subsequently, we checked whether a significant diference existed between the two groups of studies regarding every possible pair of constructs. Considering that none of the resulting p-values and t-values was significant, we concluded that non-independence was not an issue with the extant studies in our study.

## Outliers

To identify potential outliers among the primary studies, we used the sample-adjusted meta-analytic deviancy statistics (SAMD) [60]. For each pair of constructs, the SAMD values obtained for diferent studies were first ranked based on their absolute values. Subsequently, a scree plot of the ranked-order absolute SAMD values was drawn. In a few of the relationships, we observed one or two outlier studies. Following an examination of these efect sizes, one study was dropped from our data set due to an unlikely high efect size (i.e., 0.9 between trust and PU).

## Missing and Incomplete Data

Not all articles reported pair-wise correlations between variables. In cases when it was not possible to extract such correlations from the article directly, we approached the authors and asked for that information. We received 11 responses, out of which 10 were usable for our analysis. For studies without pair-wise correlations, whenever possible, we calculated those statistics by following guidelines delineated by Wu and Lederer [145]. Furthermore, in fourteen studies, we imputed correlations by converting standardized regression coeficients following the method suggested by Peterson and Brown [106, 107–109].

## Measurement Errors

Diferent research studies come with diferent error regimes; therefore, we corrected the efect sizes extracted from primary studies by dividing the pair-wise correlations by the product of the square root of the two constructs’ reliabilities [61].

Subsequently, following recommendations by Hedges et al. [25, 51], the corrected correlations were normalized and converted to Fisher’s Z scores to provide greater weights to more precise estimates. Fisher’s Z scores were then averaged for the relationship between every two constructs, and the average was converted back into the correlation coeficient [51]. We used this “transformed-back correlation” to conduct fail-safe tests, Q-homogeneity tests, and power analysis.

## Type II Error

Assessing for Type II error is an important step in a meta-analysis to ensure that meta-analytic estimates have suficient statistical power [111], which is the probability that a statistical significance test will not result in the acceptance of a false null hypothesis [24]. Therefore, to assess the risk of Type II error, we conducted a power analysis test for each of the proposed hypotheses in our research model by using “transformed-back correlations” and following the procedure suggested by Cohen [24]. The results for all relationships exceeded 0.8, the commonly accepted threshold for power analysis (see Table 1) [24].

## Heterogeneity of Efect Sizes

When conducting a meta-analysis, a heterogeneity test is a useful aid in deciding whether the observed variation between efect sizes in primary studies is more than what could be the result of sampling error alone [52, 116]. Traditionally, Q-statistics were used for this purpose. Therefore, we used the transformed-back correlations for each relationship in our proposed model to calculate the Q-statistic [42]. The results for each pair of constructs exceeded the cutof, and as such, confirmed heterogeneity in each efect size.

In addition to Q-statistic, we also used the I<sup>2</sup> heterogeneity index provided through TSSEM analysis. The index is neutral to the number of efect sizes; therefore, it is a more accurate measure of heterogeneity than Q-statistics [56]. I<sup>2</sup> indices for each of the relationships in our proposed model are provided in Table 1. The large $\mathrm { I } ^ { 2 }$ values demonstrate heterogeneous efect sizes in our model. Therefore, we used random-efects TSSEM in our analysis, as opposed to fixed-efects TSSEM.

## Discussion and Implications

## Discussion

In this paper, we presented and empirically tested a theory-based research model of the impact of recommendation quality and consumers’ trust in an RA as antecedents of their intention to adopt the system. Furthermore, drawing on HSM, the afective response model, and the literature on interactivity, we hypothesized and empirically examined the impact of RA interactivity on the proposed research model, thereby providing answers to our main research question, which initially motivated the study. By employing TSSEM, a combination of meta-analysis and structural equation modeling, we tested our proposed research model empirically by using the prior findings from 90 studies in the field that cumulatively included 20,509 cases (i.e., observations).

In line with the HSM literature that indicates the pivotal role of trust in the source of information in determining its impact on the recipient’s cognitive attitudes [152], our results indicate a strong impact from consumers’ trust on their perceived usefulness of the tool and behavioral intentions. According to our results, this impact is not subject to RA type in terms of interactivity, as our results indicate that both interactive and noninteractive RA users’ trust in the system significantly impact their PU of and intention to adopt the system.

Our findings illustrate that recommendation quality’s impact on consumers’ PU of RAs is significant only in the interactive RAs category and is non-significant in the noninteractive RAs category. This finding could be attributed to the fact that users of noninteractive RA tools are not asked to explicitly enter their needs and preferences into the system. As such, they are likely to not have a clear understanding of their preferences [73, 146]. Therefore, they are not highly capable of cognitively assessing the RA based only on the recommendations generated and, thus, tend to rely more on their trusting beliefs in the RA for that purpose. This is in line with Yoon et al. [151], who suggest that RAs with explicit elicitation methods generate higher-quality recommendations compared with RAs that use implicit elicitation methods. Given that Yoon et al.’s measurement of recommendation quality relies on users’ perceptions, we suggest that users of explicit elicitation systems are more likely to be able to assess the recommendations cognitively and, therefore, are more likely to deem them higher quality.

Similarly, our results show that recommendation quality of and trust in an RA are two important drivers of consumers’ afective attitude (i.e., satisfaction). In addition, in line with ARM, which distinguishes outcome-based and process-based afective responses to information systems, our findings confirm that RA users’ satisfaction is diferently impacted by recommendation quality (an outcome-based afective response) and trust in an RA (a process-based afective response). Specifically, we found that consumers’ satisfaction with a non-interactive RA tends to be driven more by recommendation quality whereas trust mainly drives satisfaction with interactive RAs. This can help describe some inconsistencies in the literature where some studies using non-interactive RAs [e.g., 117] report significantly weaker relationships between trust and satisfaction compared to studies using interactive RAs [e.g., 32, 77].

Considering that RAs are generally utilitarian systems, we expected consumers’ cognitive attitude (i.e., PU) to make a larger impact on their intention to adopt the tool compared with their afective attitude (i.e., satisfaction). This expectation was confirmed for noninteractive RAs, but not for interactive ones, in which the results from data analysis indicate consumers’ satisfaction makes a larger impact than PU on their intention to adopt an interactive RA. One reason for this finding could be that interactive RAs tend to highly engage participants in the task of selecting products or services. This interaction seemingly results in a strong experience the afective evaluation of which could overshadow consumers’ perceived usefulness of the recommendation agent.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our work paves the way for a better understanding of the diferences between interactive and non-interactive RAs, thereby contributing to the extensive literature on consumers’ use of these systems in several ways. First, earlier empirical studies in this stream of research mainly focused on interactive RAs, but in the past few years, they have steered toward designing and testing non-interactive RAs. To be able to use these earlier studies, researchers need to be aware of the similarities and diferences between factors that drive consumers’ perceptions of and attitudes toward these two types of RAs. Our study takes the first step toward enhancing this understanding by studying the influence of interactivity on how recommendation quality and trust in RAs, two of the most important and most studied constructs in the RA literature, impact consumers’ attitudes and behavioral intentions.

Second, our study draws on the HSM to suggest and employs TSSEM to empirically test the hypothesis that the extent to which recommendation quality impacts RA users’ cognitive evaluations (i.e., PU) is diferent for interactive and non-interactive RAs as interactive RA users are more likely to rely on the systematic cue (i.e., recommendation quality) to cognitively assess the performance of these tools. The results indicate the impact of recommendation quality on perceived usefulness to be strong and significant for interactive RAs but nonsignificant for non-interactive RAs. This finding could explain some of the inconsistencies observed in the RA literature. For instance, Zhu et al. [158], who found the relationship between recommendation quality and perceived usefulness of RAs to be nonsignificant, undertook their study in the context of non-interactive RAs, while some other scholars who found the relationship to be significant employed interactive RAs [e.g., 49]. Similarly, our results shed light on the reason for why the relationship between trust in an RA and perceptions regarding its usefulness have been observed as less strong (i.e., correlations below 0.5) in studies with interactive RAs [e.g., 112, 135], compared to studies with non-interactive RAs [e.g., 91].

Furthermore, in line with these results, which indicate that consumers’ cognitive evaluations (i.e., PU) of non-interactive RAs are mainly driven by heuristic cues (i.e., trust) and that systematic cues (i.e., recommendation quality) play a non-significant role in this process, we suggest that future studies look into designing and developing means that could help consumers better assess the quality of non-interactive RAs recommendations and whether and how they match their needs and requirements. According to the extant literature, providing explanations is the prime approach for this purpose [136]. However, it should be noted that not every explanation is generated with the aim of increasing consumers’ understanding of the system and its recommendations [128, 129, 130]. There are various reasons for providing explanations for RAs, such as increasing transparency, persuasiveness, and eficiency [128]. To date, computer science scholars have proposed several technical methodologies for generating explanations (see Zhang and Chen [157]). However, the human-computer interaction literature is lagging behind in terms of conducting empirical studies on how these diferent techniques help users of non-interactive RAs by enhancing their capabilities to understand why certain recommendations have been generated and whether a good fit exists between these recommendations and their requirements/preferences.

Third, drawing on ARM and based on the diferences between users’ outcome- and process-based afective responses to information systems, this study theorized the diferent efects of perceived recommendation quality of and trust in an RA on users satisfaction. Our empirical findings confirm that trust in an interactive RA makes a stronger impact on users’ satisfaction with the system when compared with the impact of trust in a non-interactive RA on users’ satisfaction. Previous research has demonstrated a positive impact of consumers’ trust regarding their satisfaction with RAs [7, 77]. Ample evidence in the literature also indicates a positive impact of interactivity on users’ satisfaction [31, 127]. By distinguishing between the two paths (i.e., process-based and outcome-based), through which users’ satisfaction with RAs is formed, this study extends previous research by suggesting that interactivity can impact how users’ trust in an RA increases their satisfaction with the system. Similarly, our findings depict that diferences exist between the impact of the quality of an RA’s recommendations on consumers’ satisfaction based on whether the decision aid is interactive or non-interactive such that this impact is larger for non-interactive RAs. While the positive influence of information generated by information systems on users’ satisfaction has been long acknowledged in the literature [e.g., 34], this study provides novel insights regarding the impact in the context of more/less interactive RAs.

Fourth, this study models consumers’ cognitive and afective attitudes toward RAs separately. Modeling beliefs in this disaggregated manner enabled us to compare the relative influence of each attitude in determining users’ behavioral intention, thereby providing important diagnostic information. For instance, while we expected a greater impact from PU (i.e., cognitive attitude) on users’ behavioral intention regardless of RA type, the results indicate that this is true only for interactive RAs. While previous research has suggested that users’ cognitive attitudes are the main driver of their intention to use utilitarian system features [53], we demonstrate that the interactivity of a system could also be an important determinant of how much of an impact is made by users’ cognitive and afective attitudes concerning their intention to adopt RAs.

## Practical Contributions

This study’s findings should be of interest to designers and providers of RAs as well. First, we find that higher-quality recommendations from non-interactive RAs, although successful in increasing consumers’ afective evaluations (i.e., satisfaction) of these tools, do not impact their cognitive evaluations (i.e., perceived usefulness) significantly. Instead, such cognitive evaluations are mainly driven by heuristic cues, i.e., consumers’ trust in the RA. Therefore, we suggest that RA providers and designers, who strive to increase the likelihood of consumers’ adoption of their RAs, note that although non-interactive RAs have their merits, particularly because they reduce consumers’ efort, this comfort comes at the expense of users not being able to utilize available systematic cues (i.e., quality of generated recommendations) fully when interacting with and assessing these tools. Considering that attitudes formed as a result of systematic processing are more stable and more predictive of future behavior [15], we encourage RA providers and designers to invest in devising and embracing techniques that empower consumers to gauge the quality of recommendations that RAs generate. Previous research has used explanations as the main mechanism for providing users with the rationale behind the generation of specific recommendations [e.g., 28, 136].

This study’s results also indicate the critical role of consumers’ trust in an RA concerning their intention to adopt the agent for future use. This finding carries important implications for RA developers and websites that provide RAs for their users. Currently, a greater emphasis has been placed on improving the quality of recommendations that RAs generate. Our results indicate that recommendation quality, although an important factor in increasing consumers’ intention to adopt an RA, is not as important as consumers’ trust in the agent. Therefore, RA providers should consider embracing the methods that could improve users’ trust in the agent. Previous research has provided guidance on how users’ trust could be enhanced, such as through various types of explanations [136, 138], anthropomorphism [20], avatar interfaces with diferent verbal and non-verbal cues [3, 139], personalization [71, 72] and recommendation neutrality and sponsorship [140]. In a recent study, Xu et al. [147] demonstrated how accompanying recommendations generated by RAs with advice provided by experts and fellow consumers can help users tackle various uncertainties (e.g., description, quality, and fit) associated with online shopping for products and services. Xu et al. found that consumers are likely to have strong trust in websites that integrate all three aforementioned sources of recommendation.

Our findings also indicate that, unlike non-interactive RAs, afective evaluations play a more important role than cognitive evaluations in shaping consumers’ intention to adopt interactive RAs. As such, we suggest practitioners who develop and embed interactive RAs on their websites to consider using various methods to improve users’ afective attitudes toward their RAs. One prominent approach is to employ humanoid interfaces, as they have been demonstrated to be efective in improving consumers’ afective attitudes toward RAs [77, 112].

## Conclusion

Building upon the heuristic-systematic model, the afective response model, and the literature on RA and interactivity, our main goal was to clarify the diferences between interactive and non-interactive RAs in terms of the influences of recommendation quality and trust in RAs as the two important antecedents of consumers’ cognitive and afective attitudes and their intention to adopt these systems. To that end, we developed a research model and associated hypotheses with roots in the HSM, ARM, and RA literature. Using random-efects TSSEM, the proposed research model was validated empirically based on the meta-analytical data collected from 90 studies, which collectively comprised 20,509 observations about RA users. While consumers’ higher perceptions of recommendation quality improve their cognitive attitude (i.e., perceived usefulness) of interactive RAs, we found this influence to be statistically nonsignificant for non-interactive RAs. Furthermore, the findings depict the more pronounced role of consumers’ trust in forming their afective attitude (i.e., satisfaction) toward interactive RAs when compared with non-interactive ones. Furthermore, although RAs are utilitarian tools and, therefore, cognitive attitudes are expected to make a stronger impact on adoption intention, we found that consumers’ afective attitude toward interactive RAs plays a more important role in forming their intention to continue using the system. We hope that our results bring clarity to the diferences between interactive and non-interactive RAs and provide guidance for researchers and practitioners interested in studying and employing these systems.

1. It is noteworthy that in the long history of support for the notion of multi-component view of attitudes, evaluative judgements are reflected in cognitive, afective, and conative (behavioral) responses [e.g., 13, 101]. However, recent research has focused mainly on the afective and cognitive components of attitudes [2, 29, 119, 120] considering the conative component of attitude as behavioral intention [35, 45]. Similarly, in this article, we focus on consumers cognitive and afective responses to RAs.

2. It should be noted that HSM and the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) are the two main variants of dual-process theories. While HSM and ELM share fundamental similarities, our decision to employ HSM in this study is because of its emphasis on a more complex interplay between systematic and heuristic cues to attitude change. More specifically, unlike ELM, which suggests the existence of a trade-of between the two paths to attitudes change (i.e., as systematic processing goes up, peripheral processing goes down), HSM posits that these two paths can and do take place simultaneously [141]. Indeed, according to HSM, heuristic and systematic processing could occur concurrently, and the results reinforce each other [141].
