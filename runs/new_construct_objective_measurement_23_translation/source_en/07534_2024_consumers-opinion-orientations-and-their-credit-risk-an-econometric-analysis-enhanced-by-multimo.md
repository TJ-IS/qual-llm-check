# Consumers’ Opinion Orientations and Their Credit Risk: An Econometric Analysis Enhanced by Multimodal Analytics

Qiping Wang,<sup>1</sup> Raymond Yiu Keung Lau,<sup>2</sup> Wai Ting Eric Ngai,<sup>3</sup> Jason Bennett Thatcher,<sup>4</sup> Wei Xu<sup>5</sup> <sup>1</sup>Department of Information Management, East China Normal University, China, qpwang@fem.ecnu.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR, raylau@cityu.edu.hk <sup>3</sup>Department of Management and Marketing, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong SAR, eric.ngai@polyu.edu.hk <sup>4</sup>Leeds School of Business, University of Colorado Boulder, USA / Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, UK jason.thatcher@colorado.edu <sup>5</sup>School of Information, Renmin University of China, China, weixu@ruc.edu.cn

## Abstract

The rise of financial technology (fintech) has motivated practitioners and researchers to explore alternative data sources and enhanced credit scoring methods for better assessment of consumers’ credit risk. In this study, we examine whether deep-level diversity derived from consumers’ multimodal social media posts (i.e., alternative data) can enhance credit risk assessment or not. First, we propose novel lifestyle-based risk constructs (e.g., opinion risk) to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. Second, we incorporate these lifestyle-based risk constructs into econometric models to empirically evaluate the relationship between consumers’ deep-level diversity and their credit risk. Using a credit scoring dataset provided by a fintech firm listed on Nasdaq, our econometric analysis reveals that consumers’ opinion risk constructs extracted from their multimodal social media posts are positively associated with their credit risk. Furthermore, our results show that the proposed opinion risk constructs can significantly improve the effectiveness of predicting consumers’ credit risk. Interestingly, our empirical results also show that combining the opinion risk constructs derived from images and text can significantly improve the effectiveness in credit risk prediction. This work contributes to the fintech domain by proposing novel lifestyle-based risk constructs for decision support in the credit scoring context.

Keywords: Credit Risk, Deep-Level Diversity, Econometric Analysis, Social Media, Fintech

## 1 Introduction

With the rising tide of fintech innovations, financial institutions are rapidly gravitating toward the realm of digital financial services, also known as “d-finance,” capitalizing on their cost-effectiveness and convenience. Digital microfinance (“d-microfinance”) stands out in this landscape as a notable fintech service, witnessing burgeoning interest from both researchers and practitioners. Through d-microfinance, traditional financial boundaries are transcended, with institutions leveraging internet-based platforms to promote innovative services such as online personal wealth management, online insurance, microloans, and peer-topeer lending (Cnudde et al., 2019; Djeundje et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2017). This fintech solution not only offers institutions a gateway into the vast digital consumer market but also provides a lifeline for those often marginalized by the financial system due to a lack of securable assets or comprehensive financial records (Huang et al., 2017). The societal impact of d-Microfinance is significant, as it reduces poverty by fostering financial inclusivity for countless individuals (Cnudde et al., 2019; Copestake, 2007; Durango-Gutiérrez et al., 2023).

However, while d-microfinance is a significant fintech advancement, it may bring excessive credit risk to financial institutions due to a higher likelihood of customer default (Durango-Gutiérrez et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2017; Xu & Chau, 2018). Credit risk refers to the likelihood that an individual or an organization will fail to repay a loan by a given time in the future (i.e., will default) (Noh et al., 2005). One critical contributing factor to the increased risk of default in d-microfinance is the inadequate or absence of comprehensive credit records for internet-based customers, who are the primary users of these services (Durango-Gutiérrez et al., 2023; Xu & Chau, 2018). This leads to the pressing need for financial institutions to explore alternative data sources to enhance their credit scoring processes and reduce the excessive credit risk associated with d-microfinance.

To achieve a better assessment of credit risk, researchers and practitioners have been exploring various factors for a long time. These include loan characteristics (Emekter et al., 2015); “hard” credit information, such as the demographic attributes (e.g. age, educational background, and marital status) and financial attributes (e.g. income, property ownership, and repayment histories) of consumers (Stein, 2002; Thomas et al., 2005); and “soft” credit information, such as consumers’ social relationships with others (Greiner & Wang, 2009), consumers’ behavioral characteristics (Sarlija et al., 2009; Thomas et al., 2005), and linguistic features extracted from consumers’ textual communications (Larrimore et al., 2011). This type of information is commonly known as “conventional data.” Recently, driven by fintech advancements coupled with the ubiquitous use of the internet and mobile devices, a new form of detailed, usergenerated behavioral data has emerged. This includes consumers’ social media activities, mobile phone usage patterns, online shopping behaviors, and web page activities. This wealth of information is collectively known as “alternative data” (Cnudde et al., 2019; Djeundje et al., 2021). Although this type of data (e.g., social media data) may not be directly related to consumers’ credit behaviors, studies have found that it can reflect their personalities or socioeconomic status, which can significantly influence their default behavior (Berg et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022).

For internet-based consumers, although a significant portion lacks comprehensive credit records, they are avid social media users and enjoy expressing their attitudes, values, views, and opinions—so-called deep-level diversity (Casper et al., 2013; Harrison et al., 1998). This wealth of unstructured data left by individuals on social media could potentially be used to evaluate or forecast their credit risk (Ge et al., 2017). However, previous research has primarily focused on elementary social media features, such as the existence of social media accounts (Lu et al., 2019), number of online friends (Ge et al., 2017), shallow digital footprints (Berg et al., 2020), and email usage patterns (Djeundje et al., 2021). Consumers’ deep-level diversity (Casper et al., 2013; Harrison et al., 1998) remains underexplored by researchers.

Deep-level diversity refers to individuals’ attitudes, opinions, values, and beliefs, which are not readily apparent from surface-level characteristics like age, gender, or ethnicity. Indeed, variations in values, attitudes, or beliefs among consumers can influence their willingness to repay debts (i.e., credit risk). Although various credit scoring models have previously been proposed by researchers, the literature lacks a model with a rigorous theoretical foundation to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. Furthermore, other than a recent study conducted by Shin et al. (2020), most research in information systems (IS) and fintech has only examined consumers’ opinions extracted from text (Lau et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2018). The “variety” of social media big data (Lau et al., 2016), including images, has rarely been explored to improve business decision-making. In general, images speak louder than text (Lee et al., 2015), and they provide more insights into individuals’ behavioral characteristics (Hum et al., 2011). Relying solely on text can inadvertently introduce biases, whereas visual data offers a more holistic, impartial representation of an individual’s lifestyle, expenditure patterns, and preferences, thereby fostering equitable credit assessments. Accordingly, exploring the relationship between consumers’ credit risk and the deep-level diversity captured in both the text and images of multimodal social media posts warrants further investigation.

The theory of lifestyle pattern (LP) provides a comprehensive approach to capturing consumers’ characteristics through four dimensions, namely demographics, activities, interests, and opinions (Plummer, 1971). Previous studies have shown that the dimensions of activities, interests, and opinions can effectively reflect people’s deep-level characteristics, which play a critical role in shaping their subsequent behaviors (Harrison et al., 1998; Holland, 1997). Building on this, this study proposes three novel types of lifestylebased risk constructs (i.e., activity risk, interest risk, and opinion risk) to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. By incorporating these novel constructs into econometric models, we investigate the relationship between consumers’ credit risk and their deep-level diversity extracted from multimodal social media posts. Furthermore, according to the theory of planned behavior (TPB), an individual’s future behavior is influenced by their subjective norms, attitudes, and opinions (Ajzen, 1991; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006). Accordingly, TPB offers a theoretical basis to explain the possible connection between consumers’ deep-level diversity and their future default behaviors. Our main research questions are as follows:

RQ1: Are there significant relationships between consumers’ deep-level diversity measured by the respective lifestyle-based risk constructs and their credit risk?

RQ2: Does consumers’ deep-level diversity significantly improve the effectiveness of credit risk prediction?

RQ3: Does consumers’ deep-level diversity extracted from images offer similar predictive power as that extracted from social media text?

The theoretical contributions of our study are threefold. First, grounded in theories of deep-level diversity and lifestyle pattern (LP), we developed and evaluated novel lifestyle-based constructs, namely activity risk, interest risk, and opinion risk to measure consumers’ deep-level diversity extracted from their multimodal social media posts. Second, drawing on the theory of planned behavior, we conducted an econometric analysis to empirically examine the relationship between consumers’ deep-level diversity and their credit risk. Finally, we empirically evaluated the predictive power of consumers’ opinion risk constructs derived from social media text and images, respectively. Our findings underscore the usefulness of utilizing multimodal alternative data such as social media text and images to evaluate consumers’ credit risk. As fintech solutions continuously seek innovative data streams, our empirical results accentuate the paramount potential lying in these multimodal digital footprints. The business implications of our study are significant because they provide an avenue for extracting consumers’ deeplevel diversity from multimodal social media big data to enhance an array of business decision-making processes such as credit scoring, personalized wealth management, targeted marketing, etc.

## 2 Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

## 2.1 Literature Review and Theoretical Foundations

## 2.1.1 Theory of Lifestyle Pattern

In order to develop a holistic credit scoring model that captures consumers’ deep-level diversity (Casper et al., 2013; Harrison et al., 1998), we drew inspiration from the lens of the theory of lifestyle pattern (LP) (Plummer, 1974). This theory has proven successful in capturing the behavioral and psychological traits of consumers that can impact their credit-related decisions or risks (Bernthal et al., 2005; Plummer, 1971; Wickramasinghe & Gurugamage, 2012). LP consists of four dimensions, namely demographics, activities, interests, and opinions (Plummer, 1971). While demographics capture the surface-level attributes of individuals, the latter three dimensions are effective in capturing their deep-level characteristics (Lazer, 1963). Activities (A) refer to the regular actions of individuals, including their hobbies, and spending habits. (Lin & Shih, 2012; Yu, 2011). How individuals manage their spending habits reflects their credit risk. For example, a borrower who spends money responsibly will likely be viewed as less risky than someone with a history of reckless spending. Interests (I) represent the things that individuals consider important in their immediate surroundings (Lin & Shih, 2012; Yu, 2011). This dimension delves into passions and driving forces that steer an individual’s core longterm decisions. For instance, someone interested in establishing a solid family life might first try to secure a steady job. Opinions (O) reflect individuals’ attitudes and beliefs about themselves and the world around them (Lin & Shih, 2012; Sandy et al., 2013). Far from being mere statements, opinions are laden with emotional significance and cognitive reflections. This dimension also delves into individuals’ feelings about the surrounding environment and societal issues. Collectively, the AIO dimensions delve into the intricate web of motivations, behaviors, beliefs, and values that truly define individuals. This intricate capture of deeplevel diversity is crucial for crafting comprehensive profiles, making nuanced predictions, and understanding the multifaceted nature of consumers.

Building on this foundation, our research extends the AIO framework to develop novel lifestyle-based risk constructs that aim to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. Specifically, we identify three types of lifestyle-based risk constructs, namely activity risk, interest risk, and opinion risk. Activity risk pertains to the regular activities that consumers engage in and how these activities may impact their ability to repay debts, thereby inducing credit risk. For example, if a consumer spends excessively on hobbies or luxury items, they may be more likely to face financial difficulties and default on their loans (Hackbarth et al., 2006). Interest risk refers to things that people prioritize in their immediate surroundings, which may lead to credit risk. For example, an individual who has a large family with many children may have a higher debt-to-income ratio, which could ultimately lead to higher credit risk (Dinh & Kleimeier, 2007). Finally, opinion risk refers to consumers’ attitudes and beliefs about themselves and the surrounding environment, which may also impact credit risk. For instance, if an individual has an antisocial attitude or negative views of financial responsibility, they may be more likely to engage in risky financial behavior and experience credit risk. In other words, activity risk, interest risk, and opinion risk are subsets of individuals’ activities, interests, and opinions that may impact credit risk. In this study, we focus more on the opinion dimension and its relationship with consumers’ credit risk for three reasons. First, prior researchers have explored the lifestyle dimensions of demographics (Thomas et al., 2005), consumers’ shallow behaviors (Lin et al., 2013; Sarlija et al., 2009), and interests (Cnudde et al., 2019) in the context of credit risk assessment. However, the impact of consumers’ opinions on their credit risk remains unclear. Second, while it is true that activities and interests are important considerations in financial decision-making, people often make financial decisions based on their opinions (i.e., attitudes and beliefs) about themselves and others rather than solely objective information (Jiao et al., 2013; Zhang & Li, 2011). Third, TPB posits that individuals’ behaviors are influenced by their attitudes and subjective norms. Therefore, exploring the relationship between consumers’ opinion orientations and credit risk is a logical extension of TPB’s theoretical framework.

## 2.1.2 Theory of Planned Behavior

The theory of planned behavior is a widely used theoretical framework in social psychology for comprehending human behaviors (Ajzen, 1991; Horng et al. 2013; Cucinelli et al., 2016). It posits that an individual’s future behavior is driven by their behavioral intentions, which are in turn determined by three factors, namely attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control (Ajzen, 1991). TPB provides a solid theoretical foundation for applying LP to model consumers’ behaviors (Horng et al. 2013). Specifically, previous studies have effectively applied TPB to explain individuals’ risk-related behaviors— for example, purchasing medium or high-risk financial products (Cucinelli et al., 2016), filing debt management plans (Xiao & Wu, 2008), utilizing credit cards (Rutherford & Devaney, 2009), and engaging in risky credit activities (Xiao et al., 2011).

TPB provides a rigorous theoretical foundation that can be used to explore the connection between consumers’ opinion orientations and credit risk (Ajzen, 1991; Horng et al. 2013; Cucinelli et al., 2016). According to TPB, individuals’ attitudes and subjective norms are the primary factors that influence their future behaviors. In the evolving realm of dfinance, consumers’ opinion orientations can be seen as a synthesis of both attitudes and subjective norms.

Their attitudes about loans, debt repayment, and financial planning are critical determinants for their creditworthiness. For example, if consumers’ deepseated beliefs and attitudes predispose them to view debt as a positive endeavor, their behaviors will lean toward embracing situations that might lead them into debt. In addition, if consumers’ perceived subjective norms are that (1) taking on debts to support various lifestyle activities is common or that (2) not repaying debts is socially acceptable behavior, these norms could reinforce their intention to engage in borrowing and default behaviors. By tapping into these opinion orientations through the lens of TPB, we can develop a better understanding of their future default behavior.

To summarize, by integrating LP with TPB, this study posits that a consumer’s opinion orientation is a determinant of their credit risk. This integrative approach suggests that people’s opinions, shaped by both intrinsic attitudes and societal norms, play a pivotal role in their creditworthiness and default likelihood. In essence, by examining consumers through the combined lens of LP and TPB, this research hopes to achieve a richer, more nuanced understanding of consumers’ credit behavior.

## 2.1.3 Credit Scoring in the Fintech Era: Harnessing Alternative Data

In the contemporary landscape of financial services, the intersection of finance and technology, often termed “fintech,” has ushered in unprecedented innovations. Among these, credit scoring stands out as an area undergoing significant transformation, reshaping the way financial institutions assess and engage with consumers (Berg et al., 2020). Credit scoring is a risk management technique that assesses the credit risk of borrowers. In general, there are two approaches to credit scoring, namely traditional and alternative credit scoring. Traditional credit scoring models mainly focus on structured data including consumers’ sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education level, marriage status, number of children, occupation, and job history) and financial information (e.g., income level, bank accounts, loans, and payment history) to assess consumers’ creditworthiness. For instance, Slocum and Mathews (1970) found that social class and income are reliable indicators of consumer credit behavior. Hayashi and Stavins (2012) reported that older consumers and higher-income earners tend to have higher credit scores and that those with lower education levels tend to have lower credit scores. Emel et al. (2003) stated that the average payment period of borrowers can help commercial banks filter out high-risk consumers. Similarly, Hsieh and Hung (2010) argued that a borrower’s current job status and loan credit history contribute to the evaluation of bank customers’ credit risk.

The most common credit scoring method is Fair Isaac Corporation’s credit scoring system, also known as the FICO score. The FICO score evaluates a borrower’s creditworthiness based on five factors: payment history, length of credit history, current indebtedness, types of credit used, and new credit accounts. FICO scores range from 300 (poor) to 850 (excellent). Most financial institutions in the United States use FICO scores and borrowers’ credit reports to assess credit risk. However, traditional credit scoring methods have been criticized for relying too heavily on borrowers credit history and sociodemographic characteristics, which can potentially lead to racial bias, poor risk predictability, and discrimination against borrowers with limited credit histories (Cnudde et al., 2019; Copestake, 2007; Durango-Gutiérrez et al., 2023).

Recognizing these limitations, the fintech industry, aided by artificial intelligence (AI), has paved the way for innovative alternative credit scoring mechanisms. These methods leverage alternative data to assess consumers’ credit risk. Such alternative data include borrowers’ social media data (Cnudde et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2019), mobile phone data (Agarwal et al., 2021), email usage data (Djeundje et al., 2021), psychometric data (Yang et al., 2022), website browsing behavioral data (Berg et al., 2020), and so on. For example, Cnudde et al. (2019) explored the potential of using Facebook data for credit scoring in microfinance. They found that fine-grained interest-based data (e.g., liking a Facebook page, joining a Facebook group, and interaction among Facebook users) yielded higher predictive power than that of social network data in terms of creditworthiness assessment. Djeundje et al. (2021) provided experimental evidence that a machine learning model (e.g., logistic regression, LASSO, or Neural Networks) that incorporated email usage, psychometric, and demographic features achieved higher predictive accuracy than a traditional model that used demographic features alone.

In addition, Berg et al. (2020) examined digital footprint—a trace of users’ behavioral data of accessing or registering on a website—for predicting consumer default. Using a unique dataset covering around 250,000 purchases from a German e-commerce company, they demonstrated that digital footprints complemented credit bureau information to significantly reduce consumers’ default rates. In sum, existing studies have revealed that combining traditional data (e.g., demographics and financial data) with alternative data by using machine learning methods can lead to the development of more effective credit scoring models for assessing borrowers’ creditworthiness. Although previous studies have primarily examined consumers’ sociodemographic attributes, financial data, and shallow behavioral characteristics extracted from text to assess consumers’ creditworthiness, the exploitation of consumers’ deep-level diversity extracted from both social media text and images has not been examined in the existing literature. Hancock et al. (2013) demonstrated that college students’ attitudes about credit cards significantly influence their credit card behaviors. Yang et al. (2022) found that individuals’ personality traits extracted from their textual social media posts are conducive to credit risk assessment.

Though novel credit risk constructs have been developed by fintech researchers, these constructs are derived from textual or numerical sources only (Agarwal et al., 2021; Berg et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2019). In fact, existing studies are limited by scalability (e.g., manually crafted features) and generalizability (e.g., small-scale empirical studies) in terms of developing novel constructs based on visual social media content (Li et al., 2016; Pieters et al., 2010). The adage “a picture is worth a thousand words” underscores the potency of visual information because it encapsulates nonverbalized nuances adeptly (Borth et al., 2013b; Mazloom et al., 2016). Advancing this premise, recent studies have evidenced that visual information extracted from images can complement textual information, and thus enhance various business applications such as advertisement effectiveness (Shin et al., 2020) and social media popularity prediction (Mazloom et al., 2016).

In the realm of credit scoring, consumers are increasingly expressing their attitudes, values, views, and opinions through both texts and images on social media. While textual data can provide a linear narrative of consumers’ activities, images shed light on the intricate facets of their lifestyles, spending patterns, and preferences. Images not only provide a vivid snapshot of an individual’s tangible decisions—often left unarticulated in written form—but can also corroborate or challenge assertions made in text. Solely relying on textual data can inadvertently introduce biases, whereas visual insights may present a more holistic, impartial representation of a person’s situation, leading to fairer credit assessments. Addressing these insights and gaps, this study proposes three novel types of lifestyle-based risk constructs derived from social media text and images to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity, which can be used to examine the relationship between consumers’ deeplevel diversity and their credit risk.

## 2.2 Hypothesis Development

Grounded on TPB theory (Ajzen, 1991), we hypothesize that consumers’ opinion risk extracted from social media text may be positively associated with their credit risk. As mentioned in Section 2.1.2, consumers’ opinion risk constitutes a form of attitudes and subjective norms, as proposed by TPB. To elucidate, TPB emphasizes the profound role of attitudes in shaping credit behaviors. For example, Norvilitis et al. (2006) conducted a survey of 448 students from five college campuses and found that students with hedonic attitudes about credit card use were more likely to have greater debt and decreased financial well-being. Similarly, Wood (1998) surveyed US adults over the phone and found that individuals who were more present-oriented, impulsive, and had lower levels of self-control were more likely to have lower credit scores and more credit delinquencies. Other attitudes, such as passivity, depression, or risktaking toward oneself may lead to financial difficulties (Bridges & Disney, 2010; Worthy et al., 2010). In our context, if consumers exhibit an interest in luxury or hedonistic lifestyles in their social media posts or demonstrate indifference toward financial obligations or the importance of creditworthiness, this opinion risk can lead to increased probabilities of loan default, which can ultimately increase credit risk. Moreover, individuals’ opinions about financial responsibility and their perceived ability to manage their finances can also impact their credit risk. A lack of confidence in managing their finances could escalate the likelihood of payment defaults, thereby increasing credit risk (Marafon et al., 2018).

Apart from personal attitudes, TPB also identifies subjective norms as influencing factors for credit behaviors (Ajzen, 1991). Empirical insights from Berger and Gleisner (2009), who analyzed a dataset of over 14,000 loans from a fintech lending platform, showcased the significant impact of borrowers’ subjective norms on borrowing and repaying behaviors. Wang et al. (2020) surveyed 713 subjects with microloan experience and discovered that borrowers’ moral norms (i.e., perception of disapproval of others) act as constraint factors for their default behaviors. In our context, social media often captures subjective norms and peer opinions. If a consumer’s social media posts reflect a consensus that trivializes debt or celebrates excessive spending, they may feel socially validated in adopting risky financial behaviors. Accordingly, we develop the following hypothesis according to TPB theory and existing empirical evidence:

## H1a: Consumers’ opinion risk extracted from social media text is positively associated with their credit risk.

With the rapid advancement of digital communication technology, people increasingly use images to share their activities and express opinions (Hogan, 2010; Shin et al., 2020). According to dual coding theory, both verbal (i.e., textual messages) and nonverbal (i.e., images) representations of information are vital for human cognition (Clark & Paivio, 1991). Text and images both capture the conversations people have, which shape their deep behavioral characteristics (Davis, 2010). Images, in particular, offer more clues about an individual’s characteristics, and social media images can reflect their personalities and behavioral patterns (Hum et al., 2011). Compared to text, images can capture richer semantic details and are easier for people to understand (Pittman & Reich, 2016; Rettberg, 2009; Shin et al., 2020). Accordingly, we developed a hypothesis to investigate the relationship between consumers’ credit risk and the opinion risk construct derived from images in multimodal social media posts.

H1b: Consumers’ opinion risk extracted from images in multimodal social media posts is positively associated with their credit risk.

Previous empirical research has established that consumers’ soft credit attributes extracted from alternative data can enhance the accuracy of credit risk prediction. For example, Djeundje et al. (2021) reported that consumers’ psychometric features and email usage can effectively predict their credit risk. Cnudde et al. (2019) found that consumers’ interests extracted from Facebook updates (e.g., “liking” specific posts) are more potent than their social relationship attributes (e.g., the number of friends) in forecasting credit risk. Moreover, Berg et al. (2020) provided experimental evidence that consumers’ digital footprint variables (e.g., device type and operating system) are complementary to credit bureau data in predicting defaults. In addition, based on their study of the Renrendai P2P platform, Liang and He (2020) found that semantic features extracted from loan descriptions can significantly improve the predictive power of credit evaluation models. Accordingly, we formulate the following hypotheses to examine the predictive power of the proposed opinion risk constructs.

H2a: Consumers’ opinion risk extracted from social media text can significantly improve effectiveness in predicting their credit risk.

H2b: Consumers’ opinion risk extracted from images in multimodal social media posts can significantly improve effectiveness in predicting their credit risk.

Figure 1 depicts the conceptual research framework of this study. First, consumers’ deep-level characteristics are extracted from social media text and images, respectively. Second, grounded on the theory of LP (Plummer, 1974), we develop novel constructs of activity risk, interest risk, and opinion risk to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. Finally, drawing on TPB (Ajzen, 1991), we hypothesize that consumers’ credit risk is potentially influenced by their opinion risk, using demographics, activity risk, and interest risk as control variables.

## 3.1 Lifestyle-Based Risk Constructs Derived from Text in Social Media Posts

First, we invited domain experts from the collaborating fintech firm to select relevant lifestyle subdimensions and items based on the well-known LP instrument (Plummer, 1974), which captures activity risk, interest risk, and opinion risk, respectively. Only the subdimensions of activities, interests, and opinions were applied to our text analytics process to develop the proposed lifestyle-based risk constructs.

Using the enriched topical descriptions for each lifestyle subdimension, we built a probabilistic language model (Lau et al., 2011) to retrieve the most relevant textual posts with respect to each lifestyle subdimension. These retrieved textual posts were then manually verified by domain experts to ensure their relevance for credit risk assessment. ## 3.2 Lifestyle-Based Risk Constructs Derived from Images in Social Media Posts

To extract consumers’ deep-level diversity from images, we developed an image miner based on a pretrained DCNN (Jou et al., 2015) extended with our lifestyle-related attention mechanism. In particular, the DCNN was initially pretrained to map images to 4,421 adjective-noun pairs (ANPs). An ANP is a mid-level semantic representation of an image composed of two elements: an adjective and a noun (e.g., luxury car) (Borth et al., 2013a; Jou et al., 2015). More specifically, we first identified a set of commonly used ANPs from several large textual corpora. We then retrieved eight million images from Flickr, which were annotated with the relevant ANPs using extensive crowdsourcing efforts (Jou et al., 2015). We utilized 1,082,760 annotated images publicly available from the MVSO system and 30,000 bootstrapped lifestylerelated images generated via the GAN-based (Mao et al. 2019) data augmentation method to fine-tune the DCNN via end-to-end training. Moreover, the lifestyle-related images were applied to learn the lifestyle-related attention weights via backpropagation. After successful training, we analyzed each image in our social media dataset and mapped it to 4,571 ANPs, which included the original ANPs from the MVSO system and our lifestyle-related ANPs. We categorized these ANPs with respect to three lifestyle dimensions relevant to credit risk assessment. Some examples of these ANPs are shown in Table 4. ## 3.3 Data Description

Our empirical analysis utilized a dataset provided by a large international fintech firm listed on Nasdaq. When a client applies for a microloan, the client first needs to register with the company by providing personal information, such as age, education level, monthly income, marital status, and social media accounts through the firm’s website or mobile application. The client must also consent (“opt in”) to grant the company and related third parties permission to access their personal data, including the public content of their online social media accounts and online shopping histories. Before a loan officer evaluates a microloan application, the collaborating fintech firm uses the official APIs of respective social media platforms (e.g., Weibo) to collect the clients’ multimodal social media posts. To ensure individual privacy protection and appropriate personal data usage in a research context, our study followed common research ethics (Babbie, 2004). All microloan clients had “opted in” to allow third parties to use their personal data for noncommercial purposes. Furthermore, the dataset used in our study removed the actual identities of the clients. Each active microloan in the dataset included an attribute that captured the number of loan repayment overdue days. If the number of overdue days was greater than 0, it indicated the client’s default behavior. The collaborating fintech firm’s current practice was to consider clients with longer overdue periods to be of higher credit risk.

Before conducting our regression analysis, we refined our original dataset, which initially consisted of 1,572 microloan clients. We removed microloan clients who did not have a sufficient number of posts on Weibo to ensure that our dataset was of high quality. Following this process, our dataset consisted of 1,385 microloan clients who had at least three multimodal Weibo posts and microloans established during the study period between November 2017 and October 2018. Our dataset contains a total of 192,185 multimodal Weibo posts, capturing 175,295 images from 2009 to 2018. As less than 5% of users posted videos in this period, we focused solely on text and image data in this study. Table 5 above provides a detailed description of the variables included in our dataset, with the focal independent variables being lifestyle-based risk constructs and subopinion risk constructs derived from microloan clients’ multimodal social media posts. The dependent variable was the number of repayment overdue days, which is commonly used as a proxy for consumers’ credit risk (Brezigar-Masten et al., 2021). Moreover, the other personal data of the microloan clients (i.e., demographics and financial attributes) (Wu et al., 2020) and the social media-based attributes of previous empirical studies were taken as control variables (Ge et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2013). ## 4 Main Results

## 4.1 Lifestyle-Based Risk Constructs Extracted from Text

Table 7 presents the results of our econometric investigation concerning the relationships between consumers’ credit risk and various lifestyle-based risk constructs that unveil their deep-level diversity. In particular, Model 1 (incorporating various lifestylebased risk constructs except the opinion risk construct) and Model 2 (incorporating the opinion risk construct) presented in Table 7 examine the effects of the lifestyle-based risk constructs derived from consumers’ textual social media posts. Our regression analysis reveals a significant and positive correlation between TOpinion and CRisk. In other words, consumers who display more opinionated attitudes in the opinion risk dimension (e.g., holding an antisocial attitude) are more likely to be associated with a higher credit risk. Our empirical results are consistent with previous findings obtained in consumer finance research that highlight the possible associations between consumers’ attitudes (opinions) and their credit statuses (Lea et al., 1993; Lea et al., 1995).

According to TPB (Ajzen, 1991), individuals’ subjective norms (e.g., opinions) can influence their intentions and future behaviors. This concept is particularly relevant in the realm of personal finance, where a consumer’s default behavior may be influenced by their personal beliefs and opinions about financial matters. Recent research has also shed light on an alternative explanation for these findings. Specifically, studies have shown that similarity in political affiliation between employees and employers can play a role in recruitment and promotion decisions within firms (Roth et al., 2017; Roth et al., 2020). This means that an individual’s political beliefs may make them more or less desirable as an employee (Wade et al., 2020), potentially impacting their income and creditworthiness over time.

Surprisingly, TActivity and TInterest are not significantly associated with CRisk, indicating that consumers’ activities and interests captured in their textual social media posts are not indicative of their credit risk. This finding differs from those of previous studies (Cnudde et al., 2019; Djeundje et al., 2021). According to the theory of LP (Lin & Shih, 2012; Sandy et al., 2013), the notion of activities refers to how consumers usually spend their time, while the notion of interests refers to things that consumers place importance on. Since the proposed constructs mainly capture consumers’ past and present activities (interests), they may not be effective in predicting consumers’ future default behaviors. Although consumers’ attitudes and beliefs are likely to persist during the loan’s lifetime, their activities or interests may change over time. Consequently, the constructs of activity risk and interest risk, which capture consumers past and present activities (interests), do not have significant associations with their future default behaviors. Another explanation is that previous studies adopted a different interpretation of activities (e.g., sending a message to a friend) or interests $( \mathrm { e . g . , \hbar ^ { \circ } l i k i n g ^ { \ast } } )$ a message) that arise in the virtual world (Cnudde et al., 2019; Djeundje et al., 2021). In contrast, the notions of activities and interests adopted in this study have a broader sense, and they are not restricted to online social media (Lin & Shih, 2012; Sandy et al., 2013). Accordingly, we obtained different empirical results.

In sum, H1a is supported by our econometric analysis. The theories of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991) and lifestyle patterns (Plummer, 1974; Sandy et al., 2013) underpin the development of lifestyle-based risk constructs that capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. Our study reveals a novel discovery in terms of a significant and positive correlation between consumers’ credit risk and their opinion orientation— i.e., one of the lifestyle dimensions measured by using the proposed opinion risk construct.

## 4.2 Lifestyle-Based Risk Constructs Extracted from Images

Models 3 and 4 in Table 7 show the empirical findings regarding the relationship between lifestyle-based risk constructs derived from images and consumers’ credit risk. In particular, Model 4 reveals a significant and positive relationship between consumers’ opinion risk derived from images in multimodal social media posts and their credit risk. Conversely, the constructs of activity risk and interest risk do not show any significant correlation. These results are in line with those obtained from textual social media posts (Models 1 and 2 in Table 7). Essentially, our empirical findings validate previous research that suggests visual data are effective in extracting consumer intelligence to support business decision-making (Hogan, 2010; Shin et al., 2020). Although text analytics have been utilized to develop new constructs from text to examine various business phenomena over the past two decades (Lau et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2018; Nguyen et al., 2020), our research highlights the business value of tapping into a variety of big data, including images, to derive novel constructs that can enhance business decision-making, in general, and consumer credit risk assessment, in particular. Overall, our regression analysis supports H1b. Furthermore, we compared the economic significance of the TOpinion coefficient estimate in Model 2 with that of the IOpinion coefficient estimate in Model 4. We estimated credit risk using Models 2 and 4, setting all independent variables at their mean levels. The results indicate that in Model 2, a one-standard-deviation increase in TOpinion (0.1055) from its mean value is associated with a 0.0491 increase in credit risk (after logarithmic transformation). <sup>1</sup> In Model 4, a one-standard-deviation increase in IOpinion (0.0505) from its mean value is associated with a 0.0177 increase in credit risk. This result suggests that consumers’ opinion risk extracted from social media text has a stronger economic impact on credit risk than that derived from images.

Model 5 in Table 7 combines TOpinion and IOpinion constructs, and the regression results show that both constructs show significant positive associations with CRisk. Essentially, consumers’ opinion orientations derived from social media text or images are linked with their credit risk. Similarly, the economic impact remains significant in Model 5. A one-standard-deviation increase in TOpinion (0.1055) and in IOpinion (0.0505) from their mean values are collectively associated with a 0.0495 increase in credit risk. These findings suggest that using opinion risk constructs extracted from both text and images has a stronger impact on credit risk than relying on only one modality.

We further conducted a Wald test (Avarucci & Velasco, 2009) to compare the explanatory power of lifestylebased risk constructs extracted from text and images, respectively. As shown in Table 7, the Wald statistics in Column $3 ( \chi ^ { 2 } = 3 5 5 . 4 3 , \ p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ and Column 5 $( \chi ^ { 2 } { = } 2 0 5 . 1 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ indicate that the lifestyle-based risk constructs extracted from text collectively achieve a higher model fitness compared to those extracted from images alone. Furthermore, incorporating both text and image-based constructs in Model 5 resulted in the highest model fitness $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 3 9 2 . 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This suggests that combining lifestyle-based risk constructs extracted from both modalities can better explain the variance of consumers’ credit risk than using a single modality alone.

We experimented with four different feature sets: F1 (baseline features: control variables + activity risk and interest risk constructs), F2 (the F1 feature set + the opinion risk construct derived from text), F3 (the F1 feature set + the opinion risk construct derived from images), and F4 (the F1 feature set + opinion risk constructs derived from both text and images). The control variables comprised all the controls used in the previous econometric analysis.

On average, compared to the baseline model (feature set F1), the improvements in RMSE are 30.08% (F2), 17.69% (F3), and 50.83% (F4), respectively. ## 4.4 Additional Analysis on the Varying Impact of Subopinion Risk Constructs

The econometric analysis in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 demonstrates a significant correlation between consumers’ opinion risk and credit risk. Previous studies on LP have suggested that consumers’ opinions can be divided into several subdimensions (Plummer, 1974; Yu, 2011) and that these subdimensions may influence financial behaviors. For example, how individuals perceive themselves has been observed to shape their subsequent actions (Kuhn & McPartland, 1954), and passive, depressive, or risk-taking attitudes about oneself may lead to financial difficulties (Bridges & Disney, 2010; Worthy et al., 2010). Additionally, opinions about politics and social issues can also reflect how individuals perceive societal development, and these opinions may impact their personal finance activities as well (Marron, 2017).

Based on these subdimensions, we compose three subdimension categories: opinions about themselves and the future (TF-Opinion), opinions about business and economics (BE-Opinion), and opinions about politics and social issues (PSI-Opinion). We delve deeper into the specific subdimensions of these categories to examine their relationships with credit risk. In the main econometric model, TOpinion was replaced by TTFOpinion, TBEOpinion, and TPSIOpinion, which represent the opinions on “themselves and the future,” “business and economics,” and “politics and social issues” extracted from textual posts, respectively. Similarly, IOpinion was replaced by ITFOpinion, IBEOpinion, and IPSIOpinion representing image-based content across the same topics. Table 10 summarizes the results of our analysis, with Model 1 examining the relationships between consumers’ credit risk and the subopinion risk constructs derived from textual social media posts, and Model 2 examining the relationships between consumers’ credit risk and the subopinion risk constructs derived from images. Our regression results show that two subopinion risk constructs, namely TTFOpinion and ITFOpinion, are significantly and positively associated with consumers’ credit risk. According to TPB, individuals’ subjective norms and attitudes influence their future behaviors. For example, consumers’ attitudes about hedonism can impact their spending habits, which may eventually lead to default if they overspend. In addition, we found that TPSIOpinion and IPSIOpinion are both significantly and positively associated with consumers’ credit risk. This may be due to the fact that people’s behaviors can be influenced by their political views (Shah et al., 2007). Further, individuals’ political opinions can significantly impact their interactions with others (e.g., their employers) (Roth et al., 2017; Roth et al., 2020) and society stakeholders (e.g., the government). In certain contexts, theses interactions (e.g., political affiliations between employers and employees), could influence their employability and promotion prospects (Wade et al., 2020). Such dynamics may in turn impact consumers’ incomes and credit risk. Thus, our novel findings reveal that consumers’ opinions about themselves and their future, as well as their opinions about politics and social issues, are the primary drivers of their credit risk.

In contrast, consumers’ opinions about business and economics captured in social media text (i.e., TBEOpinion) were found to be only marginally significant, while the subopinion risk construct IBEOpinion is insignificant. A possible explanation is that text is more effective than images for representing some concepts related to business and economics (e.g., a detailed textual description of a country’s business environment). Accordingly, TBEOpinion is marginally significant and IBEOpinion is not significant.

## 5 Robustness Checks

This section presents several robustness checks that verify the main econometric results presented in Section 4. First, we used a two-stage least square instrumental variable approach (2SLS IV) to address the endogeneity concern. Second, we utilized propensity score matching to reduce endogenous selection bias. Third, we adopted an alternative measure of the dependent variable to verify the relationships between consumers’ credit risk and lifestyle-based risk constructs. Fourth, alternative image representation schemes were applied to validate the robustness of our image analysis method. Finally, we controlled for predictive errors that might arise from our deep learning-based multimodal analytics methods. Three additional robustness tests are illustrated in Appendix E.

## 5.1 Two-Stage Least Square Instrumental Variable Approach

One potential concern of our study is the possibility of endogeneity due to unobserved omitted variables that may be correlated with both the independent variable and the error term. To address this issue, we proposed an instrumental variable (IV) for the opinion risk and used the two-stage least square (2SLS) method (Rivers & Vuong, 1988) to estimate our econometric model. Previous research has shown that opinion leaders on social media platforms (e.g., Sina Weibo) can influence and shape the opinions of others (Bamakan et al., 2019). Accordingly, opinion leaders may affect consumers’ opinions, attitudes, beliefs, and thus their future default behavior. Wu et al. (2015) defined a social media user as an opinion leader if they have more than 1,000 followers and the ratio of the total number of followers to the total number of friends is greater than 1. Based on this metric, we developed the instrumental variable OpinionLeader, which takes a value of $^ { \ \mathfrak { s } } 1 ^ { \mathfrak { s } }$ if a consumer follows an opinion leader on Sina Weibo and $\mathbf { \vec { \nabla } } ^ { 6 } 0 ^ { 9 }$ otherwise.

To ensure the validity of the proposed IV, we conducted two post-estimation tests. First, we conducted weak identification to examine the relevance of the IV. The values of F-statistic of the first stage regression are much larger than the critical value (Stock et al., 2002) (Fstatistic = 68.6778\*\*\* for TOpinion; F-statistic = 42.8561\*\*\* for IOpinion), indicating that our IV is not weakly identified. Second, we performed the Durbin-Wu-Hausman test (Davidson & MacKinnon, 1993) to identify the presence of endogeneity. The test failed to reject the null hypothesis ( Durbin $\chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 8 ( p = 0 . 5 0 )$ for TOpinion; Durbin $\chi ^ { 2 } = 2 . 4 1 ( p = 0 . 2 7 )$ for IOpinion), indicating that the OLS and 2SLS IV methods are considered equivalent. ## 5.2 Propensity Score Matching

One potential concern in our analysis is the potential bias caused by active social media users with certain characteristics (e.g., high income) posting more messages on social media platforms. This could result in their opinion orientations (IOpinion and TOpinion) being more prominently displayed online, leading to them being incorrectly labeled as having a higher credit risk. To address this potential endogenous selection bias, we reestimated our models using propensity score matching (PSM) analysis, which is widely used in IS research to tackle similar endogenous selection problems (Aggarwal et al., 2012). Through this approach, we were able to match clients from the treatment and control groups based on observable characteristics such as income and compare their credit risk. This approach helped attenuate the endogenous selection bias by ensuring that clients from both groups have a similar probability of disclosing their opinion orientations.

## 5.3 Alternative Measure of the Dependent Variable

In the main econometric analysis, we utilized the number of days that payments were overdue to gauge the credit risk of the consumer. Previous studies in P2P lending have demonstrated that borrowers who delay their payments beyond a 30-day window are more likely to eventually default on their obligations (Serrano-Cinca et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2020). To test the credibility of our main findings across different measures of credit risk, we redefined the dependent variable, Credit Risk, as a dummy variable. Credit Risk is set to “1” if the overdue period exceeds 30 days, indicating a higher risk profile, and “0” if the overdue period is 30 days or less, signaling a lower risk profile. Using the probit model (Kim et al. 2017), we examined how consumers’ opinion risks are correlated with the likelihood of default. ## 5.4 Alternative Image Representation Schemes

As discussed above, we utilized the MVSO package to develop our image analysis system and identified 4,421 ANPs to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity from multimodal social media posts. To reduce the noise generated during semantic image analysis, we confined our focus to the top 10 ANPs identified by our DCNN for each image’s representation. A concern that may arise is the potential variation in outcomes stemming from different image representation schemes (e.g., 11 ANPs), which could spotlight different image features. Such variations might consequently influence lifestylebased risk calculations, thereby affecting interpretations of credit risk. To address this concern and corroborate the validity of our primary findings rooted in the 10- ANP representation scheme, we embarked on tests employing diverse numbers of ANPs per image representation. We then derived the lifestyle-based risk constructs based on these alternative image representation schemes, as shown in Table 15 above. Our results indicate that the significance of the focal variable (i.e., IOpinion) remained consistent with the main econometric analysis when a range of 6 to 12 ANPs was used to represent an image. However, if more than 12 ANPs were utilized, our regression results differed from the main results because some irrelevant ANPs (i.e., ANPs with relatively low probabilities) were included in the image representation. Overall, our main analysis results are robust in relation to alternative image representation schemes, provided that a reasonable number of ANPs are selected.

## 5.5 Controlling for Predictive Errors

Deep learning is a powerful analytics method, but it can result in predictive errors that may cause measurement errors in regression models (Yang et al., 2018). These errors could lead to biased coefficient estimates, making it harder to detect the true relationship between deep-level diversity and credit risk. To address this issue, we applied the simulation extrapolation method (SIMEX) (Cook & Stefanski, 1994) to correct the errors introduced by predictive analytics methods. Although it is challenging to obtain the actual variances of predictive errors when applying our multimodal analytics methods (i.e., mBERT and MVSO) to unlabeled Weibo posts, we can estimate them based on similar labeled benchmark datasets. Since previous research has shown that state-of-the-art machine learning-based classification methods achieved comparable performance across various domains (Kratzwald et al., 2018), we thus estimated the variance of predictive errors introduced by the text analytics method (i.e., mBERT<sub>BASE</sub>) based on a named entity recognition (NER) Weibo corpus (Peng & Dredze, 2016). The F1-score achieved by the mBERT method for the NER Weibo corpus was 0.88, which is similar to the F1-score (i.e., 0.85) obtained when mBERT was used to detect consumers’ lifestyle patterns in our main study. This empirical test demonstrates that the estimated variance of predictive error can be reliably applied to the SIMEX computational process. Similarly, we estimated the variance of predictive error for the visual analytics method (i.e., MVSO) by applying it to the ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009) benchmark dataset.

By comparing the predicted lifestyle risk labels (e.g., activity, interest, opinion) with the corresponding ground truths provided by domain experts in our Weibo corpus, we obtained a 3×3 covariance matrix of the mBERT model’s predictive error. To correct for any measurement error, we used a two-stage SIMEX correction procedure. ## 6 Concluding Remarks

This study examines the relationship between consumers’ deep-level diversity captured in multimodal social media posts and their credit risk. Drawing on the theories of deep-level diversity and lifestyle patterns, we propose novel lifestyle-based risk constructs to capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. By incorporating these lifestyle-based constructs into econometric models, we empirically evaluated both the explanatory and the predictive capabilities of the proposed constructs. Based on a real-world credit scoring dataset provided by a fintech firm listed on Nasdaq, our empirical findings reveal that the opinion risk constructs extracted from text and images of multimodal social media posts are positively associated with consumers’ credit risk. Further examination of the subdimensions of the opinion risk construct shows that consumers’ opinions on themselves and their future, as well as their opinions on political and social issues, are the primary drivers of their credit risk. Finally, our empirical tests demonstrate that the proposed opinion risk construct derived from images has predictive power comparable to that of its textual counterpart. Combining the opinion risk constructs derived from images and text significantly improves the effectiveness of predicting consumers’ credit risk.

## 6.1 Research Contributions

Our research contributes to the literature in three distinct ways. First, this study enriches the IS literature by developing machine learning-enhanced constructs for conducting large-scale empirical studies (Adamopoulos et al., 2022; Shin et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). Using deep learning-based multimodal analytics, this study introduces three innovative lifestyle-based risk constructs that capture consumers’ deep-level diversity extracted from their multimodal social media posts. Among these constructs, the novelty of the “opinion risk” construct is particularly significant because of its ability to capture the intricate attitudes, beliefs, and worldviews deeply rooted in consumers’ lifestyles. In the IS domain, where decision-making has rapidly embraced digital transformation, the “opinion risk” construct offers a profound, more intricate understanding of human inclinations, choices, and behaviors. This innovative construct provides researchers with a more profound lens to tap into individuals’ deep-level diversity for examining various business phenomena such as, but not limited to, credit scoring, online shopping behavior, targeted marketing, personalized wealth management, corporate hiring, and so on.

Second, we empirically examine the relationship between consumers’ deep-level diversity and credit risk. Existing credit scoring studies have primarily focused on discernible factors such as consumers’ sociodemographic characteristics, financial attributes, loan features, and shallow behavioral characteristics (Emekter et al., 2015; Stein, 2002; Thomas et al., 2005). However, the impact of consumers’ deep-level diversity remains underexplored. While an emergent body of literature has investigated the impact of individuals’ deep-level diversity on financial outcomes (Ali et al., 2022; Maati & Maati-Sauvez, 2019) and organizational outcomes (Harrison et al., 1998), this research is the first to unveil the effect of deep-level diversity in the context of consumers’ credit risk assessment. Beyond the surface-level characteristics, deep-level diversity taps into intricate facets of individual values, attitudes, beliefs, and cognitive abilities. These deep-rooted characteristics play a pivotal role in shaping peoples’ financial behaviors, particularly in the realm of credit scoring. Understanding deep-level diversity indicates that beyond the tangible, creditworthiness may be shaped by deeply ingrained attitudes, values, and beliefs. This unveils a broader, more holistic paradigm to assess a person’s likelihood of repaying debt. By applying TPB, we draw empirical connections between consumers’ opinions—particularly regarding themselves, the future, and social and political issues— and their credit risk. This reveals that deeply held beliefs and attitudes, often dismissed as intangible or too abstract, can have real, measurable impacts on financial outcomes.

Third, the fintech domain has seen a burgeoning interest in harnessing the predictive power of finegrained user behaviors and psychometric features from alternative data sources. Examples include leveraging social capital features from social media activity data to forecast the success of crowdfunding projects (Yeh & Chen, 2022), constructing five behavioral features from P2P lending transaction data to enhance fraud detection (Xu et al., 2022), utilizing personality insights from social media text for credit risk prediction (Yang et al., 2022), and analyzing digital footprints in e-commerce for borrower default prediction (Berg et al., 2020). However, a significant research gap exists in applying consumers’ deep-level diversity to predict their credit risk. Moreover, prior research has predominantly focused on either textual or numerical data. Whether incorporating multimodal data (e.g., text and images) could improve the effectiveness of predicting consumers’ credit risk remains unclear. This research fills the existing gaps by highlighting the advantages of using multimodal data for credit risk prediction. Notably, our findings demonstrate that combining the opinion risk constructs derived from both text and images can significantly improve the predictive effectiveness of consumers’ credit risk. Our novel findings offer new insights into the burgeoning research on the value of multimodal content in credit risk prediction, encouraging researchers to further explore the integration of diverse data modalities in the fintech sector.

## 6.2 Business Implications

Our research has important implications for lending firms, loan managers, and business decision-making makers. First, without the availability of sufficient credit histories and credit scores of relatively young internet-based consumers for assessing their creditworthiness, lending firms can consider using consumers’ multimodal social media data as an alternative data source to enhance credit risk assessment. This aligns with the growing trend of social media-based credit scoring adopted by d-finance firms worldwide. By leveraging low-cost and readily accessible social media big data, loan managers and credit scoring specialists can significantly improve the effectiveness of consumers’ credit risk prediction. Second, although the sharing of visual content such as images has become increasingly popular among internet-based users, loan managers are encountering difficulties in comprehending and interpreting this type of big data. Our research provides them with a practical analytics methodology that can facilitate the “measurement” of these unstructured (textual and visual) data in a systematic and scalable manner. More specifically, loan managers can leverage the proposed opinion risk constructs to significantly enhance the effectiveness of credit risk prediction. Finally, the proposed analytics methodology and lifestyle-based risk constructs can be extended to other business decision-making processes such as consumer profiling, product co-creation, targeted marketing, personalized wealth management, corporate hiring, and so on. Our research has the potential to make a significant societal impact by proposing a more effective creditworthiness assessment methodology, which can reduce the unnecessary exclusion of consumers who are seeking financial assistance. This, in turn, can reduce poverty and boost the global economy.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations that prompt further research. First, the proposed analytics methodology focuses solely on text and image data extracted from social media platforms. Other types of social media data such as audio clips or video clips may also capture consumers’ deep-level diversity. We would like to extend our current methodology to analyze audio and video data by using generative adversarial networks (GANs) (Mao et al., 2019) in the future. Second, our multimodal social media data was collected from one social media platform only. Future work should tap into multiple social media platforms such as Twitter and Facebook to enrich our data collection and analysis process. Third, the credit scoring dataset used in this study was provided by a single fintech firm. Future work will expand our dataset by considering other fintech firms to improve the external validity of our study. Finally, the dataset provided by the fintech firm is cross-sectional, which constrains our capability to scrutinize unobservable factors that may influence our variables of interest. To deepen the rigor and validation of our findings, future research endeavors should prioritize the acquisition and analysis of panel data.

There may be a concern about the invasion of consumer privacy because the proposed methodology extracts consumers’ deep-level diversity from their multimodal social media posts. The research team has taken measures to address this issue. Prior to extracting data, explicit consent was obtained from clients to use their public social media data. Furthermore, clients’ identities were removed from the dataset before it was provided to the research team. To ensure compliance with general research ethics (Babbie 2004) and protect the privacy of subjects, the institutional review board of the funding university carefully reviewed and approved the research team’s proposal. Although our empirical findings show that consumers’ political opinion orientations can impact their creditworthiness, we do not think that consumers with a certain political opinion orientation should be included or excluded from d-finance. Rather, future research should examine how to ensure that consumers’ political opinion orientations do not influence their access to d-finance.

## Appendix A: Examples of Lifestyle Subdimensions and the Expanded Topics

To extract lifestyle patterns from consumers’ textual social media posts for credit risk assessment, the research team and the collaborating d-finance firm collaborated to identify the relevant lifestyle subdimensions with respect to activity, interest, and opinion. Using the standard instrument of lifestyle patterns (Plummer, 1971; Wells & Tigert, 1971; Vyncke, 2002), keywords were extracted from the relevant items of chosen lifestyle subdimensions. These keywords were then fed into a semi-supervised HDP topic model (Lau et al., 2018) to enrich the subdimension descriptions for credit risk assessment. Next, probabilistic language models (Lau et al., 2011) were built using these enriched descriptions (topics) to semi-automatically label Weibo posts based on the three lifestyle dimensions. These labeled Weibo posts were then used to fine-tune the mBERT transformer model to identify lifestyle-related risks in microloan clients’ social media posts. ## Appendix B: Computational Details of the Attention-based DCNN Image Analyzer

Krizhevsky et al. (2012) developed a deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) named AlexNet. This network exhibits outstanding image analysis performance in the ImageNet LSVRC-2010 benchmark competition. Figure B1 highlights the architecture of the AlexNet-based DCNN. We utilized the pretrained AlexNet used by the open-source Multilingual Visual Sentiment Ontology (MVSO) system which classifies input images with respect to 4,421 semantic labels, also called adjective-noun pairs (ANPs) such as “Grand Casino” (Jou et al. 2015). To classify input images effectively according to various lifestyle dimensions and subdimensions, we extended the AlexNet-based DCNN with an attention model that assigns stronger weights to high-level features relevant to specific lifestyle dimensions. Figure B2 illustrates our attention model, which accepts an input feature map of a size N\*N\*C, where N\*N represents a two-dimensional (2D) feature map, and C denotes the number of channels. As shown in Figure B2, the attention model first convolves an input feature map of size N\*N\*C to a size of N\*N\*16 using two consecutive convolutional layers.

Then, an attention mechanism is utilized through a locally connected 2D layer with a sigmoid activation function to learn suitable attention weights. Following this, a convolutional layer with a linear activation function is added to duplicate the learned weights across the channel dimension c times. The resulting feature map is then transformed into a single feature vector of length c by averaging the weights. This approach ensures that the attention model operates like a weighted averaging operation over the input feature map. Since the upper convolutional layers of the DCNN represent relatively large receptive fields with many potential overlapping regions, an attention model applied to these layers may not be very effective due to the overlapping features. To overcome this issue, we applied the attention model to both low-level convolutional layers and high-level convolutional layers. ## Appendix C: The Validity Test of the Proposed Lifestyle-based Risk Constructs

To evaluate the validity of the proposed lifestyle-based risk constructs, we performed a series of empirical experiments. The validity tests consist of two parts. First, we randomly invited 347 subjects who were frequent Weibo users to participate in a user study. These participants were given a briefing session of one hour, during which they were introduced to the concept of lifestyle patterns and the notions of AIOs, as illustrated in Table 3 of the main paper. They were provided with several examples for each lifestyle dimension and subdimension. Subsequently, the participants were asked to complete a questionnaire that was developed based on the classical instrument for measuring individuals AIO dimensions (Wells & Tigert, 1971). As a result, we were able to obtain the participants’ lifestyle patterns in terms of the corresponding AIO scores, which served as the ground truth. At the end of this stage, a total of 319 valid responses and their corresponding AIO scores were obtained.

For the second part of the validity test, we selected 319 subjects who provided valid responses. Each of these participants was asked to write three short articles, with a minimum of 100 words in each article, describing their activities, interests, and opinions. Additionally, they were required to submit 21 photos (with seven photos for each dimension), which best represented their AIO lifestyle dimensions. Then, we applied the mBERT model to identify subjects’ lifestyle patterns from the respective short articles (i.e., the AIO textual features). Moreover, we utilized the attention-based DCNN to extract subjects’ lifestyle patterns from the respective AIO subsets of photos (i.e., the AIO visual features). These extracted textual and visual features were then fed into the corresponding lifestyle-based risk constructs to calculate the respective AIO scores. Accordingly, we obtained a set of system-derived AIO scores for subjects. Table C1 presents the basic descriptive statistics of subjects’ AIO scores obtained from the classical AIO instrument (i.e., the ground truth) and those derived from our proposed opinion risk constructs.
