# AN INVESTIGATION OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS USE PATTERNS: TECHNOLOGICAL EVENTS AS TRIGGERS, THE EFFECT OF TIME, AND CONSEQUENCES FOR PERFORMANCE<sup>1</sup>

Ana Ortiz de Guinea HEC Montréal, 3000 chemin de la Côte Sainte-Catherine, Montréal, Québec H3T 2A7 CANADA {ana.ortiz-de-guinea@hec.ca}

Jane Webster

Queen’s School of Business, Queen’s University, 143 Union Street, Kingston, Ontario K7L 3N6 CANADA {jwebster@business.queensu.ca}

Information systems use represents one of the core concepts defining the discipline. In this article, we develop a rich conceptualization of IS use patterns as individuals’ emotions, cognition, and behaviors while employing an information technology to accomplish a work-related task. By combining two novel perspectives—the affect–object paradigm and automaticity—with coping theory, we theorize how different patterns appear and disappear as a result of different IT events—expected and discrepant—as well as over time, and how these patterns influence short-term performance. In order to test our hypotheses, we conducted two studies, one qualitative and the other quantitative, that combined different methods (e.g., open-ended questions, physiological data, videos, protocol analysis) to study the influence of expected and discrepant events. The synergistic properties of the two studies demonstrate the existence of two IS use patterns, automatic and adjusting. Most interactions are automatic, and adjusting patterns, triggered by discrepant IT events, fade over time and transition into automatic ones. Further, automatic patterns result in enhanced short-term performance, while adjusting ones do not. Our conceptualization of IS use patterns is useful because it addresses important questions (such as why negative IT perceptions persist) and clarifies that it is how (rather than how much) people use IT that is pertinent for performance.

Keywords: Emotion, affect, behavior, cognition, performance, pattern, IS use, usage, heart rate, EKG, physiology, physiological arousal, automaticity, continuance, technological effects

## Introduction

More than 90 percent of costs related to information systems are attributable to the post-implementation stage (Erlikh 2000). This represents the longest stage in the IS life cycle, the stage during which the system is actually being used by employees (Jasperson et al. 2005). As a result, the ways in which individuals use IS have been identified as critical for linking organizational investments with their corresponding benefits (Barua and Mukhopadhyay 2000; Jasperson et al. 2005). Reflecting this, individuals’ IS use has represented a core construct for the IS discipline since its foundation over 40 years ago. However, we still know little about how information systems are actually employed to accomplish workrelated tasks (Barki et al. 2007). That is, are there patterns of IS use that change over time and do these patterns affect performance?

In contrast to a focus on actual IS use, research in the field has often conceptualized and studied use in lean ways (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006): generally as a decision to (or continue to) use (Wixom and Todd 2005) or as an amount of use (frequency, duration, or breadth of features utilized: Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). Four main limitations result. First, these conceptualizations do not reflect actual patterns of use that occur naturally nor can they explain how individuals modify and adjust their use of a given IT over time (Benbasat and Barki 2007; Schwarz and Chin 2007). Second, studies often ignore the ontological dimension of technology: that is, the technology itself is absent from research models and replaced by mental representations of it (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009; Silva 2007). Third, most research has asked users to reflect on use removed from the use experience itself. Thus, this research cannot capture automatic use states or patterns that occur outside individuals’ awareness (Dimoka et al. 2011; Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009). Finally, investigations of the relation between these conceptualizations of use and performance outcomes have yielded contradictory and inconclusive results (e.g., a positive effect: Devaraj and Kohli 2003; a negative effect: Lucas and Spitler 1999; and a statistically insignificant effect: Pentland 1989).

In order to address these shortcomings, we build upon recent theorizing on the IS use construct (Barki et al. 2007; Burton-Jones and Straub 2006) to conceptualize IS use patterns as a configurational construct encompassing an individual’s emotions, cognitions, and behaviors while employing an IT to accomplish a work-related task. These patterns are configurational in the sense that the different values of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors give rise to distinct and specific types of patterns. With IS use patterns as our main theoretical core, we investigate the following research questions:

1. How do IT events (e.g., difficulties with an IT versus absence of difficulties) trigger different IS use patterns?

2. How do users transition from one IS use pattern to another over time?

3. How do IS use patterns influence short-term performance?

To examine these research questions, we bring together different theoretical perspectives: coping theory, the affect– object paradigm, and the automaticity perspective. These theoretical perspectives help to explain why and how the different components of IS use patterns (emotions, cognitions, and behaviors) change as a result of different IT events. In order to test our hypotheses, we conducted two studies, an experience sampling study and an experiment, allowing for the capture of IS use patterns as they naturally occur. Our results demonstrate the existence of two IS use patterns that are triggered by IT events and that relate differentially to short-term performance.

This paper contributes to the literature in several ways. Our conceptualization of IS use patterns describes how IT is actually used by individuals in organizations, and why some patterns (and not others) lead to short-term performance. In doing so, our research also tests previous suggestions concerning the benefits of automatic use (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009), while providing explanations for why some users hold overall negative perceptions of IT, even when most of their IT experiences have not been negative.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we describe the concept of IS use pattern. Second, drawing on the three theoretical perspectives, we develop a set of hypotheses explaining how IT events trigger different IS use patterns, how time affects the transition between patterns, and how the patterns influence short-term task performance. Next, we present our two studies. We conclude the paper with implications for research and practice.

## Theoretical Development

## Defining IS Use Patterns

Rich conceptualizations of IS usage, or a “user’s employment of a system to perform a task,” have been assessed as “a behavior (what a user does), cognition (what a user thinks), and/or an affect (what a user feels)” (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, p. 659). Here, we focus on the pattern of these three constructs, IS use patterns or the configuration of an individual’s emotions, cognitions, and behaviors when interacting with IT to accomplish a task. By configuration, we mean the “constellation of conceptually distinct characteristics that commonly occur together,” in which elements are treated together in combination, allowing for a parsimonious conceptualization of a complex phenomenon (Meyer et al. 1993, p. 1174).<sup>2</sup> These constructs help us understand how patterns arise and disappear as a result of events, allowing us to look at phenomena from a holistic approach where elements are viewed together instead of separately (Meyer et al. 1993). Consequently, an IS use pattern is a configuration or profile of distinct values, one for each of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors.<sup>3</sup>

Emotion, or core affect, is a “neurophysiological state consciously accessible as a simple, non reflective feeling that is an integral blend of hedonic (pleasure–displeasure) and arousal (sleepy–activated)” (Russell 2003, p. 147). Thus, emotion has two continuous and orthogonal dimensions: affect and physiological arousal. Affect, or “what a user feels” (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, p. 659), falls along the pleasure– displeasure dimension, whereas physiological arousal falls along the arousal–quietness dimension (Russell 2003). As a result, emotion as studied here is fundamentally different from other emotion-related IS constructs traditionally used in the IS literature. Some of these other constructs describe relatively stable emotional states formed as a result of evaluative judgments about prior IT experience (e.g., satisfaction, attitudes, Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009). Satisfaction can indicate an evaluative judgment about the IT being used and, as such, needs cognitive input (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004). However, our definition of emotion does not necessarily imply a cognitive evaluative component (Zajonc 1980): it can occur within a fraction of a second without the individual’s awareness or control (Bargh et al. 1996).

Cognitions, or “what a user thinks” (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, p. 659), involve “thinking or conscious experience” (Weitten 2001, p. 13). By conscious experience, we mean one’s recognition of thought states in such a manner that one can report on them to others (Baars and Franklin 2003). Accordingly, cognitions refer to anything occupying an individual’s mind that s/he is aware of and can verbalize (Weitten 2001). In the case of IT interactions, there can be three objects of a person’s thoughts: the computer system (such as thinking about how to format a page), the task for which s/he is using the technology (e.g., writing a report), or something else (such as mind-wandering thoughts, personal issues, daydreaming, etc.) (Smallwood 2006). We refer to the former as computer-related and the latter two as non-computer-related thoughts.

Behaviors, or “what a user does” (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, p. 659), are the actions in which individuals engage in relation to their environments (Sonnenfeld 1972). They include observable movements and motor activities, such as typing and moving the mouse. They may include behaviors directly associated with the normal use of the system, called exploitive behaviors; that is, exploitive behaviors refer to interacting with an IT in a straightforward manner to accomplish a task (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006), such as typing when writing an essay. Or, they may include behaviors directly associated with modifying an aspect of the technology, called adaptive behaviors, or the “deliberative modification-oriented activities” aimed at altering an aspect of the IT (Barki et al. 2007, p. 178), such as changing the configuration of a program.

An IS use pattern is a group of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors that occur together, and whose values change together in response to events in the user’s environment. That is, for an IS use pattern to exist, there needs to be a distinct set of values for each of the constructs: emotions (affect and physiological arousal), cognitions (computer-related thoughts and non-computer-related thoughts), and behaviors (exploitive behaviors and adaptive behaviors). These three constructs may or may not relate among themselves: “variables found to be causally related in one configuration may be unrelated or even inversely related in another” (Meyer et al. 1993, p. 1178). This means that regardless of whether each attribute is correlated with the others, if they exist together at a given point in time and they change together over time, they give rise to different types of IS use patterns.

The concept of individuals’ IS use patterns represents a rich and descriptive view of IS use (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006) and is supported by activity theory. Activity theory focuses more on the activities involved during IS interactions than on the mere quantity of IS use (Barki et al. 2007). This theory proposes that we can only analyze what goes on in people’s minds (emotionally and cognitively) in the context of activities, that is, while they act (behaviors) (Kaptelinin 1996). Therefore, we need to look at these constituents of IS use in a holistic way (Meyer et al. 1993) and as they naturally occur.

Activity theory suggests that activities are under continuous change and development: “the constituents of activity are not fixed, but can dynamically change as conditions change” (Nardi 1998, p. 35). This means that IS use patterns vary over time, as the three constructs change values as a result of the changing environment in which the IS interaction takes place. In this research, we will identify two IS use patterns that result from different IT events: (1) an automatic IS use pattern and (2) an adjusting IS use pattern. Each of these patterns, as will be explained, has a distinct configuration of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors.

## Theorizing about IS Use Patterns

We posit that distinct IS use patterns (made up of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors) appear and disappear as different IT events occur while an individual works on a task. These patterns are important, because they have consequences for performance.

When considering IT events, both expected and unexpected events can occur.<sup>4</sup> Expected IT events represent ordinary events characterized by a match between the expectations of the user and the performance of the IT (Tyre and Orlikowski 1994). Unexpected events can be either negative or positive. Discrepant IT events are unexpected negative events that occur when there is a difference between what is expected and what is taking place (Louis and Sutton 1991) and they entail a problem, a misunderstanding, or a difficulty with the IT being employed (Tyre and Orlikowski 1994). Such discrepant events can be the result of an inadvertent slippage or a technological breakdown on the part of the technology and represent both a threat and an opportunity for the user: a threat because they jeopardize the completion of the task for which the technology is being used and an opportunity because they might provide a chance for learning (Tyre and Orlikowski 1994). Discovery IT events are unexpected positive events characterized by the discovery of new functionality of the technology (Tyre and Orlikowski 1994); for example, users might improvise and discover new functionality that could help them further their attainment of task objectives. It is important to note that events are not static, meaning that events evolve and are dynamic as the situation unfolds (Peterson 1998). For example, a technological breakdown can start as a discrepant IT event and as the user adapts or tries to find a solution, s/he might learn something useful about the technology that s/he did not know before, thus transforming the event from a discrepant one to a discovery one. Furthermore, discrepant IT events do not need to be fatal; that is, discrepant IT events can be nuisances that do not obstruct the completion of the task. This paper focuses only on expected and discrepant technological events. The reason for this focus is that these types of events occur most frequently (Lazar et al. 2006) and, as will be shown in the first study, only a few discovery events were reported.

In this framework, we suggest that the behavioral components of IS use patterns can have consequences for task performance. When defining task performance, one has to differentiate between task performance as behaviors or outcomes (Campbell 1990; Sonnentag and Frese 2002). Behaviors deal more with the actions carried out to accomplish something rather than with the results of such actions (Campbell 1990; Sonnentag and Frese 2002) and are already captured with our behavioral component of IS use patterns. Therefore, we focus on task performance as an outcome orientation, that is, the resulting product in terms of effectiveness (Campbell 1990; Sonnentag and Frese 2002). In other words, performance is the degree to which the results of the task meet task goals (Campbell 1990). This definition emphasizes the short-term nature of performance and is consistent with past literature (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). Consequently, we focus on short-term performance, the performance on an immediate task, rather than on long-term performance, or performance during a longer work period (e.g., day, week, etc.).

We now turn to theoretical explanations for how different types of IS use patterns appear and disappear as a result of IT events, and how these patterns influence short-term task performance (see Table 1 for a summary of construct definitions). To do so, we draw on coping theory, the affect– object paradigm, and automaticity to develop our hypotheses. In contrast to past theories employed to explain use as a decision or an amount, these theoretical perspectives are well-suited to the study of use patterns because of the primary role they give to changes in environmental objects (here, IT events) and their effects on emotions, cognitions, and behaviors (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009). Thus, they are capable of identifying different IS use patterns that result from IT events as well as transitions between patterns over time.

## Coping Theory

Coping represents individuals’ emotional, cognitive, and behavioral efforts to adapt and deal with specific demands that generate stress (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Coping entails a dynamic interaction between a person and an event, focusing attention on how circumstances and behaviors change as the situation develops (Carver and Scheier 1994). As with IT events, coping theory posits three types of encounters that can take place: events that are benign with no harmful consequences, events that have the potential to harm, and events that have the potential for good for the individual (Lazarus and Folkman 1984).

The coping process begins when an event is appraised. Two types of appraisal occur concurrently, primary and secondary (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). In primary appraisal, an event is viewed as irrelevant (if perceived as carrying no implications for a person’s well-being), stressful (if seen as threatening or damaging to well-being), or positive (if construed as positive, enhancing well-being). During secondary appraisal, individuals evaluate their resources for dealing with the situation. These resources “refer not to what people do, but to what is available to them in developing their coping repertories” (Parlin and Chooler 1978, p. 5). These appraisals are at the heart of individuals’ emotional experiences and prepare them for immediate action (Schneider 2008). Thus, secondary appraisal represents a call for the mobilization of coping mechanisms (Lazarus and Folkman 1984).

There are a range of coping mechanisms that have been identified, often categorized as engagement or disengagement strategies<sup>5</sup> (see Skinner et al. 2003). Engagement strategies are cognitive and behavioral mechanisms directed at the modification of the stressful situation through the elimination or alteration of the source of stress (Carver et al. 1989). In contrast, disengagement strategies are behavioral and cognitive efforts that are not aimed at overcoming the stressful situation; rather, they are concerned with some other activity that provides distraction from the stressor (Gutiérrez et al. 2007).

Although coping theory provides an important overall view of the consequences of discrepant IT events, it cannot explain what happens to the components of IS use patterns when the IT behaves according to plan, that is, during expected events. The affect–object paradigm and automaticity help in explaining the specific IS use pattern as a result of expected events, as well as providing further complementary explanations for the effects of discrepant IT events.

## The Affect–Object Paradigm

The affect–object paradigm emphasizes the role of emotions, or core affect, in human behavior (Mohr 1996). According to this paradigm, humans hold representations of environmental objects (e.g., IT artifacts) in their brains that are attached to “affective tags” (core affect associated with the object/event) as a result of either learning, experience, or a genetic code (Mohr 1996). Events in the environment (e.g., an IT glitch), however, result in a change to the current core affect state attached to an object. For example, an IT error is likely to increase both the intensity of the affect attached to the IT artifact, making it more negative, and physiological arousal. As the individual encounters events and objects “associated with affects of varying strengths…[there is] one object [that] emerges as dominant. That is how choice among possible actions is made” (Mohr 1996, p. 79).<sup>6</sup> Thus, if an object elicits neutral or positive affect, it does not motivate behavioral changes with respect to that object because it does not create discomfort or stress; if an object elicits negative affect, it does motivate a change in behavior. According to this perspective, elicited core affect, rather than a well thought-out plan of action, is the primary driver of behavioral responses (Mohr 1996).

## The Automaticity Perspective

This perspective proposes that interactions with objects in the environment are often automatic (Bargh and Fergusson 2000; Louis and Sutton 1991). Therefore, when we interact with IT, our behaviors and thoughts are often focused on the task (or something else) rather than on the computer itself. According to this perspective, everyday living depends on direct perception, perception that automatically guides actions and ongoing thoughts: that is, interpretations of the events in one’s environment often occur in a timely and automatic manner without the need for active thought (Fazio et al. 1986; Glaser and Banaji 1999). It is important to note, however, that this perspective does not suggest that individuals have no thoughts or ideas in their heads (Young 2004). In contrast, it argues that thoughts directed at specific objects in the environment are minimal unless a disruption takes place (Louis and Sutton 1991).

## Development of Hypotheses

As demonstrated next, we use process theorizing to explain the dynamics of how IT events trigger different IS use patterns over time. Process theorizing is better suited to explain how events cause changes in IS use patterns (Abbott 1990; Markus and Robey 1988). By theorizing about the effects of IT events, we can distinguish specific configurations of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors that will give rise to particular types of IS use patterns.

## How IT Events Trigger Different IS Use Patterns

Our theoretical perspectives help to explain the different influences of IT events on IS use patterns. As described earlier, coping theory explains the effects of discrepant events, while the affect–object and automaticity perspectives complement it by providing explanations during both discrepant and expected events.

Expected IT Events and the Automatic IS Use Pattern. When employing software on a regular basis under normal conditions (expected IT events), we propose that users will engage in an automatic IS use pattern composed of emotions unrelated to the system, non-computer related thoughts, and exploitive behaviors.

For expected IT events, the affect–object paradigm would suggest that the emotions being experienced would be unrelated to the event: they might be negative, neutral, or positive due to other issues in the users’ work or personal lives. Because affect is unrelated to the computer system in this case, users would not have the motivation to change their behaviors with respect to the IT (Mohr 1996). This, coupled with the fact that most IS use is instrumental (Barki et al. 2007), would suggest that IT behaviors will be exploitive during expected IT events, that is, oriented toward carrying out the task for which the IT is being employed.

This conclusion about exploitive behaviors is also supported by automaticity, which suggests that users’ thoughts would be non-computer-related during expected IT events. Specifically, as the use of IT becomes habitual (Limayem et al. 2007), dealing with the IT becomes routinized and automatic (Nardi 1996; Verplanken et al. 1998). Under normal circumstances during interactions with a familiar IT, one no longer needs to think about what to do next, how to do it, or when to do it (Waller et al. 2006). That is, when technology behaves “according to plan,” it requires no active thought. Typically, much of our interactions with technology are recurrent and become regularized and routinized, and, as such, become taken-for-granted (Orlikowski 2000). Both Nardi (1996) and Orlikowski (2000) explain the inconspicuousness of technology with the example of driving a car: when learning to drive a car, shifting gears requires active thought; however, with practice, it occurs automatically, and ceases to exist in one’s conscious awareness. Thus, expected IT events represent benign stable conditions and require no active thought or adaptational responses. Thus, two consequences of expected IT events are that (1) users are free to direct their thoughts to other things, either the accomplishment of their task or something else if the task itself is also automatic (e.g., to personal issues) (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009; Smallwood 2006; Smallwood et al. 2003), and (2) behaviors are exploitive (directed at accomplishing the task rather than changing or adapting the IT). In summary, automacity and the affect– object paradigms suggest that expected IT events trigger an automatic IS use pattern:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): During expected IT events, individuals engage in an automatic IS use pattern characterized by (a) emotions unrelated to the event, (b) non-computer-related thoughts, and (c) exploitive behaviors.

Discrepant IT Events and the Adjusting IS Use Pattern. We propose that discrepant (unexpected negative) IT events will trigger an adjusting IS use pattern composed of negative emotions, computer-related thoughts, and adaptive behaviors. According to coping theory, discrepant, as opposed to expected, events elicit negative emotions (negative affect and high physiological arousal) for several reasons. First, because they break the flow of actions directed at completing a task, discrepant events represent a threat to the individual. Furthermore, because they are unpredictable, they cause more negative emotions than predictable events (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). But most importantly, discrepant events are often ambiguous: when an event occurs, the user may not know what s/he needs to do to overcome the situation nor exactly what is going on or why the event is taking place. Ambiguous events are likely to cause negative affect and physiological reactions (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). As a result, discrepant (as compared to expected) IT events will trigger more negative emotions.

The affect–object paradigm further supports this assertion by suggesting that events are classified as positive or negative within fractions of a second following their encounter (Fazio et al. 1986; Glaser and Banaji 1999). This perspective argues that our bodies are programmed to experience affect and physiological arousal when sensing the environment, attending to those environmental cues that matter to us at the moment (Aggleton and Mishkin 1986). As a result, physiological and affective responses are set into motion immediately (Zajonc 1998), triggering fast and automatic responses (Adolphs and Damasio 2001; Elfenbein 2007). Furthermore, these responses are represented in the brain as changes in body and brain states, something that results in the conscious experience of affect (Adolphs and Damasio 2001; Elfenbein 2007).

Turning from emotions to cognitions, coping theory would suggest that when individuals perceive something in the environment as threatening, they engage in different coping strategies (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Engaging in one or another strategy is decided upon the perception of the situation as being changeable or not. If a situation is perceived as changeable, then engagement coping will take place; if it is perceived as unchangeable, disengagement coping will occur (Carver et al. 1989; Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Because discrepant events are generally ambiguous and it is hard to know a priori whether the situation is changeable, they are likely to trigger engagement coping (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Engagement coping has behavioral (explained below) and cognitive components. The cognitive component includes redirecting thoughts away from other activities (such as tasks) to the discrepant event (Carver et al. 1989), thus triggering computer-related thoughts.

Research on automaticity also supports this conclusion: when there are unexpected failures or disruptions, individuals are “motivated” to direct their thoughts and actions to them (Louis and Sutton 1991). When the situation changes, such as an unexpected IT event, thoughts are directed to the IT (Kaptelinin 1996). That is, the existence of a discrepant event makes the IT, which previously appeared to be invisible, conspicuous, thus, triggering computer-related thoughts.

Turning to behaviors, coping theory suggests that discrepant IT events trigger engagement coping as explained above. Behaviorally, engagement coping is characterized by active coping and the suppression of competing activities. Active coping encompasses taking action aimed at overcoming the situation or decreasing its effects (Carver and Scheier 1982). It includes behaviors like the execution of specific adaptive efforts and the suppression of competing activities. Again, this means that individuals will direct their behaviors away from other activities (such as their tasks) to the discrepant IT event. Thus, discrepant IT events are likely to trigger adaptive behaviors.

Further support for this assertion comes from the affect–object perspective. As explained earlier, when there is a discrepant IT event, this paradigm would suggest that the environmental object with the greatest affective intensity for the individual would be the computer system. Thus, “instead of maintaining the individual in the present behavior pattern relative to the object, the signals are such as to bring change” (Mohr 1996, p. 74), making the user stop their exploitive behaviors and engage in adaptive ones aimed at overcoming the event. Thus, behaviors are directed at the stimulus that triggered the emotional response, in an effort to restore the disrupted environmental equilibrium (Adolphs and Damasio 2001).

The automaticity paradigm also supports this conclusion. It would suggest that during discrepant events, users not only direct their thoughts to the IT, but direct their behaviors to dealing with the situation. Thus, when there are technological breakdowns or inadvertent slippages, users fall into a different form of activity, characterized by an active focus on behaviors to modify an aspect of the IT in order to overcome the discrepant event (Tyre and Orlikowski 1994). Thus, the three theoretical perspectives suggest that discrepant IT events trigger an adjusting IS use pattern:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Discrepant IT events trigger an adjusting IS use pattern characterized by (a) negative emotions (negative affect and high physiological arousal) related to the system, (b) computer-related thoughts, and (c) adaptive behaviors.

Transition from Adjusting to Automatic Use Patterns over Time. Although discrepant IT events can immediately trigger an adjusting IS use pattern, coping theory would suggest that such effects are likely to dilute over time. That is, as the event continues over time, the adjusting use pattern is likely to fade away. For instance, when adaptive responses are unsuccessful, users start perceiving the situation as unchangeable (Folkman and Lazarus 1985), resulting in disengagement coping (Carver et al. 1989). Disengagement mechanisms encompass both behavioral and mental disengagement from the discrepant event. Behavioral disengagement involves the withdrawal or reduction of behavioral efforts aimed at dealing with the event (Gutiérrez et al. 2007)—in our case, the withdrawal of adaptive behaviors. This means that users may turn back to the same task they were working on before the discrepant event occurred or, if the event prevents them from doing so, they may engage in unrelated tasks. Mental disengagement is also part of the disengagement coping strategy and involves changing the focus of thoughts from the event to something else (Gutiérrez et al. 2007) in order to provide distraction (Carver et al. 1989), thus decreasing computerrelated thoughts and increasing non-computer-related ones. Therefore, as time passes by since the discrepant IT event took place, negative affect, computer-related thoughts, and adaptive behaviors are likely to decrease, thus making the adjusting use pattern disappear, replacing it with an automatic one.

The same process is likely to occur when a discrepant IT event is resolved. In this case, the discrepant event will no longer cause negative emotions: emotions are likely to be focused on something other than the IT, and there will be no need for cognitive and behavioral coping (computer-related thoughts and adaptive behaviors). Individuals are likely to resume their original tasks, triggering non-computer-related thoughts and exploitive behaviors, thus giving rise to an automatic use pattern. As a result,

Hypothesis 3 (H3): As time passes since a discrepant IT event occurred, the adjusting use pattern disappears and users return to the automatic use pattern.

## The Effects of IS Use Patterns on Short-Time Performance

The final hypothesis concerns the relation between IS use patterns and short-term performance. IS use patterns relate to performance through their behavioral component because behaviors are necessary steps for a task to be accomplished: different behaviors might result in better or worse performance (Campbell 1990; Sonnentag and Frese 2002). The behavioral component of IS use patterns allows work to be accomplished, and we propose that the different behaviors (exploitive or adaptive) affect the resulting product of that work differently.

We suggest that exploitive, but not adaptive, behaviors will relate to short-term task performance. As described above, exploitive behaviors are part of the automatic IS use pattern. In the absence of discrepant events, the automaticity perspective would argue that automatic processing is not only adequate but superior (Louis and Sutton 1991). Such automatic interactions with IT can have important consequences for short-term performance because they allow individuals to direct their behavioral efforts toward the activities that matter, in this case the task (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009). Exploitive behaviors are more likely to result in short-term performance because they have resulted in such benefits in the past and, as such, have immediate beneficial and certain outcomes (March 1991). In support of this, Burton-Jones and Straub (2006) demonstrated a positive relation between exploitive IS use and short-term task performance.

The relation between the adjusting IS use pattern, though its adaptive behavioral component, with short-term performance, however, is not clear. Although the literature demonstrates that adaptive use relates positively to long-term performance, it is less clear about short-term performance. For longer-term performance, Beaudry and Pinsonneault (2005) drew on coping theory to argue that adaptive behaviors should lead to higher individual performance because individuals’ adaptation efforts take advantage of the opportunities offered by the discrepant IT event. Similarly, the automaticity perspective proposes that adaptive behaviors would be superior to automatic ones under discrepant conditions: adaptive behaviors allow individuals to deal with the situation rather than overlooking it, which can lead to learning that would be useful for subsequent discrepant events (Louis and Sutton 1991). Further, adaptive behaviors could lead to higher benefits because they help to achieve a better fit between the system and the context for which the IT is being used (Barki et al. 2007).

In contrast, it is difficult to argue that adaptive behaviors would have a positive relation with short-term or immediate task performance. For example, Tyre and Orlikowski (1994) show how adaptive behaviors are likely to take place when demands on performance are relaxed, the assumption being that such behaviors might lower immediate performance. The literature on organizational learning also demonstrates that adaptive and reinvention behaviors can result in lower shortterm performance; this is because the outputs of such behaviors are more uncertain and time is needed to translate behavioral efforts into performance outcomes (March 1991). As a result, we do not theorize a relation between the adaptive behaviors of the adjusting pattern and performance (but we test for it), and only propose a positive relation between the exploitive behavioral component of the automatic pattern and short-term task performance. That is, we propose that

Hypothesis 4 (H4): Automatic IS use patterns, through their exploitive behavioral component, result in higher short-term task performance.

The four hypotheses are summarized in Figure 1. This diagram illustrates on the horizontal axis how hypothetical events may occur over time, with their resulting IS use patterns composed of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors. Starting with expected IT events, the figure demonstrates an automatic IS use pattern (Hypothesis 1). Next, a discrepant IT event triggers an adjusting IS use pattern, illustrating changes in values for emotions, cognitions, and behaviors (Hypothesis 2). As time passes since the discrepant IT event, its effects fade away and the automatic IS use pattern appears again (Hypothesis 3). As illustrated in the figure, expected events are not discrete like discrepant IT events, as they may persist over time. Finally, automatic IS use patterns, through their exploitive behaviors, relate positively to short-term performance (Hypothesis 4).

## Study 1: Experience Sampling Study

In order to conduct a preliminary test of some of the hypotheses in a real work environment, a first study was designed using the experience sampling method (ESM). In this ESM study, we asked participants to carry a booklet and pager during a specified period of time (two weeks). The pager randomly sounded at different times and thus participants were not required to recall anything: instead, they reported on their current activities, events, feelings, and thoughts (Csiksentmihalyi and Hunter 2003). This made it possible to know users’ cognitions and emotions during their work activities (Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre 1989).

This method is appropriate for a number of reasons. By employing open-ended questions to ask participants about their emotions and cognitions as they occurred, we avoided priming and consistency artifacts (Salancik 1984). Furthermore, since emotions and cognitions are difficult to study observationally, this method has been recommended in organizational research: “When self-reported data is necessary, collection in real time via experience sampling is preferable to retrospective reports” (Elfenbein 2007, p. 369).

For this study, we were able to capture all of the pattern components except for physiological arousal and behaviors (this type of study does not provide access to such measures without disturbing the work setting). Thus, we examined hypotheses H1–H3 (see Table 2).

## Procedure

A total of 58 employees from 13 different organizations participated in the study. For two weeks, participants carried a pager and a booklet containing questions. The pager went off one to three times per work day. Participants were asked to complete one entry (two pages of open-ended questions) in the booklet every time they were paged (see Table 1). The open-ended questions allowed for the gathering of IT events, affect, and cognitions. Before conducting this study, two pilot studies were conducted with graduate students and university staff in order to test the data gathering procedure and improve the booklet design.

## Analytic Methods

We analyzed the data following Markus’ (1983) example. That is, reported events that naturally occurred in the research setting (e.g., discrepant IT events) were used as the controls necessary for examining the theory specified under the hypotheses (Dubé and Paré 2003). The main unit of analysis was each interaction of a participant with the computer, and interactions were analyzed first within and then across individuals (Eisenhardt 1989). A list of main themes and subthemes was developed before the data were analyzed based on the proposed theories and on an analysis of the data from the pilot studies (Miles and Huberman 1994). Subthemes were added to the existing list as they were detected. One researcher coded all of the data and a second independent person coded a subset; inter-rater reliability was .83.

Out of 769 recorded interactions, there were 570 cases for which participants were using a computer, classified as 512 expected and 50 discrepant events. In addition, eight cases were discarded: five that could not be classified and three discovery (positive unexpected) events.

## Results

We report the results based on expected and discrepant IT events. Specifically, we discovered two types of discrepant IT events: short-term (39), which helps us test the immediate effects of discrepant events (H2); and long-term (11), which helps us test time effects (H3).

For expected IT events, results support an automatic IS use pattern, that is, emotions unrelated to the event and noncomputer-related thoughts (H1). Specifically, participants reported affect unrelated to the computer application being used (99.61%). Some of these emotions were related to the task being accomplished at the time (Participant 56: “I was feeling happy that we are making progress”) while others were related to something else (Participant 5: “Sadness at the sorry state of the world” when referring to something that s/he had read in the news). With respect to cognitions, most thoughts were unrelated to the computer application being used (98.44%). That is, participants reported the objects of their thoughts as either being related to work (Participant 37, while using Microsoft Word: “We have to ensure that we cover off on all possibilities in this project through this contract”; Participant 48, while using e-mail: “I am trying to visualize my potential client’s venture”) or to non-work issues, representing mind-wandering thoughts (for example, Participant 23, while using a software package for client account management: “I am thinking about the weekend, about what needs to be done for my wedding (in 26 days!) and that I hope the rest of the week isn’t busy”).

During short-term discrepant IT events, results support an adjusting IS use pattern, that is, negative emotions and computer-related thoughts (H2). Specifically, during discrepant IT events, affect was negative and related to the computer system (100%). These experiences were characterized by strong negative affect, such as frustration, annoyance, and stress. For example, Participant 34 reported an event in which s/he could not use an application because it was unable to reboot; this person reported the following: “I am impatient! I have people waiting on the phone and I can’t access the information I need. I am thinking I might throw the PC out of the window!” Another participant (Participant 51) reported formatting difficulties: “when I cut from mail and paste into Word a border appears around the block of the text,” and continued, “I am frustrated and tired.” With respect to cognitions, most people’s thoughts were related to the computer (97.30%). For example, Participant 42 reported the impact on his/her work (“another distraction from what I’m trying to do”) of a computer-related difficulty (“Windows Vista is not compatible with my Blackberry. I had to download stuff and deal with various other bugs”). For other users, their thoughts were focused on finding a solution or wondering why the error was occurring. For example, Participant 58 explained his/her thoughts while having problems with the pagination of a contract: “I am trying to frame out how to rework a table to stay on one page to be easier for the client to view.” Participant 54’s thoughts were also focused on trying to find a solution (“If I can summarize the findings differently so I don’t have to create another row”) to a formatting difficulty (“I can’t make an extra row within one cell of a table”).

For long-term discrepant IT events, results support the transition from an adjusting IS use pattern to an automatic one (H3). Supporting this, we found that these events resulted in both neutral and negative affect and that most emotions were unrelated to the system (72.72%). For example, Participant 22 reported difficulties due to the system being slow and explained his/her feelings, “I am calm,” while Participant 7 also explained that the system was slow and stated: “I have no feelings.” With respect to cognitions, we found that most thoughts were unrelated to the computer (81.81%). For example, while the system stayed frozen over time, Participant 62 reported thinking about “plans for the evening,” whereas other participants experiencing slow systems described their thoughts as being work-related (Participant 8: “This repair center tries to double bill a box”) or non-work related (Participant 13: “How long ’til lunch?”).

## Discussion

The rich data captured in this study provide preliminary support for the existence of an automatic IS use pattern during expected IT events, and an adjusting one as a result of discrepant IT events. Furthermore, our results suggest that use generally follows an automatic pattern characterized by a lack of reflection and focus on the technology. In contrast, adjusting use patterns are characterized by strong negative affect and computer-related thoughts. Finally, our results provide preliminary support for the effect of time in facilitating the transition from adjusting to automatic use patterns. In sum, these results suggest that the pattern construct reflects how IT is used in organizations.

There are several strengths of this study that enhance the validity of our findings. For example, we avoided closeended questions that can prime the existence of a given construct (Salancik 1984). Further, by capturing the emotional and cognitive components of use patterns as they occur naturally in real time, we ensured a lack of retrospective bias of users’ accounts of their experiences (Golden 1992). Despite these strengths, there are some limitations of our method that warrant a second study. First, we could not measure the behavior component of IS use patterns, and therefore we could not capture the behavior–performance relationship. As a result, the hypotheses could not be fully tested. Finally, although this study supports the proposed effects of IT events, a more controlled study is necessary to demonstrate causality.

## Study 2: Experiment

A double-blind experiment<sup>7</sup> was designed to compensate for the limits of the previous method (Jarvenpaa et al. 1985) and to test all of the hypotheses (see Table 2). In this study, students were asked to write an essay stating their opinions on a topic that had been quite controversial at their university and, as such, it was likely that they would have formed their own opinions about the topic. Because they often write with Microsoft Word, this application was used. This study focused on expected and discrepant IT events and participants were randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions: condition one (42 participants) represented the control group in which no discrepant IT events occurred (that is, only expected IT events occurred); condition two (39 participants) included short-term discrepant IT events (events that could be easily overcome); and condition three (22 participants<sup>8</sup>) included long-term discrepant IT events (events that could not be overcome). These events occurred at three different points of time (see Figure 2). We chose to implement the discrepant events as unexpected “format changes” for several reasons. First, the event needed to be something that would represent a discrepant IT event without being fatal in order to avoid tautological effects. Thus, participants could carry on with the experimental task regardless of the occurrence of the events. Second, it had to be something that might happen in “real life”: Word has features that automatically guess formatting requirements and make automatic changes throughout the document while a user is typing. Finally, different versions of these events were pretested and formatting errors proved to be the most appropriate.

The data were gathered at different points during the experiment: a background questionnaire before the task began (to measure control variables); heart rate monitoring (to measure physiological arousal), verbal protocol analysis (to capture thoughts), and video recording during the task (to capture behaviors); questions that appeared six times on the computer screen (“pop-up questions”) during the task (to capture affect); participants’ essays saved for later grading (to measure short-term performance); and a final questionnaire after task completion (to measure manipulation checks) (see Table 1 and Figure 2). Before conducting the experiment, two pilot studies were carried out in order to improve the experimental procedure and design. This also allowed us to develop the coding schemes for the audio and video data, the participants essays, and the heart rate data.

The experiment was conducted over approximately two months in a private office at a university. Because of the physiological measures, the necessity to videotape each participant, and the thinking-out-loud technique, we could only run one participant at a time. Each participant took approximately one and a half hours. The participants had two incentives to participate in the study. First, they received credit for participating in the experiment that they could exchange for 1.5 percent points in a course. Second, they were informed that the six students writing the best essays would each receive \$45 Amazon gift certificates.

## Results

Two manipulation checks were included and showed significant results in the proper directions (see Table A1 in Appendix A). In addition, potential effects of the experimental administrators and other covariates on the measures of interest were tested, indicating no significant effects (see Tables A2 and A3 in Appendix A). Hypotheses 1–3 were tested using repeated measures in SPSS, whereas H4 was analyzed using PLS. The sample of 102 participants meets the size requirements for PLS (Gefen et al. 2000).

The psychometric properties of the measures were appropriate. Analysis of the factor structures showed that all individual item reliabilities loaded above the .707 guideline (Fornell 1982) (see Table 3). Furthermore, composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha values were greater than the .80 guideline (Lance et al. 2006) (see Table 4). All of the latent variables demonstrated AVE scores greater than .50 (Chin 1998). The square root of the AVE of each construct was greater than its correlation with any other construct; in addition, the loadings for the items to their related constructs were higher than those to any other constructs (Chin 1998).

## Results for Hypotheses

For testing H1–H3, we ran repeated-measures ANOVA and plotted the data over time (please see a summary of all the results in Figure 3 and Table 2, and a detailed explanation of the tests and figures for each variable in Appendix A). H1<sup>9</sup> suggests that an automatic IS use pattern should occur during expected IT events. That is for H1 to hold, during the first minutes of the experiment (before any discrepant IT events occur), individuals in all conditions should be experiencing automatic use: high and similar amounts of non-computer related thoughts and exploitive behaviors. Results support this pattern and Figure 3 illustrates it: during the first minutes of the experimental task, individuals across conditions show high and similar amounts of non-computer related thoughts and exploitive behaviors.

H2 suggests an adjusting pattern: discrepant IT events trigger negative emotions, computer-related thoughts, and adaptive behaviors. For emotions, there is a significant drop in affect for the treatment conditions (compared to the control condition) after the first and third discrepant IT events (see Figure 3; see Table A4 and Figure A1 in Appendix A), and a significant drop in physiological arousal (in the opposite direction than hypothesized) after the first and second discrepant IT events, partially supporting H2a (see Figure 3; see Table A4 and Figure A2 in Appendix A). With respect to cognitions, there is a significant increase in computer-related thoughts (and a significant drop in non-computer related thoughts) immediately after the three discrepant IT events, supporting H2b (see Figure 3; see Table A4 and Figure A3 in Appendix A). For behaviors, there is a significant increase in adaptive behaviors (and a significant drop in exploitive behaviors) after each discrepant IT event, supporting H2c (see Figure 3; see Table A4 and Figure A4 in Appendix A). Thus, as hypothesized, there is a change in the components of use patterns after discrepant IT events, resulting in an adjusting pattern. Most of the theorized components (affect, cognition, and behaviors) of such adjusting patterns are supported, and the changes of physiological arousal, although in the opposite direction than hypothesized, also provide support for a distinctive pattern caused by discrepant IT events.

H3<sup>10</sup> proposes that discrepant event effects should fade away over time, transitioning from an adjusting pattern to an automatic one. If there are decreases in computer-related thoughts and adaptive behaviors, and increases of non-computerrelated thoughts and exploitive behaviors in condition two (short-term discrepant IT events that are fixed) and condition three (unfixable discrepant IT events that continue in time) over time, such that they become similar to the control condition, this would support H3. Results do demonstrate this (see Figure 3; see Appendix A).

Finally, for H4, there is a significant positive relation between exploitive behaviors and short-term task performance explaining 24 percent of the variance in short-term task performance, (and no significant relation between adaptive behaviors and performance, as proposed).

## Discussion

This study complements the experience sampling study by demonstrating causal effects of expected and discrepant IT events on different IS use patterns. As hypothesized, individuals engaged in an automatic IS use pattern during expected events, characterized by non-computer-related thoughts and exploitive behaviors. Discrepant IT events, instead, triggered a change from an automatic to an adjusting IS use pattern. That is, the three components of an IS use pattern—emotions, cognitions, and behaviors—changed following the discrepant IT event. As hypothesized, this adjusting IS use pattern was composed of negative affect, computer-related thoughts, and adaptive behaviors. However, the pattern composition with respect to physiological arousal was different than hypothesized: individuals demonstrated lower physiological arousal following discrepant events (rather than higher, as hypothesized). This may be due to individuals’ shift of attention from the task to the event, as attentional responses to emotionally evocative stimuli have been associated with a decrease in heart rates (Beauchaine 2001; Hamann et al. 2002).

This study also demonstrates that there is a transition as time passes from the adjusting pattern adopted immediately after a discrepant event to an automatic one (because users either resolved the event—those in condition two—or they gave up —those in condition three). Finally, results demonstrate a causal relation between the exploitive behaviors of automatic IS use patterns and short-term task performance. In summary, expected events, discrepant IT events, and time result in distinctive IS use patterns: automatic and adjusting. The existence of these patterns is demonstrated by changes in values of the three pattern components (emotions, cognitions, and behaviors).

Further examination of these results along with those of the previous study will be addressed in the general discussion. Before doing so, it is important to highlight the methodological strengths of this study because they support the internal validity of our results. First, this study investigated actual behaviors, rather than self-reported behaviors, the norm in IS research (e.g., Barki et al. 2007; Burton-Jones and Straub 2006), thus avoiding desirability biases. Second, the different data collection methods allowed for the measurement of constructs as they occurred, thus avoiding mono-method bias, priming, and consistency artifacts (Salancik 1984) often occurring in the measurement of IS use (Straub and Burton-Jones 2007). Third, the double-blind design reduces experimenter and response bias (Shadish et al. 2003). Finally, this study used physiological measurement, a method that allows for the measurement of physiological states and their changes, responding to recent calls from researchers in management (Elfenbein 2007) and IS (Dimoka et al. 2011).

## General Discussion and Conclusions

Drawing on coping, affect–object, and automaticity theories, this research conceptualizes and empirically demonstrates the existence of dynamic IS use patterns, or groups of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors that occur together as a result of different IT events. Specifically, results demonstrate that users engage in two main IS use patterns, automatic and adjusting. The most prevalent is the automatic IS use pattern, which takes place during expected IT events: it is characterized by emotions and cognitions unrelated to the IT being used, and by exploitive behaviors, that is, users interacting with an IT in a straightforward manner to accomplish a task. In contrast, the adjusting IS use pattern is triggered by discrepant IT events and is characterized by negative affect related to the IT being used, lower physiological arousal, computer-related thoughts, and adaptive behaviors aimed at modifying an aspect of the IT.

These two IS use patterns appear and disappear over time as different (expected versus discrepant) events occur. Interestingly, the effects of discrepant IT events fade over time regardless of whether the event is resolved. That is, the adjusting pattern that occurs immediately after a discrepant IT event weakens as time passes and transitions into an automatic pattern. Finally, results demonstrate that IS use patterns are important for performance: their exploitive behavioral component results in short-term performance.

## Theoretical Implications

This research makes several contributions. Responding to calls for richer conceptualizations of use (e.g., Burton-Jones and Straub 2006) , we developed a configurational construct of IS use patterns as they occur naturally in the workplace. Constructs are relevant when they can give valuable explanations to important questions (Barki et al. 2007), and we believe that IS use patterns do so in several ways. First, these patterns change over time based on IT events and discriminate between the different ways in which IT is actually used. In doing so, this research goes beyond traditional adoption/continuance theories and clarifies the direct effects of technology (as opposed to cognitive representations of the IT artifact) in relation to IS use.

Second, the behavioral component of IS use patterns helps to disentangle why specific types of use lead to performance, while other types do not, responding to calls for research in this area (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). For example, two people might be using the same technology for the same amount of time for a given task but have different short-term performance—because one has an automatic use pattern while the other has an adjusting one. That is, it is how the technology is used (the actual behaviors with the technology) that relates to performance. Future research could investigate this further. For example, although exploitive behaviors lead to better short-term performance for certain types of tasks, automaticity implies a certain lack of control (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009), and as such, exploitive behaviors might be detrimental to highly unstructured tasks requiring innovation. As described earlier, adjusting patterns may have positive impacts on longer-term performance because of the learning that occurs regarding important application functionalities or “work-arounds,” thus enabling more effective usage behaviors in future IT interactions. Consequently, IS use and work task performance are closely intertwined, implying a need for a much closer connection between IS and organizational research (Orlikowski and Scott 2008).<sup>11</sup>

Third, the prevalence of automatic IS use patterns supports recent theorizing challenging the commonly held assumption that users often reflect about IT and its possibilities (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009). That is, we might be confusing the way users appear to work (reflective about IT) with the way they actually work (unreflective about IT) (Lee 1998). This insight is important because a central focus of any scientific field should be the identification and empirical examination of its implicit theoretical assumptions (Gray and Cooper 2010; Silva 2007).

Fourth, IS use patterns also provide possible explanations for the formation of IT perceptions (Benbasat and Barki 2007). When things go smoothly—as they do most of the time—the IT goes unnoticed, and so might its benefits (the automatic use pattern). It is only when difficulties (discrepant IT events) occur that users focus on the IT, doing so in negative terms (the adjusting use pattern). Taking into consideration that emotional experiences play an essential role in the creation of memories (Anderson and Draper 1991), perceptions of IT value might be negatively laden because negative experiences have a stronger role than positive ones in their determination (Taylor 1991). This provides a complementary explanation for why negative attitudes about technologies persist and why a perceptual gap exists between how an IT actually performs and organizational members’ (users’ and managers’) perceptions of its importance (Hirschheim et al. 2003; Peppard and Ward 1999). This insight raises possibilities for future research. For example, studies could investigate whether persistent prejudices against technology and IS professionals (Akbulut-Bailey 2009) follow the same automatic and implicit associations as with other stereotypes (Banaji and Hardin 1996; Nosek et al. 2007). This would help in developing strategies to overcome such negative stereotypes so that IT can become noticeable in positive rather than negative ways. This would be helpful for (1) identifying what to do so that IT gets the favorable attention of users and decision makers, (2) developing guidelines to reduce users’ negative perceptions of technology, and (3) better understanding the reasons for why we struggle to attract students to our majors (Looney and Akbulut 2007).

Finally, this research complements traditional adoption/continuance theories (e.g., TAM) in several ways. For example, traditional research explains whether an individual will use (or continue to use) a given system based on behavioral beliefs (or evaluative perceptions of IT) (e.g., Bhattacherjee 2001), but cannot describe what happens during the utilization of a system. Our research helps to fill this gap. Furthermore, traditional research argues that behavioral beliefs are updated as users engage in direct experiences with technology (e.g., Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010), but does not explore such direct experiences. This paper partially addresses this issue: in the first study, we found that deliberative decisions about use do not appear to be reexamined during the utilization of a system, either during expected or discrepant events. Future research could focus on the criticality or number of discrepant IT events necessary for users to question their decisions to utilize a given system.

It is also worth further discussing the role of emotions in the automatic use pattern. As we suggested earlier, affect is unrelated to the system being employed and its valence is indeterminate (it can be negative, neutral, or high) in the automatic IS use pattern. This is due to the intrinsic nature of affect: during expected events, the strongest affect experienced by users relates to something else in their work or personal lives that cannot be predicted a priori. This implies that affect with respect to the system will likely be positive or neutral in the automatic use pattern because the technology functions smoothly. However, without explicitly asking individuals about their affect toward the system being utilized (and possibly introducing priming artifacts into the inquiry method), this cannot be tested. Instead, we opted to ask general questions (i.e., what are your feelings?) without artificially provoking them to think about the system.

Other patterns of IS use need to be developed, such as those resulting from discovery (positive unexpected) events. One such pattern might be characterized by positive affect, high arousal, computer-related thoughts, and adaptive behaviors. As noted earlier, we observed few discovery events, suggesting that exploratory IT use—“the search for novel and innovative ways of doing things” (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006, p. 236)—may be rare. Nevertheless they represent a key area for study as they should lead to positive attitudes and performance.

Further development of IS use patterns should also extend to the traditional task–technology fit literature (Goodhue 1998). For example, ideal profiles of IS use patterns could be identified and linked to different types of tasks and situations, creating a fit model of use patterns and tasks, in which different types of performance derive from use pattern/task pairings. This would extend TTF theory through (1) improving its precision by pairing types of use with types of tasks/situations, (2) giving it a more dynamic flavor by including transitions between ideal fit states, and (3) clarifying the types of tasks that would better fit with different use patterns.

## Limitations and Strengths

As with any research, our studies have several limitations that warrant future research. One is the focus on discrepant events that were not fatal—that is, in the second study, we only studied negative events that could be overcome or ignored.

As described earlier, other limitations include our focus on expected and discrepant events, but not on discovery events, as well as our measurement of short-term rather than longterm performance.

Despite these limitations, this investigation of IS use patterns has several strengths, in addition to those already mentioned within each study. The multiple methods had important synergistic properties that allowed for insights that would have been difficult to discover with leaner methods (Mingers 2001). These methods complemented each other by compensating for the weakness of one with the strengths of the other (Jick 1979). For example, we were able to conduct a preliminary test of our hypotheses in a natural setting with rich qualitative data from employees, which added realism to our research. We then tested our hypotheses in a controlled laboratory experiment with students via quantitative data, which added precision to our investigation. By examining the data longitudinally in the quantitative study, we could examine different types of use patterns and how they changed over time. For example, we found that the effects of discrepant IT events faded over time regardless of whether the discrepant IT event was resolved or not. Such detailed analyses added accuracy and richness to our research. Further, they allowed us to detect phenomena that could not be detected had we studied effects cross-sectionally or had we asked users to reflect on their experiences a posteriori (removed from the use experience). If the IS field wants to gain an understanding of how actual IS use unfolds and is adjusted naturally as the context changes, we need to complement commonly employed methods (e.g., surveys) with those capable of reporting rich findings longitudinally when actual use is taking place. Thus, methods such as those used here— observational, experience sampling, and physiological—may serve the field well in studying individuals’ cognitions, emotions, and behaviors that can occur outside individuals awareness during use (Heaphy and Dutton 2008).

## Practical Implications

This research has several practical implications, such as the training of students and employees. Because of the importance of difficult situations and errors for learning (Sein and Santhanam 1999), training should be reframed such that discrepant IT events are viewed as opportunities for learning. Given that discrepant IT events are unavoidable, training could focus less on error avoidance and more on helping users develop the skills to effectively deal with difficulties. The trainer could be a role model (Compeau and Higgins 1995), showing how unexpected events could be handled, and emphasizing that computing ability is an acquirable skill (Martocchio 1994), and as such, improved through overcoming difficulties. Recent research supports this approach to learning by proposing that learning is facilitated when stress is experienced near the time when the knowledge acquisition takes place (Joëls et al. 2006). Thus, trainers can emphasize the potential learning outcomes of discrepant IT events, because any knowledge acquisition is likely to be remembered in the future.

This research also has implications for IS design and performance. Our results show that better short-term performance takes place when users do not think about the system and instead focus their behaviors on the accomplishment of their tasks. This suggests that application updates should be aimed at minimizing the attention drawn to the system. One way to accomplish this would be by using intuitive interfaces, avoiding major interface changes, and making updates and new applications resemble old ones.

## Conclusion

This research represents an initial effort to (1) conceptualize IS use patterns as different configurations of emotions, cognitions, and behaviors; (2) study how these patterns change over time as a result of IT events; and (3) examine how these patterns relate to short-term performance. Our conceptualization of IS use patterns theorizes about types of use configurations, and our two studies empirically demonstrate their existence, providing an understanding of how IT is actually used in organizations. The IS field will benefit from further theoretical and empirical efforts on IS use patterns, the tasks for which they are appropriate, and their consequences for performance.
