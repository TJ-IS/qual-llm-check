# How Sentiment Divergence in Influencers' Multimodal Social Media Posts Shapes Follower Engagement?

Reza Alibakhshi, $^{1}$ Shirish C. Srivastava $^{2}$

$^{1}$ Information Systems and Technology Department, IE University, Spain, reza.alibakhshi@ie.edu $^{2}$ Information Systems and Operations Management Department, HEC Paris, France, srivastava@hec.fr

## Abstract

The growing prominence of social media (SM) influencers as key content creators on SM platforms highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of factors that drive follower engagement. Taking a sentiment-theoretic perspective, we examine the interplay between image and text sentiment in multimodal SM posts to show that alignment with followers' sentiment consistency expectations is crucial for enhanced follower engagement with influencers' SM posts. Drawing on expectation disconfirmation and negativity bias theories, we explore the impact of sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts and the moderating role of the follower community's duality tolerance on follower engagement. Analyzing a dataset of 24,000 Instagram posts, our findings suggest that sentiment divergence between the image and text within the same SM post can reduce follower engagement. Specifically, when the text sentiment is less positive than the image sentiment (negative divergence), the detrimental impact on engagement is notably more pronounced, while posts where the text sentiment is more positive than the image sentiment (positive divergence) appear to be less affected. Additionally, we find that higher duality tolerance within an influencer's community mitigates the negative effects of sentiment divergence on follower engagement. Our research contributes to the SM literature by highlighting the importance of sentiment divergence for SM influencers and their SM community's duality tolerance orientation when they are designing posts. We further provide practical insights on how to craft multimodal SM posts that are effective in enhancing follower engagement.

Keywords: Influencer, Social Media, User Engagement, Sentiment Divergence, Duality Tolerance, Image Sentiment, Text Sentiment, Expectation Disconfirmation

## 1 Introduction

The past decade has witnessed the emergence of influencers on social media (SM) platforms. These individuals' profound impact on public discourse and ability to engage vast audiences have reshaped the digital landscape (Chung et al., 2023), and they have become a pivotal category of SM content creators. An influencer creates engaging content and publishes it on

SM platforms, the aim being to cultivate an extensive follower network that gives the influencer—as the word indicates—considerable social influence (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022). According to recent global statistics, the number of influencers on Instagram alone has grown to 64 million; many millions more operate on other popular platforms such as TikTok (Andrii, 2023). The increasing salience of SM influencers underlines their importance not only for SM platforms, whose user traffic and revenue raised through activities such as advertising are being driven up by users of this type, but also to businesses that engage in online promotional activities such as SM campaigns (Hughes et al., 2019). $^{1}$

Clearly, enhancing follower engagement is a key motivator for SM influencers (Cao & Belo, 2024). Stronger and more sustained follower engagement increases influencers' social reach in their network of followers and also boosts their engagement rates on SM platforms, leading to both direct and indirect socioeconomic advantages for them (Farivar et al., 2021; Mallipeddi et al., 2022). Moreover, because it stimulates platform traffic, user engagement with SM influencers' content has become crucial for sustaining SM platforms (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022). However, the success of influencers' content in engaging followers greatly depends on how the content is crafted and presented.

Prior research has explored various factors that enhance engagement with SM posts. Some studies have focused on the nature of relationships between content creators and content consumers, such as the influencer-follower relationship (e.g., Farivar et al., 2021), while others have examined the role of content characteristics—sentiment expressed in images or text, for instance—on follower engagement (e.g., Yang et al., 2019). In the context of the influencer-follower relationship, a notable area of inquiry—and one that we wish to focus on here—concerns how the integration of multiple media formats (e.g., image and text) within a single SM post influences follower engagement. SM influencers employ emotionally charged content to strengthen their followers' engagement with their SM posts (Chung et al., 2023). However, influencers who decide to shift from single-mode (text) to multimodal (text, image, etc.) SM posts—that is, combining text and imagery in a single SM post—face the challenge of aligning sentiment across different modes within their SM posts. The critical questions are whether the sentiment conveyed in each mode featured in a multimodal SM post should align with or diverge from that of the other mode and how this convergence or divergence of sentiment across the different modes impacts follower engagement.

When images and text converge in sentiment, a post's emotional message may be strengthened, enhancing follower engagement (Li & Cho, 2022; Petrocelli et al., 2010). Conversely, divergence between these elements could lead to unclear user interpretations (Choi et al.,

2023). Our research examines this dilemma about the impact of divergent sentiment across media modes within multimodal SM posts on follower engagement. Although the integration of visuals with text has become standard practice in SM content production, understanding the influence of sentiment divergence within multimodal SM posts remains an unaddressed challenge.

Based on these considerations, our study introduces the concept of “sentiment divergence” within multimodal posts. Prior research demonstrates that users generally favor consistency in SM content (Li & Cho, 2022). Recent studies have examined how informational (in)consistency between image and text impacts follower engagement (e.g., Li & Xie, 2020; Ha et al., 2020). However, SM posts, in addition to information, also convey rich emotional cues embedded in images and text that significantly determine engagement (Mallipeddi et al., 2021). $^{2}$ Taking a sentiment-theoretic approach, we aim to understand how divergence in sentiment between images and text influences follower engagement with influencers’ SM posts, leading to our first research question:

RQ1: In the SM influencer context, how does divergence between the sentiment conveyed by the image and that conveyed by the text within the same multimodal SM post impact follower engagement?

Building on our first research question, we posit that while all users may observe sentiment divergence in a multimodal post, their interpretations of such divergence can vary, affecting how they engage with the post. Although the need for consistency in SM content is widely acknowledged (Abelson et al., 1968; Hong & Lee, 2010), the literature suggests that different SM user communities' cultural traits significantly influence their interpretation of the inconsistencies they observe (Williams & Aaker, 2002). Studies indicate that SM users' cultural orientations can affect their online behaviors, and whether or not they need coherence and consistency in content sentiment might be determined by their own cultural context (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022; Hong et al., 2016). Accordingly, some cultures might view a given instance of sentiment divergence as embodying a duality of incompatible sentiment, while others might see a harmonious blend of complementary sentiment in the duality. We thus adopt the macrocultural concept of “duality tolerance” to further suggest that certain cultural groups may perceive divergent elements as a unified whole, whereas others may see them as starkly contrasting elements (DeMotta et al., 2016; Williams & Aaker, 2002). This motivated our second research question:

RQ2: In the SM influencer context, how does the duality tolerance of the influencer's situating community alter the effect on follower engagement of sentiment divergence in the influencer's multimodal SM posts?

To answer these questions, we conceptualized sentiment divergence as the magnitude and direction of the difference between the sentiment embedded in the image and text of the same multimodal SM post. We empirically examine the effect of this construct on follower engagement using a dataset of more than 24,000 Instagram posts. Our findings suggest that divergence between the sentiment expressed in the image and text of an SM influencer post negatively impacts follower engagement with SM posts. However, the direction of sentiment divergence alters this influence, such that positive sentiment divergence—that is, presenting a more positive (or less negative) caption after the image in the SM post—does not negatively impact follower engagement. Moreover, our analysis reveals the significant role of the cultural orientation of the influencer's situating community: the higher the community's acceptance of duality, the lower the negative influence of sentiment divergence on follower engagement. Overall, our results indicate that sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts has a context-dependent influence on follower engagement.

Our study offers several contributions. Through our work, we contribute to the SM literature by conceptualizing and examining the imperative role of sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts' fostering of follower engagement (Hong et al., 2013; Johns, 2006), thereby extending prior work examining different aspects of informational inconsistency in multimodal content (e.g., Ha et al., 2020). Our study also demonstrates the importance of incorporating SM users' duality tolerance orientations to better understand the influence of sentiment divergence within multimodal SM posts (Williams & Aaker, 2002). We thus contribute to the literature on localizing SM content (Janssens et al., 2007) by demonstrating that localization may not be effective if the sentiment divergence within the SM posts is not aligned with the duality tolerance of the targeted SM community. More broadly, our study contributes to the SM literature (Mallipeddi et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2014) by conceptualizing sentiment divergence in SM posts as an element of the sociotechnical system of SM platforms and integrating the ongoing localization debate around it (Hong et al., 2016). The practical insights emerging from our findings can also help influencers better craft their multimodal content for enhanced follower engagement and sensibly consider the macrocultural orientations of the intended SM audience.

## 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 User Engagement on Social Media Platforms

Literature on SM platforms has examined user engagement behaviors in different content creation contexts, including those of individuals (Adamopoulos et al., 2018), influencers (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022), and brands (Cao & Belo, 2024). In these studies, the user engagement construct is conceptualized and measured according to users' behavior and needs—for instance, liking and commenting on the focal SM post (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022), sharing SM content with other users (Mallipeddi et al., 2021; Venkatesan et al., 2021), and creating content in personal and professional communities (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022; Goh et al., 2013; Grigore et al., 2015; Li et al., 2022). There is an emerging understanding that SM engagement should address certain psychological needs of individuals (Karahanna et al., 2015, 2018; Zhang et al., 2023). Recent studies have argued that SM engagement as a behavioral construct is rooted in individuals' innate tendencies, such as the need for affiliation with eminent figures (e.g., influencers) (Chung et al., 2023) and the search for satisfaction by consuming consistent SM content (Hartmann et al., 2021; Li & Cho, 2022). Whether SM user engagement is framed as egocentric (e.g., content sharing) or allocentric (e.g., following, liking, and commenting) behavior, it fulfills certain innate psychological needs that individuals have (Karahanna et al., 2018). For instance, for SM users, consistency in SM content's information and emotional cues is more than a preference; it is a psychological necessity because it contributes to a sense of cognitive coherency vis-à-vis the consumed SM content, enriching their engagement experience (Hartmann et al., 2021; Li & Cho, 2022).

To understand what drives these psychological needs and how they contribute to user engagement, the literature has examined the various enablers of user engagement from two major perspectives (Goh et al., 2013; Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013). The first perspective focuses on the dynamics of “relationship characteristics” between content creators and content consumers, one example of these being followers’ engagement with the content generated by influencers (Chung et al., 2023; Farivar et al., 2021). This stream of research essentially concerns how the relationship between content creators and content consumers defines the proliferation of SM engagement. Alibakhshi and Srivastava (2022), for instance, examined the effect of influencers’ self-disclosure on their follower engagement. Much of this stream of work thus explores how user engagement with SM content varies (or not) according to the type of SM relationship—“user-user” and “user-marketer,” for instance (Goh et al., 2013). The second stream of research examines the role of “content characteristics” such as linguistic features (Lee et al., 2018), emotional valence (Yang et al., 2019), textual sentiment (Berger et al., 2019; Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013), and visual attributes (Li & Xie, 2020; McDuff & Berger, 2020) in fostering user engagement with the SM content. For example, Stieglitz and Dang-Xuan (2013) studied the effect of text positivity on SM user engagement. Among these studies, a salient theme that emerges is the impact of different aspects of sentiment embedded within SM posts, such as sentiment embedded within an image (Liu et al., 2020; McDuff & Berger, 2020) or text (Mallipeddi et al., 2021; Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013). Our study situates itself within research on content characteristics while controlling for the dynamics of follower-influencer relationships. This approach allowed us to dive deeper into how sentiment divergence between the image and text of a given SM post impacts follower engagement, and as a result, we contribute to both streams of research. Specifically, we focus on followers’ psychological need for consistency in the content generated by SM influencers. Our theorization is based on the satisfaction that followers derive by engaging with a multimodal SM post whose image and text express the same sentiment (Li & Cho, 2022).

## 2.2 Content (In)consistency and User Engagement on Social Media Platforms

The consistency between visual and textual elements in SM content is crucial in influencing user engagement behaviors (Li & Cho, 2022). Although both informational and emotional consistencies play crucial roles, they operate through distinct mechanisms that warrant separate examination. Informational inconsistencies between images and accompanying text on SM platforms can prompt cognitive dissonance among users, which has been shown to negatively impact their engagement levels (Zhao et al., 2019). This cognitive dissonance arises when users encounter incongruent information in multimodal content (Jeong et al., 2019), which disrupts the coherence of the message and thus requires additional cognitive effort by users to process these information inconsistencies.

As such, information mismatches or inconsistencies between visual and textual content essentially form conflicting cues in the same content, as these different pieces of information pertain to facts about a focal entity (Moravec et al., 2019; Tsang, 2017). For instance, in online review platforms, informational inconsistencies between visual presentations and text-based descriptions of a product's or service's attributes have been shown to adversely affect their evaluations by online users by confusing users and reducing the content's effectiveness (Choi et al., 2023). Similarly, informational inconsistencies between the description of an object in visual content and salient points in its visual portrayal have been found to increase the complexity of comprehending the overall message of the multimodal content (Ha et al., 2020; van Rompay et al., 2010). Such inconsistencies and their complexities lead to difficulties in locating and remembering both sources of information and ultimately reducing user engagement with the SM content (Huo, 2015).

In synthesizing prior research on information inconsistency and its effect on user engagement, while studies have provided valuable insights into the effects of information inconsistency, they often focus primarily on overt informational mismatches between image content and textual descriptions. For instance, the effect of the visual richness of multimodal SM posts on customer engagement is weakened by longer text posts, suggesting that informational inconsistencies between visual and non-visual information can negatively impact user engagement (Zhao et al., 2023). However, we contend that these approaches may not fully capture the nuanced ways in which image and text mismatch might arise from multimodal content and how it impacts user engagement with SM posts.

Emotional and sentiment-based divergence between images and text differs fundamentally from informational inconsistencies. Informational inconsistency involves discrepancies in factual or semantic content, whereas emotional divergence between the image and text of a multimodal post pertains to conflicting affective cues that can evoke alternate psychological responses rather than cognitive ones (Peterson, 2014). Moreover, while users may comprehend informational discrepancies between an image and its associated text uniformly as a form of “discrepancy,” synthesizing inconsistent sentiment between visual and textual sources of content can be more complex and heterogeneous. Divergent sentiment in the image and text of an SM post might be understood as complementary or conflicting. Divergent sentiment might be positively or negatively evaluated even when it is considered to be self-contradictory (Williams & Aaker, 2002). Research suggests that SM users may harmoniously assess some information content cognitively, yet emotional divergence can heterogeneously disrupt the affective evaluation of content and the emotional harmony of the message, leading to divergent engagement behaviors (Russell, 2003). Moreover, while information inconsistency might be more salient for information-rich settings such as product reviews (Choi et al., 2023), whereas emotional and sentiment divergence of content might be more important in hedonic settings such as SM influencer or entertainment platforms (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022).

Accordingly, the divergence between the image and text of SM posts might operate through complex affective pathways that might not have been captured through cognitive processing of informational inconsistencies in prior research (Choi et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2019). Affective and cognitive processes function independently (Russell, 2003), and the perception of sentiment and informational mismatches in SM content can have distinct effects on users' engagement behaviors (Jeong et al., 2019; Roseman, 1984; Shin, 2017). Despite the well-studied mechanisms of informational inconsistency on user engagement, the mechanisms through which sentiment divergence might influence user engagement are distinct from those of informational divergence and require additional investigation. Of equal importance, this imperative emerges from how prior studies have examined the affective influence of unimodal SM content (e.g., textual content) on user engagement but not considered the joint effect of image and text sentiment on user engagement in the currently more prevalent multimodal forms of content.

## 2.3 Sentiment Divergence as Multimodal Sentiment Analysis

Literature focusing on SM platforms has widely examined the sentiment characteristics of content and their effect on users' online behavior (Berger et al., 2019; Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013). Topics of interest in this literature include the effectiveness of online reviews (Yin et al., 2014) and the performance of advertising content (Berger et al., 2019). Because of its well-established methodology, text sentiment analysis has been widely deployed in research examining SM user engagement (Bachura et al., 2022; Oh et al., 2022). For instance, prior studies have examined the influence of positive or negative sentiment embedded in textual SM content on user engagement (Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013). Within this stream of research, two major positions have emerged regarding the role that the sentiment expressed in textual content plays in driving SM engagement: (1) content's sentiment is the core construct driving SM engagement behaviors (e.g., Oh et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2019) and (2) content's sentiment is a peripheral construct that controls the confounding effects of sentiment on SM engagement behaviors (e.g., Adamopoulos et al., 2018; Li & Xie, 2020). Both positions' proponents have incorporated different theoretical lenses to describe the impact of sentiment on SM engagement (please see Appendix B for key studies examining content sentiment and SM engagement behaviors).

However, in response to the proliferation of multimedia content, such as SM posts containing both an image and text, a few studies have examined the impact of sentiment embedded in images on SM user engagement (Lee et al., 2018; Li & Xie, 2020). Recent studies have exhibited the efficacy of image content analysis techniques, and especially facial expression analysis, for extracting sentiment from images and photos (Choudhury et al., 2019; McDuff & Berger, 2020; Shin et al., 2020). Building on this development, other recent studies have examined the sentiment represented in visual SM content and how it impacts SM user engagement behaviors. For instance, by applying facial expression detection techniques to videos, Zhou et al. (2021) demonstrated the salient impact of visual emotions such as sadness and happiness on engagement with videos.

Synthesizing these studies, we contend that our understanding of how sentiment embedded in SM content influences SM user engagement should also incorporate the interplay between image and text sentiment. Specifically, the shift toward multimodal SM posts instead of unimodal text-based content has made examining how SM user engagement is impacted by the interplay between the sentiment embedded in an image and the sentiment embedded in the text of a multimodal post an imperative priority. Although prior studies such as Stieglitz and Dang-Xuan (2013) inform us that positive sentiment expressed in unimodal SM posts (e.g., a textual SM post) helps to foster user engagement, it is unclear how the joint expression of different levels of positive and negative sentiment in the image and text of a multimodal SM post impact user engagement. Thus motivated, our study theorizes and examines the influence of sentiment divergence between the image and text of an SM post on user engagement.

## 2.4 Duality Tolerance as an Overarching Cultural Trait for Engaging with Sentimentally Divergent Content

While our understanding of sentiment divergence in multimodal content continues to develop, a remaining imperative is how different cultural orientations might process and respond to sentiment divergence. This consideration brings us to examine duality tolerance, a cultural trait that may fundamentally influence how users from different backgrounds process and engage with content containing divergent sentiment in multimodal SM posts.

Duality is the characteristic of an entity or concept containing two distinct, often contrasting or complementary parts, that coexist and interact within a single whole. The principle of duality tolerance describes a continuing process of perceiving, accepting, and synthesizing possible contradictions in different kinds of situations, elements, and forms (Williams & Aaker, 2002). Individuals' responses to incongruent and contradictory situations are often tempered by how willing they are to accept divergent sentiment and how comfortable they are with the existence of duality in their situating environment. Studies on consumer behavior have extensively examined duality tolerance, its antecedents, and its consequences (DeMotta et al., 2016). A parsimonious construct, duality tolerance is rooted in higher-order attributes such as religion or philosophical orientations. As a result, whereas traits such as uncertainty avoidance, individualism, and indulgence tend to vary from country to country, duality tolerance is a higher-order cultural trait that may encompass several countries or communities (DeMotta et al., 2016; Janssens et al., 2007). Prior research has shown that one's acceptance of duality is predominantly shaped by one's situating culture, which determines one's tendency to be comfortable with the coexistence of contradictory elements and to accept them as dual elements (Williams & Aaker, 2002).

The overarching perspective that determines duality tolerance for a community is whether a culture is influenced by Confucian and Buddhist philosophies or by Enlightenment and Christian ones (Peng & Nisbett, 1999; Williams & Aaker, 2002). As a construct describing the aggregate behaviors of individuals in a community, duality tolerance suggests that cultures influenced by Confucian or Buddhist philosophies perceive reality to be more flexible than cultures influenced by Enlightenment or Christian philosophies (Peng & Nisbett, 1999; Williams & Aaker, 2002). While Western communities (such as North American or Western European cultures) are generally grounded in Aristotelian logic, Eastern communities tend to be grounded in Confucian or Buddhist logics. The lower propensity to accept duality in Western communities is based on three duality-inhibiting Aristotelian principles: (1) the law of identity, (2) the law of noncontradiction, and (3) the law of the excluded middle (Peng & Nisbett, 1999; Rothbaum & Tsang, 1998). Conversely, Eastern communities grounded in Confucian or Buddhist principles perceive reality to be more flexible owing to the peaceful synthesis of contradicting elements embedded in their cultural principles.

Each culture's principles lay the foundation for its community's tolerance for possible contradictions, and the differences between Eastern and Western handling of contradiction are reflected in various contexts—for instance, in how dialectical proverbs are respectively used in contemporary Chinese writing and American writing (Briley et al., 2000). The proclivity to perceive and accept duality also extends to divergent sentiment. For example, American culture views “love” as associated with a single feeling, either positive or negative, while Chinese culture accepts “love” as a simultaneous combination of both positive and negative sentiment (Rothbaum & Tsang, 1998). In a similar vein, the concept of duality tolerance can also be extended to the simultaneous experiencing of incongruent expressions of sentiment (Williams & Aaker, 2002). Prior studies suggest that experiencing divergent and incongruent sentiment elicits less favorable attitudes from individuals who have a lower proclivity to accept duality than it does from individuals who have a higher tolerance for duality (Williams & Aaker, 2002).

Focusing on this overarching cultural trait can help us to ascertain heterogeneities that might exist in terms of how different communities of SM users react to sentiment divergence in multimodal posts (through their engagement behaviors) as a form of duality. Thus, we posit that an individual's proclivity to accept duality, which is shaped at the level of the individual's community, is germane to understanding how sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts influences his or her engagement behaviors. Integrating duality tolerance in our study is appropriate because it enables a new and contextually relevant dimension of online communities that goes beyond previously examined cultural traits (e.g., individualism or uncertainty avoidance) to be fully captured.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

## 3.1 Conceptualizing Sentiment Divergence

As discussed in the previous sections, SM users prefer consistency in the SM content they access, including in terms of whether there is sentiment divergence between the image and text of a multimodal SM post. Accordingly, to better understand the relationship between sentimentally inconsistent SM posts and follower engagement, we first conceptualized the central construct of sentiment divergence in relation to multimodal SM posts on Instagram (our research context).

On image-sharing SM platforms such as Instagram, users generally encounter a multimodal SM post's image before its accompanying text. This is mostly because multimodal SM posts are laid out in a way that makes the visual aspect of the post the more “eye-catching” element of the content (see examples in Figure A1). Indeed, in a pre-analysis online survey-based quasi-experimental study (described in Appendix C), we recorded the interaction of more than 170 Instagram users with randomly presented SM posts comprising different combinations of divergent image and text sentiment. The findings of this pre-study test indicated that in all categories of multimodal SM posts, regardless of the sentiment expressed in the image or the text, the image attracted users’ initial attention. More than 92% of our respondents indicated that the image of the presented SM posts captured their attention first and with more saliency. Given that multimodal SM posts are designed as unified content, users’ initial perception of the post is therefore likely to be formed by the sentiment embedded in the image. This initial perception is followed by the sentiment derived from the text, which may or may not diverge from the sentiment the user detected in the initially observed image. Our rationale for considering image sentiment as the frame of reference for sentiment divergence in the case of an image-based platform such as Instagram is supported through several other mechanisms as well.

First, according to saliency bias, users' understanding of multimodal SM posts relies primarily on the post's most salient cue (Chen et al., 2022). By design, images are the most prominent part of an Instagram post, meaning that users' initial awareness and perception of posts on the platform are likely to be formed through them (Dellarocas et al., 2015).

Second, literature on psychology and visual attention provides evidence that visual cues attract individuals' attention more quickly and more strongly than textual cues (Peschel & Orquin, 2013). This effect is further magnified when the visual surface area of the content is increased—such as larger picture dimensions in multimodal posts. Therefore, given the saliency of image content in Instagram's post design, the increased size of SM posts' images draws the user's attention more quickly and more strongly (Dellarocas et al., 2015). Complementing the saliency of visual attention, neuroscience research shows that visual content is processed up to 600 times faster than text is. For instance, Potter et al. (2014) and Hauk et al. (2006) demonstrated that the human brain can process images in as little as 13 milliseconds, with complex visual analysis taking under 150 milliseconds. This is significantly quicker than the 3-8 seconds needed to comprehend a simple 25-word sentence. Thus, images not only attract initial attention faster on SM platforms but are also processed more quickly and easily than text.

Third, prior literature on imagery's effect on user behavior has suggested that the “image superiority effect” exists for both immediate and delayed recall of visual content. In settings where both image and text cues are present, the image superiority effect directs lasting attention toward the image and increases recall of the image content (Childers & Houston, 1984).

Accordingly, we premise our theorization regarding the sentiment divergence construct on the assumption that the image of a multimodal SM post is what users' attention initially focuses on and so functions as the basis for the post's overall initial sentiment. Utilizing the theoretical foundation of priming and anchoring effects (Dennis et al., 2020), we thus suggest that the image in a multimodal SM post primes users' perception of the post's overarching sentiment, which is anchored in the image. In turn, the interpretation of the sentiment conveyed by the text either converges with or diverges from the initial sentiment anchor—that is, the image. Any divergence between the initially anchored image sentiment and the subsequent caption sentiment can result in the perception of sentiment divergence for users. Moreover, in terms of its direction, the divergence can be either positive or negative: if the text conveys a more positive (or less negative) sentiment than the image, the multimodal SM post will contain positive sentiment divergence, whereas if the text expresses a less positive (or more negative) sentiment than the image, the multimodal SM post will contain negative sentiment divergence. These different combinations of image and text sentiment in multimodal SM posts form the magnitude and direction of our central construct, namely sentiment divergence (Figure 1). $^{3}$

Accordingly, our proposed construct of sentiment divergence effectively captures the existence of sentiment divergence between the image and text sentiment of multimodal SM posts. It encompasses the entire range of sentiment differences that an SM user might perceive in multimodal SM posts, and it can account for both significant and minor sentiment divergence in them. Regardless of whether the divergence is high or low in magnitude or positive or negative in direction, its presence is perceived and observed by followers when they interact with multimodal SM posts. Additionally, our construct incorporates the baseline for sentiment comparison, which is empirically and theoretically grounded, first and foremost, in users perceiving the post's image. Therefore, using sentiment divergence as our central construct allows us to effectively capture the gap between image and text sentiment within an SM post, as well as the potential inconsistency that divergent sentiment may create. This occurs when sentiment does not match across the image and the text, thereby disrupting the expectation of a narrative that is unified in terms of sentiment.

## 3.2 Influence of Sentiment Divergence on Follower Engagement

## 3.2.1 Magnitude of Sentiment Divergence and Follower Engagement

Our hypotheses leverage expectation disconfirmation theory (EDT) (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Brown et al., 2011; Oliver, 1977) to delineate the influence that the sentiment divergence contained in influencers' SM posts has on two follower-engagement behaviors: liking and commenting.

EDT provides a valuable lens for understanding the influence of discrepancies between users' expectations and experiences in various contexts (Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010). According to EDT, users' behavior as an outcome of their interaction with external focal objects is formed through their prior expectations, ex post experience, and subsequent disconfirmation caused by the mismatch between their expectations and experience. In the context of SM platforms, users have an established pre-experience expectation of observing emotional consistency in SM content (Choi et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2019). The disconfirmation of this expectation may alter their engagement behaviors with emotionally divergent SM content (Li & Cho, 2022). $^{4}$

Specifically, followers expect consistency in an SM post's content (Li & Cho, 2022; Zhao et al., 2019), including in terms of the sentiment the post expresses. If the sentiment expressed through the text of a multimodal SM post diverges from the sentiment expressed in the post's image, a follower interacting with the post may experience a state of disconfirmation from his or her initial consistency expectation (Zhao et al., 2019).

To alleviate the undesirable feeling created by this expectation disconfirmation, followers might collectively engage less with the sentimentally divergent SM post, just as SM content viewership might fall in other SM settings. Indeed, prior research has highlighted that SM users tend to avoid further interactions (for example, liking or commenting) with incongruent content in SM posts once they have detected its incongruence (Brown et al., 2014; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010; Wang & Song, 2020). This observation aligns with EDT propositions and how disconfirmed expectations may lead to disengagement from a focal object or event (McKinney et al., 2002). Hence, SM users may disengage with content that is imbued with incongruent information. Similarly, the divergence of sentiment across the image and text of an SM post might lead to decreased follower engagement with the content. Moreover, other ways in which followers express dissatisfaction, such as unfollowing the creator of the sentimentally divergent content, ultimately amount to a decrease in engagement with the post. Therefore, when a multimodal SM post contains an image and text whose sentiment diverges, followers' interest in interacting with the post may decrease. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1: In the context of multimodal SM posts, the magnitude of the sentiment divergence between an image and the accompanying text is negatively associated with (a) the number of likes, and (b) the number of comments that the post receives.

## 3.2.2 Direction of Sentiment Divergence and Follower Engagement

Although both positive and negative forms of sentiment divergence disconfirm followers' consistency expectations, the gravity of the negative effect of sentiment divergence in SM posts on follower engagement might be moderated by the direction of the divergence. Our theorization for the moderating effect of the direction of sentiment divergence is built upon improving or deteriorating the anchored image sentiment through the caption sentiment. This is different from positive and negative expectation disconfirmation in other contexts—for instance, service quality, in which positive and negative directions are defined in terms of improvement or deterioration of the “initial expectation.” In our study, both positive and negative sentiment divergence disconfirms the initial consistency expectation, yet the positivity and negativity of the direction may moderate the effect of the disconfirmed sentiment consistency expectation in the SM post.

Our argument for this differential influence is based on negativity bias theory (NBT), which states that positive and negative events, sentiment, or other forms of stimuli have different effects on individuals' psychological states, such that even when they are exposed to the same intensity of stimulus, individuals are more influenced by negative stimulus than they are by positive stimulus (Rozin & Royzman, 2001; Yang et al., 2019). Consistent with the tenets of NBT (Rozin & Royzman, 2001), individuals are less sensitive to positive states of divergence than they are to negative ones, and they may even evaluate positive states of divergence favorably (Rozin & Royzman, 2001; Yin et al., 2014). Moreover, prior empirical evidence suggests that positive divergence might evoke a concurrent experience of delight and satisfaction in individuals (Brown et al., 2014; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010). Therefore, in the case of positive sentiment divergence, the more positive (or less negative) sentiment of the text relative to the initially anchored sentiment of the image may create a sense of satisfaction that attenuates the negative influence of the focal SM post's sentiment divergence. Because of these two opposing impacts of positive sentiment divergence, we posit that the positive influence of the positive sentiment attenuates the negative impact that disconfirming the expectation of sentiment consistency has on followers' engagement.

On the other hand, prior evidence suggests that negative divergence and deterioration of the initial experience create psychological distress for individuals, which is exacerbated by a higher sensitivity to negative losses (Brown et al., 2014; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010). Negative sentiment divergence can create an unfavorable alteration of a follower's anchored experience of the content's sentiment, and the unfavorability of the alteration is aggravated if the alteration is negative. For instance, when followers see a caption whose sentiment is divergent from that of the image and more negative than that of the image, the caption has a twofold impact on their anchored experience: they experience divergence, which is negative in itself, and the fact that the divergence is negative worsens this effect. In contrast to cases where the positivity of the sentiment divergence ameliorates the negative influence of the sentiment divergence, the negativity of the sentiment divergence can significantly increase its negative influence on followers' engagement with an SM post. $^{5}$ Thus:

H2: In the context of multimodal SM posts, the decrease in follower engagement associated with negative sentiment divergence is higher than the decrease in follower engagement associated with the same magnitude of positive sentiment divergence in terms of (a) the number of likes, and (b) the number of comments that the post receives.

## 3.2.3 Duality Tolerance Orientation of the Situating Community and Follower Engagement

The mechanism through which sentiment divergence influences follower engagement might not be uniform across communities (Janssens et al., 2007). The duality-tolerance orientation of the followers' situating community determines the extent to which that community can perceive and tolerate divergent and incongruent sentiment (Williams & Aaker, 2002). Members of communities that are more accepting of duality are less influenced by situations imbued with incongruent or even contradictory sentiment (Peng & Nisbett, 1999; Williams & Aaker, 2002). Therefore, although individual heterogeneities may exist, we expect aggregate followers from duality-tolerant communities to react differently to sentiment divergence compared to followers from duality-intolerant communities (Hong & Lee, 2010). We posit that followers who are more accepting of duality will perceive an SM post's sentiment divergence as a close representation of reality rather than as something distressing (Hong & Lee, 2010; Williams & Aaker, 2002). By contrast, followers from duality-intolerant communities will perceive the sentiment divergence as distressing and, to reduce their psychological discomfort, will reduce their engagement with the divergent stimulus. Thus, we hypothesize that a community's acceptance of duality moderates the influence of sentiment divergence on follower engagement with multimodal SM posts. Hence:

H3: In the context of multimodal SM posts, the duality tolerance of the situating community positively moderates the negative association between sentiment divergence and follower engagement in terms of (a) the number of likes, and (b) the number of comments that the post receives.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Data Collection

Boasting a substantial user base, Instagram, the setting of our study, is one of the most prominent multimedia-sharing platforms for SM influencers (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022; Hughes et al., 2019). According to recent global statistics, there are more than 64 million influencers on Instagram, indicating just how important they are to the platform. Therefore, Instagram offers a promising context for examining how sentiment divergence in influencers' multimodal SM posts impacts follower engagement.

Our data-collection process leveraged the analytics provided by HypeAuditor, an influencer information aggregator. We randomly selected a set of verified human influencers from HypeAuditor across various sectors on Instagram—for instance, beauty and fashion—and excluded virtual influencers. We retained macro-influencers from HypeAuditor's top 100 list whose primary profession was SM influencer, and we added similar micro- and macro-influencers using suggestions that Instagram made based on the influencers we had already selected. Following prior studies (Klostermann et al., 2018), we gathered publicly available data from over 30,000 SM posts created by 65 verified and randomly selected influencers, focusing on nonpersonalized aggregate engagement metrics. $^{6}$

To increase the accuracy of our assessment of image sentiment, we eliminated SM posts that contained multiple photos or whose image contained multiple human subjects. The trimmed dataset comprised 24,658 SM posts. Out of these, we observed that only 1,347 SM posts expressed similar sentiment across their image and text, which anecdotally indicated the prevalence (95% of the sampled multimodal SM posts) of the expression of divergent sentiment. This reinforced the practical relevance of our inquiry into how and what varied combinations of image and text sentiment might impact follower engagement with influencers' SM posts.

## 4.2 Measurement

## 4.2.1 Dependent Variables

We used an SM post's number of likes and number of comments as dependent variables to measure follower engagement with influencers' posts. This choice has been widely adopted in SM studies (Karahanna et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022). To accurately measure follower engagement, we utilized organic engagement metrics that distinguished between likes and comments from an account's followers and total likes and comments from both followers and non-followers. We excluded non-follower engagement, allowing us to control for any alternative mechanisms that could bias our main hypothesized effects—for instance, algorithmic post promotion and external events.

## 4.2.2 Independent Variables

We employed three independent variables: sentiment divergence magnitude, sentiment divergence negativity, and duality tolerance of the follower community. The independent variables of sentiment divergence magnitude and sentiment divergence negativity describe the magnitude and direction of our sentiment divergence construct. To operationalize the sentiment divergence magnitude and sentiment divergence negativity variables, we first extracted image and text sentiment from each of the SM posts, using logically comparable approaches for both sources of sentiment to do so (Figure 3).

Given that image and text are inherently different forms of content, no single machine-based method can measure both image and text sentiment. And human perception, which is the basis of ground truth for many social science studies, processes image and text in separate hemispheres of the brain. Comparing image sentiment and textual sentiment is therefore, by nature, a daunting endeavor. To address this challenge, our study relies on the procedural logic of sentiment analysis in the textual content. Specifically, the measurement of sentiment in unimodal content, such as textual posts, has predominantly relied on a process comprising two steps: (1) identification of positive/negative emotional cues, and (2) aggregation of these cues.

In the first phase, we extracted the embedded positive/negative emotional cues from each post's image using Microsoft Azure and from its associated text using the text2emotion Python library. The Computer Vision REST API of Microsoft Azure is built on a convolutional neural network method proposed by Yu and Zhang (2015), which generates weights for various facial emotional cues. Recent studies such as Zhou et al. (2021) have established the accuracy and reliability of this image-processing service for assessing facial expressions and detecting content (Choudhury et al., 2019; Li & Xie, 2020). Next, following the second step of the sentiment analysis literature, we aggregated the emotional cues for identifying the same discrete emotions (happiness, surprise, anger, fear, and sadness) to make the image and text further comparable in their expressed emotions. Therefore, we calculated scores for the facial and textual emotions of happiness, surprise, anger, fear, and sadness.

![](/api/attachments/RG7W8AV8/fulltext/images/1c809cf6913c80105357325fde4e16fdcd26e330dd6bb055fcdb0d8e381a6625.jpg)  
Figure 3. Operationalization of the Sentiment Divergence Construct

Next, we selected the most salient emotion in each image and text as representative of the overall sentiment it conveyed to users. We did so for several reasons. First, research suggests that in mainly hedonistic, less cognitively intense activities such as consuming Instagram content, individuals' experience is primarily influenced by the most salient emotion (Treue, 2003). Therefore, we assume that, unlike a context such as online review platforms, which are intended to impart information, Instagram's hedonistic nature encourages users to focus on the prominent emotions expressed in images and text. This assumption is consistent with visual selective attention theory, which states that individuals pay attention to the stimulus (emotion) that is the most expressive and not to other accompanying stimuli (Peschel & Orquin, 2013; Treue, 2003). Second, this method is preferable to alternatives such as total emotion aggregation because it sidesteps biases that could lead to inaccurate sentiment scores in posts with divergent emotions—returning zero aggregate emotion, for instance. $^{7}$ We thus adopted Roseman's (1984) theoretical structure to classify the most expressive sentiment as positive (e.g., happiness), negative (e.g., anger, fear, and sadness), or neutral (e.g., surprise). This method established the magnitude and the direction (positive/negative) of the image and text sentiment.

Finally, we calculated the sentiment divergence magnitude variable as the magnitude of the difference between the image sentiment and the caption sentiment of the focal SM post. We calculated the sentiment divergence negativity dummy variable of each SM post in the dataset by comparing the post's image sentiment with its caption sentiment. Sentiment divergence negativity indicates the direction of the divergence and is 1 if the caption sentiment is more negative (or less positive) than the image sentiment, and 0 otherwise.

We defined the duality tolerance dummy variable as our third independent variable. This variable took the value of 1 for SM posts from influencers situated in duality-tolerant communities and the value of 0 for SM posts from influencers situated in duality-intolerant communities.

To operationalize duality tolerance, we adopted the identification strategy of Williams and Aaker (2002), identifying whether our influencers belonged to Eastern or Western cultural backgrounds. This methodology is well supported by consumer behavior literature (Briley et al., 2000; Peng & Nisbett, 1999) as a valid approach for measuring duality tolerance. Duality tolerance, which we defined based on prior studies' definitions (Williams & Aaker, 2002), is a macrosocietal trait that is rooted in societies belonging to Western or Eastern religious and philosophical orientations. Therefore, by identifying which cultural backgrounds corresponded to our influencers and their followers—that is, being Eastern or Western—we determined their overarching duality tolerance orientations. Williams and Aaker (2002), among others, depicted the effectiveness of this approach to identifying the duality tolerance of individuals and their situated communities. Therefore, we assigned an influencer from a Western cultural background (e.g., an American or French influencer) a duality tolerance score of 0 and an influencer from an Eastern cultural background (e.g., an Indian or Chinese influencer) a duality tolerance score of 1. This assignment reflected the macrolevel cultural distinctions that influence consumer behavior and engagement on social media. To determine the Eastern or Western classification of our influencers, we followed the approach employed by Medappa and Srivastava (2020) and used influencers' nationality and residency location from their verified Instagram bios to confirm their cultural background.

In the next step, consistent with the principle of network homophily (McPherson et al., 2001; Song et al., 2019), we deemed each influencer's network of followers to have the same duality tolerance as the influencer. There are several compelling reasons for this measurement approach. First, extensive prior research has demonstrated that similarity among individuals in a social network significantly shapes their connections within that network (Song et al., 2019). This similarity not only influences the formation of connections such as users' following similar-minded influencers but also correlates with similarity in engagement behaviors (Adamopoulos et al., 2018). Such behavioral mirroring within networks further underscores the central role of shared traits and preferences between followers and the influencers they choose to follow. The interactions and engagement that unfold between influencers and their followers serve to reinforce and perpetuate each other's cultural norms and values (Cao & Belo, 2024; Chung et al., 2023), thereby contributing to a cohesive and shared cultural identity.

Second, influencers wield substantial power over their followers' behavior, which in turn significantly shapes followers' beliefs and worldviews (Chung et al., 2023; Farivar et al., 2021). This influence extends to aspects such as duality tolerance, with followers often adopting and reflecting the attitudes and values promoted by the influencers they follow. Therefore, the persuasive power of influencers in reinforcing specific cultural values further reiterates why followers and influencers are likely to share similar duality tolerance preferences.

Third, it is well established that SM influencers typically attract followers who share similar values and cultural backgrounds, which is evident in their content creation and consumption preferences (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022). This selective affinity reinforces the homogeneity of values within an influencer's follower community, further contributing to their tolerance of duality.

For these reasons, our assumption that the duality tolerance score is shared by both the influencer and their followers is theoretically well supported. Nevertheless, we also conducted several empirical robustness tests to support our identification procedure. First, in a survey-based quasi-experimental study described in Appendix C, we asked our respondents to indicate whether or not they prefer to follow SM influencers from the same cultural background. We observed that the majority of our respondents (more than 90%) indicated that they prefer to follow SM influencers who are culturally similar to themselves. Second, we performed a community detection analysis on a subset of our dataset, and this further supported our assumption of homophily between influencers and their followers (Appendix D). This test indicated that followers belonged to segregated communities of influencers and all influencers in our dataset had their unique set of followers. This further supports the homophily between influencers and their followers in each community. Third, we performed a two-sample clustered t-test on the cultural dimensions of the influencers' subgroups, which indicated clear differences in the cultural dimensions of the duality-tolerant and duality-intolerant subgroups of influencers. Taken together, although individual differences might exist at a user-level identification, our community-based measure of duality tolerance effectively measures the aggregate duality orientation of both the influencer and their followers.

## 5 Results

The results from Table 3 (please see Appendix F for detailed results) show that our control variables are well chosen, as is indicated by their significant coefficients in Models 1 and 5. From Models 2 and 6, we find that sentiment divergence magnitude is negatively related to follower engagement in terms of number of likes (-0.126, p < 0.01) and comments (-0.182, p < 0.01). These results support H1a and H1b.

Our results further indicate that negative sentiment divergence reduces follower engagement, with a decreased number of likes (-0.132, p < 0.01) and comments (-0.137, p < 0.01), per Models 3 and 7. The interaction of sentiment divergence magnitude and direction in Models 4 and 8 shows negative cases of sentiment divergence have a stronger negative impact on number of likes (-0.225, p < 0.01) and comments (-0.294, p < 0.01). By contrast, positive sentiment divergence shows no significant marginal effects. These results support H2a and H2b. However, positive sentiment divergence does not negatively impact follower engagement, suggesting an interesting difference in the effects of positive and negative divergence.

## 5.1.1 Validity of the Sentiment Divergence Construct and Comparability of Image and Text Sentiment

To validate the sentiment divergence construct, first, we considered the varying salience of image and text sentiment in multimodal SM posts. The images of influencers' SM posts differed in terms of their compositional elements: Some had simple backgrounds and featured only the individual's face, whereas other

images contained various items. The literature suggests that user attention to facial emotions can be affected by the presence of non-face objects in images (Cordel et al., 2019). This effect can bias the measurement of image sentiment in a study such as ours. Similarly, the readability of the textual content in multimodal SM posts may differ from one post to another, which can further bias the perception of text sentiment. To systematically address these potential biases, we accounted for the weights of image and text sentiment within the sentiment divergence construct (see Appendix G for details). The results of this additional test indicate that after considering existing image objects and text readability, our main results remained consistent.

## 5.1.2 Validity of the Theorized Effects of Sentiment Divergence on Follower Engagement

To validate our theorized effects, we conducted a falsification test of our main findings for sentiment divergence. The presence of both visual and textual sources of sentiment is essential for the formation of sentiment divergence, which in turn affects follower engagement with multimodal SM posts. If one of these sources is missing or turned into another format (e.g., the image is replaced with text), the influence of sentiment divergence on follower engagement should not be observed. In line with what the literature widely accepts (Medappa & Srivastava, 2019), our falsification test refuted the existence of sentiment divergence in contexts other than multimodal SM posts. We performed the falsification test by using a dataset of Instagram posts' with image files consisting entirely of text on a plain background. The results of this test (Appendix K) did not align with our main findings, supporting the validity of our theorized effects of sentiment divergence on follower engagement.

Next, we performed an additional test that omitted small sentiment divergences as potential measurement errors in the sentiment divergence variable. We did so primarily because such divergence might not be perceivable to followers or might be created by our methodological approaches. We thus omitted the SM posts with sentiment divergence smaller than the 10th percentile of the absolute values of the sentiment divergence magnitude variable and reran the main analysis (Appendix L). The results of this test yielded effects identical to those found in the main analysis.

Our next test was designed to address the possibility that our identification of sentiment divergence might perceive all divergence as contradictory. In situations where sentiment divergence results from image and text sentiment of the same valence (either both positive or negative), the divergence could instead form an overall reinforcing pattern rather than a contradicting one. To address this, we created a subgroup comprising the dataset's SM posts whose image and text had the same valence—that is, the sentiment of one reinforced that of the other—and tested our hypotheses again. The results of this test closely replicated our main findings and refuted the concern about reinforcing sentiment divergence (Appendix M).

Lastly, we accounted for the varied nature of liking and commenting engagement behaviors because one may argue that while liking is a binary choice, commenting may enable more nuanced expression of positive and negative sentiment. Therefore, while the hypothesized negative effect on liking is valid, commenting may be affected differently through an alternative mechanism of followers expressing dissatisfaction with SM posts—that is, collectively leaving more comments that express a negative sentiment. To examine this, we tested whether sentiment divergence influenced the average sentiment polarity of comments on SM posts (please see Appendix N). Results indicated that sentiment divergence does not prompt followers to express negative comments.

## 5.1.3 Endogeneity and Causality of the Identified Effects

We addressed endogeneity in our econometric model using the control function approach (Ferreira et al., 2019), identifying the influencers' age and gender indicated in their SM post images as candidate instruments. These independent variables influence sentiment expression in facial expressions (Fan et al., 2021; McConatha et al., 1994). However, no previous studies have established that an influencer's age or gender is linked to the engagement level of their followers. Hence, these influencer-level independent variables are connected to the emotional expression in images (and thus sentiment divergence) but not to follower engagement. We thus used our image-processing algorithm to extract age and gender as our independent variables and confirmed their validity for our study (please see Appendix O). The control function analyses provided consistent results with our main findings, indicating that our results are exogenous.

Lastly, we countered potential strategic content manipulation by SM influencers, which might challenge the exogeneity of our sentiment divergence construct. Influencers might deliberately design content with certain sentiment divergence attributes because they have previously had success in using these. We conducted a Granger causality test to determine whether image sentiment, caption sentiment, and sentiment divergence from previous posts influenced these variables in future content (Peng et al., 2022). The results showed no such effect and robustly refuted the possibility of strategic content manipulation in our dataset (please see Appendix P).

## 6 Limitations

Despite the significant contributions of our study, we acknowledge several limitations that suggest fertile avenues for future research. While our theorization is robust at the aggregate level of followers' behavior, there exist individual heterogeneities in our research context that warrant further investigation in more controlled research designs. These include users' repeated exposure to specific SM posts, changes in attitudes, and divergent engagement behaviors rather than mere disengagement with divergent posts. Instagram's rapid content dissemination and its design feature of removing posts a user has already interacted with from their feed make repeated engagements with the same content unlikely at the aggregate level of followers. However, direct visits to an influencer's profile could lead to repeated exposure, an area our dataset does not address but is worth exploring. Additionally, while followers' attitudes towards influencers can evolve over time, significant shifts in followers' sentiment that may arise from recurring sentiment divergence in the influencer's SM posts are more likely to prompt followers to unfollow these influencers. This suggests that attitude adjustments are closely tied to relationship status between followers and influencers, which is a promising avenue for future research. Our use of an archival dataset also limits our ability to examine herding or similar social learning behaviors that may have biased the engagement metrics we observed. We believe that these behaviors are relevant in many contexts—for example, advertising or marketing SM content. However, given that followers typically engage with influencers out of personal admiration for these individuals (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022), we believe that their engagement is likely to be driven more by personal affinity than by others' actions. Nonetheless, exploring how social learning behaviors impact engagement dynamics presents a promising area for future inquiry.

Methodologically, there exist additional points that could be improved in future research. Although we strove for robust comparisons between image and text sentiment, inherent differences between image and text content may affect comparability. Future research could adopt more comprehensive approaches using human interpretations of image and text sentiment. Additionally, users' perceptions of image and text sentiment might involve complex and intuitive processes that our methodology may not fully capture, such as perceiving sentiment divergence as complementary patterns of divergence. This complexity offers a fruitful direction for future studies. Our measure of duality tolerance also focuses on communities rather than on individuals. While this approach is robust for community-level analysis, future research could examine individual-level duality tolerance for a more nuanced understanding. Exploring sentiment divergence in other contexts or media, such as firm-generated content or videos, could also expand our understanding of sentiment divergence as a future extension of this study. Our study centers on the influencer-follower relationship on Instagram and the effect of sentiment divergence on user engagement. Future research should investigate other types of relationships on different social media platforms such as X (formerly Twitter) and TikTok to broaden the insights offered by our study.

## 7 Implications

## 7.1 Implications for Theory

Our study offers several significant theoretical contributions to the literature. We introduce and analyze the construct of sentiment divergence through a context-specific theorization process (Hong et al., 2013; Johns, 2006). By focusing on the magnitude and direction of sentiment differences between images and text captions, our dual consideration of defining properties of sentiment divergence allows for a complete understanding of how sentiment varies within a post. Our theorization recognizes that sentiment differences can range from subtle to significant and can be either positive or negative—properties that are less reflected and adaptable to information consistency in SM posts (Li & Cho, 2022). As such, sentiment divergence moves beyond traditional information (in)consistency measures in multimodal posts (e.g., Ha et al., 2020) by capturing nuanced properties that may not have been fully captured in prior research (Huo, 2015; van Rompay et al., 2010).

Specifically, unlike binary information (in)consistency measures (i.e., SM posts or content being consistent or inconsistent) (Koh et al., 2022), sentiment divergence offers a continuous spectrum that encapsulates its defining properties of magnitude and direction of sentiment differences. Continuity of this construct means that even minor variances in the sentiment of multimodal posts can be identified and analyzed, acknowledging their potential impact on user engagement and how users might perceive and react differently to low or high magnitudes of differences. Sentiment divergence further establishes a framework that enables a comparison between multiple sources of sentiment in a focal content based on how users perceive multimodal content (Li & Xie, 2020)—for instance, which content is perceived first or is weighed more than the other. Moreover, sentiment divergence better reflects users' psychological processes when engaging with SM posts (Karahanna et al., 2015, 2018), better aligning with their interaction patterns on these platforms.

These nuanced features thus underscore the value of sentiment divergence as a novel construct that complements existing informational (in)consistency perspectives (Ha et al., 2020; Klostermann et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2019). Recent studies such as Li and Cho (2022) and Wang and Song (2020) have emphasized the critical role of information (in)consistency for successful SM posts. We build on these findings by emphasizing that the convergence or divergence of image and text sentiment in multimodal SM posts is also crucial for their effectiveness (Klostermann et al., 2018) and perhaps more complex than the dichotomous consistency/inconsistency of SM posts' information. Our study further complements prior research by demonstrating that not all kinds of sentiment divergence impact content performance negatively (Ha et al., 2020).

As one of our core theoretical lenses, we further contribute to the EDT literature in several ways. Our study extends EDT by highlighting the role of emotional or affective expectations of SM users rather than focusing solely on cognitive or performance-based expectations as in prior studies (Bhattacherjee, 2001; McKinney et al., 2002). Moreover, unlike traditional EDT constructs, which often deal with binary confirmation/disconfirmation effects (Oliver, 1977), sentiment divergence presents a continuous effect of affective disconfirmation on users' online behavior. This perspective allows for a more nuanced understanding of emerging disconfirmation from affective divergence in SM content. Moreover, we introduce duality tolerance as a novel antecedent in the expectation disconfirmation process (Williams & Aaker, 2002), showing how cultural factors influence users' responses to disconfirmed expectations.

We further contribute to the use and understating of NBT in SM studies. Specifically, we introduce a secondary layer of negativity effect through sentiment divergence that complements the effect of overall negativity/positivity in SM posts (Stieglitz & Dang-

Xuan, 2013). Our theorization suggests that the negativity of sentiment divergence is independent of the negativity or positivity of the image or text, indicating a joint effect of visual and textual stimuli that is more complex than single positive or negative events considered in traditional NBT (Kanouse & Hanson Jr, 1987; Rozin & Royzman, 2001). By demonstrating that the relationship between image and text negativity is reshaped through divergence, we show that the negativity dominance proposed in original NBT propositions, i.e., the whole (image and text in SM post) being more negative than the sum of its part, becomes less salient. Sentiment divergence reshapes the overall perception of the post, where the divergence between elements significantly influences user perceptions of the whole positive/negative combinations of images and texts (Rozin & Royzman, 2001). By introducing the potential influence of a secondary negativity effect, our work thus extends NBT by accounting for complex interactions in multimodal content where the congruence and coherence between elements are as important as the sum of the elements themselves.

Regarding the broader literature on SM user engagement (Cao & Belo, 2024; Yang et al., 2019), our study introduces duality tolerance as a cultural moderator of SM engagement behaviors. Following previous research on the cultural determinants of SM behaviors (Hong et al., 2016), we propose the acceptance of duality as a new factor rooted in individuals' cultural backgrounds (Janssens et al., 2007) and extend its relevance to the sociotechnical context of SM influencers and follower engagement with multimedia posts. This novel perspective offers insights into how individuals synthesize and accept varied elements in online settings and how such cultural preferences influence their subsequent behaviors (Zheng et al., 2021).

Lastly, our study opens avenues for future research directions. While our work adopts a variance theory perspective, examining sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts can be expanded through additional perspectives. Future research could investigate the antecedents of sentiment divergence, such as content creators' moods, emotions, creative styles, cultural influences, and strategic communication choices, and explain why and how sentiment divergence may arise in SM posts. Understanding these antecedents is crucial for developing strategies to prevent or leverage sentiment divergence to enhance follower engagement. Moreover, further studies could explore sentiment divergence through configurational perspectives. Examining specific combinations of factors influencing the impact of sentiment divergence, such as content-creator pairings, audience characteristics such as expertise and need for cognition, or platform dynamics such as image or text dominance, may provide further insights into how divergent sentiment in SM posts may drive online behaviors. Research could also delve into process theory-building approaches—such as examining the temporal effects of sentiment divergence over time or exploring user interaction processes in interpreting and reacting to divergent sentiment in other media.

Additionally, sentiment divergence can be examined in different contextual applications, such as audio-textual sentiment divergence in music or sentiment divergence in videos and movies. It could also be valuable to explore its impact on behaviors beyond engagement—like willingness to pay in e-commerce settings, consumer perceptions, brand loyalty, or decision-making. Analyzing platform-specific considerations, such as how features unique to each SM platform interact with sentiment divergence to impact user behavior, can offer insights into platform-specific strategies. By considering these perspectives, future research can certainly expand the understanding of sentiment divergence and its implications across various contexts (Avgerou, 2019). Sentiment divergence thus lays the groundwork for a richer exploration of sentiment interactions in multimodal content within and beyond the SM context.

## 7.2 Implications for Practice

Our study offers several practical implications. Given that influencers are interested in enhancing their follower engagement, our study underscores the critical importance of strategic SM content creation, particularly in the multimedia-rich environment of modern SM platforms. Moving beyond the prior research insights that SM influencers can leverage on the impact of positive textual and visual sentiment on user engagement (Choudhury et al., 2019; Oh et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2019, 2022), we demonstrate that an appropriate combination of image and text sentiment within the same SM post fosters greater follower engagement. At a time when SM usage is transitioning towards more image-based content (Li & Xie, 2020), our findings can help influencers manage image-text sentiment divergence to create effective SM content. More specifically, our study underscores the salience of having congruent emotional expressions in visual and textual elements of multimodal SM posts. In addition, positive sentiment divergence emerges as an effective strategy in situations where divergent sentiment expression in multimodal posts is required.

Given the billion-dollar market size of SM influencers (Chung et al., 2023), even marginal improvements in follower engagement through sentimentally consistent posts can significantly boost the efficiency of SM content, translating into millions of dollars in increased revenue or avoided losses. Our findings on the varied effects of positive and negative sentiment divergence provide influencers with practical guidelines for crafting content that maximizes user engagement. Influencers often need to communicate on a variety of topics, including ones that require them to express negative sentiment—social causes, product reviews, or experiences within their domain of expertise, for instance. This task becomes more complex in the context of multimodal posts. However, our findings suggest that by maintaining positive sentiment divergence (or having no significant sentiment divergence) in their multimodal posts, influencers can foster user engagement. Taking this approach gives influencers the flexibility to design engaging content that does not compromise their follower engagement, even when they need to express negative sentiment in their posts.

Our results also underscore the importance—especially for SM influencers—of considering an online audience’s cultural background as an antecedent of its engagement behaviors (Sutanto et al., 2021). Although combining mixed sentiment in SM content can enhance message persuasiveness for some audiences (Hong & Lee, 2010; Janssens et al., 2007), our results make clear to influencers that intolerance for divergent sentiment among certain audience groups can undermine a message’s persuasiveness and followers’ subsequent engagement with it. By extending the concept of duality tolerance (Hong & Lee, 2010; Janssens et al., 2007) to the SM influencer setting, our findings emphasize the importance of considering its role in enhancing the effectiveness of expressing divergent sentiment in multimodal SM posts. Our findings exhort influencers to localize their messages based on the target audience’s cultural orientation (Oh et al., 2013). For instance, an Eastern influencer who wants to target a Western audience would need to be aware that that audience’s sentiment-divergence preferences may be different from their own when crafting posts targeting the audience. Such a consideration can also help to guide collaborative relationships between firms and influencers on SM platforms (Hughes et al., 2019; Mallipeddi et al., 2022). In response to the insights from our study, firms might partner up with influencers whose content creation styles align with the preferences of the firm’s target audience (Chung et al., 2023). For example, a firm targeting a duality-tolerant audience in East Asia might choose an influencer who is known for creating sentimentally divergent content that can attract attention and engagement from the target audience.

Finally, our study has implications for SM platforms, specifically in terms of their content-recommendation algorithms and innovative capabilities. By understanding the relationship between sentiment divergence and SM engagement, SM platforms can fine-tune their content-recommendation algorithms by prioritizing content with positive sentiment divergence or content that aligns with users' duality tolerance preferences. This can lead to more personalized and engaging user experiences, ultimately increasing user satisfaction and retention on the platform (Benlian, 2015). Furthermore, our study can guide SM platforms in enhancing the features of their multimodal content creation tools. Our results might prompt them, for example, to integrate pre-publication insights about content consistency measures into these tools, which would allow content creators to gauge a post's sentiment divergence before it is published.

## 8 Conclusion

The prevalence of multimodal posts on SM platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, and X (formerly Twitter) provides numerous possibilities for influencers to curate their professional content. However, whether varied combinations of textual and visual sources of content help in achieving the desired objective of increased follower engagement depends on a host of contextual factors. We contend that to successfully foster follower engagement, it is crucial to carefully consider the interplay of image and text sentiment within the same multimodal SM posts. Our research, grounded in users' sentiment consistency expectations and EDT, utilizes a large Instagram dataset on SM influencers to reveal the impact of sentiment divergence and duality tolerance on follower engagement with influencers' multimodal SM posts. Through our analysis of influencers on Instagram, we conclude that the presence of sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts negatively affects follower engagement. However, we further find that negative sentiment divergence, which arises from a text expressing a sentiment that is less positive (or more negative) than that of the image, results in a greater decrease in engagement compared to positive sentiment divergence. Emphasizing the impact of sentiment divergence's directionality, we observed that followers perceive positive sentiment divergence as an improvement on their initial perception of the SM post's sentiment. We further found that communities with high duality tolerance were not negatively affected by sentiment divergence and continued to engage with such SM posts. However, less tolerant communities were highly intolerant of sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts, leading to decreased engagement. Our results reiterate the need to consider the interplay between the image sentiment and text sentiment of multimodal SM content when it comes to catering to followers' needs and, therefore, encouraging them to engage with posts. They also demonstrate the importance of considering the complex role that cultural, social, and societal moderators of online behaviors play in driving SM engagement.

## Appendix C: Survey-Based Quasi-Experimental Study on Interaction with Multimodal SM Posts

In the context of our study, we implemented a survey-based quasi-experimental study directed at SM platform users, with the objective of validating two critical assumptions underpinning our research. Our first goal with this study was to validate the assumption that in a setting parallel to that of our research setting—namely, multimodal SM posts on Instagram—images are indeed the component of the posts that receive the initial attention. Our second goal was to confirm the homophily assumption, which posits a similarity between followers and influencers in terms of their cultural attributes and, consequently, their tolerance of sentiment divergence.

We deployed an online survey-based quasi-experimental study on Amazon Mechanical Turk to investigate these assumptions. The survey was accessible for approximately 35 days, and we amassed a total of 221 responses during this period. Following the exclusion of incomplete submissions, we retained a robust sample of 174 comprehensive responses. In terms of SM activity, our sample revealed widespread use of SM platforms (including Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, LinkedIn, etc.) and was predominantly composed of Instagram users, with over 81% of respondents having personal accounts on the platform. All participants reported substantial engagement with SM platforms. Around 91% of the respondents confirmed daily interaction with SM platforms, thereby establishing their familiarity and competency within these digital environments. This regular engagement underlines the relevance and applicability of their responses to our study.

Our participant demographics also showed a diverse representation. There were 95 male respondents, 77 female respondents, and two respondents who chose not to disclose their gender. We also noted cultural diversity, with respondents from various regions, including southern Asia, Confucian Asia, Arab countries, English-speaking countries, Latin America, Germanic Europe, Eastern Europe, Latin Europe, Nordic Europe, and Sub-Saharan Africa. The age range of our respondents spanned from 22 to 70 years, with an average age of approximately 41.93 and a standard deviation of 10.93.

Because our survey-based quasi-experimental study aimed to understand how users interact with multimodal SM posts, we provided participants with four sample Instagram posts (see Figure C1) and recorded their reactions to these. These posts' images and texts were generated using AI services (generated photos for images and ChatGPT for texts) and were attributed to a fictitious SM account. This measure was taken to prevent any potential confounders, such as the subject matter of the image, from influencing interaction with the posts. Once participants had answered some preliminary questions about their SM usage and the platforms they frequent, they were randomly assigned one of the four posts. Subsequently, they were asked which component of the post (image or text) grabbed their attention first. We also sought their input on their preference when it comes to following SM influencers, specifically inquiring whether cultural similarity plays a role in their decision to follow certain SM influencers.

Congruent with our theorization, an overwhelming $92\%$ of respondents reported that the image content in the sample SM posts was the first component to grab their attention. This strong inclination towards visual content affirms our assumption about its saliency effect and dominance in capturing user attention. This finding lends credence to our theorization of the sentiment divergence construct, suggesting that image sentiment serves as the reference measure for determining sentiment divergence direction, as it captures the user's attention first and is processed faster than textual content.

## Appendix D: Network Homophily and Community Detection for Duality-Tolerant and Duality-Intolerant Subgroups

To test our assumption of homophily between SM influencers and their followers, we performed community detection through a modularity index analysis of the network of influencers and their followers. According to the literature on complex network theory (Blondel et al., 2008; Newman, 2006), the modularity index analysis measures the extent to which a focal network is divided into modules (i.e., the community of nodes) by identifying the community of nodes that are characterized by strong interconnections in that focal network. Keeping the same number of nodes and the same degree for each node for a given network, the modularity index analysis compares the number of edges inside a cluster with the expected number of edges that would be observed in the same cluster if the network was random (Blondel et al., 2008; Newman, 2006).

We implemented this methodology for influencer-community detection in our network of influencer-followers. Accordingly, we extracted a unique subset of edge list relationships between influencers and followers through the followers' comments on the SM posts in our study's dataset. This subset was primarily selected because of the extensive size of the network of influencers-followers (more than 72 million edge list relationships in our dataset), which imposed a major computational challenge on our community detection analysis. Therefore, we opted for a randomly selected subset of influencer-follower edge lists. By extracting the directed influencer-follower edge list from comments on each SM post in our dataset, we accounted for unique relationships between influencers and their followers. The result was a network of 178,376 nodes with 179,664 edges for 17 randomly selected influencer accounts. We then utilized the community detection algorithm proposed by Blondel et al. (2008) to detect the influencer communities in our network dataset and its modularity index. Following prior IS research (Guo et al., 2016), the modularity analysis with a resolution of 1 resulted in 15 identified communities with a modularity score of 0.642. We found that three out of the 17 accounts were owned by Brooklyn-based influencers whose networks of followers were very similar, thus finding that all three accounts and their followers constituted one influencer-follower community. This resulted in 15 influencer communities out of 17 SM influencers. The identified communities showed that followers of the 17 influencers in our dataset uniquely belonged to only one of the highly clustered communities. Accounting for smaller communities through different network resolutions, both higher (resolution = 4, modularity = 0.640) and lower (resolution = 0.5, modularity = 0.642) resolutions found a similar number of influencer communities in our network. Figure D1 represents the network layout of the identified influencer communities in different colors.

The results of the modularity index analysis support our assumption of network homophily through the unique and highly clustered community of influencers-followers (McPherson et al., 2001; Reagans, 2010; Song et al., 2019). This provides robustness for our identification strategy of duality-tolerant and duality-intolerant subgroups and the homophily between influencers and their followers.

## Appendix G: Construct Validity: Scene Simplicity and Text Readability

Multimodal SM posts differ in terms of their visual content's compositional elements. Some images of influencers might have a plain background and feature only the human subject's face. Others might be crowded with objects. Followers' attention toward facial emotional stimuli can be influenced by the existence of objects other than the human face in the SM image post (Cordel et al., 2019). This can bias the measurement of image sentiment in studies such as ours. Similarly, the readability of the textual content in multimodal SM posts can also differ from one post to another, and this heterogeneity can further bias the perception of text sentiment in our study. Thus, it is necessary to systematically alleviate such biases within the sentiment divergence construct.

To this end, we performed an analysis that accounted for the weights of image and text sentiment within the sentiment divergence construct through two variables: scene simplicity and text readability. Scene simplicity, expressed through Equation (G1), captures the plainness of the image, taking into account the different embedded objects (Cordel et al., 2019). Similarly, text readability captures the readability of text through the normalized Flesch reading ease score (Berger et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2015), which we operationalized through Equation (G2). These variables are scaled between 0 and 1 as their lowest and highest values. Accordingly, we recalculated the magnitude and direction of sentiment divergence through weighted image and text sentiment and reestimated the main regression analysis. We also included the interaction effects between scene simplicity and image sentiment and those between text readability and caption sentiment.

s c e n e s i m p l i c i t y _ {i j} ^ {i m a g e} = \frac {1}{1 + N u m b e r o f O b j e c t s _ {i j}}\tag{G1}
$$

$$
t e x t r e a d a b i l i t y _ {i j} ^ {c a p t i o n} = \frac {F l e s c h S c o r e _ {i j}}{1 0 0}\tag{G2}
$$

## Appendix I: Robustness of the Proposed Sentiment Measurement Procedures and Image and Text Sentiment's Comparability

A concern that might affect the validity of the proposed image and text sentiment in our study is the heterogeneous extraction approaches (i.e., software) in our methodology section. Even though our operationalization of the image and text sentiment is built upon the procedural logic of sentiment analysis in textual content (Berger et al., 2019), one might question whether the extracted sentiment for image and text would be comparable because of the use of two different sets of software.

The traditional measurement of sentiment in unimodal content such as textual posts has predominantly relied on the identification of positive and negative emotional words/cues in the content and calculating their aggregate as the measures of specific discrete emotions or sentiment (Hartmann et al., 2021, 2022). This process comprises two main steps: (1) identification of the positive/negative emotional words/cues, and (2) aggregation of the positive and negative emotional words/cues to generate the measures of specific discrete emotions or sentiment. Our study uses two different sets of software to complete these two steps of identifying the emotional cues in the image and text content, which has the potential to create bias in the comparison between image and text sentiment and thus in the construction of sentiment divergence.

To address this, we conducted a robustness test in which we measured the image sentiment as in our main analysis but extracted the sentiment score of the text directly through the same set of software—that is, Microsoft Azure Cognitive Service. This ensured that (1) both methods relied on the same pretrained service and dataset, (2) the procedural logic of the text sentiment accurately replicated the traditional text sentiment analysis methods used in prior studies (Berger et al., 2019), and (3) operationalization of the sentiment divergence measure was not significantly affected by the use of different methods for image and text sentiment extraction.

We obtained new caption sentiment scores for the SM posts in our dataset using the Microsoft Azure Cognitive Service. We then replicated our main study's process of forming sentiment divergence. By so doing, we created a new sentiment divergence measure for all the SM posts in our dataset. In the next step, we reran the main analysis and estimated the effect of this sentiment divergence measure on the liking and commenting engagement of SM influencers' followers.

Table I1 shows the results of this test. As is evident, the results closely replicate the main findings in our study. Therefore, we can conclude that despite the use of different methods, image and text sentiment can be compared robustly and through several sentiment and measurement approaches. This further ensured the robustness of the sentiment divergence constructs in the context of our study.

## Appendix J: Visual Sentiment Magnifiers and Comparability of Image and Text Sentiment

Although our study utilized the same procedural logic for image and text sentiment formation as that of prior text analysis studies (Berger et al., 2019), a valid concern for this procedural logic is the differential capability of visual and textual content in expressing certain emotions through magnifying cues. For instance, while a text can magnify sadness by using word cues such as very, substantially, or absolutely before sad emotional cues, one might argue that images are not capable of doing so. Therefore, the identification of the emotional cues for the same emotions in image and text content would not yield comparable measures.

Although this concern is valid, since visual content and textual content are two distinct media formats with inherent differences, there are visual attributes that can potentially act in similar ways and make the comparison between the two modes of sentiment expression more robust—specifically, an emotion may be magnified by the intensity of the facial expression through which it is expressed. Our main identification method for an image’s discrete emotions integrated the intensity with which the emotions are expressed, treating it in a similar to the verbal magnifiers in text. However, to ensure the robustness of this algorithm, we employed another test to examine whether our image-processing algorithm accurately considers emotion magnifiers.

Specifically, we examined how the magnitude or expressiveness of certain facial expressions correlates with the extracted intensity of their corresponding emotions. We measured the correlation in images between smile intensity in the analyzed facial expressions and the magnitude of the happiness emotion. This test can depict whether expressive happiness is created through the intensified smile posture of human subjects in our dataset.

The results for this test indicate a high correlation between these two properties extracted from images (0.998). Also applicable to facial expressions of other emotional cues such as sadness or anger, this test shows that our image-processing algorithm integrates contextual magnifiers in images as visual counterparts of verbal sentiment magnifiers. Therefore, despite the inherent differences between a text's verbal magnifiers and an image's and nonverbal magnifiers, the SM posts' images contained contextualized magnifiers that were accounted for in our image sentiment construct. This further ensured the comparability of image and text sources of the sentiment expressed in the SM posts.

## Appendix K: Falsification Test for the Influence of Sentiment Divergence on Follower Engagement

According to the notion of falsifiability, to prove that the effect of sentiment divergence on follower engagement is reliable, it should be disprovable in a counterfactual setting or scenario. Within our theorization process, the influence of sentiment divergence on follower engagement is dependent on the existence of visual and textual sources of sentiment in a unified multimodal SM post. Therefore, for the effect of sentiment divergence to be valid, we should be able to falsify it in a counterfactual scenario. For instance, when one source of sentiment is omitted (e.g., an SM post containing only an image or caption) or when one source of sentiment is transformed (e.g., an image that contains only textual content). We implemented this idea to falsify the existence of sentiment divergence in contexts other than multimodal SM posts and thus reveal its reliability in the context of multimodal SM posts comprising both image and text sentiment. Thus, the purpose of our falsification test is to establish the existence of the proposed mechanism by delineating a counterfactual scenario in which the theorized effects are disprovable.

Accordingly, we performed a falsification test by transforming the image sentiment in Instagram posts (see Figure K1). First, we collected a sample of 3,510 random SM posts in which the image file contained only textual content—for example, a slogan. We then extracted the text from these images using the optical character recognition (OCR) feature of Microsoft Azure Textual Analytics Service. Next, using the procedure described in the paper, we calculated the sentiment divergence magnitude and sentiment divergence negativity variables as the difference between the image sentiment (which, in this case, was the sentiment of the text represented in the image) and the caption sentiment. On the dataset thus constructed, we performed a hierarchical regression similar to that of our main analysis (see summary stats in Table K1).

## Appendix L: Small Sentiment Divergences as Measurement Errors

The collected dataset in our study reveals a high prevalence of sentiment divergence in multimodal SM posts. However, one might argue that the extensive presence of sentiment divergence might be the result of the potential biases/noises in measuring image and text sentiment. In other words, for small sentiment divergences, the observed difference between image and text sentiment might be due to errors in our measurement methods or our use of different software, and users might not perceive it. This concern may be valid, since our study is among the first to conceptualize image sentiment and to systematically link differential image and text sentiment.

To address this concern, we performed a test in which we excluded sentiment divergence measures that might have been created by errors in our methodology and may thus have only accounted for the perceivable and salient sentiment divergence.

To this end, we excluded SM posts with sentiment divergence below the 10th percentile of our dataset's absolute values. We then repeated the main analysis, assessing the impact of sentiment divergence magnitude and direction on posts' likes and comments. The findings mirrored our primary empirical analysis, demonstrating the robustness of our results and alleviating concerns regarding biases in the image and text sentiment estimations.

## Appendix M: Differential Combinations of Image and Text Sentiment—Reinforcing and Contradicting Mechanisms of Sentiment Divergence

Our theorization about the effects of image and text sentiment divergence on engagement with multimodal SM posts via likes and comments takes the effect of the magnitude and direction of sentiment divergence into account. However, the same magnitude and direction of sentiment divergence can arise from various combinations of image and text sentiment. For instance, a positive sentiment divergence can be created through (1) a negative image with a caption that is negative but less negative than the image, (2) a negative image with a positive caption, or (3) a positive image with a caption that is more positive than the image. These complex scenarios can create alternative mechanisms that can potentially limit our identified effects for positive and negative sentiment divergence.

Specifically, while we treated all forms of divergence as contradictory, there may have been cases where image and text sentiment divergence with the same valence created a “reinforcing” sentiment divergence rather than a “contradicting” one. The following points and diagram illustrate how various sentiment divergences can be categorized as either “reinforcing” or “contradicting”:

\- Reinforcing sentiment divergence: Both image and text exhibit similar-valence sentiment (i.e., both positive or both negative).

\- Contradicting sentiment divergence: Image and text sentiment differ (i.e., one positive and one negative).

Figure M1. Reinforcing and Contradicting Variations of Positive and Negative Sentiment Divergence

These subcategories of reinforcing and contradicting positive and negative sentiment divergences raise the question of whether this type of divergence leads to different effects on follower engagement. Notably, contradicting sentiment divergence is equivalent to our original sentiment divergence concept. Therefore, the primary concern focuses on the potential varying impact of reinforcing sentiment divergence on follower engagement.

To address this, we created a subgroup of reinforcing sentiment divergence by selecting observations featuring an image and a text whose valence was the same. In making the new dataset, we only retained observations with a sentiment divergence magnitude greater than the 25th percentile, thus ensuring that the reinforcing effect is perceivable and that there were no small magnitudes of sentiment divergence that could have been missed by users or may have been caused by our estimation errors.

After creating the reinforcing subgroup, we performed our main analysis on it to determine whether our overall findings would be replicated. Our results for the magnitude and direction of sentiment divergence on follower engagement were generally consistent with our main findings. Although the direction of sentiment divergence was nonsignificant for likes and partially significant for comments (which is potentially because of the same valence in all observations in the reinforcing subgroup), the overall effect of positive and negative sentiment divergence corroborated our main findings. These results suggest that despite the nuanced combinations of image and text sentiment divergence in SM posts, the proposed magnitude and direction attributes can comprehensively capture the effect of sentiment divergence on follower engagement. This further reinforces our study's theorization and primary findings.

## Appendix N: Alternative Mechanism for Increased Negative Commenting

On SM platforms, commenting and liking are engagement behaviors that offer distinct ways to interact with sentimentally divergent posts. Liking, as an expression of satisfaction with content (Karahanna et al., 2018), presents a binary choice for users: Either like a sentimentally divergent post or ignore it. However, commenting behavior does not work in this way.

Specifically, while users may refrain from liking a post to express dissatisfaction, they might collectively leave more comments to convey negative feelings or dissatisfaction through this medium. Such behavior, known as “firestorms” in SM settings (Hartmann et al., 2021; Herhausen et al., 2019), occurs when users express negative sentiment towards subjects like brand content or political campaigns. This nuanced aspect of commenting creates a situation in which the negative effect of sentiment divergence does not deter users from commenting but may instead increase their negative commenting engagement.

To investigate potential biases in the effect of sentiment divergence on commenting behavior, we conducted a robustness test to determine whether the magnitude and direction of sentiment divergence influenced the average sentiment of comments on SM posts. If average comment sentiment is negatively affected by sentiment divergence, an alternative mechanism may exist, potentially biasing our theorized effect of sentiment divergence on commenting behavior.

We assessed the sentiment polarity of approximately 280,000 comments on over 7,000 randomly selected SM posts in our dataset, calculated the average sentiment of comments for these posts, and reran our main analysis with this variable as our primary dependent variable. To examine whether sentiment divergence prompts users to express negative comments with equal frequency, we also controlled for the number of comments on the selected posts.

The following table displays the test results, indicating no significant effect on the average sentiment of comments for negative and positive cases of sentiment divergence. We observed a small negative effect at a 0.05 significance level for the sentiment divergence negativity dummy variable, but this alone does not substantiate the described alternative mechanism. Therefore, we can conclude that on entertainment-based SM platforms like Instagram, where interactions with influencers are mostly hedonistic (Alibakhshi & Srivastava, 2022), the presence of sentiment divergence primarily discourages follower engagement rather than prompting engagement with the expression of negative sentiment. This highlights the nuanced differences between SM platforms, where content consumption (engagement) is more hedonistic and pleasure-seeking, and information-seeking and utility-driven settings like online reviews and word-of-mouth content (Pfeffer et al., 2013).

## Appendix O: Control Function Approach: Endogeneity and Causality

To robustly estimate the influence of sentiment divergence on follower engagement, it is imperative to account for possible endogeneity within the main dependent variables of our study—that is, sentiment divergence magnitude and sentiment divergence negativity. To address this issue, we adopted the control function approach to address endogeneity concerns in nonlinear models (Mallipeddi et al., 2021).

We identified the age and gender of the human subjects (influencers) in the images of our SM posts as potential instruments for implementing the control function approach. Existing literature indicates that these influencer-level instruments are both theoretically and empirically associated with the extent and valence of individuals' emotional expression, which can subsequently impact their emotional expressions in images (Fan et al., 2021; McConatha et al., 1994). However, no consistent relationship has been found between the age and gender of influencers and the engagement levels of their followers, which fulfills the initial requirement for suitable instruments.

To assess their empirical validity, we conducted Wald chi-square and Wu Hausman tests. Table O1 presents the results of this stage, in which we extracted the age and gender of influencers in images using our image analysis algorithm. These factors serve as valid instruments for our study.

## Appendix P: Identification of Strategic Content Manipulation

A valid concern in the setting of SM influencers is the strategic manipulation of content creation. Content creators such as influencers can learn from the success of their prior expression of sentiment via images and text, and if they do this, they can strategically design SM content with certain sentiment divergence properties. This can negate the exogeneity of the focal construct of sentiment divergence.

To alleviate this concern, we performed a Granger causality test (Moqri et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2022) to show whether image sentiment, caption sentiment, and sentiment divergence in prior SM posts influence these variables in the subsequently published SM content of the same SM influencers. We considered each of the SM influencers and their SM posts as a panel comprising consecutive content creation points. We then examined how image sentiment, text sentiment, and sentiment divergence in their prior SM posts at time t-1 influenced the same variables in their SM posts at time t and vice versa.

The results of our analysis showed nonsignificant $\tilde{Z}$ statistic of the Granger causality test for all the relationships between these variables. Our findings show that neither prior expressed sentiment nor divergence causes the currently expressed sentiment. We did not observe any reversed effect either. Taken together, the Granger causality tests show that sentiment divergence in our dataset's SM posts is not the product of strategic manipulation by the content creators; therefore, we can assert the exogeneity of the sentiment divergence construct in our study.
