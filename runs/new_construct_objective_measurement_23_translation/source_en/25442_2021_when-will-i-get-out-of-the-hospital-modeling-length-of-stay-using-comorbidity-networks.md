# When will I get out of the Hospital? Modeling Length of Stay using Comorbidity Networks

Pankush Kalgotra <sup>a</sup> and Ramesh Sharda<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Harbert College of Business, Auburn University Auburn, AL 36849 US; <sup>b</sup>Vice Dean, Watson Graduate School of Management, Regents Professor of Management Science and Information Systems, Spears School of Business, Oklahoma State University, OK 74078, USA

## ABSTRACT

A reliable and accurate estimate of the expected hospital length of stay (LOS) of a patient is important to patients, medical providers, and insurance companies. Predicting hospital Length of Stay (LOS) is a complex and ill-structured problem, driven by many factors such as a patient’s individual characteristics, treatment plans, and disease-interactions. In this paper, we develop a novel model to predict the expected LOS at the time of admission by combining network science and deep learning. We propose a two-dimensional construct of latent comorbidities comprising historical and probable comorbidities that a patient does not currently manifest but could likely develop during the course of hospital stay. The probable comorbidities are derived from a network comprising relationships among diseases observed in 3.2 million patient records in hundreds of US hospitals. We employ this construct of latent comorbidities in deep learning models to predict patients’ LOS using almost 10 million other patient visits belonging to various disease categories. Implementing these models and analyses required a high-performance computing (Big Data) facility. The average mean absolute percent error of our models across all categories of diseases was 29.8%, which is the best in the current state-of-the-art. Our primary contribution is in developing a generalizable method to create a predictor construct for recognizing underlying relationships through network analyses, which can then be used in a deep learning model to predict an exogenous dependent variable.

## KEYWORDS

health informatics; network analytics; deep learning; long short-term memory; comorbidity; comorbidity index; hospital length of stay; hospitalization prediction

## Introduction

Prediction of hospital length of stay (LOS), like many other healthcare issues, is a wicked, complex, ill-structured, and dynamic problem. A problem may be defined as complex and ill-structured when its underlying state is unknown; it has multiple competing outcomes; it is afected by the interaction of several factors; and the environment under which it is tackled is dynamic and unpredictable, with time-delayed consequences for actions taken [26, 63, 86]. Predicting LOS at the time of admission is a complex problem because the underlying reasons for a hospital stay may not be clearly identified, and several patient-level, disease-level, hospital-level, treatment-level, and other factors (e.g. medical injuries during hospitalization) interact with each other in a dynamic environment. Of course, insurance companies are eager to determine ways in which a patient’s LOS can be shortened without adding to the risk of readmission. Hospitals themselves would like to know a patient’s expected LOS for optimal resource planning, among other reasons. Therefore, research on LOS prediction has been ongoing, and novel approaches are needed to improve this prediction. Given the importance of predicting LOS at the point of admission to diferent stakeholders such as patients, hospitals and insurance companies, and because previous attempts at predicting LOS sufer from low accuracy and limited applicability of proposed models across multiple diseases [53], our study attempts to create a deep learning predictive model for estimating the expected length of stay for all types of patients using the information available at the point of admission. We note that the objective of this paper is to create a predictive model to estimate the expected LOS but not build an optimization model to identify the right LOS. Deciding when to let a patient go home (which will clearly impact the LOS) to optimize a patient’s well-being and reduce likelihood of their readmission is a separate problem that has been studied by other researchers [68]. Our approach for measurement/prediction of the LOS could provide inputs into the prescriptive analytics problem of the right LOS. Given that hospital inpatient care constitutes nearly one-third (29%) of all healthcare expenses in the United States<sup>1</sup>, it is important to correctly estimate LOS early to manage workloads across departments as well as assist in a more accurate insurance precertification [68, 91].

In this study, we use the concept of comorbidities to predict LOS. The presence of an additional disease in addition to the primary disease is known as a comorbidity [23]. Comorbidity has been considered as an important factor in predicting health outcomes (such as mortality, LOS, readmission, etc.) because two diseases jointly can have a diferent health outcome than the same two diseases occurring individually at diferent times. In other words, the concurrent presence of two diseases has an aggregated efect on the patient health, which may be diferent than the sum of the efects of two diseases, as discussed in the syndemic theory by Singer [87]. The eforts and strategies required to treat/cure multiple diseases simultaneously in a patient can be diferent from curing them independently, and thus can also impact the length of stay in a hospital [1, 5, 13].

When a patient visits a hospital, he/she comes with one or more primary complaints in addition to other known comorbidities, generally recorded on an admission note by the referring physician. The primary complaint and known comorbidities are observed at the time of admission. However, LOS might also be afected by some diseases that are not present at the time of admission or may manifest during the visit. In this study, we propose a concept of latent comorbidities to improve the LOS prediction. The comorbidities which are not present at the time of admission are defined as latent comorbidities. We conceptualize the construct latent comorbidities in two dimensions: historical comorbidities and probable comorbidities. The historical comorbidities are the diseases a patient had in the past (which may or may not be present during admission) whereas the probable comorbidities are the diseases a patient may develop during that specific hospital visit. Historical comorbidities can explain the past experience of a patient with diferent diseases, whereas the probable comorbidities can provide information on how a patient’s visit is likely to unfold. Thus, the latent comorbidities can provide information about the past and future states of a patient, and can be used to model factors that may help improve the prediction of

LOS at the time of admission. Our primary method novelty is in this idea of creating a multi-dimensional construct using historical and probable patterns., which can then be employed as a predictor construct in a deep learning model.

The historical comorbidities are discernible from the medical records of a patient. However, to estimate the probable comorbidities, we adopt a network methodology to examine the complex interactions of diseases among each other. A network is a representation of interconnected parts, or nodes, of a system linked through well-defined connections or edges [64]. Barabási [4] and Rathkopf [75] have argued that the network method presents a powerful way to explain the interrelations of diseases. By applying the network methodology, we are able to model the disease interactions and characterize their impact on LOS. Specifically, we create a network of diseases, also known as a comorbidity network, comprising the relationships among diseases to compute the probable comorbidities. The connections of the observed diseases in the comorbidity network are used to identify probable comorbidities. This approach allows us to employ these historical and probable diseases to create a predictive model for estimating LOS at the time of admission. We adapt the network methodology in its ability to predict outcomes such as LOS, which are external to the network. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such use of a comorbidity network for predicting LOS. Our approach is novel because it incorporates the current information, past behavior and future possibilities.

The concept of predicting probable comorbidities at the time of admission to predict LOS has multiple practical implications for the hospitals and insurance companies. At the arrival of a patient, the hospital has to get precertification for the payment from the insurance company based on a treatment plan for the current condition of the patient. If new complications arise after the admission, the treatment plan changes and the request for payment to the insurance company also changes. This afects the revenue management for the hospital and the insurance company. Also, through our method of predicting probable comorbidities at the time of admission, a better care plan may be followed by the providers using the insights on the current and probable diseases, which may include iatrogenic illnesses (that can be the results of diagnostic and therapeutic procedures undertaken on a patient [48]). Thus, it can firstly help minimize iatrogenic illnesses, and secondly, help inform efective revenue management strategies for the hospitals and insurance companies.

To explain the approach, we present Figure 1 showing diferent types of comorbidities considered in the model. Consider a patient who visits the hospital with a complaint of diseases i.e. admission time comorbidities. We should note that a patient may report some symptoms, and initial diagnostics tests could lead to identification of these diseases, eventually leading to a decision/recommendation of admission for the patient. That is the time when an expected length of stay is ideally projected to receive precertification from an insurance provider as well as to plan for hospital resources. We identify the direct connections of admission time comorbidities, referred to as the probable comorbidities, from the comorbidity network. The diferent probable diseases could have a distinct impact on LOS, based on the diseases present at the time of admission. This dimension of latent comorbidities captures the risk for patients to develop specific diseases. In addition, it captures the seriousness and severity of the current health state of a patient. Let us assume that the said patient had some historical diseases in the past, historical comorbidities, which are retrieved from the patient’s medical history, although not necessarily reported by the patient during the current visit. Together, historical comorbidities and probable comorbidities make a two-dimensional construct of latent comorbidities.

Our objective is to employ disease-linkages inferred from historical records through network modeling and use these linkages for predictive analytics by two-step model building. In the first-step model, a network is inferred from the history of patients (an independent set of patients). In the second-step model, properties derived from the network models are used further for predictive analysis of an outcome (i.e. LOS), which is exogenous to the network. In other words, the predicted future diseases and historical comorbidities in addition to the known information are used for creating a predictive model for LOS. To build a predictive model, we adapt a long short-term memory (LSTM) deep learning model. LSTM is an artificial recurrent neural network (RNN) model, which can model multidimensional variables. LSTM-RNN is best suited for our approach because we have defined the construct of latent comorbidities in two dimensions: historical comorbidities and probable comorbidities.

We emphasize here that we primarily focus on the predictions in this paper. Comorbidity has been used most often in previous research to explain the length of stay retrospectively by using the information of a full visit, as noted by Lu et al. [57] in a systematic review. It has rarely been used to predict LOS because the information about patient health at the admission point is limited. We note that the purpose of predictions is diferent from the explanations as elaborated by Shmueli [83]. The predictive modeling focuses on minimizing the diference between actual and predicted values whereas the explanatory modeling focusses on establishing a causal relationship between diferent factors. Our contributions in this paper corroborate with the roles of predictive analytics discussed by Shmueli and Koppius [84]: generating new model/theory, developing new measures, compare competing models, improving existing models, assessing relevance and assessing predictability.

To develop our comorbidity network and make predictions, we use the Cerner electronic medical record (EMR) data warehouse containing information on more than 24.7 million patients across 662 US hospitals over 16 years (2000-2015). Cerner provides a deidentified data warehouse to encourage health analytics research. One of the Cerner partners in this initiative is Oklahoma State University’s Center for Health Systems Innovation<sup>2</sup> The use of this massive dataset is one of the major strengths of our study. The large dataset allowed us to create models at diferent levels (network level and patient-visit level) using mutually exclusive datasets. Because of the large dataset, we were also able to develop separate comorbidity networks for men and women. Some previous studies have controlled for gender but rarely focused on and reported diferences in males and females explicitly, as noted by Short, Yang, and Jenkins [85]. It is important to study gender diferences because men and women might respond diferently to a disease in the presence of another disease as reported by Kalgotra, Sharda, and Crof [41]. Some thought leaders, such as Ovseiko, et al. [69] and Johnson, et al. [39], have argued for the recognition of gender diferences in health research, and we respond to their call in this study. Finally, the massive dataset allowed us to create models for patients with diferent classes of diseases. Due to the lack of availability of suficient data, past research largely studied the impact of just a few diseases or on a small set of patients. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to create LOS prediction models for almost all disease categories in one large efort.

The network derived in our study is one of a class that emerges without the intentions of its nodes, often referred to as an unintentional or an implicit network [80, 99]. An implicit network emerges from a common underlying behavior of nodes such as comorbidities in a comorbidity network. This class difers from traditional or explicit networks, which are developed from the intentional actions of their members. The traditional networks such as online social network, world wide web, road network, power grid network and airport network are explicitly created. However, networks such as a co-citation network based on references in papers, a product-product network based on users purchases, a drug-drug network based on prescriptions, and a comorbidity network based on co-occurrences of diseases in patients are all implicit networks. Traditionally, network models have been used for link predictions, [56], and for studying the impact of the network on its nodes which are internal to the network [17]. In this paper, we take the application of an implicit network to the next level, exploring how it impacts the performance of an external variable, such as a patient’s LOS, which is not a part of the network itself. In other words, we study how the collective behavior of the nodes in an implicit network afects an exogenous outcome.

Our study contributes to the Information Systems literature by proposing to use an inferred network for predicting an exogenous outcome. Broadly, our contribution is in solving a problem using network methodology to predict future events (probable comorbidities) and then use them in conjunction with historical events (historical comorbidities) and current events (observed comorbidities) to predict an outcome (LOS), which is external to the network. The characteristics of the generalized problem space where this method is applicable are presented in Table 1. The table also includes the corresponding characteristics of our healthcare/LOS context. Our method can be applied to solve the problems where entities (customer, patient, etc.) implicitly perform some activities or events in a transaction, interactions of which (events or activities) can impact another related but diferent outcome for an entity (during diferent transactions). When only some minimum or incomplete information about the events by an entity is available (incomplete transaction), the interactions modeled through the network analysis can be used to predict the probable events in the first step. Then, when these probable events are coupled with the historical and known characteristics, the outcome of the incomplete transaction can be predicted more accurately. We also include a general flowchart of our method in Appendix A to illustrate how this approach can be applied to other problems.

In the past, implicit networks have been examined where the outcome of a node itself was predicted. For example, Dhar et al. [17] analyzed a co-purchase network of books and predicted a book’s demand. Our model is diferent because we are making the prediction of an outcome of the source using the inferred network. This is analogous to predicting an attribute/outcome for the customers who bought various books, which leads to the book network in Dhar et al. study [17]. For example, could the book network be used to model/ predict the lifetime value of a customer, credit risk, or political afiliation of a customer at a granular level using her current purchases, historical purchases, and likely purchases (by employing the book network)? Our approach can also be applied to study similar problems in other domains. For instance, could an inferred network of web pages be used to predict the credit score of a user using the information about his landing page in a browsing session? Similarly, it may be used to estimate purchasing power of a tourist staying in a hotel by employing a network of activities performed by tourists. Recently, psychologists and psychiatrists have begun to explore use of Big Data and deep learning models for predicting human behavior. See for example, a recent special issue in a Nature afiliated journal [76]. We submit that an inferred network-based approach will be an even more powerful method in those problems where interrelationships from the past can impact future activities/ events/characteristics in unique ways, all of which can then become predictor variable in a diferent problem.

Our approach presents an integration of implicit network methodology and a deep learning model. By developing this combination, we contribute to the analytics literature. Shmueli and Koppius [84] argued in their commentary that a construct and a method development using analytics is a theory contribution. In addition, we add to the existing knowledge by building predictive models using our network approach that outperform the extant models. Creating a better model than the existing models that provides business value [2, 29] is another contribution of analytics as discussed by Shmueli and Koppius [84]. The proposed network-based models coupled with deep learning can improve decisionmaking outcomes and can be embedded in LOS estimation modules.

## Literature Review

## Prediction of Length of Stay

The problem of predicting LOS has been studied for many years. A list and review of related studies may be found in Table 2, which includes the performance of extant models, and whether the models were applicable at the point of admission. A majority of the previous studies considered just one or a few classes of diseases in patients or focused on particular events such as heart surgery, knee operation, etc. This is due to the limited availability of data and mostly a focus to explain how LOS is related to a specific class of diseases. The models developed using such few types of patients cannot be generalized to other diseases. In our study, we develop models for a broad group of patients admitted due to many disease categories.

In addition, the majority of past studies utilized data from an entire hospital visit including lab tests, other known comorbidities emerging from lab reports, procedures, etc. retrospectively. Such analyses are common to better explain and understand the scenarios, but obviously those models are not applicable for prediction. In fact, the extant scales for comorbidity such as Charlson index, [9] also require data from a full visit. In such studies, the primary purpose is not to predict LOS but to understand and identify important factors afecting LOS. However, our network approach uses only admission level information to predict LOS.

A few previous studies have used a classification approach to categorize a hospital visit into a class (long, medium, short stay, etc.) such as Carter and Potts [8] and Hachesu et al. [31]. ## Length of Stay and Comorbidity

Comorbidity has been shown to be related to LOS in the past, as the presence of two or more conditions requires more care and resources to cure jointly. In the literature, there is no consensus on the definition of comorbidity. The concept has been theorized using four distinctions by Valderas et al. [94]. The first is based on the nature of the health conditions occurring concurrently. The second is based on the relative importance of the co-occurring conditions where one disease is given more importance than the others, and the presence of another condition is considered a comorbidity. The third distinction is based on the chronology of development of the conditions considering that one disease can lead to another. The fourth distinction considers illness burden and patients’ socioeconomic conditions that can play a role in the presence of multiple diseases. In these four distinctions, Valderas et al. considered patients’ clinical and socioeconomic factors to conceptualize comorbidity [94]. Jakovljevic and Ostojic [37] alternatively defined comorbidity in three diferent ways based on only diseases diagnosed in patients: first, when two diseases are present simultaneously but independently; second, when one disease causes another, making them interdependent; third, when multiple diseases are present regardless of their causal relationships. We adapt this third definition by Jakovljevic and Ostojic, and define comorbidity as the presence of multiple diseases simultaneously during a hospital visit [37].

Many scales have been proposed to measure comorbidity. For example, de Groot et al. identified twelve diferent indexes, concluding that the Charlson Index, the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CISR), the Index of co-Existent Disease (ICED), and the Kaplan Index are reliable and can be used in clinical research [16]. Since CISR, ICED, and Kaplan require clinical judgment and information, we consider the Charlson Index in our model because it is possible to compute it based on medical records. Originally created for predicting mortality, the Charlson Index assigns weights to 19 diferent medical conditions. Some studies have found it to be related to LOS. For instance, Librero, Peiró, and Ordiñana conducted a simple bivariate analysis, finding that LOS increases with the Charlson Index score [52]. Rochon et al. also found a significant relationship between LOS and comorbidity indexes, however with low efect $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 6 )$ [78].

Past studies have rarely used such comorbidity scales for LOS prediction purposes because information about an entire visit is needed to know all of a patient’s diseases (Table 2 lists some of those studies). If information about comorbidities at the point of admission is not available, it cannot be used to make predictive models for LOS. Therefore, a mechanism is required to first predict comorbidities and then use it for LOS predictions. Most previous studies have focused on one disease at a time. For instance, Hachesu et al. [31] studied comorbidities of coronary artery disease, Yang et al. [96] considered sepsis patients, Lowell and Davis [55] included schizophrenia and afective disorder, Thombs, et al. [92] examined burn injury, and Lyketsos, et al. [58], Furlanetto and da Silva [27], Stecker et al. [90], and Huntley, et al. [36] analyzed psychiatric-related comorbidities. Therefore, the previous models are applicable to only specific categories of patients and lack generalizability across disease categories.

Our literature review identifies three research gaps in this specific problem. First, as noted just above, most past studies restricted their analyses to a few categories of diseases, and thus their models are only applicable to the specific types of patients. Second, the existing comorbidity scales such as Charlson Index consider only a subset of diseases. Our network approach measures all disease relationships from a database encompassing millions of patients in the United States. Finally, to the best of our knowledge, no one has applied an implicit network approach and used its properties to predict LOS. Our study attempts to fill all these research gaps.

## Latent Comorbidities Construct Development

We begin by explaining the notations used for our construct development followed, by a description of the method for creating latent comorbidities. Let the universal set of n diseases be denoted by $D = \{ 1 , 2 , \dots n \}$ . A patient during a hospital visit, V, presents him/ herself with a set of known diseases, k, where $k \subseteq D .$ . The set k is measured as the observed comorbidities as these are the only diseases known at the time of admission. However, the set k does not contain all diseases of that full visit as some diseases may be diagnosed later during the same visit. We denote the set of all diseases during the full visit by v where $\nu \subseteq D$ and $k \subseteq \nu .$ In addition, before the visit V, the patient might have a set of past diseases measured as historical comorbidities, h $( \subseteq D )$ . So, there are three diferent sets: known diseases at the time of an admission (k), all diseases during a visit (v), and historical diseases (h). Based on the known diseases, k, we develop a method to predict the risk of additional comorbidities (probable) using a comorbidity network as described below.

## Comorbidity Network

In our comorbidity network, diseases that are likely to co-occur during a hospital visit are connected to one other. Since the focus is on relationships based on co-occurrences during the same visit and the time diference between diagnosis of two diseases may be too small to derive the direction or sequence of diagnosis of diseases during the same visit, we created edges between diseases with no direction. For example, an edge between congestive heart failure and rheumatic heart disease represents an undirected connection between two nodes representing these two diseases. This is a parsimonious approach where we do not make an assumption of directionality, which makes our method applicable to the patients even where directionality of the diseases is unknown. This parsimonious approach leads to models that are more broadly applicable. A model incorporating the timing of the diseases (directionality) could potentially be even more accurate, but such information in practice may be unavailable, leading to even fewer datapoints for machine learning. Our models performance improvements without knowing the directionality is a strength of the approach. Our network is implicit, and links between diseases are inferred considering the set of diseases during a hospital visit from an analysis of the millions of observed patient encounters in a hospital, $\nu = \{ 1 , 2 , 3 , \ldots t \}$ where t≤n.

We denote $C = ( D , E )$ as a loop-less, undirected, and weighted comorbidity network, where $D = \{ 1 , 2 , \dots n \}$ is the node set of C, and $E \subseteq D \mathrm { X } D$ is the edge set of C. The edges in a comorbidity network are derived from the x number of hospital visits across the patients. The symmetric n x n matrix $A _ { C } = \left( a _ { i j } \right) _ { \left( i j \right) \in { \cal D } \mathrm {  ~ X ~ } { \cal D } } ,$ where $a _ { i j } > 0 \mathrm { i f } ( i , j ) \in E$ is an edge of C and $a _ { i j } = 0 { \mathrm { ~ i f ~ } } ( i , j ) \notin E ,$ , is called the adjacency matrix of C.

In the past, the strength of an edge between diseases or comorbidities $( a _ { i j } )$ has been modeled using a simple Pearson’s correlation coeficient [19, 34]. However, several researchers such as Ahlgren et. al [3] and Kalgotra et al. [43] have criticized the use of correlation for modeling co-occurrences and have recommended Salton Cosine Index (SCI) [81]. SCI is defined as the inner product of two vectors divided by the product of their lengths. For binary vectors, the cosine measure is also called the Ochiai coeficient [7, 66–67, 98]. In this study, we adopt Ochiai coeficient to quantify the relationship between two diseases as the co-occurrences are denoted by binary vectors. Ochiai coeficient, $a _ { i j } ,$ of two diseases i and j is calculated as in equation 1, where $c _ { i j }$ is the number of hospital visits with i and j together, $c _ { i }$ is the number of hospital visits having i disease, and $c _ { j }$ is the number of hospital visits having j disease. Here, $c _ { i j } \leq \left( c _ { i } c _ { j } \right)$ and $( c _ { i j } , \ c _ { i } , c _ { j } ) \leq x .$ : The Ochiai cosine similarity has been used in the past to find phenotype overlaps [12, 49–51], and we propose this as an appropriate measure for calculating the strength of a comorbidity.

$$
a _ {i j} = \frac {\left(c _ {i j}\right)}{\sqrt {\left(c _ {i} * c _ {j}\right)}}\tag{1}
$$

The n x n comorbidity adjacency matrix $A _ { C }$ contains $a _ { i j }$ of all pairs of diseases as shown in Equation 2. Since the network is symmetric and loop-less, $a _ { i j } = a _ { j i }$ , and $a _ { i j } = 0$ where i=j. This matrix is a transition matrix containing Ochiai index, which is used to identify and quantify latent comorbidities for a patient at the point of admission as described below.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} 0 & a _ {1 2} & \ldots & a _ {1 n} \\ a _ {2 1} & 0 & \ldots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a _ {n 1} & \ldots & & 0 \end{array} \right]\tag{2}
$$

## Latent Comorbidities

We infer probable comorbidities from the network defined in the previous section based on the set of known diseases, k, at the time of admission. Equation 3 shows the computation of probable diseases denoted by COM. It is calculated by multiplying the binary array of the list of known diseases, Pres $= ( k _ { I } , k _ { 2 } , k _ { 3 } \dots k _ { n } )$ , with the adjacency matrix $A _ { C } .$ In the adjacency matrix, theoretically, a disease can be connected to n-1 other diseases. Therefore, to keep the predictions at a manageable level and select the number of connections of a disease, we compared the median and mean of the distribution of the count of diseases across hospital visits. The distribution of the count of diseases was right skewed with a mean of 5.01 and a median of 4. To include more information in the model, we selected the higher number and consider the top five connections of a disease. The resulting array contains the connections and strengths of direct links of the known disease denoted by $\left( p _ { I } , p _ { 2 } , p _ { 3 } \ldots p _ { n } \right)$ . Our construct of latent comorbidities is a combination of historical and probable comorbidities. We create a two-dimensional construct with historical comorbidities as one dimension and probable comorbidities as the second dimension. The binary array of historical diseases, $h i s = ( h _ { I } , h _ { 2 } ,$ $h _ { 3 } \ldots h _ { n } )$ is concatenated with COM and reshaped to two dimensions in the shape of 2Xn as presented in Equation $^ { 4 , }$ denoted by uCOM. From the formulation, this measurement involves a sequence describing presence of a disease i in history $\left( { { h } _ { i } } \right)$ and likelihood of it being in future $\left( { p _ { i } } \right)$ . An algorithm to create uCOM construct for a hospital visit is added in Appendix B.

$$
C O M = P r e s ^ {*} A _ {C}
$$

$$
= \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} k _ {1} & k _ {2} & \ldots & k _ {n} \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} 0 & a _ {1 2} & \ldots & a _ {1 n} \\ a _ {2 1} & 0 & \ldots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a _ {n 1} & \ldots & & 0 \end{array} \right]
$$

$$
= \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} p _ {1} & p _ {2} & \dots & p _ {n} \end{array} \right]\tag{3}
$$

$$
u C O M = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c c} h _ {1} & h _ {2} & h _ {3} \ldots & h _ {n - 1} & h _ {n} \\ p _ {1} & p _ {2} & p _ {3} \ldots & p _ {n - 1} & p _ {n} \end{array} \right]\tag{4}
$$

## Modeling

To create the predictive models, we first identify the predictor variables from the previous research as discussed in literature review. The variables from past research used in our LOS models include: demographics variables such as age, gender, marital status and race; visit information such as the patient type (inpatient or emergency), admission type (emergency, urgent, elective, new born, trauma center, etc.), admission source (physician referral, emergency room, clinic referral, transfer from another health care facility, transfer from a hospital, born inside this hospital, normal delivery, etc.); hospital information such as the size (measured by number of beds), acute status, teaching facility, census region, and urbanrural status. In addition, there is some evidence that insurance type also afects LOS, with Medicaid insurance holders having longer stays than others [54, 59]. Therefore, we consider the insurance type as well. Besides, Huntley et al. [36] showed that LOS was afected by the number of previous admissions indicating the critical condition of a patient. Therefore, we create a variable as a counter measuring the visit number for the patient. In addition, the readmission days (time interval between current and last visit) have also been shown afecting the health outcomes for which we create a variable measuring after how long this patient is getting admitted again. We also add a variable measuring the number of days a patient has already spent in the hospital/s prior to the current visit, which is another surrogate measure for a patient’s condition. Finally, we also calculate the Charlson Index from the diseases known at the time of admission. As noted earlier, it was calculated as the weighted sum of scores of known diseases as suggested by D’hoore, Sicotte, and Tilquin [18]. ## Predictive Models

We create models using three sets of variables: patient characteristics, Pchar, known or observed comorbidities, Pres and latent comorbidities, uCOM. To understand the value added by our proposed measure, uCOM, we create a model without uCOM and then a model with uCOM. First, a model of observed diseases (referred to as MPres), is developed where the diseases recorded at the time of admission are modeled, in addition to the Pchar. Therefore, our model of observed comorbidities named MPres, los is a function of patient characteristics, Pchar, and Pres (observed comorbidities) as presented in Equation 5. Then, the measure uCOM is added to MPres to create a model for latent comorbidities named MuCOM as presented in Equation 6. In our model, MuCOM, we propose that combining historical and predicted comorbidities in a two-dimensional comorbidity measurement for a patient in addition to the current diseases will better explain the current state of the patient’s health and therefore, help predict the time needed to take care of this patient in the hospital.

MPres: los = f(Pchar, Pres)(5)

MuCOM: los = f(Pchar, Pres, uCOM)(6),

where f is a function representing a predictive model. It must be noted that the historical comorbidities would not be available for the patients visiting the hospital for the first time. Therefore, the model using ONLY historical comorbidities will not be applicable to all types of visits. Our two-dimensional comorbidity measurement for a patient at the time of admission using historical and probable comorbidities (The diseases present at the time of admission are included separately in the same model) is practically more robust because it is applicable to all types of hospital visits.

The deep learning artificial neural network predictive models were created in case of MPres and MuCOM<sup>4</sup> For the latent comorbidity model, MuCOM, a multi-input neural network model containing Long-Short-Term-Memory (LSTM) layer is created. Our twodimensional formulation of latent comorbidities, uCOM, involves a sequence of a presence of a disease in history and future. In other words, that historical comorbidities are used to inform the predicted comorbidities. Therefore, a method such as LSTM is the best to model this sequential data. LSTM, a recurrent neural network (RNN) model was first explained by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber [35]. It is a sequence learning algorithm, which is not sensitive to the gap between data points. It is a gradient-based learning algorithm in which error flows constantly through special self-connected units called as memory cells connected to the regulators such as input gate, forget gate and output gate units. The core of a memory cell is a linear unit called the Constant Error Carousel (CEC) whose activation is called a cell state. The input gate decides whether to keep or override the information in a memory cell, the forget gate is responsible for deciding to keep or forget the information from previous steps, and finally, the output gate controls the output from the memory cell and decides the information to forward to next hidden state. For the mathematical formulation of LSTM, refer to the seminal work by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber [35] and Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins [28].

## Data Analysis

We obtained data from the Center for Health Systems Innovation (CHSI), a center at Oklahoma State University that houses and conducts research on data provided by Cerner Corporation, a major Electronic Medical Record (EMR) provider. The data warehouse contains visit records of 58 million unique patients across 662 US hospitals (2000-2015). In the database, the diseases are recorded using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) till the third quarter of 2015 after which the diseases are required to be recorded using ICD-10 coding system. Therefore, to maintain consistency in our dataset, we use data till the third quarter of 2015. According to ICD-9 coding system, an ICD-9 code has three, four, or five digits, with the first three representing the broader category of a disease, and the fourth and fifth representing sub-divisions of a disease. For example, the ICD-9 code for personality disorder is 301. At four-digit level (301.x), there are ten types of personality disorders and at five-digit level (301.xx), two other specific personality disorders are coded. Notably, we have used an ICD-9 code of a disease at five-digit level, which captures the detailed version of the disease and severity in some cases. In some cases in ICD coding, the detailed version of a disease is at the three- or four-digit level as well. In such cases, the most detailed version at three- or four-digit level are considered. We did not perform any aggregation and used the same version of the disease as noted by the physician. Most of the past research aggregated the ICD-9 codes at the threedigit level to understand the impact of a category of a disease on LOS. However, we used the full ICD code without any aggregation which aligns with the practice and represents the true condition of a patient. We have a total of 15,584 unique diseases across all patients.

The steps performed in processing the massive dataset containing medical records of 24.7 million unique patients are outlined in a flowchart in Figure 2. The flowchart also presents the number of patients and hospital visits at each step in the process. Briefly, we used information about the demographics of patients, hospitals, types of visits, and diseases. We had 128 million hospital visits with disease records from 24.7 million unique patients. [15] and CRoss-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM) [40, 82] for our analysis. First, information about the patients, admissions, diseases diagnosed, and hospitals were merged. The exact date/time of a disease development is understandably missing; nevertheless, the presence of a disease at the time of admission is known. In our study, we used the information about a complete visit to create the networks but used only diseases present during admission for creating predictive models.

## Deep Learning Models

Deep learning neural network architecture was adapted for our modeling. For the observed comorbidities model, MPres, a fully connected neural network model was created for each category of patients. For each category, the number of input neurons equals to the number of variables in Pchar plus the number of diseases present across all the visits (i.e. universal set of diseases in each category). If the number of distinct diseases recorded across all patients a category is r (where r≤15,584), the total number of input neurons is 48+r, where 48 represents the variables created for seventeen factors described in Table 3. The Pchar variables were rescaled between 0 and 1 using min-max scaler [71], since scales were of diferent range. The loss function considered for selecting the best model was mean absolute error since it is easy to compute, understand and explain. In case of MuCOM, a multi-input neural network model containing Long-Short-Term-Memory (LSTM) layers and fully connected layers was created for each category of patients. For the Pchar variables and observed comorbidities, the same architecture was used as described in MPres. In parallel, the LSTM architecture was used to add the efect of uCOM in the model. ## Hyperparameter Tuning and overfitting controlling

Here, we discuss the process of hyperparameter tuning and measures taken to control the model overfitting. For hyperparameter tuning of the Mpres, the grid search method was adapted where one to six hidden layers and 10 to 3000 hidden neurons with a step size of ten in each were compared. A larger number of layers and neurons were leading to overfitting whereas the smaller number of neurons and layers resulted in underfitting. In addition, we compared diferent dropout regularization proportions ranging from 0.3 to 0.7 [89]. The purpose of dropout is to randomly drop a specified proportion of weights at a particular point in the model. The activation functions used in all the layers was Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) which computes max(0,z) meaning the negative output from a node becomes zero and range of output is (0 to ∞). For the MPres, the final model included three hidden layers containing 300, 100 and 100 hidden neurons respectively and a dropout proportion of 0.5 before the output layer. The loss function considered was mean absolute error since it is easy to compute, understand and explain. For the optimization, we compared Adadelta [97] and Adam [45] methods. The architecture, number of input neurons and parameters estimated by each model are listed in Table 4. Appendix D lists the optimizer selected in each model.

In MuCOM model, the uCOM was added parallelly to the variables in MPres where uCOM was given input to the LSTM layer. In the grid-search, one to 3000 LSTM outputs with a step size of five were tested followed by one to six hidden layers. Since comorbidities can have a diferent impact on diferent categories of patients, diferent models were selected. The models selected are presented in the footnotes of Table and further details are listed in Appendix D. The dropout regularization was used to avoid overfitting [89]. The outputs of two parallel dropout layers: one from MPres architecture and one from LSTM architecture were concatenated. Finally, the information from the Pchar variables, Pres and uCOM was available, which was fed to a fully connected layer with 60 neurons. Since there were multiple features interacting with each other, another dropout layer was added to avoid overfitting. Finally, the output of each model was estimated. The architecture, number of inputs and parameters estimated by each model are listed in Table 4 and the number of neurons and optimizer selected are further added in Appendix D. A similar approach has been used by Ebrahimi, Nunamaker Jr and Chen [21] recently.

To further confirm the robustness of the parameters selected, we divided the two categories of patients<sup>6</sup> into three sets: development, training and validation where development set was used for hyperparameter tuning. One category was the patients with certain conditions originating in the perinatal period and the second category was the patients with diseases of the sense organs. The best models selected during the parameter tuning using the development set where half the dataset used for training and remaining for validation were the same as the models presented in the Table. The results of diferent parameters of parallel LSTM layer are displayed in Appendix E. The grid values presented in the Appendix E are 1) LSTM outputs (1, 10, 15, 20, 25), 2) neurons in each fully connected layers (10, 20, 100), and 3) optimizer (Adam and Adadelta). The consistency of the results (best models) confirmed our selected architectures to be the best models.

A common metric to evaluate the under- and over-fitting in machine learning is the loss function that finds the fit of a model by minimizing the error. In addition, the loss function calculated based on the validation datasets efectively assesses the over- and under-fitting as explained by Kohavi [46]. We used mean absolute error as the loss function where final models were selected based on the minimum value of loss function in the validation dataset. By selecting the model based on validation datasets, we ensured the generalizability of the model while controlling the over and underfitting. Moreover, we have used dropout regularization in our models, which is commonly used to control overfitting in deep learning models [89].

## Results

Before running a predictive model, we were interested in learning the additional variance explained by uCOM in LOS so as to compare the performance of this approach with existing models for understanding variations in LOS. We created hierarchical linear regression models to study the variance explained by three diferent set of variables: patient characteristics, Pchar, known diseases or observed comorbidities, Pres and latent comorbidities, uCOM. The first model, Baseline 1, was created with Pchar and Pres (i.e. variables in MPres). The second model is created using Pchar, Pres and uCOM (i.e. variables in MuCOM). In the second model, uCOM was flattened into one dimensional array with a shape of 1X2r to run a traditional linear model. The average improvement in the variance explained by uCOM was 3.6%, calculated by weighing the number of visits in each category.

Since our primary purpose was to create predictive models, we elaborate on the results from the deep neural networks (See Table 6). The loss function during our training was mean absolute error (MAE), so we compare the MAE for MPres and MuCOM deep learning models. Both models of MPres and MuCOM performed well, with MuCOM deep learning neural network outperforming in all categories. The value added by the uCOM construct in terms of MAE was 1.9% (weighted by the number of visits in each category) across all category of patients. In terms of number of days, the improvement in a better forecast of LOS due to uCOM is 244,518 days across all categories of patients. Table 6 includes the improvement in days in each model (calculated by subtracting MAE of MPres from MAE of MuCOM and then multiplying the result with the number of visits in each category). Specifically, uCOM added more value in the LOS prediction for patients admitted due to the disorders of skin and subcutaneous tissue, genitourinary system, sense organs, and mental disorders. On the other hand, uCOM did not contribute much in the patients with disorders of respiratory system, pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium, conditions originating in the perinatal period and congenital anomalies. The mean absolute percent error (MAPE) of the best models i.e. MuCOM was also computed, which ranges from 13.5% for the patients admitted due to the disorders of sense organs to 56% for patients admitted due to neoplasms. The weighted average MAPE across all MuCOM models was 29.8%, which is quite low, indicating the superior performance of our models. Many of the previous studies do not report MAPE, but the reported MAPE have ranged from a low of 40% to a high of 65%. Rocheteau, et al. [77] recently reported MAPE of 63%, Lowell and Davis [55] reported MAPE of 65% and Zolbanin et al. [100] reported MAPE of 40.55 %. Compared to these numbers, our average MAPE of 29.8% is superior.

In Table 6, we observe that the proposed construct has more predictive power in some categories than the others. The results indicate that latent comorbidities improved the predictive performance for the patients admitted with disorders representing symptoms as these are likely to be diagnosed with other critical diseases, which would be ultimately responsible for the hospital stay. For instance, the latent comorbidities improve the prediction in the patients admitted with the disorders of sense organs by 8.89%, and by 5.6% in the patients with the diseases of skin and subcutaneous tissue. On the other hand, for the patients admitted with neoplasm, respiratory disorders and circulatory disorders, the latent comorbidities did not contribute substantially to impact the forecast of the hospital stay. A potential explanation for this may be that certain diseases are chronic or even terminal, whereas other diseases (such as the diseases of sense organs) may be more like symptoms that may lead to other comorbidities being discovered and thus the model is able to provide a greater contribution to the predictive power. With respect to diferences in the MAPE, the models with lower MAPE indicates that our model has been able to capture the variations in the data. In other words, prediction of the LOS is a less complex problem for patients with lower MAPE than other patients. On the other hand, the models with higher MAPE indicate that the patients have more variation and there may be other unknown factors that afect the actual LOS.

To further understand the variation in the performance of our models, we calculated the Pearson correlation coeficient between the coeficient of variation of LOS (ratio of standard deviation to mean) and the percentage improvement by the proposed construct in nineteen models. The coeficient of variation measures the dispersion of values of a variable around the mean. In other words, it quantifies the heterogeneity or complexity of the problem. We found a significant positive correlation of 0.34 $( \mathrm { n } { = } 1 9 , \mathrm { t } { = } 1 . 5 , \mathrm { p } { = } 0 . 0 7 6 )$ at the significance level of 0.1<sup>7</sup>, which indicates that our proposed construct improved the prediction performance better in the models for an outcome variable with higher dispersion than for an outcome variable with less dispersion. This finding suggests that our proposed deep learning predictive models work well in the complex problems where the dispersion of outcome variable is high, typically a more challenging situation for machine learning. Although this finding should be validated by employing a similar comparison in other problem domains, it is interesting to realize that the combination of a new construct to predict future state of predictor variables and deep learning modeling may lead to better prediction outcomes when the dependent variable is highly dispersed.

We also present another metric to understand the performance of our models considering the error with a tolerance of zero and one day in Table 6. This follows a similar analysis reported by other LOS researchers such as Tsai et al. [93]. The predicted values of LOS were rounded of to integers to compute these metrics. The number with tolerance of zero day implies the number of visits diagnosed with exact value i.e. if a patient stayed for six days, our model predicted six days. Similarly, tolerance of one day means the predicted value has maximum error of one day. In other words, the prediction is within the margin of ±1 day. For instance, the model for patients with infectious and parasitic diseases predicted an exact LOS figure for 52.3% of the visits in the validation dataset, whereas the number increases to 69.7% if an error of one day is allowed. Our models did a good job in predicting exact number for visits with Injury and poisoning (81.8%), diseases of the sense organs (81.8%) and diseases of the musculoskeletal system and connective tissue (73%). With tolerance of one day in error, these numbers rise to 92.1%, 91% and 87.8% respectively. These numbers indicate a high performance by our models. On the other hand, the models with the lowest performance in terms of tolerance of zero and one day are neoplasms where 27.9% visits are predicted with the exact LOS number and 59.4% if one day error is neglected. The numbers are low for the visits with diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs, diseases of the circulatory system, congenital anomalies and endocrine, nutritional and immunity disorders. Across all categories of patients, our models predicted 58% of visits with the observed LOS. When an error of one day is allowed, our models predicted 79.8% of visits accurately. These numbers are superior to those reported by Tsai et al. [93] where the authors reported an accuracy of 36% without any error margin. With a tolerance of one day, Tsai et al. reported an accuracy of 67%. Clearly, our models’ predictive performance is superior.

To understand the impact of diferent variables on the predicted length of stay, we conducted a feature ranking analysis by using a method described by Kraskov et al. [47] and Ross [79]. It is a nonparametric method based on the entropy estimates from z-nearest neighbor distances (we used z=3). We note that uCOM was flattened from a two-dimensional array to one dimensional array as the feature ranking method adopted requires onedimension array as inputs. In Appendix F, we list top ten features in each category of patients. In majority of the categories, the patient type, age and Charlson index have appeared to be highly important. In addition, the results indicate that probable comorbidities play an important role in predicting LOS. For instance, fever and other physiologic disturbances of temperature regulation during hospitalization is likely to increase LOS in patients admitted due to infection, a finding also reported by Diringer et al. [20]. Similarly, probable pneumonia in patients with disorders of respiratory system can impact the LOS. In patients with conditions originating in the perinatal period, we found probable jaundice can afect the length of stay, a finding supported by the previous research [60]. Other common probable comorbidities across the group of patients are esophagus reflux, heart failure, coronary atherosclerosis and acute kidney failure. Please refer to Appendix F for a complete list of top variables in each category.

## Discussion

One of the strengths of our network method, and in particular the model using latent comorbidities, is that it includes multiple diseases, and can therefore, capture multiple interactions. Network positions of diseases were used to identify other diseases that can occur with the observed diseases and help predict LOS. In addition, the multidimensionality nature of our construct, uCOM, including history and probable diseases together made it applicable to all visits because it would not have been applicable for the first hospital visit of a patient if it included only historical diseases (Of course, the first visit has no history information). Furthermore, our construct is flexible and expandable to incorporate additional dimensions.

## Practical and theoretical implications

Our LOS prediction approach using the features extracted from the inferred network improved the model performance in all patient categories. Notably, the MPres model, which is considered the baseline is our own contribution. We combined variables that have been employed in multiple past papers and created deep learning models using the combination of these variables. Thus, our baseline model in itself is a summary of much of the previous research in LOS modeling and an application of deep learning to this problem domain, providing a tougher baseline model to beat. Comparing our models to the past studies in terms of performance, these performed better overall, explaining variance in the data ranging from 28% to 65% using the information present at the time of admission with an average $\mathbf { \bar { R } } ^ { 2 }$ of 0.439. The MAPE ranges from 13.8% to 56% with an average value of 29.8%. The MAE of our models ranged from 0.41 days to 2.02 days with an average value of 1.02 days, which is quite low. The value added by latent comorbidities construct, uCOM, in MAE was 1.9%. Although this improvement may appear small, its practical impact is significant. In terms of number of days improved due to uCOM, it amounts to a better forecast of 244,518 days, equivalent to over 88,278 visits when computed by considering the average LOS in each category of patients. Pfuntner, Wier, and Steiner estimated that the aggregate cost for all hospital stays was \$387.3 billion in 2011 with a mean of \$10,000 per stay [72]. This implies that the value added by our method in a more accurate LOS forecast equates to approximately \$882.8 million. Given the size of payments from insurance companies and Medicare to hospitals, an improvement of 1.9% in predicted LOS is equivalent to a better forecast of transfers of \$882.8M. In other words, our proposed comorbidity variables add \$882.8M to the forecast accuracy of cash flows between healthcare payers and providers. Therefore, the practical improvement due to our construct is significant. Because the problem of LOS prediction is particularly wicked (the term used by Gupta [30] in his ISR editorial), notorious and ill-structured, any reasonable improvement in the accuracy is highly desirable. In a wicked complex problem, we need to focus not on a percent change, but the practical implication of that percent gain. For example, in a study on predicting demand of books, Dhar et al. observed improvement of only 0.00035 in mean squared error [17]. However, it was deemed practically significant due to the large volume.

The use of our model to predict LOS can help hospitals better plan and allocate resources. In addition, the study of comorbidities can certainly help in clinical care and management [94]. Knowledge of the relationships among diseases can help predict and diagnose likely co-existing diseases, and disease network properties can help physicians prioritize treatment of diseases based on the position of a disease in the network. Based on the probable diseases, these networks can help providers select the best treatment plans. The relationships among diseases can also help pharmaceutical companies consider multiple related diseases when developing new drugs.

Our major contribution is in extending the use of network methodology and present its applicability in predictive modeling. Specifically, through our approach, we present a coupling of implicit network modeling and deep learning LSTM modeling, which helped us in making predictions using a complex system of disease-interactions. In addition, we found that the proposed deep learning approach performs better for the outcome with high dispersion than the outcome with less dispersion. Our paper has both theory and method innovations where properties of a complex system were inferred using network methodology, and later used to make predictions of attributes (such as LOS) of the entities (such as patients) from where the complex system (disease-interactions) emerged. The method proposed in this study is generalizable to other problems. For instance, our method can be used to predict readmission rate, mortality and other health outcomes of patients by considering the comorbidities not apparent at the time of admission to a hospital. As noted in Introduction, the method is also applicable to many other business problems where a large dataset can be employed to learn inferred relationships among the activities, and then the resultant network model becomes one of the predictor constructs for modeling an external outcome.

## Limitations and future research

Like most research, our study has a few limitations. First, comorbidities were measured based on electronic medical records and therefore, only the diseases recorded in the hospitals’ EMR were included. A patient could go to a hospital with a diferent EMR system, could move from a diferent part of the country and the historical diseases data may not be available to the Cerner EMR. It is perhaps impossible to capture someone’s lifetime medical history in one record in many open societies. Hence, this limitation exists in all studies that are based on medical records. It is certainly our hope that by employing millions of patient encounters to build likely historical and probable comorbidities, we have a better model than any model based on a small dataset. Second, we considered gender diferences while developing comorbidity networks. However, we also recognize that health disparities also exist based on race and ethnicity [24–25, 42]. We included race as a factor in the model but comorbidity networks by race difer as well, as reported in a paper in Scientific Reports by Kalgotra et al. [42]. Including such disparities is a next step of our research. Third, our models showed a more accurate forecast with respect to the existing models, but there is still room for improvement. We did not use patients’ laboratory reports or consider other external factors such as hospital conditions, level of care and quality of physicians, which could make a significant diference. Due to HIPAA regulations, the location and time related variables were not used in this study. Moreover, a physician or hospital may difer in their treatment styles. These diferences in treatment styles which are likely not fully recorded in an EHR illustrate the complex nature of the LOS prediction problem. Such diferences in treatment styles as well as of course, patients’ genetic characteristics lead to the dificulty of providing a more accurate LOS prediction at the time of a patient’s admission. This means that there will always be a major variation in LOS prediction, and any small gains in accuracy are still quite significant. Although not at the physician level, we have considered hospital level factors such as size, teaching or not, etc. in our model to control for some of these efects. Moreover, creating the latent comorbidities construct from a large dataset is likely to mitigate the impact of treatment diferences.

Fourth, we randomly partitioned the data for training and validating the models. In future, we plan to use other partitioning approaches as suggested by Zolbanin et al [100]. Fifth, LOS for visits with multiple known diseases belonging to diferent organ systems was predicted by multiple models. However, this limitation is mitigated by the fact that each visit at the end is classified into one Major Diagnostic Category (MDC) code. Sixth, we selected top five connections to predict the probable comorbidities. The choice of how many probable comorbidities to consider in a predictive model remains an empirical and theoretical research issue. It is a tradeof between parsimony of a model and its predictive performance. Finally, we employed our proposed approach to predict LOS; in the future, this approach can also be used it to predict other medical outcomes such as readmission and mortality.

## Conclusion

Patients, medical providers, and insurance companies all want to know when a hospitalized patient can be discharged. Predicting hospital LOS is a complex and ill-structured problem. In this paper, we have presented a novel model to predict the expected LOS at the time of admission by using a construct of latent comorbidities comprising historical and probable comorbidities. The probable comorbidities were derived from the network analysis. This construct of latent comorbidities was employed in deep learning models to predict patients LOS using almost 10 million other patient visits belonging to various disease categories. The average mean absolute percent error of our models across all categories of diseases was 29.8%, lower than any existing models. Our primary contribution is in developing a generalizable method to create a predictor construct for recognizing underlying relationships through network analyses, which can then be used in a deep learning model to predict an exogenous dependent variable. Thus, our study adds to the growing literature of information systems, analytics, comorbidity, and network science.
