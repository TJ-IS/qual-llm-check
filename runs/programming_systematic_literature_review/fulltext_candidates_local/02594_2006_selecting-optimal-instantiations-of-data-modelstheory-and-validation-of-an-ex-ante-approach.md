# Selecting optimal instantiations of data models—Theory and validation of an ex ante approach

P.L. Bowen <sup>a</sup>, R. Debreceny <sup>b</sup>, F.H. Rohde <sup>a,\*</sup>, J. Basford <sup>a</sup>

University of Queensland, UQ Business School, 4072, Australia

<sup>b</sup> University of Hawaii, College of Business Administration, Honolulu, 96822, USA

Received 20 July 2004; received in revised form 7 October 2005; accepted 13 October 2005 Available online 4 January 2006

## Abstract

The schema of an information system can significantly impact the ability of end users to efficiently and effectively retrieve the information they need. Obtaining quickly the appropriate data increases the likelihood that an organization will make good decisions and respond adeptly to challenges. This research presents and validates a methodology for evaluating, ex ante, the relative desirability of alternative instantiations of a model of data. In contrast to prior research, each instantiation is based on a different formal theory. This research theorizes that the instantiation that yields the lowest weighted average query complexity for a representative sample of information requests is the most desirable instantiation for end-user queries. The theory was validated by an experiment that compared end-user performance using an instantiation of a data structure based on the relational model of data with performance using the corresponding instantiation of the data structure based on the object-relational model of data. Complexity was measured using three different Halstead metrics: program length, difficulty, and effort. For a representative sample of queries, the average complexity using each instantiation was calculated. As theorized, end users querying the instantiation with the lower average complexity made fewer semantic errors, i.e., were more effective at composing queries. <sup>D</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Models of data; Data representations; Object-relational databases; Relational databases; Query languages; Query complexity

## 1. Introduction

Over the last decade many organizations have expanded their transaction processing databases, implemented enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, and built enterprise-wide data warehouses. Maximizing their returns from these investments requires organizations to make these data repositories available directly to knowledge workers for operational, tactical, and strategic decision making [48]. Knowledge workers can access these data repositories via a wide variety of end-user analytical tools including graphical query interfaces, report writers, OLAP cube builders, and data mining tools as well as the more traditional database query languages [44,46].

The value of the analyses made by knowledge workers depends on the quality of the information captured by and retrieved from the enterprise’s data repositories. Obviously, the accuracy of the data retrieved is affected by the accuracy of the data stored. One hundred percent accurate stored data does not, however, guarantee that the data retrieved will be accurate. The accuracy of the data retrieved also depends upon the semantic<sup>1</sup> accuracy of the end-user composed queries.

This research presents and validates a methodology for evaluating, ex ante, the relative desirability of alternative instantiations of a model of data. In contrast to prior research, each instantiation is based on a different formal theory. A database is an instantiation of a formal theory of a model of data. Different sets of end users may be presented with different representations (external views/schemas) of the same instantiation. End users update data and retrieve information from the instantiation of one or more of the external schemas. These relationships are depicted in Fig. 1. An application of this general notion to a specific formal theory, i.e., relational database theory, is also depicted in Fig. 1. A relational database system is <sup>b</sup>based on a formal theory called the relational model of data<sup>Q</sup> [15, pg. 15]. Thus, when the organization develops a database using a relational database management system, they are developing an instantiation based on the formal theory of <sup>b</sup>the relational model of data.<sup>Q</sup> Once the organization’s instantiation of the relational database has been created, the organization must then decide which different representations (external views/ schemas) of the relational instantiation to provide to the end users. For example, the organization may provide a set of third normal form (3NF) schemas for data capture and a set of first normal form (1NF) schemas for data retrieval.

Basford et al. [3] investigated the effect of providing end users with different external schemas based on the same instantiation of a database. In particular, they empirically tested the theory that, when faced with multiple possible data representations, end users perform information retrieval tasks better using the data representation that yields queries with lower average complexity. Basford et al. [3] used two external schemas: one in 1NF and another one in 3NF. Prior to conducting their experiment, they constructed a representative set of queries and, ex ante, evaluated the average complexity for the query formulations in each external schema. The 1NF schema produced, on average, less complex queries. As predicted by the theory, Ref. [3] found that end users who were given an external schema that, ex ante, led to less complex queries performed better.

Organizations are constantly faced with deciding which external schemas to offer to their end users. Less frequently, organizations are faced with the more difficult and more important task of evaluating different instantiations of databases based upon different formal theories of models of data. This paper examines whether the algorithm tested by Ref. [3] to optimize external schemas based on the same underlying model of data holds when comparing instantiations created via different formal theories of models of data.

This paper extends prior research in the following ways. First, this paper increases the precision and formalism of the methodology advocated to select from competing schemas. Second, Ref. [3] provided an initial test of the theory when optimizing two external schemas/views, a 1NF schema versus a 3NF schema, derived from the same instantiation of a relational database. The research reported in this paper tested whether the theory could be extended to compare an instantiation developed from one underlying theoretical model of data (relational) with an instantiation developed from a different underlying theoretical model of data (object-relational—(OOR)). The results presented in this paper are especially noteworthy in that the experimental participants had extensive prior experience with querying relational instantiations but no prior experience querying OOR instantiations. In spite of this bias toward the relational instantiation, end users composed more accurate queries using the OOR instantiation. That is, even with the substantially greater enduser training and experience, using the data representation based on the alternative model of data, the theory for selecting the most desirable data representation for end-user queries was validated by the experimental results.

Organizations need to identify the optimal data representation for information retrieval for each database prior to implementation for a number of reasons. These reasons include minimization of learning costs, maximization of end-users’ proficiency in satisfying their information retrieval tasks, and enhancement of enduser communication with colleagues. This research will benefit organizations, database designers, and end users by helping them to evaluate, ex ante, the efficiency and effectiveness of alternative data representations (whether different instantiations of databases, or different end-user schemas/views). Because the critical success factors of organizations differ, organizations information retrieval requirements also differ. Thus, the combination of database instantiation and external schemas/views will typically differ from organization to organization.

## 2. Theoretical framework and proposition development

## 2.1. Overview

To facilitate their decisions, knowledge workers need to identify information requests that will provide insights into the operational, tactical, and strategic problems they face. To extract the data they need, end users must map these information requests into queries. The formulations of these queries depend on the data representation<sup>2</sup> they access, i.e., different data representations require different query formulations to satisfy the same information request. Alternative data representations, because of differences in the complexity of the query formulations, affect end-users’ abilities to compose correct queries [44].

Researchers assert that as task complexity increases, performance decreases [see, e.g., Refs. 7,29,36]. Furthermore, as task complexity increases, end-users’ confidence in their solution to the problem decreases [11,21]. Relative to information retrieval, Ref. [29] found the complexity of queries is positively associated with both the number of query errors (effectiveness) and the time required to prepare the queries (efficiency). Furthermore, the more complicated logic associated with complex queries makes end users more aware of the possibility that errors may occur, thereby reducing their confidence in the correctness of their queries. Numerous empirical studies have substantiated the negative impacts of greater complexity and reduced queryschema congruence on the ability of end users to efficiently and effectively retrieve information [see, e.g., Refs. 4,6,8,14,29,39,44]. Thus, end users faced with a variety of data representations are expected to perform better when interacting with the data representation that, on average, yields queries with lower complexities.

## 2.2. Sampling theory

The principles of sampling theory, i.e., that knowledge gained through the examination of a sample can be used to draw conclusions about the population from which the sample was drawn, have been applied in quality assurance for many years [22,30,32,41,42]. Sampling theory prescribes that the sample should be randomly selected so as to reduce bias and should be representative so as to increase the reliability of the conclusions.

In this research, the ex ante evaluation process begins by selecting the data representations, DR , j = 1 to n, the organization wants to examine. The organization then needs to create a set of representative information requests, RIR<sub>i</sub>, i = 1 to m, with which end users are typically confronted in their operating environment [26]. Each information request is mapped into its appropriate query for each of the competing data representations. Complexity metrics are calculated for each query formulation associated with each of the data representations. Thus, for each Data Representation, $\operatorname { D R } _ { j } ,$ there will be m complexity values, i.e., one complexity value for each RIR. Each RIR is assigned a weight indicative of the relative frequency and importance of the RIR being accurate, i.e., the larger the business impact from using the output of the RIR, the greater the weighting. The weighted complexity values for each data representation can then be summed. This process is repeated for each data representation. The ex ante evaluation theory asserts that the data representation that, on average, yields the least complex queries is the data representation that optimizes end-user query performance.

This evaluation procedure can be represented mathematically with the following expression. Given n data representations and m representative information requests, the data representation that should be selected is determined by the following algorithm:

arg min $D R _ { j } \left[ \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { m } \Im _ { i i } \right]$

## 3. Framework for validating the algorithm

## 3.1. Alternate data representations

Fig. 1 illustrates that different data representations can be generated from the same instantiation of a formal theory of a model of data. An example of this notion is also depicted in Fig. 1 where, for the same instantiation of a relational database, alternate data representations can be manifested as multiple external schemas/views.

The relational model of data is only one model of data and, thus, instantiations of relational databases are instantiations of only one type of model of data. Alternative models of data include object-oriented and objectrelational.<sup>5</sup> Each model of data has its own associated formal theory. Moreover, each different formal theory leads to instantiations of different types of databases. Because they are based on different theoretical models of data, these instantiations exhibit different functionalities and different strengths and weaknesses [43]. Each instantiation can provide the basis for a number of alternate data representations manifested as different external schemas/views. Fig. 2a depicts this situation for two formal theories of models of data. Basford et al. [3] tested the algorithm presented in Section 2.2 using two alternate external schemas/views derived from an instantiation of a relational database. This verification by Ref. [3] is depicted by the dashed boxes in Fig. 2b where external schema/view 1 was in first normal form and external schema/view 2 was in third normal form.

As stated above, each different formal theory leads to instantiations of different types of databases. Each of these instantiations can be considered as alternate data representations. Furthermore, alternate external schemas/views can be derived from the underlying instantiation of a particular theory of a model of data. This research empirically tests the algorithm presented in Section 2.2 using two instantiations derived from two different models of data.

## 3.1.1. Instantiations tested

Two different database instantiations were developed from two different formal theories of models of data. The first was an instantiation of the relational model of data (relational) and the second was an instantiation of an object-relational (OOR) model of data. Fig. 2b depicts this situation for the two formal theories of models of data. This comparison is depicted by the double-lined boxes in Fig. 2b.<sup>6</sup>

The relational model of data is the most widely used model in database technology [15,19,25,40]. Implementations of the relational model include a simple, portable, expressive language (usually SQL) with a multitude of efficient implementations [40]. At present, many organizations rely on data stored in relational databases to enable rapid responses to changing conditions [20].

Although the relational model is currently the predominate choice for data base implementations, a number of researchers have advocated using the objectoriented paradigm to model real-world phenomena through the primary building blocks of object and class [see, e.g., Refs. 5,18,27,31,34]. Object-oriented (OO) database technology represents an alternative to the dominant relational model. OO database technology overcomes some of the problems in modeling complex data types and relationships that occur using the relational model [17]. The question of which database format is better, relational or object-oriented, has produced considerable debate. Date [15] asserts that object classes and relational domains share essentially identical characteristics. Zdonik [49], however, argues that object oriented database management systems provide a greater level of support to the query and update processes than relational database management systems. OO advocates have asserted that users’ interactions with object schemas should be more productive than users interactions with traditional relational schemas. This expectation of increased productivity arises in large part from the perceived lower level of complexity of tasks using the object paradigm. The lower complexity levels derive from direct support of the major tenets of abstraction including generalization/specialization hierarchies (GSHs), other features such as abstract data types (ADTs), and tight coupling of the data structure and methods (functions) [9,12]. These features have led to the prediction that an increasing number of companies will be investing in this new technology [17].

Compared with relational systems, however, objectoriented database management systems have not yet fully developed sophisticated controls in the areas of data capture, flexible query development, backup and recovery, and concurrency [33]. The strengths and weaknesses of both the relational and object-oriented systems have led to hybrid systems that allow analysts and designers to exploit the power of the object-oriented paradigm but within a relational implementation. The resulting object-relational (OOR) databases support abstract data types and the concepts of inheritance without losing the advantages of the relational model [1,38]. Thus, OOR provides OO features layered over the relational model [10,16,47].

This increased complexity heightens the probability that end users querying 3NF instantiations will make more errors than those querying OOR instantiations.<sup>7</sup> Such differences between the syntax of equivalent OOR and 3NF queries highlight the differences in complexity end users confront when they query different data representations derived from instantiations of different models of data.

## 3.3. Metrics for measuring complexity

During the past 30 years more than 100 alternative metrics of software complexity have been proposed (see, e.g., Ref. [23,24,37]). Fortunately, most of these software complexity metrics are highly correlated with each other [2,33]. Halstead’s [23] suite of metrics provide a carefully developed and thoroughly tested set of measures for assessing software complexity and, in the context of this study, for calculating the complexity of each query for each schema.

Halstead specified three increasingly sophisticated complexity metrics: program length, difficulty, and effort. Halstead’s program length metric (N) provides a simple, linear measure of program complexity. The length metric is the total number of operators $( N _ { 1 } )$ (keywords and punctuation) plus the total number of operands $( N _ { 2 } )$ (variables and constants) in a query, i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { N { = } N _ { 1 } { + } N _ { 2 } . } \end{array}$ 8

Halstead’s difficulty measure is based on the assumption that end users experience greater difficulty based, in part, on the number of unique items in the query [45]. Halstead’s difficulty metric improves on the program length metric in two important ways. First, it includes a term that counts only the unique occurrences of each operator $\left( \eta _ { 1 } \right)$ and operand $( \eta _ { 2 } )$ where $\eta = \eta _ { 1 } + \eta _ { 2 }$ . Second, it produces a normalized metric by dividing an expression containing the total and unique count of operators and operands by an analogous expression for the minimum conceivable counts of operators $( \eta _ { 1 } ^ { * } )$ and operands $( \eta _ { 2 } ^ { * } )$ where $\eta ^ { * } = \eta _ { 1 } ^ { * } + \eta _ { 2 } ^ { * }$ Thus, Halstead’s difficulty metric is the volume of a program (or query), $V { = } N \mathrm { l o g } _ { 2 } \eta$ , divided by its potential volume, $\begin{array} { r } { V ^ { * } { = } \eta ^ { * } \mathrm { l o g } _ { 2 } \eta ^ { * } } \end{array}$

Halstead’s effort metric represents the total number of elementary mental discriminations required to generate a query [23]. Halstead’s effort metric assumes that the mental effort required to implement any algorithm varies with the square of its volume, rather than linearly. That is, as queries become more complex, values of the effort metric increase at an increasing rate. Halstead’s effort metric is calculated by dividing the square of the volume by the potential volume, i.e., V<sup>2</sup> / V\*.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Experiment

In a laboratory experiment, 59 student participants<sup>9</sup> composed and executed SQL queries for a database in either relational (3NF) or object-relational (OOR) format (see Appendix A). Prior to this experiment, all participants had received more than 10 h of classroom training<sup>10</sup> and had participated in two prior, two-hour SQL quizzes. At the beginning of the two-hour experimental session, participants received a brief description of the hypothetical setting, a diagram of the database schema, a text specification of their tables and the attributes in each table, and the ten information requests. These ten information requests (see Table 1, Panel A) are typical of information requests that occur in business situations. The ten information requests, together with the model query solution for each of the two treatments, are contained in Appendix B.

The experimental interface provided the participants with one information request at a time. For each information request, the participants could revise and resubmit their query as many times as they wished. Once an information request had been deemed completed by the participant, the participant could not return to it. The participants’ interaction with the database was logged both at their individual workstations and at the server. After each query attempt was executed, the system displayed the SQL result, i.e., either the rows returned by the query or a syntax error message. When participants indicated that they were satisfied with the result they obtained for a particular request, they were prompted to specify their confidence that the results for that query were correct on the following five point scale: not at all confident, not confident, neutral, confident, and very confident. After indicating their confidence level, participants proceeded to the next information request. The correct query formulations were generally increasing in complexity.

To control for experience and education effects, participants were assigned to one of two groups using the following procedure. Students were ranked according to their GPA and the student with the highest GPA was assigned to group 1, the next highest to group 2, to group 2, to group 1, etc. This method of assignment was intended to make the two groups as equivalent as possible.<sup>12</sup> Each group was then randomly (by coin toss) assigned to either the OOR or 3NF treatment. Thirty participants were assigned to the OOR group and 29 to the 3NF group. Self-reported demographic data indicated that the majority of participants had no significant exposure to either databases or SQL prior to taking the subject.

## 4.2. Operationalizing the variables

## 4.2.1. Dependent variables

The dependent variable for Proposition 1 is the number of semantic errors. The total number of semantic errors was determined by counting the number of semantic errors in the last attempt for each information request, i.e., the minimum number of changes to reach a semantically correct solution.<sup>13</sup> That is, the number of semantic errors was determined by counting the number of changes required to change the participants query such that it was semantically correct.<sup>14</sup> Information requests that were not attempted by a particular participant were not included in the scoring. Furthermore, the final question being attempted at the end of the twohour period was not included when it was obvious to both data coders that the SQL query was not complete. Two experienced individuals independently performed the coding task. They cross-checked their error coding sheets for correctness and consistency and resolved any differences. Given the criteria of making the minimum number of changes to reach a semantically correct solution, after reexamining each query and each coder’s solution, the coders were always able to reach agreement on the number of errors, if any, in each participant’s query.

The dependent variable for Proposition 2 is the length of time (in min) participants took to complete the query for each information request. The values for this variable were determined by examining time stamps in the log files.

The dependent variable for Proposition 3 is the participants’ self-assessed confidence levels. Participants entered this measure on the following scale: not at all confident, not confident, neutral, confident, very confident. The values were transformed using a five point scale as follows: ratings of not at all confident, transformed to 1, through to ratings of very confident, transformed to 5.

## 4.2.2. Independent variables

The independent variable was the group (instantiation of the data model). Group was a categorical variable with values of 3NF or OOR.

To ensure that the effect of end-user ability had been taken into account, grade point average (GPA) was used as a covariate. Recall that the information requests were generally in order of increasing difficulty of the SQL challenges they posed to participants. The information request (query) number was a categorical variable with values of 1 to 10. The results of the two data models tested were analyzed by information request number, that is, group is <sup>b</sup>nested<sup>Q</sup> within information request number. The results for the main model and effects are presented in Section 5 and Table 2. The results of the query-by-query analyses are analyzed in Section 5 with relevant tables presented in Appendix C.

## 5. Results

The results indicate that, in absolute terms, participants generated fewer errors (5.93 per information request for 3NF versus 3.45 per information request for OOR) and required less time (9.54 min per information request for 3NF versus 3.45 min per information request for OOR) when composing queries using the OOR instantiation. Participants, however, reported lower confidence using the OOR instantiation (3.28 for 3NF versus 2.84 for OOR).

## 5.1. The effect of 3NF versus OOR on user errors

Because, for each of the sample of ten information requests, the OOR instantiation yields less complex queries, Proposition 1 implies that users of the OOR instantiation would generate fewer semantic errors than users of the 3NF instantiation. Nested MANCOVA results indicate that the users’ accuracy is significantly associated with the instantiation $( F _ { 9 , 3 4 8 } = 1 5 . 5 1$ $p { = } 0 . 0 0 0 1$ , two-tailed test) (Table 2, Panel A). The mean results confirm that the relationship is in the direction predicted. On a query-by-query basis, for eight of the ten information requests, results (seven being significant) were in the direction predicted (Appendix C, Table C.1).<sup>15</sup> These results provide strong support for Proposition 1.

## 5.2. The effect of 3NF versus OOR on time taken

Because, for each of the sample of ten information requests, the OOR instantiation yields less complex queries, Proposition 2 implies that users of the OOR instantiation would take less time to formulate the appropriate queries than 3NF end users. Nested MAN-COVA results indicate that the length of time to prepare queries is not significantly associated with the instantiation $( F _ { 9 , 3 2 7 } = 1 . 1 8 , p = 0 . 3 0 1 3$ two-tailed test) (Table 2, Panel B). While the overall results are not statistically significant, the mean results confirm the relationship is in the direction predicted. The results could have been affected by the fact that the participants were much more familiar with querying instantiations of the relational data model.<sup>16</sup>

## 5.3. The effect of 3NF versus OOR on user confidence

Because, for each of the ten information requests, the OOR instantiation yields less complex queries, Proposition 3 implies that users of the OOR instantiation would exhibit higher confidence in the accuracy of their queries than 3NF end users. Nested MANOVA results indicate that the users confidence is significantly associated with the instantiation $( F _ { 9 , 3 1 5 } = 2 . 2 5 , ~ p = 0 . 0 1 5 2 , ~$ two-tailed test) (Table 2, Panel C). The mean results, however, indicate that the relationship is in the direction opposite to that predicted. On a query-by-query basis, for six of the ten information requests, results (two being significant) were in the direction opposite to that predicted (Appendix C, Table C.1). This unexpected result is likely caused by the users being more familiar with querying 3NF instantiations. Another possible explanation is that the OOR users may have been surprised by the simplicity (low complexity) of their query formulations.<sup>17</sup>

## 6. Implications

This research provides important implications for improving end-user queries. The results support the assertion that the data representations associated with less complex query formulations for a representative set of information requests produce more effective enduser queries. The use of three different complexity metrics led to the same data representation being predicted as optimal. All three metrics were approximately equally effective at predicting performance within each data representation.

Organizations can apply the theory to help reduce query complexity and enhance end-users’ ability to extract, efficiently and effectively, desired information from organizational data repositories. When faced with alternate data representations, organizations can use a set of representative information requests to determine which data representation is associated with the least complex query formulations and, thus, optimize enduser query performance.

The findings of this study significantly impact current information systems (IS)/information technology (IT) teaching and research. The current environment encourages database designers to use the relational model with a normalization level of 3NF or higher [34,35]. This study found that for the set of representative information requests used here, the OOR instantiation (the data representation associated with the least complex queries) allowed end users to produce queries that were more effective (accurate) than their 3NF counterparts.<sup>20</sup> The results of this study imply that organizations can use the approach advocated in this paper to examine, ex ante, the relative desirability of different database instantiations developed from different formal theories of models of data. Each organization has a different set of critical success factors. Different sets of critical success factors typically require different configurations of theoretical models of data, different instantiations of those models, and different sets of external schemas. This ex ante approach can be used by organizations to evaluate alternative configurations prior to making investments in specific database products or training. That is, the theory can be used by organizations to improve the return on their investments in their data repositories.

## 7. Conclusions, limitations, and future research

This research extended the applicability of a theory for evaluating, ex ante, the relative desirability of alternative data representations for end-user queries. The theory asserts that the data representation that yields the lowest weighted average complexity for a representative sample of information requests is the most desirable data representation for end-user queries. The theory was tested in an experiment that compared two different database instantiations developed from two different formal theories of models of data. The first was an instantiation of the relational model of data (relational) and the second was an instantiation of an object-relational model of data (OOR). The sample queries for the OOR data representation yielded lower weighted average complexity for the representative sample of information requests and, as such, were predicted to be more desirable than the relational data representation. As predicted, users querying the OOR data representation performed more effectively than those querying the 3NF data representation. These results occurred in spite of bias toward the relational instantiation. That is, the participants had extensive prior experience with querying relational instantiations but no prior experience querying OOR instantiations. Thus, even with the difference in enduser preparation favoring the relational schema, the theory for selecting the most desirable schema for end-user queries was validated by the experimental results, i.e., end users composed more accurate queries using the OOR instantiation.

There are a number of limitations to this study. First, the study did not calculate other metrics and compare them against the Halstead metrics. Future studies could use (or create) other complexity metrics. Second, the study used students as participants. The participants have, however, received training in IT and businessrelated subjects such that their level of query proficiency was likely to be typical of end users in many organizations. Third, the order in which the information requests were received by the participants was not random.

Future research could investigate which specific characteristics of queries cause users the most difficulty. The research should be validated with users in realworld settings with targeted training. Second, a more in-depth analysis needs to be conducted to determine why the OOR users were not more confident than 3NF users even though they made significantly fewer errors. Such research could lead to a better matching of confidence with query accuracy and thus improve the effectiveness of decision making processes. Third, future research could propose new metrics to deal with the multi-faceted issue of data representation complexity and how various facets affect user queries. Creation of new, more robust query complexity metrics would be beneficial for research into many other aspects of user queries.
