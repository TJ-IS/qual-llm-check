Abstract. This study examines the team-level effects of pair programming by developing a research model that accounts for mediators and moderators of the relationship between pair programming and team performance. We hypothesize that pair programming helps software development teams establish backup behavior by strengthening the shared mental models among developers. In turn, backup behavior attenuates the negative effect of task novelty on team performance. We collect data from the software developers, Scrum masters, and product owners of 62 software development teams in a global enterprise software firm and find broad support for our research model. The study makes important contributions by shifting attention to the team-level effects of pair programming and by explicating mediating and moderating mechanisms related to the roles of shared mental models, backup behavior, and task novelty. The results underline the importance of viewing pair programming as a context-specific practice that helps establish backup behavior in teams. In terms of implications for practitioners, our results show that pair programming can be a valuable element of team governance to create shared mental models and backup behavior and to achieve high team performance when teams face high levels of task novelty.

## 1. Introduction

Software development organizations and digital ventures must continuously adapt to changing customer needs and develop high-quality software in tandem with the speed of the market (Huang et al. 2017). Firms increasingly use agile software development to cope with the needs of a more dynamic competitive environment (Ramasubbu et al. 2015). For example, large software providers, including Microsoft, Adobe, and SAP (e.g., Schmidt et al. 2014), often use Scrum or Extreme Programming (XP), two popular agile methods for software development. XP proposes a set of software development practices, among which pair programming is a central and widely adopted one (Balijepally et al. 2009). When using pair programming, two software developers share one computer to jointly design, develop, or test software code (Williams et al. 2000).

Although prior work suggests that assigning a task to two programmers instead of one can increase the quality of the developed software code and the speed with which the task is completed, most existing studies on the performance effects of pair programming focus on the individual and dyadic levels (Balijepally et al. 2009, Mangalaraj et al. 2014) and not on the team settings in which real-world software development usually takes place (Maruping et al. 2009a). Therefore, in this study, we sharply focus on the team level in considering how pair programming alters teamwork factors that contribute to team performance. To more definitively address questions regarding the effects of pair programming, our team-level study explicates the theoretical mechanisms underlying performance effects (Dyba et al.˚ 2007). In particular, we theorize and empirically examine mediators and moderators of the relationship between pair programming and team performance. In terms of mediators, we study how pair programming affects team performance by altering the team’s cognitive structures and behavioral patterns, and in terms of moderators, we study the contingent role of task novelty.

Our theoretical model draws on the teamwork liter ature (e.g., Salas et al. 2005) to understand how pair programming helps to create the structures and processes needed for teams to thrive, even in the face of task novelty. If team members frequently require new capabilities or processes in carrying out their activities (i.e., if task novelty is high), software development teams may particularly benefit from close collaboration during the actual software development work to capitalize on the synergies between the capabilities and efforts of team members (Marks et al. 2001, Burke et al. 2006). We argue that institutionalizing pair programming helps teams to establish backup behavior by increasing the shared mental models among team members (Mohammed and Dumville 2001, Porter et al. 2003). In turn, backup behavior positively affects team performance in teams facing high task novelty. To validate our research model, we conducted an empirical study on the use of pair programming at Alpha (pseudonym), one of the largest enterprise software firms worldwide. Our study builds on numerous formal and informal interviews at Alpha; the main part of our study is a survey of 62 software development teams at Alpha, which draws on unique data from 377 software developers as well as from the Scrum masters and product owners of the 62 teams.

Our findings provide support for our developed research model, and we make at least two important contributions.<sup>1</sup> First, we examine the team-level effects of pair programming, thus extending prior work that has mostly examined outcomes related to the pair’s focal task or other individual-level factors such as satisfaction (e.g., Balijepally et al. 2009). Second, we draw on the extant teamwork literature, particularly works on backup behavior and team adaptation (e.g., Porter et al. 2003, Burke et al. 2006), to understand the theoretical mechanisms underlying the effect of pair programming on team performance. Together these contributions provide important implications for further research and practice.

## 2. Background and Theoretical Framework 2.1. Background

Although the notion of collaborative programming has a long history, pair programming has gained widespread popularity during the last two decades though the formulation of XP.<sup>2</sup> Prior work has made impressive strides in understanding the adoption of pair programming (e.g., Mangalaraj et al. 2009), its adaptation over time and in different circumstances (e.g., Vidgen and Wang 2009), and its application for educational purposes (e.g., Salleh et al. 2011). Given the potential benefits of, as well as the substantial resource requirements for, pair programming, a large body of research has assessed the outcomes of pair programming (e.g., Balijepally et al. 2009, Mangalaraj et al. 2014).

Our review of this body of work on the performance effects of pair programming reveals at least two areas that deserve further attention. First, the majority of prior studies on the performance effects of pair programming have focused on the focal dyad and its immediate task; few studies have accounted for team-level effects. For example, prior work has studied the quality of the code developed by pairs versus individuals, the respective velocity with which the code is completed, and the effort of pair programming as opposed to solo programming (Williams et al. 2000, Hannay et al. 2009). Overall, this line of work suggests that pair programming increases code quality and velocity, but these benefits come with increased overall effort compared with solo development (e.g., Williams et al. 2000) It is noteworthy that not all studies have confirmed positive results, particularly those related to enhanced quality and reduced time to market (e.g., Arisholm et al. 2007). Some attempts to reconcile these inconsistent findings suggest that the effects may hinge on contextual factors, such as task characteristics or pair composition (Lui and Chan 2006, Domino et al. 2007, Hannay et al. 2009). Despite the important insights generated by these studies, few, if any, large-scale empirical studies on the link between pair programming and the performance of professional software development teams exist.<sup>3</sup> This is surprising given that in a professional context, pair programming is mostly embedded in team settings, and its application will therefore likely have teamlevel effects. For example, software development in organizations is often carried out by teams following the agile project management method Scrum (Schwaber and Beedle 2001).

Second, the majority of prior studies examining the performance effects of pair programming do not articulate the underlying mechanisms and do not engage with the rich body of prior literature on teamwork and team processes in other settings (e.g., Marks et al. 2001, Salas et al. 2005, Burke et al. 2006). Two notable exceptions are the experimental studies on pair programming versus solo programming by Balijepally et al. (2009) in the context of software development and by Mangalaraj et al. (2014) in the context of software design. However, these studies focused on dyadicand individual-level outcomes, and they do not reveal the underlying theoretical mechanisms to explain the effects of pair programming at the team level.

## 2.2. Hypothesis Development

Our key argument is that pair programming creates team-level performance effects by fostering backup behavior among team members. Pair programming increases the shared mental models among team members and thereby backup behavior in teams. In turn, backup behavior helps teams attenuate the negative performance effect of task novelty. Figure 1 shows our research model.

We focus on backup behavior, defined as the provision of resources and task-related effort to a teammate to prevent that teammate from failing to reach his or her goals (Porter et al. 2003), as a key mediator for two reasons. First, establishing backup behavior in software development teams is not well understood and may be difficult to achieve. In contemporary software development that often relies on autonomous teams, central authorities or processes for shifting responsibilities in case of work overload or lack of capabilities may be missing. For example, despite the regular interaction between team members prescribed by Scrum (Schwaber and Beedle 2001), prior work suggests that “[w]hat people should do to provide backup is not specified clearly in Scrum” (Moe et al. 2010, p. 489). Second, existing descriptive and conceptual work provides indications that pair programming is linked to backup behavior in teams. Coman et al. (2014) provide a descriptive longitudinal account of two software development teams and study pair programming, where programmers collaborate to work on a joint task, and backup behavior among developers that can happen within and outside pairs. Moe and colleagues surmise that in the context of self-managed teams, such as Scrum teams, pair programming may contribute to establishing backup behavior (Moe and Dingsøyr 2008, Moe et al. 2010).

Prior teamwork literature suggests that backup behavior plays a critical role in the process of team adaptation, defined as a team’s adjustment to changing environments in terms of nonroutine situations that the team faces and that have the potential to disturb the current team processes (Burke et al. 2006, Rosen et al. 2011). Team adaptation theory suggests that teams react to nonroutine situations by iterating a number of stages. They (1) assess and build a coherent understanding of the new situation, (2) adjust their plans, (3) execute the new plans, and (4) learn from their response to the new situation (Burke et al. 2006, Rosen et al. 2011). Backup behavior is a key behavioral pattern in coping with nonroutine situations during the plan execution phase of team adaptation (Burke et al. 2006, Rosen et al. 2011). In fact, team adaptability has been described as the “[a]bility to adjust strategies based on information gathered from the environment through backup behavior and the reallocation of intrateam resources” (Salas et al. 2005, p. 560). Building on these insights, we expect that teams are more likely to need backup behavior when they face high levels of task novelty, which we define as the degree to which the activities that team members perform require capabilities or processes that are new to some or all members of the team. For example, in the context of enterprise software development, teams working on analytics solutions may face high task novelty because development activities require rapidly evolving capabilities and processes related to big data and artificial intelligence.

Next, we discuss how backup behavior attenuates the negative effect of task novelty on the performance of software development teams and how pair programming helps teams establish backup behavior.

2.2.1. How Backup Behavior and Task Novelty Affect Team Performance. We propose an interaction effect of backup behavior and task novelty such that backup behavior among team members helps teams mitigate the negative effect of task novelty on team performance. We provide three rationales for this effect related to responding to task misallocation, to leveraging team members’ excess capacities, and to integrating team members’ capabilities and efforts.

First, backup behavior likely makes misallocations of tasks less consequential. If the task novelty is low, it is likely that the teams have developed sufficient metaknowledge to adequately assign tasks to team members (Faraj and Sproull 2000, Hoegl et al. 2003) For example, in Scrum teams, team members pick and work on a new backlog item once the previous one is considered “done” (Schwaber and Beedle 2001). In the case of low task novelty, team members are likely to pick backlog items for which they possess the required capabilities and leave others to fellow team members who possess the respective knowledge and skills. By contrast, if the task novelty is high, such metaknowledge may be missing, and the tasks may be allocated to team members lacking the required capabilities or the preexisting knowledge to develop these capabilities. Backup behavior may make the misallocation of tasks less consequential. Backup behavior is associated with higher team performance if the team members have an objective and legitimate need for backup (Porter et al. 2003, Barnes et al. 2008). The misallocation of tasks to team members results in such an objective and legitimate need for backup, and we therefore expect backup behavior to improve team performance if tasks are misallocated as a result of high task novelty. By contrast, if teams have not established backup behavior (and therefore objective and legitimate needs for backup remain unaddressed), nonoptimal task allocation may be particularly problematic as developers continue to struggle with their tasks, slowing down the team or reducing the quality of the output.

Second, we expect backup behavior to help ensure that excess capacity of team members is used to support teammates, which is particularly critical if task novelty is high. If task novelty is high, the capabilities and processes required to perform the team’s tasks may be entirely absent in the team. Task novelty may also mean that only one or a few team members possess the capabilities and processes required to perform tasks, potentially resulting in an imbalanced workload distributions and an overload for some team members (Porter et al. 2010). In both cases, task novelty likely undermines the ability of team members to produce high-quality software in an adequate period of time (Boh et al. 2007, Chow and Cao 2008). Even if other teammates do not possess the needed capabilities, we expect backup behavior to attenuate the negative effect of task novelty on team performance if teammates with excess capacity carry a share of the workload in terms of developing and applying the required capabilities and processes. By contrast, teams that fail to provide each other backup may show lower team performance because they forgo opportunities to make use of available resources.

Third, backup behavior may help teams better integrate the complementary capabilities and efforts of team members, which we expect to be particularly valuable if teams face high levels of task novelty. Contemporary software development methods, such as Scrum, iterate rapidly between phases of planning and evaluation on the one hand and phases of coding on the other hand (Conboy 2009; Fowler and Highsmith 2001).<sup>4</sup> Fast iterations between these phases may help teams routinize the process of responding to evolving customer requests but may not suffice for teams to handle task novelty in terms of changes in the capabilities and processes required to fulfill the tasks (Moe and Dingsøyr 2008, Moe et al. 2010). Related studies on team adaptability suggest that this is because when faced with task novelty, teams move from a composition mode to a compilation mode (Kozlowski and Klein 2000, Burke et al. 2006). When working in a composition mode, a team’s outcome is the aggregation of relatively similar contributions, with tasks being subject to pooled interdependence. When working in a compilation mode, the overall outcome follows a “patterned emergence model,” and team members focus on and bring in different capabilities—thus engaging in “different types and amounts of actions during performance” (Burke et al. 2006, p. 1192). Given that backup behavior implies that team members interact during the coding phase by providing each other with task-related effort, backup behavior likely helps teams integrate the capabilities of developers to successfully adopt a compilation mode when the task novelty is high (Burke et al. 2006). By contrast, if backup behavior in teams is absent, it may be more difficult to integrate complementary capabilities during the coding phases, thereby resulting in lower team performance.

Hypothesis 1. Backup behavior moderates the negative effect of task novelty on team performance such that the effect is attenuated in teams with high levels of backup behavior and prevails in teams with low levels of backup behavior.

2.2.2. How Pair Programming Enhances Backup Behavior in Teams Through Shared Mental Models. We argue that pair programming likely helps teams establish backup behavior by strengthening the shared mental models among team members. Shared menta models are “team members’ shared, organized understanding and mental representation of knowledge about key elements of the team’s relevant environment” (Mohammed and Dumville 2001, p. 90).

How does pair programming influence shared mental models? We expect pair programming to help teams strengthen the shared mental models among team members because it enables interaction and communication during coding phases. Practices in contemporary software development are mostly geared toward interaction and communication during planning/ evaluation phases. For example, teams that follow Scrum hold sprint planning, daily stand-ups, and retrospective meetings (Pikkarainen et al. 2008)—and even those development teams that rely on a traditional waterfall approach often engage in collaborative planning and evaluation (Vidgen and Wang 2009). Although such communication among team members can contribute to the development of shared mental models to a certain extent, developing shared mental models may often require the exchange of implicit knowledge through socialization during actual task work (Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995). This is particularly the case in knowledge work, such as software development, where expert knowledge is frequently implicit in nature (Ryan and O’Connor 2013).

Even in contemporary software development with its focus on collaboration, the coding phase is often carried out by individuals working on their computers without interacting with others (Moe et al. 2010), complicating the development and maintenance of shared mental models (Levesque et al. 2001). By contrast, when relying on pair programming, software developers engage in intimate observation and discussions during coding phases, which we expect to strengthen shared mental models of team members. For example, pair programming likely contributes to the convergence of the involved developers’ visions regarding the future development of the software as well as its underlying architecture (He et al. 2007). The shared mental models of pairing members likely propagate to the team level as teams rotate pairing partners (Vidgen and Wang 2009) and because the implicit knowledge of pairing partners is externalized in the form of code, code comments, and other types of documentation (Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995).

How do shared mental models contribute to backup behavior? To provide backup, team members need to be able to assess whether the provision of assistance is required and to actually provide assistance (Dickinson and McIntyre 1997). Assessing whether assistance is needed and providing assistance are more likely to be possible if the team members are “on the same page” regarding the specifics of their current activities (Salas et al. 2005). This is particularly the case in the context of software development because software developers create products that can be used in various ways and contexts and that often have unforeseen dependencies across different components (Lee 1999, Banker and Slaughter 2000, Markus and Silver 2008). Thus, software development teams likely require a higher degree of shared mental models in order to be able to provide backup than they would in case of more mechanistic tasks, which have often been the subject of prior experimental studies on backup behavior— for example, relatively simple simulations of combat situations that could be unambiguously described, and whose outcome could be easily observed (Porter et al. 2003, Barnes et al. 2008). Hence, we argue that in the context of software development, shared mental models likely increase team members’ ability to assess the need for assistance and to provide assistance.

On the basis of the foregoing discussion, we posit that the practice of pair programming will help teams establish backup behavior because it strengthens the shared mental models of team members.

Hypothesis 2. Team members’ shared mental models mediate the positive effect of pair programming on backup behavior such that pair programming increases backup behavior because it positively affects shared mental models.

In addition to the indirect effect of pair programming on backup behavior mediated by shared mental models, pair programming will likely also have a residual direct positive effect on backup behavior. In particular, the use of pair programming will increase the willingness of team members to offer and accept assistance and support independent of shared mental models for three key reasons (Porter et al. 2003, Salas et al. 2005). First, teams that regularly rely on pair programming will likely develop behavioral routines of monitoring and assisting teammates, which can be expected to extend to situations outside the pairs (Gersick and Hackman 1990, Coman et al. 2014). Second, when conducting pair programming, developers jointly evaluate and discuss the potential consequences of coding-related decisions. These reflections will likely increase the developers’ awareness that the issues faced by teammates can slow down and complicate their own work, and vice versa (Burke et al. 2006), besides contributing to shared mental models. Third, team members working in pairs share joint experiences, which may contribute to an atmosphere of psychological safety, making team members more comfortable in offering and accepting assistance and thus reducing the “social cost” of assisting each other (Edmondson 1999, Lee 2002).

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Organizational Context

One of the unique strengths of this study is that it draws on real-world data from one of the largest enterprise software firms in the world, which we refer to as Alpha. The organization had more than 90,000 employees and revenues of more than US\$20 billion in 2018. Alpha develops a diverse set of software products for mostly larger business clients—including enterprise resource planning applications and customer relationship management applications—which are offered as on-premises and cloud-based solutions and rely on different programming languages. Alpha’s development teams contribute to the ongoing development, operation, and maintenance of these software products.

Traditionally, software development and maintenance at Alpha were organized around development projects with individual developers and project managers and followed a waterfall-type software development methodology. After a large organizational transformation around 2008, Alpha started to rely on self-managed teams as the main organizational unit, which followed the agile project management meth odology Scrum. Scrum delegates authority to development teams, which are responsible for the functionality and quality of the developed code. In addition to granting autonomy to teams, Scrum also provides discipline by defining explicit roles, meetings, and artifacts. Scrum specifies several roles in a development team. The software developers are responsible for the actual development work, a Scrum master is responsible for establishing the Scrum work mode and eliminating impediments, and a product owner represents the customers of the Scrum team and defines the development targets. Scrum teams follow an iterative work mode defined by a sprint—a time period with a length of one to four weeks, which is characterized by a number of team meetings prescribed by the Scrum method. During a sprint, Scrum teams “burn down” a sprint backlog of the features to be implemented, with the goal of presenting a working software prototype after each sprint (Schwaber and Beedle 2001). At the time of data collection, Alpha had more than 400 Scrum teams in its main development location.

Although Scrum had helped the organization work iteratively and in close collaboration with internal and external customers and thus be able to better accommodate requirements changes, the teams still struggled when changing tasks could not be addressed with the existing capabilities and processes. The organization realized that “Scrum alone is not enough because it defines only the project management aspects” and because “it deliberately omits technical practices” (Alpha training materials). To counteract these shortcomings, Alpha decided to train its Scrum teams in agile work practices, including pair programming.<sup>5</sup> During a one-week period of classroom training, entire development teams were trained through hands-on exercises. For example, with regard to pair programming, the teams were advised not only to alternate frequently between the driver (who is actively writing code) and the navigator (who is observing and giving feedback) but also to “alternate pairing within [the] team usually every 1–2 days” (Alpha training materials). Afterward, experts coached the teams in their usual work environment for another three weeks. Alpha’s expectation was that developing in pairs would help teams “[d]o it right from the beginning” and achieve “[k]nowledge and skill distribution” in the teams (Alpha training materials). Despite the company’s investment in the training program, the teams were not obliged to use pair programming in their daily work. Instead, they could independently decide whether and to what extent they would develop code in pairs. Hence, the case of Alpha provides an ideal setting for studying the effects of pair programming on teamwork factors and on team performance in the context of self-managed teams.

## 3.2. Data-Collection Procedure and Sample

We collected data from software development teams at Alpha by developing survey instruments that leveraged our experience and insights into the organization. Before developing and administering the survey, one of the authors was embedded in the studied organization over a period of two years, working part time for Alpha and at the same time pursuing a PhD in information systems (IS). During this time, we conducted interviews with various stakeholders to become acquainted with the organizational context and Alpha’s software development approach. In particular, we interviewed more than 30 developers from various teams, 15 project managers, and several trainers in software development practices who had insights into more than 50 teams. Moreover, one of the authors attended the training program where pair programming was introduced to developers. We developed survey instruments by adapting existing scales to the context of our study and by developing new measures as appropriate. To ensure construct validity, we conducted pretests with several of Alpha’s senior developers, as well as with IS researchers. In addition, we validated the adapted scales using a cardsorting exercise with more than 150 undergraduate and graduate management and IS students. We modified and refined several items based on the feedback from these validation exercises.

All the data for our study were collected in person and on site at Alpha using standardized, paper-based questionnaires. We used role-specific questionnaires to collect insights from different perspectives, thus effectively using a procedural remedy for common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003). Specifically, the variables used to test our hypotheses come from different questionnaires filled out by different groups of respondents. The data on team performance were collected from product owners. The data on task novelty were provided by the teams’ Scrum masters. The data on backup behavior, shared mental models, and pair programming were collected from the software developers in each team (see the appendix for details on the items, respondents, and scales). Participation in the study was completely voluntary, and the teams jointly made the decision to participate. We incentivized teams to participate by announcing a team-specific, comprehensive report for each team that we would compile based on the study’s results, as well as a lottery at the end of the data collection, which offered team events for two winning teams.

We asked 120 colocated development teams at Alpha’s main development location to participate, and we obtained full data sets for 62 teams. On average, 7.1 team members responded per team (including software developers and the Scrum master), which reflects a team internal response rate of 79%. In total, we collected team internal data from 377 software developers and 62 Scrum masters. To collect these data, we asked teams to participate in a 30- to 60-minute meeting. After a short introduction, we distributed the respective paper-based questionnaires to the developers and the Scrum master, to be completed individually. At least one researcher was present in the room to clarify questions while the participants were filling out the questionnaires. The opportunity to thoroughly explain the study, clarify questions, and observe participants filling out the paper-based questionnaires further increased our confidence that the collected data would be of high reliability. After collection of the team-internal data was completed, we approached the product owners of the participating teams to fill out questionnaires related to the teams for which they were responsible. In total, we collected responses from 34 product owners who were responsible for the 62 teams in our sample. After completing the data-collection process, all questionnaires were transcribed redundantly by two research assistants to ensure correctness of the analyzed data.

The average team size (including the software developers and the Scrum master) in the sample was nine team members, and more than 75% of the team members had worked for at least one year on their team. Table 1 provides additional descriptive information on our sample.

## 3.3. Measures

Team Performance. We conceptualize team performance (PERFORMANCE) as the comparative performance evaluation of a software development team by its key stakeholders, such as product owners acting as customer representatives in a Scrum context. The salience of the different facets of team performance may vary depending on the context. In some cases, producing error-free code may be the primary goal of the development efforts (e.g., when developing accounting solutions). In other cases, innovativeness may move to the fore (e.g., when introducing a new mobile analytics solution). Although the ontime and on-budget completion often has been central to the measurement of software development team performance, software development work is not lim ited to one-off projects; it also includes ongoing efforts to develop, operate, and maintain software products. Thus, on-time and on-budget completion may not apply. To overcome the challenges associated with the context sensitivity of team performance and to capture the varying facets relevant in particular contexts, we used a stakeholder perspective and assessed the degree to which teams fulfill expectations. We used four items, which we adapted from existing studies. An example item is as follows: “Reports on the performance of this team are always favorable.” The items for team performance were rated by the product owners on a sevenpoint Likert scale, with anchors from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”).

Task Novelty. We used three items to measure task novelty (NOVELTY). One example item was the following: “Concerning the last six months, the team faced tasks for which there was a clearly known way to solve them.” The Scrum master of the team provided his or her assessment for the team on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”).

Pair Programming. We measured the use of pair programming (PAIR) by asking the software developers of the teams to rate their personal adoption level of pair programming. We used two items to assess the use of pair programming. For example, we asked software developers: “How much of your code do you develop with a programming partner?” The developers rated their personal behavior on a tenpoint scale, with anchors ranging from 0.0 (“0%–9%”) to 0.9 (“90%–100%”).

Shared Mental Models. Measuring shared mental models (SHARED) is a challenging endeavor because of the context-dependent nature of mental models (DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus 2010, Mohammed et al. 2010). Given our emphasis on the coding phase instead of the planning/evaluation phase, we focused on task-related shared mental models and measured shared mental models related to the application domain of the software, the underlying technology, the development procedure, and the overall project vision (Mohammed and Dumville 2001, He et al. 2007).<sup>6</sup> We derived six items from prior work on task-related knowledge areas that are generally relevant for software developers (He et al. 2007). The developers indicated their level of agreement regarding their work relationship with other team members (e.g., “The two of us, we have a similar understanding of our software architecture”). We used a seven-point Likert scale, with anchors from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). In the appendix, we describe the procedure we followed to measure shared mental models in more detail.

We controlled for several variables to account for alternative explanations. We controlled for the size of the team (TEAMSIZE). In addition, we controlled for the number of other teams working on the same product (MULTITEAM). To exclude interpersonal aspects within the studied teams as a possible alternative explanation for observed differences regarding shared mental models, backup behavior, and team performance, we also controlled for team member turnover (TURNOVER) and for relational team cohesion (RELATION), defined as the extent to which team members see their relationship as a “unifying force or an object of attachment in its own right” (Lawler and Yoon 1996, p. 89). To exclude the possibility that teams with less pair programming would also follow the prescriptions of the Scrum methodology with regard to daily meetings to a lower extent—thus providing an alternative explanation for differences in our dependent variables—we controlled for the rigor with which teams held daily stand-up meetings (STANDUP). Note that the vast majority of teams rigorously held daily stand-up meetings (a mean value of 6.4 on a scale from 1 to 7), also providing evidence that the teams in our sample faithfully followed Scrum. We further controlled for the influence of technological factors, such as the programming language that the teams were using through dichotomous variables that represented the use or nonuse of $C / C { + } { + } / C { \# }$ (PROGC), Java/JavaScript (PROGJAVA), and the firm’s proprietary programming language (PROGPROP). In addition, we controlled for the modularity of the architecture underlying the software that the teams were working on (MODULARITY).

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Main Findings and Contributions

Our study was motivated by the observation that prior empirical studies of pair programming mostly emphasized implications for the focal dyad and its immediate task and have rarely considered team-level performance effects or the theoretical mechanisms underlying these effects. We developed a research model accounting for mediators and moderators of the teamlevel performance effects of pair programming, and we tested our model with data collected at one of the largest enterprise software firms in the world. In particular, we analyzed quantitative data from 62 software development teams that were applying pair programming to varying extents. Our findings suggest that pair programming increases backup behavior in teams, both directly and via shared mental models. In turn, backup behavior attenuates the negative effects of task novelty on team performance.

Our study provides three main contributions. First, by studying the performance effects of pair programming at the team level, we extend prior work that has mostly assessed the performance effects for the focal dyad of programmers and their current task (e.g., Dawande et al. 2008, Balijepally et al. 2009, Mangalaraj et al 2014). Although these studies are informative—for example, when deciding between pair and solo programming for a specific task (Dyba et al.˚ 2007) or in educational contexts (Salleh et al. 2011)—existing work has rarely considered that contemporary software development is usually conducted in teams (Maruping et al. 2009a) and that the effects of pair programming are also likely to manifest at the team level.

Second, our study proposes theoretical mechanisms grounded in existing literature on teamwork and team adaptation (Salas et al. 2005, Burke et al. 2006) to explain the effect of pair programming on team performance. This is an important contribution because it begins to shed light on the cognitive structures and behavioral patterns through which pair programming affects team performance. Although prior conceptual work has pointed to the role of software development practices in influencing shared mental models as one aspect of team cognition (Yu and Petter 2014), few, if any, empirical studies have examined the effects of applying pair programming on team members shared mental models.

Third, our work also contributes by providing insights into how a global enterprise software firm creates software products for the digital age leveraging pair programming. Specifically, we present findings from ongoing teams working on the development, operation, and maintenance of enterprise software products. In such contexts, the role of task-related shared mental models in providing backup may be particularly pronounced because the software that the team works on is complex and continuously evolving, which may potentially deteriorate the shared mental models over time (Levesque et al. 2001).

## 5.2. Research Implications

Our findings and key contributions have a number of implications for research. First, our study has impli cations for the body of knowledge on the performance effects of pair programming. By shifting the focus to team-level effects and by considering how pair programming alters the cognitive structures and behavioral patterns in teams, our study adds a novel perspective and related empirical evidence that may help reconcile findings on the performance effects of pair programming (Arisholm et al. 2007). Consistent with frequent calls for theory-based empirical studies in the context of software development organizations (e.g., Dyba and Dingsøyr ˚ 2008, Dingsøyr et al. 2012), our study extends existing work on pair programming by shedding light on the theoretical mechanisms underlying the team-level effects of pair programming. This has wider implications for team-level studies in the more general context of agile software development (Maruping et al. 2009a, b). Whereas these studies examined the direct effects of a set of agile practices and some moderators, including project control, requirement changes, or expertise coordination, our findings show that accounting for mediation effects in terms of the underlying theoretical rationales provides additional critical insights (Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan 2007). By accounting for the effects of pair programming on backup behavior mediated by shared mental models, our study responds to calls to examine the effect of software development practices on team cognition (Davern et al. 2012). Our result that pair programming contributes to the shared mental models among team members may help explain findings in prior work, which have observed a “jelling” period before pair programming becomes effective (e.g., Williams et al. 2000). Our findings suggest that one reason for this jelling period may be that shared mental models need to develop for the positive effects of pair programming to unfold.

Second, our findings have implications for studying the conditions under which pair programming is most effective. We focused here on task novelty, which relates to the activities of team members and the degree to which these activities require novel capabilities and processes. This is a departure from prior work on pair programming, which has focused on task complexity as a contingency factor (Hulkko and Abrahamsson 2005, Arisholm et al. 2007, Dyba˚ et al. 2007). Theorizing about the interaction effect of backup behavior and task novelty helped us unveil a so far unrecognized reason for why the effects of pair programming vary across teams in different contexts. On the basis of our findings, we conclude that it may be beneficial for teams to rely on pair programming not necessarily for complex tasks only but more generally if the task novelty is high. This enables teams to develop the cognitive structures and behavioral patterns to react to change if necessary (i.e., if situations arise in which new capabilities and processes are required).

Our findings that task novelty creates a need for backup behavior within software development teams and that pair programming helps teams establish backup behavior have implications for wider work on agile software development, which has often recog nized the ability to adapt to change as the essence of agility (e.g., Sarker and Sarker 2009).<sup>14</sup> By drawing on team adaptation theory and integrating pair programming, backup behavior, and task novelty in one theoretical framework, we extend this prior work and provide evidence that pair programming contributes to team adaptability and thus to the core goals of agile software development.

Third, our findings have implications for prior work on the tension between control and flexibility in software development, which has emphasized the value of outcome control to balance control and flexibility (Harris et al. 2009, Maruping et al. 2009a, Ramesh et al. 2012). In software development projects spanning a predefined time, the expected outcomes are often known ex ante, and the requirements may change frequently but are usually well defined. In other contexts, such as innovating software products, pure outcome control may be more difficult to implement because the criteria for evaluating outcomes are often ambiguous (Harris et al. 2009). Prior work suggests that in such contexts, agile software development enables emergent outcome control, a control mode characterized by a continuous setting of scope boundaries and ongoing feedback (Harris et al. 2009). Our findings complement this line of work on output control and emergent outcome control by pointing to disciplined autonomy as another mechanism through which pair programming helps simultaneously achieve control and flexibility (Mithas and Kude 2017). To the extent that teams have the discretion to use softwar development practices such as pair programming, these practices reflect a voluntary application of standardized work process templates. Because pair programming nurtures backup behavior among team members, software development teams are disciplined by implicit behavioral norms, which may help firms meet company-wide goals. At the same time, work process autonomy fosters flexibility, which may contribute to creativity and innovativeness. Thus, the notion of disciplined autonomy may have wider implications for work on IT governance to address competing ten sions at multiple organizational levels to sustain digital innovation and transformation efforts in the contempo rary economy.

Fourth, our findings have implications for teamwork studies, and particularly those on backup behavior (Marks et al. 2001, Porter et al. 2003). Although prior work suggests that backup behavior in teams is particularly likely if there is a legitimate need (Porter et al. 2003), our study is among the first to examine the interaction effect of backup behavior and task novelty on team performance. Our findings suggest that task novelty can lead to legitimate needs for backup behavior, and thus, backup behavior is particularly conducive to team performance if the task novelty is high. Our findings also suggest that in situations of low task novelty, teams may, in fact, be better off not providing backup to each other. This supports prior work indicating that backup behavior may have adverse effects on teamwork if there is no legitimate need for backup (Barnes et al. 2008). Our study also has important implications for prior work on the antecedents of backup behavior. For example, work in nonsoftware contexts suggests that backup behavior can be facilitated through goal orientation and planning (Porter 2005), team training (Shuffler et al. 2011), and team composition and climate (Smith-Jentsch et al. 2009). We add to this stream by studying a work practice helping teams establish backup behavior that is specific to the software development context. To the extent that work practices comparable to pair programming are applicable beyond software development, our findings have wider implications for teamwork factors and team performance in other knowledge-intensive contexts.

## 5.3. Managerial Implications

By emphasizing the impact of pair programming on shared mental models and backup behavior, our study provides decision makers with key factors to consider when deciding on whether to apply the practice. Applying pair programming seems particularly beneficial if team members lack shared mental models and fail to provide backup to each other. However, backup behavior is not a silver bullet for software development teams, and the positive effects of providing backup on team performance may depend on task novelty. In teams facing high levels of task novelty, backup behavior—and thus pair programming’s positive effects on backup behavior—may be particularly valuable. However, in teams facing low levels of task novelty, backup behavior might actually slow down teams and thus affect performance adversely. This suggests that the decision to apply pair programming should not only depend on the characteristics of the immediate task but also consider the team and the environment in which it works.

Decision makers can learn from our study that pair programming may be particularly well suited in the context of the agile project management method Scrum. Scrum suggests self-managed, autonomous teams, which often lack a central authority that would reassign the work if needed. In such a context, backup behavior does not emerge by itself (see also Moe et al. 2009, 2010). Instead, it may be necessary to actively institutionalize work practices that foster backup behavior.

Our findings suggest that pair programming can be one such work practice that fosters backup behavior both directly and through contributing to a team’s shared mental models. Therefore, pair programming complements Scrum in that, if applied together, the two approaches can contribute to a team’s disciplined autonomy.

## 5.4. Limitations and Future Research

Some limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the results of our study. Although providing empirical insights from one of the largest enterprise software firms worldwide is a key strength of our study, and even though the organization’s software developers were heterogeneous (e.g., in terms of the software they developed), the unique context of our study may limit the generalizability of our findings. In addition, our study is cross-sectional; hence there is a need for caution in attributing causality.

We call for further research studying the team-level performance effects of pair programming in other settings. For instance, future research may study the development of other types of software products (e.g., mobile apps, computer games, embedded software for connected cars) or one-off software development projects to provide further empirical grounding or boundary conditions for our findings. Our study may also provide a template for examining other software development practices, such as refactoring or test-driven development, or work practices similar to pair programming in other nonsoftware contexts.

Future work may collect and analyze longitudinal data to assess causality and may complement our cross-sectional view on pair programming and team cognition by developing a process model of how shared mental models—and, consequently, backup behavior—emerge in teams as a consequence of applying pair programming. Such a process view may account for the agency of team members and may also consider the factors leading to higher or lower adoption rates of pair programming. A process model may also consider contextual factors related to the emergence of shared mental models facilitated by pair programming (e.g., team member turnover), which we considered as a control variable. Moreover, we assumed that pair programming and shared mental models are the shared properties of team members, such that shared mental models emerge among all team members. Future studies may examine compilation processes (e.g., in cases where pairs are not rotating or where teams are not meeting regularly), which may result in “islands of shared mental models,” with so far unknown consequences for backup behavior and team performance. More generally, future research may explicitly consider pair rotation to better understand the impact of more or less pair rotation on teamwork factors.

We also call for additional research on the mediating role of other facets of team cognition. For example, prior work has unveiled that transactive memory systems (TMS) can contribute to the success of software development teams (Faraj and Sproull 2000, Maruping et al. 2009b). TMS build on the division of cognitive work and the distribution of knowledge among team members—as opposed to shared mental models, which focus on the overlap of cognitive structures. Thus, it would be interesting to study the role of TMS as an alternative pathway linking pair programming and backup behavior.

Further theorizing about the notion of disciplined autonomy can be particularly valuable in extending the nascent literature on ambidextrous strategies (Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008, Mithas and Kude 2017, Kude et al. 2018). For example, IS researchers have pointed to the need to manage trade-offs related to knowledge management and firm performance by developing ambidextrous capabilities or IT systems (Tafti et al. 2007, Im and Rai 2008, Saldanha et al. 2017). However, the extent to which such ambidexterity can be enabled by work practices remains to be studied. Understanding these enablers of ambidexterity can be a valuable contribution to this emerging body of work, which has so far suggested structural, temporal, contextual, and IT systems–related notions of ambidexterity (Mithas and Rust 2016).

To conclude, this study provides further evidence that pair programming can be a valuable software development practice. We focus on a central aspect that prior work has mostly neglected—namely, that pair programming is usually applied in development teams and has effects beyond the pairing dyad of developers and their immediate task outcome. Given the close collaboration among team members and team autonomy inscribed in many contemporary software development practices, a focus on teamwork factors seems promising to better understand development practices and software development teams and the ability to develop software in a way that fits the requirements of digitized, high-clock-speed industries.
