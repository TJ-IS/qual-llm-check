# An Empirical Study of Users as Application Developers

Suzanne Rivard \* and Sid L. Huff

School of Business Administration, The University of Western Ontario, London, Ontario, Canada N6A 3K7

A study was conducted in ten organizations of 272 non-DP professionals (“users”) who develop their own administrative computer-based applications. The data are used to create taxonomies of such users and of the applications they develop. Findings concerning the distribution of work time, application development time, relative advantages and disadvantages of user developed applications, and other factors are summarized. Evaluations of user developed applications activity by both users and DP managers are reported, and users’ perceptions of importance and satisfaction with 18 different factors related to this activity are analyzed.

Keywords: User developed applications, user programming, end user programming, fourth generation languages.

## 1. Introduction

The past 20 years have witnessed dramatic changes in the business data processing (DP) environment. Sequential batch transaction processing technology has given way to on-line systems, database management, and decision support. At the same time, the scope of DP has grown to encompass telecommunications, office information systems, and distributed processing networks. In the midst of these rapid changes, one factor has, until recently, remained constant: the final responsibility for designing and building application systems; this rested primarily with DP departments.

Today this is being challenged, because of a new, emerging phenomenon. Individuals with little or no formal DP training are today developing and using their own computer-based applications. This phenomenon has been termed “User Developed Applications,” or UDA [15]. UDA refers to more than just the applications; it is meant to include the entire range of organizational practices and policies that form the framework within which users develop their own applications. The latter includes many types of computing activities outside the scope of “development and use of computer applications by end users.” UDA is indeed one mode of user computing and is sufficiently important in its own right to deserve special study.

The emergence of UDA is having a major impact on relationships between users and their DP departments. Whereas in the traditional system development process the user was involved only tangentially during the actual design and programming stages, UDA by definition makes the user the application developer from beginning to end. UDA is the fulfillment of Sammet's early suggestion that a necessary step in significantly raising the productivity of programmers would be “to make everybody his own programmer, just as almost everybody drives his own car rather than obtaining the services of a professional driver” [20, p. 730]. With respect to UDA, the role of DP changes from providing the core development expertise to providing assistance, education, access to development tools, to computing resources, to data, etc. That is, the DP department becomes a system facilitator instead of a system developer.

The UDA movement has developed rapidly, with widespread discussion and tentative acceptance today. Many organizations are currently experimenting with UDA. Some are beginning to commit significant sums of money to providing staff, software, computing resources and other types of UDA support. Because of this rapid development, there has been very little comparative empirical research on UDA. Much of the available literature reports on the experiences of a single organization as perceived by one person, hence only limited generalizations are possible (a number of such reports are included in the IBM Guide/Share proceedings – e.g., [18]). With the exception of the study by Rockart and Flannery [16,17], the remaining literature is mainly of the normative, opinion-based variety. Martin [10] provides a summary along these lines, supported by anecdotal evidence and brief descriptions of some UDA software tools.

There are many important issues that are not yet well understood. These include:

a) what are the common characteristics of users who successfully develop their own applications?

b) what types of applications are being developed?

c) what types of software tools are used?

d) how is user productivity affected by the applications?

e) how do users evaluate their experiences?

In the balance of this paper, the background of UDA is briefly discussed, the results of a study of UDA practices in ten organizations are reported, and some answers to the above questions are provided.

## 2. Background

The effectiveness of the data processing function has been significantly hampered by a variety of problems, including large project backlogs; projects running months or years behind schedule; system maintenance requirements that absorb a large proportion of staff resources; and completed applications which fail to meet user requirements $[12]$ . Solutions have been sought along two major routes: first, by improving the productivity of DP professionals through such techniques as application generators, structured programming, or chief programmer teams $[21]$ ; and, second, by better bridging the gap between DP professionals and users $[7]$ .

Recently, it has been suggested that application development by users themselves can provide solutions to both problems, first by relieving the DP professionals of some of their workload, and second by alleviating some of the DP-user communications problems $[10,12]$ . The idea is not new. In 1969 Sammet $[20]$ argued in favor of making everyone his own programmer. Similarly, Boehm foresaw a trend toward the day when most problems could be programmed by a user “in less than an hour … with one day of specialized training” $[2]$ . And, in a study of the future of data processing, Dolotta et al. suggested that, “this appears to be the only way, barring some startling breakthrough, in which the growth of the data processing industry can be sustained” $[5]$ .

Only quite recently has UDA become practical. Growth in computer literacy of white collar workers has helped to remove some of the previous constraints such as a general fear of using computers, or a disinclination to use a terminal because it was viewed as a low-status, technical activity. Organizations often establish an official support group, staffed by a combination of DP professionals and technically cognizant individuals drawn from various user functions, as a first step toward UDA. IBM coined the term “Information Center” for this group $[3,6,9]$ , and established the first such Information Center at their Canadian headquarters in 1975, because “all the information requirements of a widely varying user community could not be satisfied by a central group of DP professionals” $[18]$ .

The Information Center is an organizational entity whose mission is to provide services that allow users to access, manipulate, and display computer-based data. This includes provision of programming tools, training, and ongoing support. The Information Center may also be responsible for preparing special data files, ensuring data security, and making sure there is sufficient computer capacity available.

## 3. Study Methodology

The study was carried out in 10 companies, among the 150 largest Canadian companies. Initially, these 150 companies were contacted to determine the extent of their experience with UDA, and the type of UDA support they provided. Responses were obtained from 108 firms. Of these, twenty five indicated that there were ten or more users in that firm actually involved in developing their own applications. From these 25, ten were selected so as to obtain a mix of UDA experience and support approaches. Four of the companies were chosen because they had had substantial experience (2 to 3 years) with providing programming tools and support to users through a special group of DP professionals. Another three were chosen because they had had experience with UDA itself, but had chosen not to implement a formal UDA support group. The remaining three were in the process of implementing such a group. Table 1 summarizes several aspects of the firms studied.

The study involved two main phases: personal interviews, followed by a survey. The main purpose of the interviews was to explore the entire concept of UDA, as practiced in each of the ten firms. Because of the shortage of published comparative descriptive material, it was felt that a clinical study was required to help the investigators understand the appropriate questions to ask and the issues to address. Structured and unstructured interviews were conducted with DP managers, with DP professionals in charge of supporting users developing their own applications, and with users. Secondary sources of data included responses of the DP managers to a profile questionnaire [8], internal documents, and direct observation.

The survey phase was carried out using an instrument that was developed and pretested during the interview phase. The survey phase was aimed at obtaining a broader base of quantified information, so as to better understand such things as who the users are, what they do, how they are impacted by, and how they evaluate, UDA. The survey questionnaire was sent to 1074 users in the 10 companies. The overall response rate was 40%. However, for various reasons, some of the returned questionnaires were not usable. Some were returned after the cutoff date (these were used in a test for non-response bias; none was detected [14]), some were completed by individuals who did not themselves develop applications, a few respondents held positions within the firm's DP department, etc. The net usable response rate was 25% (272 usable questionnaires).

## 4. A Taxonomy of UDA Users

One of our first questions concerned the characteristics of users involved in developing applications. During the interview phase, distinct patterns emerged concerning the reasons why users developed applications, for whom they were developed, etc. This led to the delineation of three UDA user types. We named these: Micro DP Department, Staff Analyst, and Opportunity Seeker.

Micro DP Department Users. This category includes users who respond to requests from other users in their department (and sometimes from other departments). For most of them, developing applications for others is a formal duty. This work is similar in many ways to that of the main DP function, though on a much smaller scale. Micro DP Department users face some of the same problems encountered by the main DP group: users not always knowing what they want, and frequent requests for changes to applications. Development backlogs were also frequently associated with these users.

Staff Analysts. This group consists of users who perform studies that are used by other managers to control, plan, or make decisions. They have titles such as business analyst, financial analyst, or business planner. In general, the Staff Analysts develop applications that they themselves use to solve problems or provide information for others. Whereas Mico DP Department users respond to procedural requests from managers (e.g., “write a program to provide me the following report”), the Staff Analysts generally receive non-procedural requests (“carry out an analysis of the following issue”). In most cases the Staff Analysts decide whether or not to develop a computer application to perform the necessary analysis.

Opportunity Seekers. These are individuals who have considerable expertise in their own function, who often work in a managerial capacity such as supervisor or budget director, but who typically work within a staff department. They have some similarities with Staff Analyst type users, since they also are specialists. However, the Opportunity Seekers are more independent, more creative, and proactive. They generally do not wait until somebody asks for a new application, but actively identify problems which could be solved or analyzed using a computer. Opportunity Seeker users are often “fast track,” rising managers, who view computer-related skills as a job asset providing a relative advantage in their work.

This breakdown may be contrasted with earlier categorizations by McLean [12], and Rockart and Flannery [17]. McLean suggested a three-way breakdown: DP professionals, DP amateurs, and non-DP trained users. Rockart and Flannery derived a finer, six-way breakdown: nonprogramming end users, command-level users, end-user programmers, functional support personnel, end-user computing support personnel, and DP programmers. McLean's "DP professionals", and Rockart's "DP programmers" correspond quite closely to our Micro DP Department users. Mc-

Lean's “non-DP trained users,” and Rockart's “non-programming end users,” “command-level users,” and “end user computer support personnel” mainly include users who do not actually develop applications, but use applications developed by others, or provide support to others. Such individuals were not included in our study. McLean's “DP amateurs,” and Rockart's “end-user programmer” categories span both our Staff Analyst and Opportunity Seeker classes. Thus, we eliminated those individuals who did not really develop applications, and attempted to classify more precisely those who did.

The 272 questionnaires were classified using discriminant analysis. Table 4 displays the mean values of each of the classification variables. The arrows in the table indicate the presence of a statistically significant difference between groups, at the 0.05 significance level. Some of the significant differences are rather surprising. For instance, Opportunity Seeker users had significantly longer experience in developing their own applications than either of the other groups, probably because Opportunity Seeker users have been “in front of the pack” in their organizations.

Another interesting observation is the surprisingly high percentage of applications that are produced by Micro DP Department users but operated by subordinates. This suggests that Micro DP Department users are becoming specialized in their function of developing user applications.

## 5. A Taxonomy of Applications Developed by Users

One of the fundamental empirical questions we addressed was: What kinds of applications do users develop, and does this pattern vary according to the type of user? Respondents were asked to provide detailed information about one or two recent applications. From the responses and interviews, a six-way typology resulted:

1. Model applications, financial analysis and other analytical models parameterized to allow "what if" questions.

2. Transaction Processing applications, similar to Rockart & Flannery's "routinized batch" category [16,17]. These applications are run on a regular basis, and update files (which are created and controlled by the user). Some DP managers were concerned by this. Such applications were more common in some firms than in others, which is partly explained by the differences in the software and hardware tools available to users, and by the differences in the firms' control policies and procedures.

3. Data Analysis applications, which are used to perform analysis of data files, generally using statistical packages such as SPSS [13] or SAS [19]. Relatively few of these were identified and they were mainly developed by users in marketing and economic studies.

## 5.2. Application Development Duration

Figure 2 compares the time required to develop applications by the three categories of users. As expected, the applications developed by users are small, by DP standards. For all three, a small percentage took more than three months. For the Staff Analyst and Opportunity Seeker users, the mode of development time is typically “one day to one week”, whereas Micro DP Department users’ times are almost equally distributed across all six categories. Apparently Staff Analysts and Opportunity Seekers were generally not able to afford time away from their regular duties to develop larger applications, whereas the Mico DP Department users tended to seek out the larger, more challenging assignments.

While most user developed applications are small, some did require more than 3 months of effort. Many DP managers may find it surprising that users are developing systems requiring such an effort.

## 5.3. Computer Background of UDA Respondents

The survey instrument included a number of questions about the background and prior data processing experience of the respondent. About 13% indicated they had absolutely no prior knowledge of, or experience with, computers. An approximately equal percentage indicated prior use (but no programming experience). Nearly 75% reported having at least a “good knowledge” of computing methods.

An analysis of variance was performed to determine whether there was a significant difference in computer background between the three types of users. The data suggests that Micro DP Department users do tend to have a stronger background in computing and DP than do other users. However, the differences were not statistically significant.

## 6. Advantages and Disadvantages of UDA

In both interviews and questionnaires, we attempted to pinpoint what UDA users perceived to be the major advantages and disadvantages of developing their own applications.

## 6.1. Productivity Increase

Clearly, a major benefit is improved productivity. Two categories of applications were examined: one-shot, and regularly run applications. Users were asked to compare the time to develop and run the application to the time it used to take or would have taken to perform the same task otherwise. The question was asked for the one-shot application that required the longest, and shortest time to develop. Users were also asked to compare the time it took to run a typical regularly-run application (including data entry), to the time it used to take or would have taken using other means.

The results are given in Table 5. Productivity factor is the ratio of the time (as estimated by the respondent) it used to take or would have taken to carry out the task without the computer-based application, to the time it takes using the application. These results are consistent with other earlier findings [18].

## 6.2. Advantages Relative to DP Department Development

The survey respondents were asked to indicate what they felt was the most important advantage of developing their own applications (as opposed to having the DP department do it). By far the most frequently mentioned was timeliness of application development; either this alone, or in conjunction with another feature, was cited by over 50% of the respondents as the most important benefit. This finding is not at all surprising.

The users interviewed and surveyed generally understood that it may take them more time to develop an application, but it is developed much faster because of the reduced queueing time.

## 6.3. Some Disadvantages

UDA is not without problems. While 10.5% of the respondents indicated that they perceived no disadvantage to UDA, 33.1% of the users stated that their lack of expertise with programming and the use of computers was a major disadvantage.

Users indicated particularly that they felt considerable time was wasted searching for a way to accomplish a given task. Nineteen percent of the respondents indicated that UDA was too time consuming. Some explained that UDA very often infringes on their “normal” duties. On the other hand, some users (5.1%) claimed that they did not have enough time to spend on UDA because they also had other duties. An explanation for this difference involves the centrality of UDA activities to the user’s overall job: the less central, the more UDA activities are perceived as taking time away from other work. However it is viewed, the time required for UDA activities, particularly learning to use unfamiliar tools, is perceived as a problem.

Other disadvantages are related directly to lack of user expertise. Seven percent of the respondents indicated that the programs they write are not as efficient as those written by DP professionals. This is also a concern of many DP managers.

Some users indicated that they did not document their applications and are the only ones who know how the applications work. However, this and other security and validity issues, such as the lack of audit trails, were mentioned by only 7.8% of the respondents, whereas DP professionals were quick to point to these as major problem areas.

Finally, 6.8% of the respondents cited DP-related disadvantages, such as inadequate support by DP, poor response time on the mainframe, and general reluctance by DP to allow users to develop their own applications.

## 7. Users' Evaluations of UDA

Respondents indicated on a 5-point scale the perceived importance of, and their satisfaction with 18 aspects of UDA. The mean satisfaction and importance scores were calculated for each dimension, and are plotted in Figure 3 following a technique suggested by Alloway [1].

Overall, it appears that the users in this sample are reasonably satisfied with their UDA experience. None of the average satisfaction scores is lower than 3.1, on the 5-point scale. By dividing the graph into four quadrants the various aspects may be usefully categorized. ## 8. Discussion

## 8.1. The Users

From the description of each of the categories of the user taxonomy, as well as from the computer background of the users, we conclude that we have not yet reached the point at which “everybody is his own programmer” [20]. A relatively high percentage of the users belonged to the Micro DP Department category (about 40% in both interviews and survey). Moreover, nearly 75% of the survey respondents had had at least some exposure to programming languages. To pursue Sammet’s analogy further, if we compare the DP professionals to race car drivers, then most of the users who develop applications are more like chauffeurs.

What is holding up faster progress toward UDA? One frequently raised issue concerns the ease of use of the available programming tools. McCracken [11] suggests that the user's software tools should have a natural syntax, should be interfaced with a database management system, should provide a query language plus a report generator, and have the ability to insure data integrity and validity. Martin adds a call for “graphics generation capability and a common style of dialog and syntax” [10]. While certain software companies advertise some of their UDA products - often termed “fourth generation languages” - as meeting these criteria, none of the UDA tools used in the 10 companies were judged by the users to be effective along all these dimensions [15].

Most users interviewed indicated that they required relatively long learning periods and considerable practice to become proficient with the software tools. This helps explain the emergence of the Micro DP Department user category. It also helps to explain our observation that many users would use a particular programming tool for a variety of different types of applications, in some cases applications for which the tool had clearly not been designed. Rather than invest more time to learn to use a new tool, users would make do with one they already knew how to use, though not well suited for the application at hand.

As an economy measure, a number of the department heads had decided to designate one person as a local expert for a particular software tool, then funnel UDA requests to that person. In fact, a measure of the “user-friendliness” of UDA tools in a company might be the inverse of the size of the micro DP Department user group.

## 8.2. Relationships Between Users and DP Departments

Of the four aspects falling in the SUCCESS quadrant, only Ease of Use of Tools is under DP influence. On the other hand, all six aspects in the KILLER quadrant are DP-influenced.

The clear implication is that DP departments find themselves in a very challenging position. Many firms are apparently primed to give DP much of the blame when things fail, but little of the credit when they succeed. Maybe individuals tend to rate lower their satisfaction with aspects controlled by “outsiders,” while rating higher their satisfaction with aspects they control.

Availability of staff to provide user assistance may be a problem, since a small number of consultants support a much larger number of users. Moreover, in periods of UDA growth, consultants are busy training new users, and thus are unavailable for providing debugging assistance. Since users generally feel that a bug in one of their programs demands immediate attention, dissatisfaction, as well as wasted time, may result.

Insuring an appropriate computer response time is a problem in several of the firms. Often, users have to “compete” with production systems, user support staff frequently had to negotiate with the DP operations managers to insure acceptable response time.

The availability of workstations is often under the control of the DP departments. Workstations are usually provided in a central “walk-in center;” however, users would sometimes have to wait to use them. In most firms, user departments could obtain their own workstations provided they could cost-justify and pay for them. Even then, some users indicated that having to share a workstation was sometimes a problem.

In interviews, many users commented on inadequate formal training. Particular complaints included the relatively short period of time usually devoted to training with a given tool, together with the large amount of material covered during the training period. Users indicated that they did not have time to fully assimilate what they were taught.

The issue of the consultants' knowledge of the UDA software tools was raised in some of the firms studied. Some users spent a considerable amount of time using the software tools and soon became quite sophisticated. The consultants, however, did not have much opportunity to practice advanced techniques, as they were busy teaching fundamentals, preparing course material, helping to debug novices' programs, etc. Experienced users have often advanced to the point where the kinds of problems they encountered are beyond the base knowledge and experience of the consultants. Once again, delays and frustration can result.

## 8.3. Responsibility and Control Issues

Here, concerns focused on access to, and use of “corporate” data by users. In none of the ten firms were users allowed to change corporate data elements; read-only access was the norm. In half the firms, the practice was for DP to provide extracts of corporate files for users, so that users never accessed the base files. In the other firms, read access to the base files was sometimes available, otherwise extract files were used. Even the definition of “corporate data” was not widely agreed on, although this was frequently interpreted by DP to mean data related to applications that were under the DP department’s control.

A related concern involved the accuracy of the information produced by user-developed applications. As Davis has pointed out [4], many users are unable or unwilling to apply strict quality assurance to the applications they develop, leading to the likelihood of conflicting values for some of the company's key measures (e.g., department expenses). Such problems are especially worrisome in cases of user-developed transaction processing applications, since these are most likely to become imbedded in a department's operating procedures. If they produce unreliable outputs, incorrect decisions may be made, possibly causing substantial harm to the firm. Moreover, since these types of applications tend to be used repeatedly, such errors may occur many times. Many DP managers reported a sense of frustration; while such errors would clearly be the user's "fault," as data processing professionals the DP managers knew that they would also feel responsible.

Other related concerns expressed by DP managers included the lack of proper documentation, audit trails, and other operating controls (e.g., hash totals) in user developed applications. However, they also felt that to any attempt to install rigid controls over UDA – for example, requiring every user-developed application to be checked by a DP professional before it was used – would be self-defeating and probably impossible to implement. Some saw self-documenting, very-high-level programming tools as a partial answer. User education in system development practice, and publication of application development guidelines were also suggested as useful approaches. Unfortunately, all such efforts work against the primary critical success factor of UDA: the reduction of development time for smaller-scale, user oriented applications.

## 8.4. UDA Economics

The DP managers indicated that it was critical that they be able to assure top management that UDA was indeed profitable. In all the firms studied, DP was responsible for demonstrating that the UDA investment was cost-beneficial.

User productivity was shown to be improved through the introduction of UDA, and many DP managers used productivity-centered arguments as a major reason for moving ahead with UDA in their firm. On the other hand, one frequently cited problem concerns the relative expertise of users, as compared to DP professionals, in using a given software tool. Over one-third of the users surveyed indicated that their lack of programming expertise was a major disadvantage in their UDA activities; they recognised that it took them longer to arrive at a workable solution than it would a DP professional. This does not necessarily mean that the firm as a whole is making inefficient use of its human resources, since many users were paid at a lower rate than the DP professionals. Also, user-DP communications time is reduced when users develop their own applications. Finally, because users spend little time on documentation and other “DP hygiene” factors, additional resources are saved (although the longer-term cost of such shortcuts may well outweigh the savings achieved). Thus, at least in the firms studied, the net economic impact on personnel utilization is unclear, and represents an important area for further research.

A second area of economic impact concerns efficiency of user-written applications. Seven percent of the users cited application inefficiency as a major disadvantage. While basic computing power is now inexpensive, it is still not free, and some UDA software tools are notoriously inefficient. Chargeback mechanisms did provide some rough guidance to users as to when it might be cost-beneficial to improve the efficiency of an application. However, few users were sufficiently skilled in using software tools to do this (if, indeed, it was possible to do it at all without resorting to procedural languages and DP department expertise).

The impact of UDA on DP departments themselves is also an important economic issue. Some researchers have argued that a DP department benefits from UDA because its backlog of new applications and its system maintenance requirements will decrease as users take over more of the application development responsibility [18]. However, in none of the ten firms did the DP department managers indicate that either of these aspects were important reasons for their firm's initiation of UDA. In fact, none of the firms even bothered to try to measure the impact of UDA on either of these factors.

## 9. Summary

User developed applications is a new phenomenon spreading rapidly within many organizations. This article reported the results of an in-depth investigation of UDA in ten large Canadian firms. The study identified three major categories of users, and found that they differ significantly along several dimensions.

This study confirmed the prior assumption that, for users, a major advantage of UDA is the rapidity with which new computer applications can be developed (as compared to the time required by the firm's DP department). On the other hand, many users identified their lack of experience with programming and difficulty in using the available software tools as major disadvantages. The study findings also raised important issues related to the control of UDA by DP, and to the cost-benefit aspects of UDA.
